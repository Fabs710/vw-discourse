I will be direct.

## My position
As the regional state government and anchor shareholder, I **cannot support a restructuring path that relies on German plant closures or compulsory redundancies as the baseline solution**. That would violate the regional interest, damage the company’s role as an economic anchor, and undermine the co-determination balance that has long protected this group’s stability.

At the same time, I do **not** support a purely defensive stance that ignores the competitiveness problem. The company does need a real cost and capacity adjustment. So yes: labour-side contributions, productivity gains, working-time flexibility, redeployment, and temporary concessions are all legitimate parts of a settlement.

But the burden must be shared, and the industrial base must remain intact.

---

## To management
You keep saying closures and deeper headcount reductions must remain on the table because the economics require it.

I am not persuaded by that assertion yet.

What I hear from you is:
- a real cost problem,
- a real capacity problem,
- and a strong preference for maximum restructuring flexibility.

What I do **not** yet hear is proof that the company has exhausted the non-destructive instruments.

The expert has already said a no-closure package can plausibly deliver **€600m–€900m**, possibly more. That is not trivial. So I reject the idea that closures are automatically necessary.

If you want me to take closure seriously, then bring me the numbers:
- the annual savings target,
- the savings from voluntary exits and attrition,
- the savings from working-time and productivity measures,
- the savings from selective capacity consolidation,
- and the residual gap after all that.

Until then, “closures remain on the table” is not a case; it is pressure.

And I want to say this clearly: **a 2030 framework is not automatically incompatible with restructuring**. If the package is designed properly, medium-term site and job protection can coexist with a real transformation. What is incompatible with trust is using restructuring as a pretext to hollow out the domestic footprint.

---

## To the works council
I agree with you on the core principle: **no closures as the starting point, no compulsory redundancies, no dismantling of co-determination**.

That is the right line for a company that is central to a region’s industrial structure.

Where I would push you is this: the workforce side also has to help make the alternative credible. If you ask the state to defend site security, then the negotiated package must be concrete and enforceable:
- how much working-time flexibility,
- how much temporary employee contribution,
- what voluntary exit volume,
- what redeployment capacity,
- what productivity gains,
- and over what time horizon.

I will back you against closures. I will not back a package that simply says “no” without delivering a serious alternative on the numbers.

---

## To the union
Your willingness to discuss labour-side contributions is important, and I acknowledge that.

But I need to be equally frank: if the contribution is too small, too temporary, or too vague, then management will keep arguing that the gap cannot be closed without harsher measures. So the union should not just defend the principle of burden-sharing; it should help define a package that is large enough to matter.

I am prepared to support:
- temporary working-time reductions,
- bonus or financial concessions,
- voluntary exits,
- qualification and redeployment,
- and productivity measures.

I am **not** prepared to support:
- compulsory redundancies,
- plant closures,
- or an open-ended erosion of job security.

---

## To the controlling family
You are right that value destruction from endless conflict is unacceptable.

But value is also destroyed if the company sacrifices its domestic base, its skilled workforce, and its social legitimacy in the name of “decisiveness.” A company like this does not remain strong by treating its plants and employees as disposable.

If you want long-term value, then support a settlement that preserves:
- domestic sites,
- skilled industrial capacity,
- co-determination,
- and a stable workforce.

That is not sentimental. It is the basis of resilience.

---

## To institutional investors
You are right to insist that the package must be economically credible. I agree with that.

Where I disagree is with any implication that credibility automatically requires closures or a harsh site-rationalisation path. The expert’s own ranges show that a negotiated no-closure package may be sufficient, depending on the actual gap.

So my question back to you is simple:
- What is your threshold for credibility?
- What annual savings do you require?
- What level of headcount reduction would you accept without closures?
- And are you prepared to support a package that is enforceable and durable even if it avoids plant shutdowns?

If the answer is “only closures will do,” then say that plainly. But do not present it as neutral economics without proof.

---

## What I would support
I can support a settlement built on:
- voluntary exits and natural attrition,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary compensation moderation,
- productivity improvements,
- selective capacity consolidation **without closures**,
- and investment commitments for future technologies and products.

I would also support a strong monitoring mechanism through co-determination, so that implementation is tracked and savings are real.

What I will **not** support:
- German plant closures as a baseline,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or a deal that treats regional employment as the variable to be sacrificed first.

## Bottom line
My line is unchanged:

- **No plant closures as the starting point**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No erosion of co-determination**
- **Yes to real restructuring**
- **Yes to measurable employee-side contributions**
- **Yes to a durable settlement that restores competitiveness while preserving the company’s regional role**

If management wants state support, it must prove that non-destructive measures are insufficient. Until then, I will continue to use the state’s blocking position to protect jobs, sites, and the company’s long-term anchor role in the region.
