We share the core line already stated by the works council: **no plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, and no destruction of the collective wage framework**.

Management’s current approach is not a restructuring in the collective-bargaining sense; it is a demand that the workforce absorb the main pain while ownership and management seek to preserve strategic room for themselves. That is not acceptable.

Let me be very clear on what we can and cannot support:

- **We can support** a negotiated contribution from labour.
- **We can support** temporary capacity reductions.
- **We can support** working-time adjustments, redeployment, training, and productivity measures.
- **We can support** forgoing bonuses or other negotiated financial contributions if they are part of a balanced package.
- **We can support** a durable settlement that secures the sites and gives the company breathing room.

But we **cannot** support:
- plant closures in Germany,
- compulsory layoffs,
- open-ended insecurity,
- or a one-sided cost-cutting package that shifts the burden almost entirely onto employees.

If management genuinely wants a solution, then it must put something credible on the table in return:
1. **Binding site guarantees**
2. **Job security guarantees**
3. **A fair sharing of sacrifice**
4. **Concrete investment commitments for future products and the transition**

The company’s competitiveness problem is real. But competitiveness will not be restored by breaking trust, gutting the workforce, and turning industrial relations into a zero-sum exercise. That path would damage morale, productivity, and the long-term viability of the brand.

So our position is straightforward: **we are prepared to negotiate hard on labour costs and capacity, but only within a framework that protects jobs, protects sites, and preserves the collective bargaining standards that define fair industrial relations.** If that framework is rejected, then the conflict will escalate.
