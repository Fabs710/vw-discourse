I will be equally direct.

## To the works council and union
You keep repeating “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no breaking guarantees” as if stating red lines were the same as solving the restructuring problem. It is not.

I accept that:
- a cosmetic deal is worthless,
- the company needs durable change,
- labour-side contributions are part of any serious package,
- and prolonged conflict would damage everyone.

But I do **not** accept your core conclusion that plant closures and deeper headcount reductions can simply be ruled out.

The expert’s own scenario work shows exactly why:  
a no-closure package may deliver **substantial** savings, but whether it is **sufficient** depends on the actual size of the structural gap. And that gap has still not been closed on the numbers.

### Where I agree
I agree that:
- voluntary exits matter,
- attrition matters,
- redeployment matters,
- working-time flexibility matters,
- productivity gains matter,
- and capacity can sometimes be reduced without an outright closure.

Those are all useful instruments.

### Where I disagree
I disagree that these instruments, by themselves, can be assumed to deliver a durable solution at the scale required. You are asking us to accept that:
- every site stays open,
- no compulsory redundancies occur,
- guarantees remain intact through 2030,
- and yet the company somehow still achieves a structural cost reset.

That is not a plan. That is a wish.

If you want me to treat no-closure as credible, then you need to show:
1. the annual savings target,
2. the savings already secured,
3. the savings from each of your preferred measures,
4. and the residual gap that remains after those measures.

Until that is done, closures remain a legitimate restructuring instrument, not a rhetorical threat.

## To the state government
I respect your role and I acknowledge your desire to preserve regional stability. But “no closures as the starting point” cannot become an absolute political veto over necessary restructuring.

You say closures are not the only economically serious path. That is true.  
But neither is a no-closure settlement automatically economically serious.

The company is facing:
- shrinking European demand,
- intense low-cost competition,
- overcapacity,
- and a cost base that is still above target.

If we freeze the footprint and protect every legacy guarantee, we do not solve that problem. We postpone it — and at a higher cost.

So I reject any framing that treats the domestic footprint as untouchable. The domestic footprint must become economically viable, not just politically preserved.

## To the controlling family
You are right about one thing: a destructive confrontation would be value-destroying. I agree.

But value is also destroyed by pretending that an uncompetitive structure can be preserved indefinitely through voluntary measures and goodwill. That is not long-term value protection. That is delay.

If the family wants credibility in capital markets, it needs to support a restructuring that actually changes the economics. That means:
- real capacity reduction,
- real labour-cost relief,
- real headcount adjustment,
- and a package that restores margin discipline.

If a closure-free alternative can genuinely do that, fine — prove it.  
But I will not pretend it is proven when it is not.

## To institutional investors
You are the closest to our economic logic, and I understand your insistence on hard, enforceable savings.

Where I push back is your tendency to treat closures and site rationalisation as just another option among many. In reality, if the structural gap is large enough, closure-equivalent capacity withdrawal becomes economically necessary.

The expert has already indicated the threshold:  
- around **€600m** in annual structural savings, a no-closure package may be feasible;
- around **€800m–€900m**, it becomes much harder but still possible with deep concessions;
- above that, closures or closure-equivalent measures become much more likely.

So the question is not ideological. It is whether the company can credibly reach the required level **without** structural footprint change.

If the answer is yes, I am open.  
If the answer is no, then I will not sign off on a soft compromise that leaves the company underperforming.

## Where management stands
Let me state this plainly:

We are **not** backing away from our core line.

We need:
- a **durable reduction in German production capacity**,
- a **substantial reduction in labour costs**,
- a **material reduction in headcount**,
- and a restructuring that restores the brand’s return-on-sales path and frees funds for future products and technology to 2030.

I am open on the mechanism:
- closures,
- consolidation,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- working-time changes,
- employee contributions,
- or a combination.

But I am **not** open to:
- a package that keeps every site untouched,
- a blanket exclusion of compulsory headcount reduction,
- or a 2030 guarantee that locks in today’s cost structure regardless of economics.

That is the line.

## What would move us
We are not asking for surrender. We are asking for a **credible, quantified alternative**.

If the workforce side wants us to move away from closure and deeper structural reduction, then it must put forward a package that is:
- sufficiently large,
- enforceable,
- durable,
- and clearly enough to close the gap.

That package must show:
- concrete headcount reduction,
- measurable labour-cost relief,
- meaningful capacity consolidation,
- and a verified path back to competitiveness.

If it cannot do that, then closures and stronger restructuring remain on the table.
