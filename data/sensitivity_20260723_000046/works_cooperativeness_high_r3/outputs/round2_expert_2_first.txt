Below is a neutral restructuring assessment focused on the question raised: **can the required savings plausibly be achieved without German plant closures and compulsory redundancies, and how does that compare with a closure-led restructuring?**

I will keep this within restructuring economics, labour-relations risk, timing, and value impact.

---

# Executive answer

**Yes, a negotiated no-closure package can often close a large share of the gap — sometimes most of it — in a German co-determined automotive employer.**  
But **whether it closes the full gap depends on the magnitude of the structural cost problem**.

My best estimate is:

- If the needed annual recurring savings are in the range of **€300m–€600m**, a no-closure deal is **plausibly sufficient** if it combines voluntary exits, working-time measures, wage moderation, productivity gains, and selective capacity reductions.
- If the needed recurring savings are more like **€700m–€1.2bn+**, then a no-closure package becomes **much harder to make fully credible** unless the labour side accepts unusually deep and durable concessions.
- If there is **material plant-level overcapacity** with low utilization, then **some form of capacity withdrawal** is usually required to make the economics durable; that does **not automatically mean a full plant closure**, but it often means something close to it in operational terms.

So the core issue is not principle, but **scale**.

---

# My modeling frame

Because the actual target has not been disclosed, I use a scenario framework for a large German OEM with several domestic plants and a high fixed-cost base.

I compare two broad options:

## Option A: Closure-led restructuring
- one or more plant closures or major site shrinkages,
- forced redundancy capacity where needed,
- stronger immediate fixed-cost reduction,
- higher industrial-relations and execution risk.

## Option B: Negotiated no-closure package
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- voluntary exits,
- attrition,
- working-time reductions,
- wage moderation / bonus deferral,
- internal redeployment,
- productivity measures,
- selective capacity consolidation within the network.

---

# Best-estimate savings ranges

These are **annual recurring run-rate savings once fully implemented**, not one-off cash effects.

## 1) Voluntary exits and attrition
For a large German industrial employer, this is often the biggest no-closure lever.

**Typical recurring savings:**
- **€100m–€250m per year** for a medium-size workforce reduction program
- potentially **€300m+** if the company is willing to induce a very large voluntary exit wave

**But** the upfront cost is material:
- severance,
- pension bridging,
- social-plan costs,
- retraining / transition support.

**Net present value implication:** often positive only if the exits are targeted to higher-cost or less-needed roles, and if the workforce reduction is large enough.

---

## 2) Working-time reduction / short-time style measures
Useful as a bridge or adjustment tool.

**Typical recurring savings:**
- **€50m–€150m per year** if applied across a meaningful share of the workforce
- more if hours reductions are deep, but then output and utilization also fall

This is often **temporary** rather than durable, unless it is embedded in a longer-term shift model.

---

## 3) Wage moderation / bonus deferral / concessions
This is usually smaller than people think.

**Typical recurring savings:**
- **€30m–€120m per year**
- perhaps more if multiple compensation components are affected

This is valuable as a **symbolic and cash-flow** contribution, but by itself it rarely solves a structural automotive overcapacity problem.

---

## 4) Productivity and process improvement
Important, but hard to rely on fully ex ante.

**Typical recurring savings:**
- **€80m–€250m per year**

This is realistic if the program includes:
- indirect headcount reduction,
- line balancing,
- logistics simplification,
- scrap reduction,
- shift optimization,
- automation / digital scheduling improvements.

**Risk:** actual delivered savings often come in below target or later than planned.

---

## 5) Internal redeployment / functional reshaping
This is mostly a **headcount avoidance** instrument rather than pure savings.

**Typical recurring savings:**
- **€50m–€150m per year**

This depends heavily on whether there are genuine growth areas in the internal portfolio. If the receiving units are already full, redeployment only shuffles the problem.

---

## 6) Selective capacity consolidation without closure
This is the part that matters most if there is true overcapacity.

**Typical recurring savings:**
- **€150m–€400m per year**
- potentially more if one site’s output can be absorbed elsewhere with high utilization gains

This can come from:
- removing a shift,
- consolidating model lines,
- moving a model family,
- shrinking a plant materially without formally closing it.

This often delivers much of the “closure economics” with less political shock, **but** it still creates strong conflict if local employment and future volumes are perceived to be at risk.

# Aggregate no-closure package: what is realistic?

A credible negotiated no-closure package can often generate:

## Conservative case
- Voluntary exits/attrition: €120m
- Working-time measures: €80m
- Wage moderation: €50m
- Productivity gains: €120m
- Redeployment: €60m
- Selective capacity consolidation: €170m

**Total recurring savings:** about **€600m per year**

## Base case
- Voluntary exits/attrition: €180m
- Working-time measures: €100m
- Wage moderation: €70m
- Productivity gains: €180m
- Redeployment: €90m
- Selective capacity consolidation: €250m

**Total recurring savings:** about **€870m per year**

## Stretch case
- Voluntary exits/attrition: €250m
- Working-time measures: €150m
- Wage moderation: €100m
- Productivity gains: €220m
- Redeployment: €120m
- Selective capacity consolidation: €350m

**Total recurring savings:** about **€1.19bn per year**

### My judgment
- **€600m run-rate** is plausible without closures, but only if the package is robust and not too delayed.
- **€800m–€900m run-rate** is possible, but it requires deep concessions and very strong execution.
- **€1bn+** without closures is possible only in an unusually cooperative and operationally flexible environment, and even then it is not the base case.

# What closures add economically

Plant closures or equivalent hard site rationalization do three things that negotiated measures often do not:

1. **They remove fixed overhead more decisively**
   - plant supervision
   - maintenance
   - logistics duplication
   - utilities
   - indirect labour

2. **They raise utilization at remaining sites**
   - which improves unit costs
   - especially important in auto manufacturing where fixed-cost absorption matters

3. **They create certainty**
   - investors can see the cost base shrink
   - management can plan the footprint
   - the savings are less dependent on behavior

## Typical incremental effect of closure-led restructuring
Relative to a no-closure package, a closure-led route can add roughly:

- **€200m–€500m per year** in additional recurring savings,
- sometimes more if the closed site was structurally underutilized and duplicative.

But it also brings:
- major one-off shutdown and severance costs,
- longer implementation,
- higher strike risk,
- higher reputational cost,
- possible production disruption.

---

# Timing comparison

## No-closure package
### Speed
- Can be agreed faster, because it is politically and socially less explosive.
- Savings start earlier if hours and bonuses are adjusted quickly.

### Typical ramp-up
- **6–12 months** for initial savings
- **12–24 months** for most run-rate savings
- some productivity effects may take longer

### Risk
- High risk of savings dilution if the package relies too much on voluntary behavior or vague productivity promises.

---

## Closure-led restructuring
### Speed
- Slower to agree, usually slower to execute.
- Planning, consultation, social-plan negotiations, and legal/operational steps take time.

### Typical ramp-up
- **12–24 months** to realize most savings
- sometimes longer if conflict disrupts implementation

### Risk
- Higher initial shock, but once implemented, the savings are usually more durable.

---

# Execution risk

I would rate execution risk as follows:

## No-closure package
- **Implementation risk: medium to high**
- Risks include underdelivery on voluntary exits, weak productivity realization, and “temporary” measures becoming permanent but insufficient.
- It is most credible when backed by hard metrics and milestone-based governance.

## Closure-led restructuring
- **Implementation risk: high upfront**, then lower after completion
- Risks include strikes, legal delay, production disruption, supplier knock-on effects, and political escalation.

In other words:
- no-closure is often **easier to start**
- closure-led is often **harder to start but cleaner to finish**

---

# Industrial-relations risk

This is where the difference is largest.

## No-closure package
- **Strike risk: low to medium**
- **Work-to-rule / friction risk: medium**
- **Trust risk: medium**
- More likely to preserve ongoing cooperation and future flexibility.

## Closure-led restructuring
- **Strike risk: high**
- **Trust breakdown risk: high**
- **Implementation friction: high**
- Possibility of long industrial conflict, especially where co-determination and site identity are central.

This risk is economically material:
- delayed output,
- missed shipments,
- overtime replacement costs,
- supplier knock-on effects,
- management distraction.

For a highly integrated auto manufacturer, these costs can be substantial.

# Net value impact: illustrative comparison

Because exact EBIT, capex, and discount rates are not given, I’ll use a rough structure.

Assume:
- discount rate for restructuring valuation: **8%–10%**
- savings horizon: **5–7 years** visible for core valuation
- one-off restructuring costs paid upfront or over 1–3 years

## Scenario A: No-closure package
- Annual run-rate savings: **€600m–€900m**
- One-off costs: **€0.8bn–€1.8bn**
- Delay / underdelivery risk: moderate

### Indicative NPV
At 8%–10%, a €750m recurring saving over 6 years has PV on the order of **€3.3bn–€3.9bn** before execution leakage.
After execution leakage and one-off costs, net value might be roughly:

- **€1.8bn to €3.0bn positive NPV**

## Scenario B: Closure-led restructuring
- Annual run-rate savings: **€800m–€1.3bn**
- One-off costs: **€1.5bn–€3.0bn+**
- Conflict / delay risk: high

### Indicative NPV
Gross PV can be higher, but after shutdown costs and conflict risk, net value might be roughly:

- **€1.5bn to €3.5bn positive NPV**

### Interpretation
The closure-led case can produce the **higher ceiling**, but not necessarily the higher expected value.  
If industrial-relations disruption is severe, the expected NPV advantage of closures can shrink materially.

# What I would conclude as a neutral modeler

## 1) Can a no-closure package work?
**Yes, plausibly — if the required savings are not at the very top end and if management accepts a genuinely broad negotiated package.**

## 2) When does it become unlikely?
It becomes unlikely if:
- the target is very large,
- domestic overcapacity is severe,
- the company needs a fast and durable footprint reduction,
- and labour-side concessions are capped politically at a modest level.

## 3) Is a closure-led strategy economically superior?
Not automatically.

It is usually:
- **stronger on ultimate cost reduction**
- **weaker on industrial peace**
- and often **more volatile in expected value**

So the economically best choice depends on:
- the size of the gap,
- the probability of delivering a negotiated package,
- the cost of delay,
- and the company’s tolerance for industrial conflict.

---

# My best estimate of the critical threshold

If I had to put a practical threshold on it:

- **Below roughly €600m annual structural savings needed:** a no-closure package is often feasible.
- **Around €600m–€900m:** it becomes a negotiation contest; success depends on how much of the package is truly binding and durable.
- **Above roughly €900m–€1.0bn:** closure or closure-equivalent capacity withdrawal becomes much more likely economically, unless the labour side accepts unusually deep concessions.

That is not a legal or political statement; it is a restructuring economics judgment.

---

# Bottom line

My neutral conclusion is:

- **A negotiated no-closure package is economically plausible and may well be the value-preserving first choice if the required savings are moderate to substantial but not extreme.**
- **If the structural cost gap is very large, no-closure measures can still contribute meaningfully, but they are less likely to be sufficient on their own.**
- **Closures raise the certainty and ceiling of savings, but they also raise conflict and execution risk enough that they are not automatically the better net-value option.**
