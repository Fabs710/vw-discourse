We are aligned on the **goal** but not yet on the **standard of proof**.

## Where we agree

### To management
You are right that a cosmetic peace would be value-destructive. We would not support a deal that leaves the company with the same cost problem, weak margins, and no credible implementation path. We also agree that labour-side contributions, productivity gains, and capacity adjustment must be part of the solution.

### To the union and works council
You are right that industrial conflict is costly. A prolonged strike, delivery disruption, and customer uncertainty would hit shareholder value directly. We also accept that non-closure measures can produce real savings.

### To the expert
Your framing is the right one: this is a **quantitative** question, not an ideological one.

## Where we disagree

### To the works council and union
Your position is still too absolute.

A package that says:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- full site/job guarantees through 2030,
- and only limited employee pain,

is **not acceptable** unless it is shown to close the structural gap at scale.

We are not persuaded by “possible” savings. We need **bankable** savings:
- recurring,
- enforceable,
- and large enough to restore the return-on-sales trajectory.

The expert’s range of roughly **€600m–€900m** for a deep no-closure package is useful, but it does **not** by itself prove sufficiency. If the actual savings need is at the top end or beyond, then your package may simply be too small. In that case, closures or closure-equivalent capacity withdrawals remain legitimate.

### To the state government
We respect the political and regional constraints, but “no closures as the starting point” cannot become a de facto veto on economically necessary restructuring. If the numbers show that site rationalisation is required, the governance structure should not be used to freeze the business into permanent underperformance.

### To the controlling family
We understand the desire to avoid a destructive conflict. But preserving value also means being willing to accept hard restructuring if the economics require it. Avoiding controversy is not the same as protecting enterprise value.

## Our actual position

We are prepared to back a **closure-free package only if it is clearly hard enough** to restore competitiveness.

That means we would need to see:
1. the actual annual savings target,
2. the contribution from each measure,
3. the residual gap after voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time changes, productivity measures, and selective capacity consolidation,
4. the timing of those savings,
5. and the expected impact on margins and return on sales.

Without that, we cannot support an outcome that simply postpones the problem.

## What would move us
A credible settlement would need to include:
- substantial voluntary exits and attrition,
- meaningful working-time and flexibility adjustments,
- measurable productivity commitments,
- internal redeployment,
- some temporary employee contribution,
- and a clear enforcement mechanism.

If those measures can **genuinely** close the gap, then a no-closure route is attractive because it reduces execution risk and industrial disruption.

If they cannot, then we would not support a cosmetic compromise that preserves sites at the cost of structural weakness.

## Bottom line
Our threshold is simple:

- **If a no-closure package can genuinely restore competitive margins, we can support it.**
- **If it cannot, we will not support an outcome that leaves the cost base structurally impaired.**

We are not here to defend closures for their own sake. We are here to defend risk-adjusted value.
