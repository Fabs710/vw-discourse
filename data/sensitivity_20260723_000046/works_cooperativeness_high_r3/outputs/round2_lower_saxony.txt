I will be equally direct.

The state government does **not** accept the management position that plant closures and compulsory redundancies are the necessary baseline. Nor do we accept the idea that a binding job and site protection framework through 2030 is somehow “incompatible” with restructuring. That is not a given; it is a choice of instrument and burden-sharing.

At the same time, I do **not** support a purely defensive posture that ignores the competitiveness problem. The company does need a durable reduction in cost and capacity, and labour-side contributions will likely be part of any serious settlement. But that contribution must be structured in a way that preserves the industrial base, protects regional employment, and avoids a permanent erosion of co-determination.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, investors, and the other shareholder side on one point: **a cosmetic deal is worthless**.  
If the package does not materially improve the company’s economics, it will fail later and create even more damage.

I also agree that there must be:
- a **quantified savings target**,
- a **durable implementation path**,
- and **real contributions from all sides**, not only employees.

## Where I disagree
I disagree fundamentally with the idea that the only way to get there is through closures or compulsory redundancies.

That is not the only economically serious path. It is simply the most socially destructive one.

The state’s position is that the company must first exhaust a package built around:
- voluntary exits,
- natural attrition,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time adjustments,
- temporary capacity reductions,
- training and qualification,
- productivity improvements,
- and a negotiated, time-bound labour contribution.

If those measures are properly designed, they can deliver meaningful savings **without** sacrificing the company’s German footprint.

## To management
You keep asking for a “minimum package” without closures. The answer is: bring the numbers, not just the threat.

If you can demonstrate that non-destructive measures truly cannot close the gap, then we will examine that claim. But you have not yet shown that closures are necessary rather than preferred.

And I will be very clear:  
A restructuring that relies on threatening sites into submission is not a credible governance strategy for a company that is a central pillar of the regional economy.

You say 2030 guarantees are too restrictive. I say the opposite: without credible medium-term protection, there is no trust, no workforce acceptance, and no stable implementation. A company like this cannot be turned around through permanent insecurity.

## To the works council and union
Your insistence on no closures and no compulsory redundancies is legitimate. I support that as the starting position, not as a bargaining gimmick.

But I also expect the workforce side to be serious about the hard part: there must be measurable contributions. That means we should not talk only in principles. We need concrete volumes:
- how much working-time flexibility,
- how much wage moderation or bonus deferral,
- how much redeployment,
- how much productivity gain,
- and over what period.

If labour wants the state to defend site security, then labour must help make the alternative package enforceable and credible.

## To the controlling family
You are right that value destruction from endless conflict is unacceptable. But value is also destroyed by pushing through a restructuring that tears up the company’s social contract and undermines its domestic base.

So I would urge you to use your governance weight to support a solution that preserves:
- German plants,
- employment stability,
- co-determination,
- and long-term industrial capability.

A strong regional footprint is not a sentimental preference. It is part of the company’s resilience, skill base, and legitimacy.

## To institutional investors
You are correct that the result must be economically credible. But your definition of credibility seems too narrowly tied to immediate capacity cuts and headcount reduction.

A credible package can also be:
- legally binding,
- quantitatively sufficient,
- and operationally enforceable,
while still avoiding closures and forced layoffs.

What I need from you is not just “more reduction,” but a clear statement of what magnitude of savings would satisfy you **and** whether you would accept a package built around voluntary and negotiated instruments if it reaches that magnitude.

## The state’s line
Let me state it plainly:

- **No plant closures as the starting point**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Preservation of co-determination**
- **Binding site and job guarantees must remain on the table**
- **Labour can and should contribute, but fairly**
- **Management and ownership must also accept responsibility**
- **The domestic industrial base is not disposable**

If management wants state support, it must bring forward a plan that satisfies those conditions. If it cannot, then it should not expect political backing for a restructuring that weakens the company’s role as a regional anchor.

## [SUMMON: Industrial restructuring and labour-cost expert]
We need a neutral, quantified assessment of whether the required savings can be achieved without closures and compulsory redundancies. The discussion is currently too abstract. An independent expert should model:
- savings from voluntary exits and attrition,
- working-time and productivity measures,
- capacity consolidation without site closure,
- and the residual gap versus management’s target.

Until that analysis is on the table, I will not accept the claim that closures are unavoidable.

## Bottom line
We are prepared to support a hard restructuring.  
We are **not** prepared to support a restructuring that destroys jobs and sites in Germany as the price of adjustment.

If management wants a deal, it should come back with a quantified alternative that preserves the industrial base and shares the burden fairly. If it cannot, then it is asking the state to endorse a social and economic contraction, and that is not something we will do.
