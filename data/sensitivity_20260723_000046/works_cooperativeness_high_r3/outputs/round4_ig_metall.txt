## To management
You are still trying to convert a real competitiveness problem into a one-sided restructuring story: closures, forced headcount cuts, and weakened labour standards first, with “credibility” used as the justification.

I do **not** accept that framing.

What I **do** accept is:
- the company needs a durable solution,
- a cosmetic deal would be worthless,
- labour-side contributions are part of any serious package,
- productivity, flexibility, and capacity adjustment may all be needed,
- and prolonged conflict would damage everyone.

What I **do not** accept is:
- compulsory redundancies,
- plant closures as the default instrument,
- or a restructuring that shifts the pain mainly onto employees while management and owners preserve all strategic flexibility.

You keep saying the economics must lead. Fine. Then prove the economics **of your preferred instrument**.

The expert has already said a negotiated no-closure package can plausibly deliver roughly **€600m–€900m annually**, possibly more. That means your claim that closures are necessarily required is **not proven**. It may be your preferred route, but preference is not necessity.

So here is the standard I will hold you to:
1. show the actual annual savings target,
2. show the residual gap after voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time changes, productivity measures, and selective capacity consolidation,
3. show why that gap cannot be closed without destroying sites or jobs,
4. and show how your package avoids turning workers into the sole adjustment variable.

Until you do that, closures remain a management demand, not an unavoidable fact.

## To institutional investors
You are right about one thing: we cannot accept a fake compromise that leaves the company uncompetitive. But you are wrong if you think “meaningful restructuring” automatically means closures or forced layoffs.

The expert’s model is exactly why your position is too blunt. It shows that a no-closure package can be economically plausible, depending on the scale of the gap and the strictness of implementation. So your real question should not be “closures or nothing?” It should be:
- what level of recurring savings is actually required,
- what part can be secured through negotiated instruments,
- and what residual gap remains after those have been exhausted?

If your threshold is that the package must be deep and enforceable, then say so in numbers. Don’t hide behind generic demands for “credible margins” while ignoring the industrial-relations and social costs of destabilising the workforce.

A deal that triggers strikes, undermines co-determination, and damages production continuity is **not** financially credible just because it looks sharper on a spreadsheet.

## To the controlling family
You say you want to protect long-term value and avoid a destructive conflict. On that, we agree.

But then you must stop treating the workforce as the easiest lever and start treating labour as a partner in burden-sharing. If the family genuinely wants long-term value, it should support:
- German plants,
- skilled industrial capacity,
- co-determination,
- and stable employment relations.

That is not sentimental. It is what keeps the company viable in the long run.

Backing a hard closure-first strategy may look decisive, but it also risks years of resentment, industrial conflict, and damage to the brand. That is not value protection; that is value destruction with a different label.

## To the state government
Your position is the closest to ours, and I appreciate that you reject closures and compulsory redundancies as the starting point.

But I want to be equally clear: “no closures as the starting point” must not become “closures later if management pushes hard enough.” That loophole would simply move the conflict into a second round after trust has already been broken.

So yes, the state should insist on:

- no compulsory redundancies,

At the same time, I agree that the workforce side must contribute seriously. That is why we have already signalled openness to:
- temporary working-time reductions,
- bonus or financial concessions,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- training,
- and productivity measures.

But that contribution must be part of a **balanced** package, not a surrender package.
