I agree with the works council on the **red lines**: no plant closures as a bargaining starting point, no compulsory redundancies, and no deal that shifts the whole burden onto the workforce.

But I want to be equally clear to everyone: **a durable settlement will require measurable employee-side contributions**. We are not here to defend every existing cost item unchanged. We are here to defend **jobs, the collective wage framework, and fair burden-sharing**.

### To management
You keep saying the only credible path is closures, forced headcount cuts, and deeper labour-cost reductions. I do **not** accept that as a given.

If you want to claim that alternative instruments cannot deliver the needed savings, then show the numbers:
- How much capacity reduction is required?
- How much cost reduction is required?
- What savings come from voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time adjustments, and productivity measures?
- What gap remains after those measures?

Until that is demonstrated, “closures remain on the table” is not a plan; it is a threat.

That said, I will not pretend there is no restructuring problem. There is one. So here is our position:

- We can negotiate **temporary working-time reductions**.
- We can negotiate **forgoing bonuses** or other financial concessions.
- We can negotiate **productivity and flexibility measures**.
- We can negotiate **voluntary** workforce reduction instruments.
- We can negotiate **capacity reductions** if they are done without destroying sites.

What we cannot negotiate away is:
- compulsory redundancies,
- plant closures,
- open-ended insecurity,
- or the collective wage framework itself.

### To institutional investors
You are right on one point: a cosmetic deal is useless. But you are wrong if you think the only credible reduction path is forced layoffs and closures.

There is a difference between:
- **real structural improvement**, and
- **a socially destructive shortcut**.

The workforce is prepared to contribute, but those contributions must be part of a balanced package. If you want support from labour, then the company must quantify the savings target and accept that savings can come from multiple instruments, not just job destruction.

### To the controlling family
You say you want value protection, and I understand that. But value is not protected by crushing morale, undermining co-determination, and triggering a prolonged industrial conflict. That destroys production, trust, and long-term capability.

If the family wants a stable enterprise, then it should back a settlement that includes:
- binding site security,
- a no-compulsory-redundancy commitment,
- a negotiated labour contribution,
- and clear investment commitments in future products.

That is the kind of package that preserves enterprise value. Not a confrontation that makes employees pay while ownership keeps strategic flexibility untouched.

### To the state government
We welcome your stance that closures should not be the starting point and that there should be no forced redundancies. But we need to be careful not to turn “as far as possible” into a loophole for management to come back later and demand the very things we are rejecting now.

If the state uses its blocking position, it should do so to support a package with:
- protected sites,
- protected jobs,
- fair contributions from all sides,
- and a serious industrial strategy.

### On the concrete package
If the company genuinely wants a deal, then this is the kind of framework we can discuss:

1. **No compulsory redundancies**
2. **No German plant closures**
3. **Job and site guarantees for a defined period**
4. **Temporary capacity reductions and working-time measures**
5. **Employee financial contribution, limited and time-bound**
6. **Management and ownership also contribute**
7. **Investment commitments for future products and technologies**
8. **A jointly monitored implementation path through co-determination**

That is a fair basis. It does not freeze the company. It does not deny reality. But it also does not sacrifice the workforce as the sole adjustment variable.

### Bottom line
We are willing to negotiate hard on **capacity, flexibility, and labour cost**. We are **not** willing to accept:
- closures,
- forced layoffs,
- or a one-sided restructuring built on employees bearing all the pain.

If management wants an agreement, it must come back with a package that proves fairness, not just pressure. If it does not, then it should expect industrial resistance, not compliance.
