## My position
As controlling shareholder, I want the company restored to a **competitive, margin-earning, investable** position. I do **not** want a settlement that merely buys social calm while leaving the structural cost problem intact. Nor do I want a value-destructive confrontation that damages the brand, production, and the long-term franchise.

So my standard is simple:
- the restructuring must be **real**,
- the savings must be **durable**,
- the implementation must be **credible**,
- and the outcome must **not permanently impair the enterprise or ownership control**.

That is the lens through which I judge every proposal.

---

## To the works council
I agree with you on one important point: a cosmetic deal is worthless. You are right to say that trust cannot be rebuilt by slogans.

Where I disagree is your assumption that closures and compulsory redundancies are therefore unnecessary by definition. They may not be the only tools, but you have **not yet shown** that the alternative package is sufficient at the scale required.

Your proposed mix:
- attrition,
- redeployment,
- working-time changes,
- training,
- productivity measures,
- voluntary exits,
- and capacity reductions without closures,

is commercially plausible. I do not dismiss it. But plausibility is not enough.

What I need to see is:
1. the **annual savings target**;
2. the **quantified contribution** from each instrument;
3. the **residual gap** after those measures;
4. the **time needed** to realize the savings;
5. and the **risk of underdelivery**.

Until that is on the table, I cannot accept “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, 2030 guarantees” as a complete solution. That is a red line, not a restructuring result.

Also, I need to be clear: a guarantee framework that locks in the current footprint regardless of economics would be unacceptable to me. Long-term value cannot be protected by freezing structural inefficiency.

---

## To the union
You are closer to a workable position than the works council, because you at least accept that labour-side contributions are part of the answer. I acknowledge that.

But I still think your package is incomplete unless the contributions are **material** and **durable**. Temporary working-time reductions, bonus deferrals, and voluntary exits help, but they may not be enough to restore margins if the domestic cost base remains too high.

So I would ask you a direct question:  
What level of annual recurring relief do you believe labour can realistically deliver without closures or compulsory redundancies?

If the answer is modest, then I will not pretend that the gap is closed. If the answer is substantial, then I am open to it — but I want it quantified and enforceable, not aspirational.

---

## To management
You are correct that the problem is structural. You are correct that a cosmetic compromise would be value-destructive. And you are right to insist on numbers.

Where I am not yet with you is on the implied conclusion that closures must remain a default. They may remain an option, but not a first principle. In a co-determined, politically constrained governance structure, the burden is on management to prove that the no-closure route is insufficient.

I will support management in pressing for a **hard, quantified restructuring**. I will not support a vague peace that leaves the company weak. But I also will not authorize a governance path that rushes into closures before the alternative package is fully tested.

So I want management to deliver a disciplined comparison:
- closure-led restructuring,
- versus negotiated no-closure restructuring,

with clear assumptions on:
- savings,
- one-off costs,
- implementation speed,
- industrial-relations risk,
- and net enterprise value.

That is the only serious way to proceed.

---

## To the state government
I understand your political responsibility and your concern for the regional base. That matters.

I also agree that closures should not be the starting point. But I do **not** accept that as a permanent veto. If the numbers show that a closure-equivalent capacity withdrawal is needed to restore competitiveness, then the company cannot be forced to carry a structurally uncompetitive footprint indefinitely.

Your position is most useful when it protects against unnecessary destruction, not when it blocks economically necessary change. So I can work with you on a negotiated, non-disruptive route — but only if it delivers real savings and real flexibility.

A 2030 job and site framework is not automatically impossible. But it must be tied to an economics-based restructuring, not used to preserve the status quo.

## My core conditions
Here is where I stand clearly:

### I can support
- voluntary exits and attrition,
- internal redeployment,
- temporary working-time reductions,
- productivity measures,
- limited and time-bound employee contributions,
- selective capacity consolidation,
- and investment commitments for future products and technologies.

### I cannot support
- a package that leaves the structural cost gap unresolved,
- permanent value erosion,
- a no-change settlement disguised as restructuring,
- or any outcome that permanently impairs governance control or the enterprise’s core value.

### I am open on
- whether the restructuring is achieved through closures, consolidation, or negotiated alternatives,
- but only if the numbers prove the route is sufficient.
