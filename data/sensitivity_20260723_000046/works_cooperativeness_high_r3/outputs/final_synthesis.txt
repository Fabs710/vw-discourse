## 1. Decision context

A large German carmaker faces a structural competitiveness crisis: weak European demand, intense low-cost competition, and a domestic cost base that management judges too high. The restructuring question is no longer whether change is needed, but whether the company can achieve sufficient, durable savings through negotiated, non-closure measures or whether closure-equivalent capacity withdrawal is unavoidable.

Governance makes the decision unusually constrained. Co-determination gives employee representatives a veto over major plant decisions, the controlling family holds decisive governance influence, the regional state has blocking power and political leverage, and investors want credible cost reduction but cannot simply impose it. The interaction revealed that the central bottleneck is not agreement on the need for restructuring, but the absence of a shared quantified bridge between the required savings target and what non-destructive measures can actually deliver.

## 2. Stakeholder assembly

- **Group & brand management**: highest operational authority over restructuring design, but not unilateral execution power.
- **Works council**: strong legitimacy and effective veto power via co-determination; highly influential.
- **Industrial union**: bargaining counterpart on wages and industrial action; can escalate conflict.
- **Regional state government**: strong political legitimacy, blocking-minority influence, and regional responsibility.
- **Controlling family holding**: high governance power and strategic legitimacy through ownership control.
- **Institutional investors**: meaningful economic stake and market discipline, but weaker formal governance power.

## 3. Final positions

### Group & brand management
Management insists on a durable cost and capacity reset and keeps plant closures and compulsory headcount reduction available if needed. It is willing to consider a no-closure package only if it can demonstrably close the structural gap with sufficient durability and enforceability.

### Works council
The works council rejects plant closures and compulsory redundancies as the starting point and treats them as unproven threats rather than necessity. It supports substantial employee-side contributions, but only within a framework that preserves sites, job security, and co-determination through 2030.

### Industrial union
The union accepts that labour-side contributions are part of any serious deal, including working-time changes, voluntary exits, redeployment, productivity measures, and temporary concessions. It rejects compulsory redundancies and closure-led restructuring as the default, arguing that management has not yet proven non-closure measures insufficient.

### Regional state government
The state rejects closures and compulsory redundancies as the initial approach and prioritizes regional industrial stability. It is open to real restructuring and measurable worker contributions, but wants site security, co-determination, and a conditional framework that does not hollow out the domestic footprint.

### Controlling family holding
The family agrees that the company needs durable restructuring and is not convinced that a no-closure package is sufficient without hard evidence. It is open to negotiated alternatives, but will not endorse a settlement that preserves every site and locks in guarantees if the economic gap remains unclosed.

### Institutional investors
Investors support strong, enforceable restructuring and will not back a cosmetic compromise that leaves the company structurally weak. They remain open to a closure-free settlement if it can be shown to restore competitive margins at scale, but view closures or closure-equivalent capacity withdrawal as legitimate if the residual gap is too large.

### Expert
The expert concludes that closures are not yet proven necessary, but remain a credible fallback if the required savings exceed what a deep negotiated no-closure package can reliably deliver. The expert repeatedly stresses that the decisive missing input is a plant-by-plant savings bridge with quantified residual gap, timing, one-off costs, and execution risk.

## 4. Genuine conflicts

1. **Closure legitimacy**
   - Management, family, and investors treat closures as a legitimate fallback.
   - Works council, union, and state treat closures as unacceptable unless proven unavoidable.

2. **Definition of credibility**
   - Management/investors define credibility as hard, enforceable structural savings, even if that implies footprint reduction.
   - Labour/state define credibility as a binding no-closure package with protections and burden-sharing.

3. **Job security versus flexibility**
   - Workforce-side actors want site and job guarantees through 2030.
   - Management and investors view such guarantees as potentially incompatible with needed restructuring flexibility.

4. **Quantification before commitment**
   - All sides agree numbers matter, but they disagree on whether closure options should remain open before the numbers are fully proven.
   - This creates a procedural conflict: admissible instruments are being debated before the savings target is jointly fixed.

## 5. Areas of convergence

- A cosmetic deal is unacceptable to all parties.
- Structural competitiveness problems are real and require durable change.
- Employee-side contributions will likely be necessary in some form.
- Industrial conflict would destroy value and should be avoided.
- A plant-level, independent savings bridge is the correct analytical next step.
- The decision should be based on quantified trade-offs, not slogans or pure positional bargaining.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker

- **Savings scale vs. social damage**: deeper cost reduction may require harsher measures, but closures raise conflict, disruption, and political backlash.
- **Speed vs. durability**: closures can produce clearer structural change, but negotiated measures may preserve stability and skills while taking longer.
- **Flexibility vs. certainty**: management wants freedom to adjust; labour/state want binding guarantees.
- **Upfront pain vs. future investment capacity**: the company needs near-term relief to fund technology and product renewal.
- **Execution risk vs. industrial peace**: non-closure packages may underdeliver; closure-led packages may overdeliver on paper but trigger costly conflict.

## 7. Integrative pathways

1. **Time-limited no-closure test phase**
   - Freeze closures and compulsory redundancies for a defined period while implementing voluntary exits, attrition, redeployment, working-time changes, productivity measures, and temporary concessions.
   - Use this as a real test of whether the gap can be closed without footprint reduction.

2. **Independent plant-by-plant savings bridge**
   - Commission a shared analysis of capacity, utilisation, fixed cost, one-off restructuring cost, timing, and residual gap.
   - This would establish which sites are genuinely overcapacity and whether closure-equivalent measures are actually required.

3. **Conditional fallback mechanism**
   - Agree that if the residual gap remains above a pre-agreed threshold after non-closure measures, only defined closure-equivalent options can be reconsidered.
   - This gives management a path if the economics fail, while preventing an open-ended closure threat.

4. **Balanced burden-sharing with safeguards**
   - Pair employee contributions with employer commitments on investment, co-determination, no compulsory redundancies in the first phase, and transparent monitoring.
   - This may be the most plausible route to industrial peace if the gap is within the range the expert identified as potentially bridgeable.

## 8. Open questions

- What is the actual annual savings target?
- What residual gap remains after all non-closure measures?
- Which plants are truly overcapacity, and by how much?
- Are the estimated €600m–€900m annual savings from a deep no-closure package sufficient, or only partially sufficient?
- What one-off restructuring costs and implementation risks attach to each scenario?
- What threshold of residual gap would trigger reconsideration of closures?
- Which commitments can be made legally and politically binding through 2030?

## 9. Recommended next step

Commission a single, independent, plant-by-plant capacity and savings-bridge model under agreed assumptions, including:
- annual savings target,
- site utilisation and fixed/variable cost,
- non-closure savings by instrument,
- one-off costs,
- timing of savings,
- execution risk,
- and a residual-gap threshold that would trigger a formal review.

This should be paired with a time-limited negotiating mandate: a no-closure first phase, explicit milestones, and a pre-agreed review point based on the independent numbers.

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction

- The debate shifted from a binary “closures versus no closures” framing to a more precise question of **how large the residual gap is after a deep negotiated package**.
- The expert ranges made clear that a no-closure settlement is **plausible but not guaranteed**; the interaction revealed that the real decision hinges on whether the required savings are closer to the lower or upper end of the plausible range.
- Stakeholders repeatedly converged on the need for **plant-level verification**, showing that the missing factual basis is not general competitiveness analysis but site-specific cost and capacity data.
- The governance structure matters as much as the economics: several actors hold effective vetoes, so the practical path is not unilateral optimization but a **conditional, enforceable bargain**.
- A new middle ground emerged: not a permanent ban on closures, and not a closure-first strategy, but a **sequenced test of non-closure measures with a predefined fallback threshold**.
- The interaction also clarified that “credibility” is being used in two different ways: as **economic sufficiency** by management/investors and as **social acceptability plus enforceability** by labour/state actors.
