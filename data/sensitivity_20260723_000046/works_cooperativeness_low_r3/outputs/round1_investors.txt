From the perspective of dispersed institutional investors, the issue is not ideology or preserving any particular arrangement. It is whether this restructuring restores the company to a **credible, competitive margin profile** fast enough to protect long-term equity value.

## Where we agree
We agree with management, and to a degree with the family owners, on the core diagnosis:

- the German cost base is too high,
- structural overcapacity must be addressed,
- temporary fixes alone are not enough,
- and the company needs durable savings, not cosmetic concessions.

We also agree that prolonged drift or a compromise that merely postpones the problem would destroy value. From a capital-markets standpoint, that is the worst outcome: high uncertainty, continued margin erosion, and a weaker strategic position against lower-cost competitors.

## Where we disagree
We do **not** support a “deep restructuring” if it becomes a euphemism for a drawn-out confrontation that damages the business more than it helps it.

Specifically, we are not persuaded by:
- open-ended conflict,
- a path that triggers prolonged strikes,
- a solution that is politically comfortable but financially inadequate,
- or any package that leaves the cost base still structurally uncompetitive.

We also do not accept the idea that all German sites are automatically untouchable if that prevents the company from achieving the savings required. If the economics require consolidation, management should not rule that out just to avoid hard decisions. Markets will punish hesitation more than they will reward symbolism.

## Our position
We want a settlement that delivers:
1. **verified, durable cost reductions**,
2. **a clear route back to competitive margins**,
3. **deal certainty**,
4. **and limited disruption**.

That means we are open to alternatives to plant closures and compulsory redundancies **only if** they genuinely deliver comparable, lasting savings. If the alternative is merely temporary wage moderation, deferred payments, or vague efficiency promises, that is not enough.

## On the workforce side
We understand the resistance to closures and forced layoffs. But from our standpoint, preserving every legacy commitment at the expense of competitiveness is not a defensible strategy. Shareholders cannot underwrite structurally impaired margins indefinitely.

So if the employee side wants to avoid harsher measures, it needs to present something that is:
- quantified,
- durable,
- enforceable,
- and sufficient to restore returns on sales.

A “fair” settlement that does not fix the economics is not fair to shareholders, because it destroys value.

## On management
Management has our support only insofar as it is pursuing a credible restructuring. But credibility will be judged on outcomes, not rhetoric. If management comes back with a plan that is too weak to move the margin trajectory, then it will have failed.

We would rather see:
- a serious capacity reduction,
- a meaningful labor-cost reset,
- and a clear investment case for future products,

than a watered-down deal that preserves peace but leaves the company fragile.

## On the family and state
We recognize the governance reality: you have the blocking power. But blocking power does not change the economics. If the outcome does not improve competitiveness, the capital markets will reprice the company accordingly.

So our message is simple:
- **No cosmetic settlement**
- **No prolonged, value-destructive conflict**
- **No outcome that leaves the cost base uncompetitive**

But we are **not** asking for disruption for its own sake. If there is a negotiated package that produces real savings and a credible margin recovery, we will back it.

## Bottom line
We will support **the most credible path back to competitive margins**, even if it is uncomfortable.  
We will not support a settlement that buys social peace while leaving the company structurally impaired.

If the parties want our support, bring us a package with:
- quantified long-term savings,
- clear implementation certainty,
- and a believable return to competitiveness.

Anything less will not be enough.
