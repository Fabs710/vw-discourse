I will be equally direct.

## First: what I agree with
I agree that this company needs a **durable** restructuring, not a symbolic one.  
I also agree that temporary fixes alone are not enough, and that the cost base, capacity situation, and investment needs must be addressed.

And yes: I accept that a negotiated settlement is better than a prolonged confrontation.

## But let me be absolutely clear: where I disagree
I **do not** accept the premise that “durable” restructuring requires:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or the dismantling of the historic job-security framework.

That is not an industrial solution. That is a decision to shift the burden of management failure onto the workforce.

## To management
You keep saying that a deep restructuring must produce “near-closure-level savings.” Fine. Then show us the numbers for a **real no-closure plan** that actually achieves that.

Not slogans. Not pressure. Not “we may need closures.”

A credible alternative must demonstrate:
- how capacity is reduced **without shutting a German plant**,
- how headcount is adjusted **without forced layoffs**,
- how overhead and indirect cost are cut,
- how investment is secured,
- and how the remaining structure stays viable to 2030.

Until you can prove that a closure-based path is superior even after strike risk, delay risk, and governance conflict, you have **not** justified a closure threat. You have only announced one.

## To the family holding
You talk about stewardship and long-term value. Then act like stewards.

Long-term value is not created by breaking the company’s social compact and poisoning trust across the German sites. Once you tear up job security, you do not get that trust back cheaply. You get years of conflict, lower commitment, and a damaged industrial relationship.

If you want value preservation, back a solution that preserves industrial capability and workforce stability. If you force the opposite, you will own the consequences.

## To investors
You want risk-adjusted NPV. Good. Then include the full risk picture:
- strike escalation,
- implementation delays,
- reputational damage,
- political backlash,
- loss of co-determination trust,
- and the cost of permanently degrading labor relations.

A restructuring that “wins” on paper but provokes industrial war is not a good investment decision.

And no, a blanket threat of closures is not a substitute for a model. If you want us to take your argument seriously, show that the supposed savings survive the real-world risks of a confrontation.

## To the state government
Your line against closures and forced redundancies is correct. But it must not become a soft escape hatch for management to reintroduce closures later under a different label.

We need binding site protection, not political statements.  
We need an enforceable commitment that every German plant remains open.  
We need protection against compulsory redundancies.  
And we need a real co-determination role in any implementation.

Anything less means the workforce is being asked to pay now and hope later. We do not accept that.

## To the union
We are aligned on the essentials:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no permanent wage erosion,
- no one-sided adjustment on the backs of workers.

Where I want to sharpen the point is this: if there is to be any workforce contribution, it must remain **strictly temporary, fair, and reversible**, and it must be matched by binding guarantees.

But let me be frank: I do not accept the idea that we should start bargaining from the assumption that the company can only be saved if workers give up more rights. That is exactly the logic we are resisting.

## On the “hybrid” talk
The facilitator and analysts keep pushing a “hybrid” as the middle ground.

I am not impressed by labels.

A hybrid is only acceptable if it preserves:
1. all German sites,
2. no compulsory redundancies,
3. binding job and site guarantees to 2030,
4. co-determination in implementation,
5. and fair, time-limited contributions from capital as well.

If the hybrid is just a polite way of smuggling in closures later, then it is not a compromise. It is a trap.

## On the analysts
I acknowledge the technical point: a light no-closure package is probably insufficient.

But that does **not** mean closures are necessary. It means the package must be **deeper** on the levers that do not destroy sites:
- working time,
- shift models,
- voluntary exits,
- internal transfers,
- early retirement,
- investment,
- product allocation,
- and support-function rationalisation.

That is where the serious work is. Not in turning plant closure into the default answer.

## What we will accept
We are prepared to discuss a package that includes:
- capacity reduction **without** site closure,
- voluntary workforce reduction,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary and fair employee contributions,
- investment commitments,
- product commitments,
- and binding co-determination in implementation.

## What we will not accept
Let me repeat the non-negotiables clearly:

- **No plant closures in Germany**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No permanent erosion of job security**
- **No restructuring by intimidation**
- **No agreement without binding guarantees through 2030**
- **No fake hybrid that only postpones closure decisions**

If management, owners, or investors cannot accept that, then they are not offering a settlement. They are offering conflict with a nicer name.

## Bottom line
The burden is still on management and capital to prove that a no-closure restructuring can work.  
Not “might work,” not “maybe,” but **work in practice** — with real numbers, real safeguards, and real respect for co-determination.

Until then, our position stands:
**every German plant stays open, no compulsory redundancies, and any workforce contribution must be temporary, fair, and reciprocal.**

If you want to proceed seriously, bring a quantified no-closure plan that meets those conditions. If you cannot, then do not pretend the workforce is the obstacle. The obstacle is your refusal to accept that industrial competitiveness and plant closures are not the same thing.
