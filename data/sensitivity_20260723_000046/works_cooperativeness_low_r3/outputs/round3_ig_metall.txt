I will respond directly.

## Where I agree

I agree with the Works Council, the state, and even parts of management on one point: **this company needs a durable settlement, not a temporary pause**.  
I also agree that the cost base, capacity situation, and future investment needs have to be addressed in a way that can actually be implemented.

I further agree that a negotiated solution is preferable to a prolonged industrial conflict. Nobody benefits from months of uncertainty, especially not the workforce.

## Where I disagree

What I do **not** accept is the repeated attempt to turn “durable” into a synonym for:
- **plant closures**,
- **compulsory redundancies**,
- and **permanent wage erosion**.

That is not a balanced restructuring. That is asking employees to carry the bulk of the adjustment while capital keeps its options open.

That is not fair, and it is not a stable basis for a settlement.

## To management

You keep saying the current German cost and capacity structure is not viable. I do not dismiss that concern. But you have still not shown that closures or forced redundancies are the only way to achieve a serious result.

If you want to argue that the numbers require more than temporary concessions, then bring forward a **quantified no-closure package** that includes:
- real capacity reduction,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary instruments,
- internal redeployment,
- targeted labour-cost measures,
- and binding investment commitments.

Until then, “we may need closures” is not a solution. It is a threat.

And to be clear: I will not bargain away the collective wage framework just because management wants a faster accounting effect.

## To the works council

We are aligned on the essentials:
- **no plant closures**,
- **no compulsory redundancies**,
- **protect the collective wage framework**,
- and **do not make workers the sole shock absorber**.

Where I would sharpen the point is this: employee contributions are on the table, but only if they are **fair, temporary, reciprocal, and tied to binding guarantees**.

That means:
- limited wage restraint or waived special payments,
- no permanent lower tariff regime,
- no unilateral rewriting of guarantees,
- and no “contribution” that simply becomes a disguised wage cut.

If management wants us to sell any contribution to members, it must be matched by real security.

## To the state government

Your insistence on no closures and no forced redundancies is the right political line. But that has to be more than rhetoric.

If the state wants a serious settlement, it should support:
- binding site protection,
- investment commitments for German plants,
- and a restructuring path that preserves industrial capacity.

It should not act as a bystander while the workforce is pushed to finance the adjustment alone.

## To the family owners

If you are serious about long-term stewardship, then prove it in the bargaining room, not just in statements about value.

Long-term ownership means accepting a settlement that protects industrial trust and future labor peace. If you force through a deal that breaks the workforce’s confidence, you may get short-term cost relief and long-term conflict.

That is a bad trade.

## To investors

You are right that the company needs durable savings and implementation certainty. But you are wrong if you think that only closures can create seriousness.

Seriousness is measured by:
- whether the package actually reduces costs,
- whether it can be implemented,
- and whether it avoids triggering a destructive labor conflict.

A package that saves money on paper but provokes strikes, delays, reputational damage, and governance conflict may look clean to a spreadsheet, but it is not necessarily the better business decision.

## What we can accept

We remain prepared to discuss a package that includes:
1. **Temporary employee contribution**
2. **Capacity reductions without site closures**
3. **Voluntary instruments instead of forced layoffs**
4. **Working-time flexibility**
5. **Investment commitments for German plants**
6. **Protection of the collective wage framework**
7. **Binding job and site security**

That is the only kind of shared-sacrifice package we can defend to our members.

## What we will not accept

- Plant closures in Germany
- Compulsory redundancies
- A permanent lowering of wages or tariff standards
- Open-ended insecurity
- A restructuring where workers contribute and management simply keeps the threat of closure on the table

If that is the proposal, then there is no agreement from our side.

## My position on the core issue

Let me be explicit:  
**A no-closure solution is not a “nice-to-have.” It is the condition for serious bargaining.**

If management, owners, and investors want labor to contribute, then they must first accept that the burden cannot be shifted entirely onto workers. The company must preserve jobs, preserve sites, and preserve the collective bargaining framework.

If that cannot be accepted, then the conflict will continue, and the responsibility for that will not lie with the workforce.

## Bottom line

We are ready for a real settlement.  
We are **not** ready to sign off on a restructuring that sacrifices jobs and worker standards as the price of managerial convenience.

Bring us:
- a credible no-closure plan,
- binding protection against compulsory redundancies,
- and a fair, time-limited contribution from all sides.

Without that, there is no basis for agreement.
