I will be very clear.

The positions from management, the family holding, and investors still rest on the same flawed premise: that the only “credible” restructuring is one that destroys German sites, threatens compulsory redundancies, and weakens the job-security framework. I reject that premise.

## Where I agree
I agree on three points:
- The company needs a **durable** solution, not a temporary postponement.
- The cost base and capacity situation must be addressed.
- A negotiated outcome is better than an open-ended escalation.

But agreement on the diagnosis does **not** mean acceptance of the medicine you keep proposing.

## Where I disagree
I disagree with the idea that closures or forced layoffs are somehow necessary to prove seriousness.

That is not seriousness. That is an admission that management has not brought forward a credible industrial alternative.

If the business is structurally challenged, then the burden is on management and capital to design a **no-closure, no-forced-layoff plan** that works in reality. So far, what I have heard is a list of threats, not a convincing plan.

## On management’s argument
Management says: “We need durable, quantified savings, therefore closures cannot be ruled out.”

No. That is not a conclusion; it is a negotiating tactic.

You have not demonstrated that:
- all capacity reduction options within the existing German footprint have been exhausted,
- internal redeployment and working-time tools have been fully used,
- voluntary adjustment instruments cannot deliver the needed staffing change,
- or that investment and product commitments cannot be tied to an alternative package.

Until you prove that, closures remain a choice — not a necessity. And we will not treat them as inevitable.

## On compulsory redundancies
This is a hard red line.

We do not accept compulsory redundancies as a restructuring tool. Not as a first step, not as a fallback, not as a bargaining chip.

If the company wants to adjust staffing, it must do so through:
- natural attrition,
- internal transfer,
- early retirement,
- voluntary instruments,
- working-time changes,
- and negotiated measures.

Anything else is simply shifting corporate risk onto workers who did not create this mess.

## On the demand for “durable” labor cost reductions
We hear the call for durable savings. Fine. But let’s be honest about what “durable” means from the capital side: it often means permanent wage erosion, permanent insecurity, and permanent leverage against labor.

That is unacceptable.

If workers are asked to contribute, then the contribution must be:
- time-limited,
- reversible,
- fair,
- and matched by binding guarantees.

A workforce contribution without site guarantees is not a partnership. It is a one-sided extraction.

## On the family holding
You say you want long-term value. Then act like long-term stewards.

Long-term value is not created by breaking the historic employment compact and forcing through a restructuring that poisons trust across the entire group. If you break the job-security framework now, you will not just damage one round of bargaining — you will damage the company’s future labor relations for years.

That is a strategic mistake, not responsible governance.

## On investors
You keep demanding “measurable equivalence.” I have a counter-test:

**Can you demonstrate that plant closures and forced redundancies are economically superior to a negotiated no-closure package once you include strike risk, implementation delays, reputational damage, and governance conflict?**

Because if you cannot, then your “hard-nosed” stance is not value-maximizing. It is just short-term pressure.

The market does not only price savings. It prices instability too.

## On the state
I welcome the state government’s support for no closures and no forced redundancies. That is the only responsible position for a regional anchor shareholder to take.

But let me also be direct: if the state is serious, it should not allow itself to be dragged into a compromise that quietly opens the door to plant closures later. We need binding protection, not political ambiguity.

## What we are prepared to discuss
We are not refusing restructuring. We are refusing destruction.

We are prepared to discuss:
- capacity reduction without plant closure,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary and fair workforce contributions,
- voluntary programs,
- internal reallocation,
- investment commitments,
- product guarantees,
- and a binding role for co-determination in implementation.

That is a real package. It is not “symbolic.” It is the only package we will seriously negotiate.

## What must be part of any acceptable package
If the others want any chance of agreement, then the package must include:

1. **All German plants remain open**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Binding job and site guarantees through 2030**
4. **Time-limited workforce contributions only**
5. **Investment commitments for each German site**
6. **A genuine co-determination role in shaping and monitoring implementation**
7. **Capacity adjustments without plant shutdowns**

Without those conditions, there is no basis for agreement.

## Bottom line
I am not here to soften the workforce’s position for the comfort of management, owners, or investors.

If you want a settlement, bring a real no-closure plan with binding guarantees and a serious contribution from capital as well — not just demands on workers. If you cannot do that, then do not pretend that a confrontation is being caused by the employees. It will be caused by your refusal to respect jobs, sites, and co-determination.
