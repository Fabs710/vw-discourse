I will be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on one point: **this company needs a durable restructuring, not a cosmetic delay**.  
I also agree that temporary fixes alone are not enough, and that the future product and technology agenda to 2030 has to be funded from a stronger cost base.

I also accept the technical point made by the analysts: a **light** package of wage restraint and attrition will **not** close the gap. The restructuring has to be deeper than that.

## Where I disagree — and strongly
What I do **not** accept is the attempt to turn “no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” into absolute preconditions, regardless of whether the economics work.

That is not a restructuring strategy. That is a veto on adjustment.

The company has:
- structural overcapacity,
- a cost base that is too high,
- and a return-on-sales target it is not meeting.

If we do not reduce costs and capacity materially, we will not regain competitiveness. And if we do not regain competitiveness, we will not have the funds to invest in the future business.

## To the Works Council
You say closures are not necessary and that management has not proven otherwise. I disagree with the framing.

We do **not** need to prove closures are desirable. We need to prove the company can be made competitive. If a no-closure package can do that, I am open to it. But so far, I have not seen a package that gets close enough.

You ask for:
- all sites open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- guarantees through 2030,
- and temporary workforce contributions only.

That combination is not credible unless you can show:
- substantial permanent capacity reduction,
- meaningful and durable labour-cost relief,
- lower overhead,
- and a cost position that actually matches the market.

Without that, it is a statement of intent, not a solution.

And let me be clear: if the package cannot remove enough cost and capacity, then preserving every site becomes an unaffordable preference, not a responsible answer.

## To the Union
You are right that workers should not be the only shock absorber. But your line that employee contributions must remain strictly temporary and reversible is too weak for the scale of the problem.

Temporary concessions do not solve a structural cost gap. They only postpone it.

If the company wants a serious settlement, then the workforce contribution must be:
- durable enough to matter,
- measurable,
- and tied to real structural change.

I do not accept a deal where labour gives a little for a short time, but the company remains stuck with excess capacity and an uncompetitive base.

That is not a settlement; it is a deferral.

## To the State Government
I understand your political responsibility and your desire to preserve industrial presence. But I reject the implication that preserving every site is the same as preserving jobs in the long run.

If we keep an uncompetitive structure, the risk is not only lower margins — it is future erosion of the entire industrial base.

So yes, I am open to a hybrid solution. But I am not accepting unconditional site preservation if the numbers do not work.

The state should support a solution that is:
- economically credible,
- implementation-safe,
- and structurally durable.

Not one that simply avoids the hard decision.

## To the Controlling Family Holding
I agree with you on one thing: long-term value is not created by pretending the current structure is sustainable.

But stewardship also means being honest that a no-closure path has to produce near-closure-level savings if it is to be taken seriously. I have not seen that evidence yet.

If a hybrid package can deliver comparable savings, I will consider it. If not, we need the option to take stronger measures.

That is not ideology. That is governance.

## To Institutional Investors
You are right to insist on quantified, durable savings and implementation certainty. That is exactly the standard I am applying.

But I would say this plainly: a package that merely preserves industrial peace while leaving the company structurally impaired is not value-creating. Nor is a package that looks balanced on paper but does not materially reset the cost base.

The capital markets should support the option that:
- restores margins,
- reduces overcapacity,
- and funds future investment.

If the no-closure alternative can do that, fine. If it cannot, then we cannot let sentiment override economics.

## On the analysts
The analysis was helpful because it removed the illusion that a light package is enough. That said, it also showed something important: a **deep hybrid** may work.

That is the real opening here.

Not “all sites protected at any price.”  
Not “closures by default.”  
But a serious hybrid that removes real cost, real capacity, and real overhead.

That means:
- capacity consolidation,
- line and shift reduction,
- voluntary exits,
- internal transfers,
- early retirement,
- working-time changes,
- and durable labour-cost relief.

If that package is deep enough, it deserves support. If it is not, then it is not equivalent.

## What management is prepared to discuss

We remain prepared to discuss a package that includes:
1. **material capacity reduction**
2. **substantial voluntary workforce reduction**

That is the standard for a serious deal.

## What will not move me
These will not move me:
- open-ended job guarantees,
- blanket exclusion of closures under all circumstances,
- permanent protection of all legacy structures,
- or temporary concessions that do not materially improve competitiveness.

Those are not substitutes for restructuring.

## My bottom line
I am not insisting on closures as a first choice.  
But I am insisting on a **durable structural reduction in cost and capacity**.

If the employee side wants to rule out closures and compulsory redundancies, then it must bring a package that is economically equivalent — not rhetorically equivalent.

If it cannot, then management’s position remains unchanged: we need structural realignment, and we must retain the ability to take the measures required to achieve it.
