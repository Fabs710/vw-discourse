Here is the finance-model assessment of the specific question: **can a deep no-closure / no-forced-layoff package be economically equivalent to a consolidation scenario, and under what conditions?**

## Short answer
**Yes, but only in a narrow set of cases.**  
A no-closure package can match consolidation on **risk-adjusted NPV** only if it produces:

1. **material permanent capacity reduction**,  
2. **large and durable fixed-cost removal** outside the plant footprint,  
3. **meaningful headcount reduction**, even if voluntary,  
4. **multi-year labor-cost relief**, and  
5. **high implementation certainty**.

If the package relies mainly on **temporary wage restraint, hiring freezes, and attrition**, it is usually **not equivalent** to closure/consolidation on a present-value basis.

---

## Financial logic
The key issue is not whether savings exist, but **where they come from**.

### Consolidation / closure typically removes:
- direct labor
- plant-level supervision
- utilities and maintenance baseline
- site overhead
- logistics duplication
- some working capital and local support cost

### No-closure packages usually retain:
- the full plant fixed-cost floor
- most site infrastructure
- a large portion of indirect labor
- local maintenance and utility cost
- duplicated support functions

So the no-closure plan must compensate by cutting more from:
- labor costs,
- shifts/line hours,
- salaried overhead,
- and group support functions.

---

## What “equivalent” means in modeling terms
The right comparison is **risk-adjusted NPV** over a 5–7 year horizon, not headline annual savings.

For each scenario, model:
- recurring annual savings,
- one-time restructuring charges,
- timing of savings ramp,
- implementation risk / probability-weighted execution,
- strike or delay risk,
- residual stranded cost,
- and any working-capital or capex effects.

The better plan is the one with the higher **probability-weighted NPV**.

---

## Practical equivalence test
As a rule of thumb:

- If the no-closure package achieves only **40–60%** of the recurring savings of consolidation, it is **not** economically equivalent.
- If it reaches roughly **70–90%** of recurring savings, with low execution risk, it **may** be close enough to compete.
- Below that, it usually underperforms unless the closure case has unusually high one-time charges or severe execution risk.

---

## Why a “light” no-closure deal usually fails
A package built around:
- wage moderation,
- temporary special-payment waivers,
- hiring freeze,
- and voluntary attrition

usually does **not** remove enough fixed cost.

The problem is that the plant base remains intact, so the company keeps paying for:
- buildings,
- plant management,
- maintenance,
- site services,
- utilities,
- and support layers.

That fixed-cost floor is the main reason closures often win on NPV.

---

## What a credible no-closure package would need to include
To be finance-credible, it would need all of the following:

### 1) Permanent capacity reduction
Not just lower output for a year, but fewer:
- shifts,
- lines,
- hours,
- or product variants.

### 2) Substantial staffing reduction
Preferably through:
- attrition,
- early retirement,
- voluntary severance,
- internal transfers,
- non-renewal of fixed-term staff.

### 3) Durable labor-cost relief
Temporary concessions help cash flow, but they do not solve a structural cost gap unless they are:
- large,
- multi-year,
- and likely to be renewed or embedded in a new structure.

### 4) Overhead reduction
To offset the fixed-cost floor, the company must also cut:
- engineering support,
- plant administration,
- procurement/logistics duplication,
- HR and maintenance layers,
- and corporate support allocated to the footprint.

### 5) High utilization in the remaining footprint
If capacity is reduced but utilization does not rise, unit cost can worsen.  
So the model has to show the remaining plants run at materially better utilization.

---

## A rough scenario framework
Without actual numbers, I would frame it like this:

### Scenario A: Consolidation
- largest fixed-cost removal
- highest one-time charge
- fastest structural reset
- usually strongest NPV if execution is clean

### Scenario B: Deep no-closure package
- all sites remain open
- substantial line/shift reduction
- large voluntary exits
- multi-year labor contribution
- overhead cuts
- moderate one-time charge
- can compete only if savings are deep and durable

### Scenario C: Light no-closure package
- wage restraint
- hiring freeze
- attrition
- some flexibility
- usually insufficient versus consolidation

## What I would expect in a spreadsheet
To test equivalence, you need at minimum:

- current domestic headcount by site
- plant-level fixed cost base
- variable labor cost per vehicle
- overhead allocation by site/function
- volume and capacity by plant
- closure savings estimate
- severance / transition / transfer costs
- capex required to reconfigure remaining sites
- strike-risk and delay assumptions
- discount rate
- planning horizon

Without those inputs, any claim of equivalence is only directional.

---

## Workforce impact assumptions
From a finance standpoint:
- **no-closure** generally means fewer immediate job losses, but more uncertainty about whether enough cost is removed
- **consolidation** usually means larger immediate workforce impact, but stronger recurring savings

If the no-closure path is to be credible, it still usually requires **meaningful headcount reduction**.  
So “no forced redundancies” does **not** mean “no jobs lost”; it means the jobs must be removed through voluntary or natural mechanisms, which can be slower and less certain.

That timing difference matters in NPV.

---

## My conclusion
### Economically:
- **A deep hybrid/no-closure package can sometimes match consolidation.**
- **A purely temporary or light package usually cannot.**

### Condition for equivalence:
The no-closure plan must produce **near-closure-level recurring savings**, with:
- permanent capacity reduction,
- major overhead cuts,
- substantial voluntary workforce reduction,
- and durable labor-cost relief.

### Most likely outcome absent detailed concessions:
If the employee-side package excludes closures and compulsory redundancies **and** limits concessions to temporary measures, then it will **probably fall short** of consolidation on NPV.
