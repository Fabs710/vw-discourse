From the capital markets’ perspective, the discussion has become more disciplined, but we still do **not** have a package that is clearly good enough on **risk-adjusted value**.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family holding, the state, and the analysts on the central diagnosis:

- the German cost base is too high,
- temporary fixes are not enough,
- overcapacity has to be addressed,
- and the company needs a durable return to competitive margins.

I also agree that a prolonged strike or drawn-out conflict would destroy value. Markets will not reward industrial peace if it is bought with continued margin erosion.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** support a settlement that is mainly designed to preserve appearances while leaving the economics weak.

That means:

- no cosmetic compromise,
- no package that looks “balanced” but does not materially reset the cost base,
- no deal that substitutes vague promises for measurable savings,
- and no outcome that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive just to avoid difficult headlines.

I also do **not** accept the employee side’s position that all German sites must remain open regardless of economics. If the numbers do not work, capital cannot be expected to underwrite permanent underperformance.

At the same time, I am also not persuaded by a threat-based closure strategy unless it is tied to a **credible, quantified** restructuring path. Markets want execution, not brinkmanship.

## What the analyst clarified
The specialist analysis was useful because it narrowed the real issue:

- a **light** no-closure package is not enough,
- a **deep** no-closure or hybrid package **might** be enough,
- but only if it removes enough fixed cost, capacity, overhead, and labor cost, with high execution certainty.

That is exactly the right test.

So the question is not “closures good or bad” in the abstract. The question is:

**Can the alternative package get close enough to closure-level savings on a risk-adjusted NPV basis?**

If not, then it is not an acceptable substitute.

## My position on the employee-side line
I respect the rejection of forced redundancies as a default. I also accept that workforce contributions can be part of the answer.

But I do **not** accept:
- permanent protection of all legacy arrangements,
- open-ended job guarantees that freeze the structure,
- or temporary concessions that expire before the structural problem is solved.

A temporary contribution is only valuable if it is deep enough and paired with structural change. Otherwise it is just deferred pain.

## My position on management
Management is right to insist that the current structure is too expensive. But management still has to prove that its preferred path is the **best** path.

If there is a deep hybrid package that achieves near-equivalent savings with less instability, that is preferable from an investor perspective. But if the alternative is too weak, then management should not be prevented from taking stronger structural measures.

So I am open to either route — but only on one condition:

**It must be quantified, durable, implementable, and sufficient to restore margins.**

## What would move capital markets
We would support a deal if it includes all of the following:

1. **Durable, quantified savings**
2. **A credible reduction in overcapacity**
3. **Meaningful labor-cost relief**
4. **Material fixed-cost removal**
5. **Implementation certainty**
6. **A believable investment case for future products**
7. **Limited disruption relative to the value created**

If a no-closure or hybrid package can do that, fine. If it cannot, then management should not be boxed into a structure that keeps returns impaired.

## What will not move us
We will not support:

- a settlement that mainly preserves labor peace,
- an outcome that leaves margins structurally impaired,
- open-ended insecurity without real savings,
- or a compromise that fails the economics but is dressed up as “responsible.”

## Bottom line
Our standard is simple: **risk-adjusted value creation**.

- If a **deep hybrid/no-closure** package can genuinely approach consolidation economics, then we will consider it.
- If it cannot, then the company should not be forced into a weak compromise that preserves every site and still undercuts competitiveness.
- But if management goes for harsher restructuring, it must be disciplined, credible, and tied to clear savings — not just used as leverage.
