Thank you. Let me respond from the perspective of the controlling family holding, acting through governance and with a long-term ownership horizon.

First, I agree with one point that has been made by several of you: **this company needs a durable industrial future, not a delay tactic**. We cannot manage our way out of this crisis with slogans, and we cannot protect value by simply postponing hard choices. The cost base, capacity situation, and competitiveness challenge are real.

That said, I need to be equally clear about where I disagree.

## Where I agree
- **The company must restore margins and competitiveness.**
- **A negotiated solution is preferable to an open-ended confrontation.**
- **The restructuring must be durable, not cosmetic.**
- **Investment in future products and technology matters.**

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the idea that all German sites are untouchable regardless of economics.

I also do **not** accept open-ended job guarantees as the price of any change. A promise that freezes the structure while the market moves on is not stewardship; it is value erosion.

And I do **not** accept a solution that relies mainly on temporary measures, deferrals, or symbolic contributions while leaving the structural cost problem unresolved.

## Our core position
From the owners’ side, the priority is straightforward:  
**protect the long-term value of the enterprise by restoring competitiveness, while avoiding a confrontation that permanently damages the company.**

That means:

- We support **real structural savings**.
- We support **capacity reduction where it is economically necessary**.
- We support **labour-cost measures that are durable, not just short-term optics**.
- We support **investment commitments where they are backed by a credible restructuring plan**.

But we will not support a package that:
- preserves every structure unchanged,
- refuses any serious adjustment to overcapacity,
- and expects the owners to underwrite a declining cost base indefinitely.

## On plant closures
I am not here to announce a closure for its own sake. But I will say this plainly:  
**If the numbers require structural consolidation, then site decisions cannot be ruled out in advance.**

If the workforce side wants to rule out closures entirely, then it must explain how the same level of savings will be achieved in a durable way. Not through promises, not through vague efficiency gains — but through verifiable, lasting changes that materially improve the cost base.

## On compulsory redundancies
We know how explosive this is, and we would prefer to avoid a route that causes unnecessary instability. But I also will not endorse a settlement that makes workforce reductions impossible in practice if they are needed for the business to survive and compete.

If there is a credible alternative that delivers equivalent savings through attrition, redeployment, working-time flexibility, and negotiated contributions, then that is preferable. If not, then insisting on a blanket exclusion of employment reductions is not a serious answer.

## On the union and works council position
I understand the insistence on social responsibility and co-determination. Those are real features of this company’s governance, and they must be respected.

But co-determination does not mean that every existing arrangement is permanent. Nor does it mean that the company can be kept competitive by asking capital to absorb all the pain while labour contributes only conditionally.

If the employee side wants to avoid harsher measures, then it needs to engage on:
- working-time flexibility,
- capacity adjustments,
- temporary moderation in compensation,
- removal or adjustment of legacy cost items,
- and operational changes that actually move the economics.

## On the regional government
I appreciate the state’s role as an anchor shareholder and its concern for regional stability. But stability is not preserved by denying reality.

A prolonged stand-off, or a refusal to acknowledge that some German capacity may be excess to requirements, would itself threaten the regional base far more than a disciplined restructuring would.

## What would move us
A proposal could gain our support if it contains:
1. **A credible, quantified improvement in margins and competitiveness**
2. **Durable savings, not just deferred costs**
3. **A labor contribution that is meaningful and shared**
4. **Maximum avoidance of unnecessary instability**
5. **A clear investment path for the future product portfolio**

## What would not move us
We will not be persuaded by:
- blanket refusals to consider site changes,
- permanent guarantees that lock in the current structure,
- or solutions that look peaceful in the short term but leave the company weaker.

So my message is simple:  
**We are open to a negotiated settlement, but only if it genuinely restores competitiveness and does not sacrifice the long-term value of the company for the comfort of preserving everything as it is.**

If the other sides want the company to remain strong for the next generation, then they need to engage with structural reality, not just defend current positions.
