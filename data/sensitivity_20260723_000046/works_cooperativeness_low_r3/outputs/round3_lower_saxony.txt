I’ll be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the analyst and with all sides on one basic point: **this company needs a durable restructuring, not a pause button**.  
I also agree that the cost base, capacity situation, and investment needs must be addressed in a way that is **economically credible** and **implementable**.

And I accept the analyst’s core technical point: a **light** no-closure package is unlikely to be enough. If there is to be a no-closure solution, it has to be **deep, durable, and verifiable**.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the leap that because a light no-closure package is insufficient, therefore plant closures or forced redundancies become the default answer.

That is too quick, and it is not good enough for a company that is a regional anchor, a co-determined enterprise, and a major public interest case. A difficult restructuring is not automatically a closure-based restructuring.

What the analysis actually says is more nuanced:
- no-closure can fail if it is cosmetic,
- but a **hybrid** or **deep no-closure** path can work if it removes enough cost, capacity, and overhead.

That matters. Because it means closures are **not proven necessary** yet.

## On management
Management keeps framing this as if the choice were:
- accept closures and layoffs, or
- accept failure.

I reject that binary.

What I expect from management is a real comparison of:
1. a consolidation scenario,
2. a deep no-closure scenario,
3. and a hybrid scenario that removes capacity without shutting down plants.

So far, you have argued the case for structural pressure, but you have not shown that every non-closure path has been exhausted. The analyst’s own answer confirms that the question is still open.

That is important: **open** does not mean “anything goes,” but it also does not mean “management’s preferred answer wins by default.”

## On the works council and union
I support your rejection of forced redundancies as a starting point, and I support your insistence that the workforce must not be made the sole shock absorber.

But I also need to be candid: if the only acceptable employee-side answer is “all sites open, no forced redundancies, full guarantees through 2030,” then you are setting a very high bar.

If you want the state and the broader shareholder base to defend that position, then the no-closure package has to be genuinely strong:
- real capacity reduction,
- meaningful output concentration,
- durable labor-cost relief,
- serious overhead cuts,
- and measurable implementation milestones.

A slogan is not a restructuring.

## On the analyst’s findings
The analyst’s conclusion is actually the most useful thing we have heard so far.

It says:
- a **light** no-closure package is not enough,
- a **deep** no-closure package might be enough,
- and a **hybrid** is often the most realistic compromise technically.

That is the right place to focus. Not on theoretical purity, but on whether the company can get:
- enough fixed-cost removal,
- enough utilization improvement,
- and enough durable cost relief.

From the state’s perspective, I am prepared to explore that — but only if it protects jobs and industrial presence in our region.

## What would move me
I would be prepared to back a restructuring if it includes all of the following:

1. **No full plant closures in Germany unless a hybrid/no-closure package demonstrably fails**
2. **No compulsory redundancies as the default mechanism**
3. **Permanent or multi-year capacity reduction**
4. **Substantial voluntary workforce reduction**
5. **Durable, quantified labor-cost relief**
6. **Overhead and support-function cuts**
7. **Binding investment commitments for German sites**
8. **A credible product roadmap**
9. **Formal involvement of co-determination in implementation**

That is the minimum for a balanced solution.

## What will not move me
I will not support:
- closures as a first resort,
- forced layoffs as a bargaining chip,
- a “temporary contribution” that simply becomes a permanent insecurity regime,
- or a restructuring that weakens our co-determination and veto role.

Those are not acceptable from a state shareholder acting in the regional and public interest.

## My view on the best path
At this stage, the most serious route is **not** “closure versus no-closure” as an abstract fight. It is to test whether a **hybrid package** can match the economics closely enough while preserving the regional industrial base.

That means:
- capacity reductions,
- line consolidation,
- stronger specialization,
- voluntary exits,
- working-time changes,
- labor-cost contribution,
- and support-function rationalization.

If that package can get near the required savings, then it deserves support.

If it cannot, then management will have to prove why not — and only then can any discussion of plant decisions begin. But we are not there yet.

## Bottom line
I am **not** persuaded that closures or forced redundancies are necessary at this point. The technical analysis says the issue is still open, and that a serious hybrid may work.

So my position is:
- **No automatic closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Yes to a deep, quantified, hybrid restructuring**
- **Yes to binding investment and job safeguards**
- **Yes to co-determination in implementation**

If management, owners, and investors want our support, bring a package that proves the economics without sacrificing the region’s industrial anchor. If you cannot, then do not expect the state to bless a solution that fails its social and governance test.
