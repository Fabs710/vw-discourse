I will respond directly.

## Where I agree
I agree with the works council, the state, and even parts of the technical assessment on one fundamental point: **this company needs a durable restructuring, not a temporary patch**.

I also agree that:
- the cost base is too high,
- capacity has to be reduced,
- and the solution must be implementable, not just politically convenient.

And yes, I agree that a settlement is preferable to a prolonged conflict. Nobody benefits from months of industrial escalation.

## Where I disagree
What I do **not** accept is the repeated attempt to turn “durable” into a code word for:

- **plant closures**,
- **compulsory redundancies**,
- and **permanent wage deterioration**.

That is not a balanced restructuring. That is a one-sided transfer of the crisis onto the workforce.

If management and capital want labor to contribute, then they must also contribute. Workers will not be the only ones absorbing the pain.

## To management
You keep saying the company needs “near-closure-level savings.” Fine. Then show us a **real no-closure package** that delivers that, rather than using closures as a threat to force concessions.

A serious alternative must be quantified and must include:
- capacity reduction,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary instruments,
- internal redeployment,
- overhead cuts,
- and binding investment commitments.

What I have not heard yet is a credible demonstration that closures are superior once you include:
- strike risk,
- delay risk,
- implementation conflict,
- reputational damage,
- and the long-term cost of breaking labor trust.

A spreadsheet that ignores industrial reality is not an argument.

## To the works council
We are aligned on the essentials:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no permanent weakening of the collective framework,
- and no restructuring where workers carry the whole burden.

Where I sharpen the point is this: any employee contribution must be **temporary, fair, reciprocal, and reversible**.

That means:
- limited wage restraint or waived special payments,
- no permanent lower tariff regime,
- no disguised wage cuts,
- and no contribution without binding site and job security.

If management wants this to be sellable to members, the package must be visibly fair.

## To the state government
Your position against closures and forced layoffs is the right one. But it must be more than political signaling.

If the state wants a serious settlement, it should back:
- binding site protection,
- investment commitments,
- and a restructuring path that preserves industrial capacity.

It should not let itself be drawn into a compromise that quietly reopens closures later. We need enforceable protection, not ambiguity.

## To the family owners
If you are serious about stewardship, then prove it in the bargaining room.

Long-term value is not created by breaking the employment compact and forcing through a restructuring that destroys trust across the sites. If you do that, you may win a cost argument today and lose industrial peace for years.

That is not responsible ownership. That is short-term pressure dressed up as strategy.

## To investors
You are right that the company needs durable savings and implementation certainty. But you are wrong if you think only closures can create seriousness.

Seriousness means:
- measurable savings,
- credible capacity adjustment,
- real cost relief,
- and a settlement that can actually be executed without triggering destructive conflict.

A package that looks tidy on paper but triggers strikes, delays, and political backlash is not the better business decision.

## On the analyst’s point
The technical assessment was useful because it clarified the real question:

- a **light** no-closure package is probably not enough,
- a **deep** package might be enough,
- and a **hybrid** may be the most realistic technical compromise.

I do not reject that analysis. I reject the conclusion some seem eager to jump to: that because a light no-closure plan fails, closures must therefore be accepted.

That leap is not justified.

## What we can accept
We remain prepared to discuss a package with:
1. **capacity reduction without site closure**
2. **voluntary workforce reduction**
3. **working-time flexibility**
4. **temporary employee contributions**
5. **investment commitments for German plants**
6. **protection of the collective wage framework**
7. **binding job and site security**

That is a real restructuring path. It is not a symbolic gesture.

## What we will not accept
Let me state the limits clearly:

- **No plant closures in Germany**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No permanent erosion of wages or tariff standards**
- **No open-ended insecurity**
- **No restructuring where labor contributes and capital keeps the closure option alive**

If that is the proposal, then there is no agreement from our side.

## Bottom line
We are ready for a serious settlement.  
We are **not** ready to sign off on a restructuring that sacrifices jobs and worker standards while management and owners preserve all their options.

Bring a credible no-closure plan, binding protection against compulsory redundancies, and a fair contribution from capital as well. Then we can negotiate seriously.

If you cannot do that, then the conflict is not caused by the workforce. It is caused by the refusal to respect jobs, sites, and collective bargaining.
