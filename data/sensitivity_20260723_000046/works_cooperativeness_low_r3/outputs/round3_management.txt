Let me respond plainly.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on one central point: **the company needs a durable solution, not a cosmetic one**.  
I also agree that temporary fixes alone are insufficient, and that a settlement is preferable to a long industrial conflict.

I further agree that any viable path must be:
- economically credible,
- implementable,
- and capable of funding the company’s future product and technology needs through 2030.

## Where I disagree — and strongly
What I do **not** accept is the repeated attempt to define “durable” as automatically meaning:
- **all German sites preserved**,
- **no compulsory redundancies** under any circumstances,
- and **binding job guarantees through 2030** regardless of the economics.

That is not a strategy. That is a refusal to confront the structural problem.

The company has **too much capacity** and a **cost base that is too high** for the market we are in. If we pretend otherwise, we will not restore competitiveness; we will simply postpone the deterioration.

## To the Works Council
You say management has not proved that closures are necessary. I say the burden is now on the employee side to show how a no-closure solution delivers **comparable, durable savings**.

So far, what I have heard is a list of objections, not a credible industrial alternative.

A real alternative would need to specify:
- which capacity comes out,
- how staffing is reduced without forced redundancies,
- how labour costs come down in a durable way,
- and how the resulting structure closes the gap to competitors.

If that cannot be quantified, then it is not a solution to the company’s problem.

And let me be explicit: **I do not accept a blanket ban on closures or compulsory redundancies**. Those options remain on the table if the economics require them.

## To the union
I appreciate that you are prepared to discuss temporary employee contributions, working-time flexibility, and voluntary instruments. That is more constructive than a pure no-response posture.

But I need to be equally clear:  
A **temporary** contribution does not fix a **structural** cost problem.

A deferred bonus, a limited wage concession, or a short-term working-time adjustment may help bridge a gap, but they do not restore the economics on their own. If the package is only temporary, then it is not enough.

If you want to avoid harsher structural measures, then the package must produce:
- **durable** savings,
- **measurable** cost relief,
- and a **credible reduction in overcapacity**.

## To the regional state government
I respect the state’s role and its concern for regional stability. But regional stability is not protected by preserving an uncompetitive cost structure.

If the business weakens, jobs are still lost — just later, and in a worse form.

So I am not prepared to accept the idea that German sites are untouchable as a principle. That would be irresponsible governance.

## To the controlling family owners
You are right to insist on competitiveness and value preservation. I agree with that objective.

But I also want to be clear that a compromise which looks politically elegant while leaving the company structurally impaired will not work. We need a package that actually changes the economics.

If there is a credible alternative to site consolidation that delivers the same savings, then fine — let’s evaluate it. But “no closures” is not a strategy by itself.

## To institutional investors
You are correct to insist on quantified, durable savings and implementation certainty. That is exactly the standard we should use.

But I would caution against thinking that the best outcome is simply the most brutal one. The best outcome is the one that:
- restores margins,
- reduces overcapacity,
- protects the future investment base,
- and can actually be executed.

If a negotiated no-closure package can genuinely do that, I will consider it. If it cannot, then management must retain the ability to take structural measures, including site decisions.

## What management is prepared to discuss
Let me be concrete.

We remain open to a negotiated package if it includes:
1. **real capacity reductions**
2. **durable labour-cost relief**
3. **working-time flexibility**
4. **voluntary instruments for workforce adjustment**
5. **a credible investment path for German plants**
6. **product commitments that support the 2030 strategy**
7. **implementation certainty with clear governance**

That is the standard for a serious deal.

## What will not move me
These will not move me:
- open-ended job guarantees,
- a blanket exclusion of closures,
- permanent protection of every legacy arrangement,
- or temporary concessions that do not materially improve competitiveness.

Those are not acceptable substitutes for structural adjustment.

## Bottom line
I am prepared to negotiate seriously. But I will not pretend that preserving the current footprint is automatically compatible with restoring the company’s competitiveness.

If the employee side wants to rule out closures and compulsory redundancies, then it must present a package that is **economically equivalent** in savings and durability. If it cannot, then management’s position remains unchanged: **we need structural realignment, and we will not rule out the measures required to achieve it**.
