From the perspective of the regional state and as a long-standing anchor shareholder, I need to be very clear as well:

We **do** share the diagnosis that the company needs a durable industrial future. I do not dispute that the cost base must improve, and I do not expect management to pretend that structural pressures do not exist. But I **do** dispute the idea that the only serious path forward runs through plant closures, compulsory redundancies, and a one-sided shift of the burden onto the workforce.

That is not a sustainable restructuring; it is a high-risk confrontation that would damage the company’s social legitimacy, its industrial relations, and the regional economy that this company anchors.

What I agree with:
- The company needs lasting competitiveness, not delay.
- Capacity and cost adjustments may be necessary.
- A negotiated solution is preferable to escalation.

Where I disagree:
- **Plant closures in Germany are not an acceptable default option.**
- **Compulsory redundancies are not an acceptable instrument.**
- **Open-ended job insecurity is not compatible with a responsible restructuring.**
- **Any attempt to sideline the state’s co-determination and blocking rights is unacceptable.**

I also want to respond to management’s framing: saying that “not every site can be untouchable” may be true in a purely financial sense, but this is not a purely financial decision. This company exists in a regional, social, and political ecosystem. It has commitments, governance structures, and a workforce that has carried it through prior transitions. If management seeks legitimacy for change, it must earn it through a plan that preserves core sites, avoids forced layoffs, and shares the burden fairly.

To the Works Council and union: I support your core line that there must be no closures and no compulsory redundancies. But I would also say that if there is a credible package that preserves sites and jobs while delivering real, durable savings, then it deserves serious examination. That can include:
- temporary and negotiated workforce contributions,
- working-time flexibility,
- adjusted capacity utilization,
- waivers or deferrals of variable payments,
- investment commitments tied to future products and plant modernization.

What it cannot include is a blank check from labour while capital asks for sacrifice from everyone else. Fairness matters here. If the workforce is asked to contribute, then management and owners must also put something meaningful on the table: investment certainty, product commitments, and a restructuring that does not treat German employment as disposable.

So my line is this:
**We are open to a negotiated restructuring, but only one that protects German sites, avoids compulsory redundancies, respects co-determination, and secures the company’s long-term role as a regional economic anchor.**  
Anything that crosses those boundaries will face resistance from us, including at board level.
