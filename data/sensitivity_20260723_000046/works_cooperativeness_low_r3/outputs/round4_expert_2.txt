Yes. From a restructuring finance perspective, the key question is whether the **deep hybrid / no-closure package** can deliver **near-closure-level recurring savings on a risk-adjusted basis**.

## Technical answer
A no-closure package is economically credible as a substitute for consolidation only if it closes **most** of the gap that plant closure would remove, especially:

- **fixed manufacturing overhead**
- **duplicate site-level functions**
- **installed capacity / underutilization**
- **headcount**
- **plant utilization**
- **implementation certainty**

A light package of temporary labor concessions would not be enough. A deep hybrid can work, but only if it is materially structural.

## Practical threshold
As a working benchmark, I would typically expect a credible no-closure package to achieve roughly:

- **70–90% of the recurring annual savings** of the consolidation case, **on a risk-adjusted basis**

That is not a legal or accounting rule; it is a practical finance threshold. Below that range, the no-closure case often looks too weak unless closure itself carries unusually high one-time costs or execution risk.

## What has to be true for equivalence
To be treated as close to economically equivalent, the package should include:

1. **Material permanent capacity reduction**
   - line consolidation
   - shift reduction
   - specialization of sites
   - elimination of duplicate production structures

2. **Substantial headcount reduction**
   - primarily voluntary exits, attrition, early retirement, internal transfer
   - not just a hiring freeze

3. **Durable labor-cost relief**
   - wage restraint, reduced special payments, working-time changes, or similar
   - but with a multi-year effect

4. **Overhead and support-function cuts**
   - administrative, logistics, engineering, and plant support cost reduction

5. **High implementation certainty**
   - clear milestones, governance, and timing
   - low risk of reversal after the concession period

## Typical headcount scale
As a rough guide:

- **<5% reduction**: almost always too small
- **5–10%**: meaningful but usually insufficient if overcapacity is real
- **10–15%**: potentially serious
- **15–20%+**: can begin to look comparable, depending on the cost structure and mix of direct vs indirect labor

The cost impact depends heavily on whether reductions hit:
- direct production labor
- indirect site support
- overhead / administrative functions

## Why closures are hard to replace
A closure/consolidation case usually removes:
- fixed site overhead
- duplicate functions
- underutilized capacity

A no-closure case can capture some of that, but not all. If utilization remains weak, the fixed-cost burden remains high even if wages are reduced.

## Risk-adjusted NPV framework
For an apples-to-apples comparison, the model should include:

### Benefits
- recurring annual savings
- margin improvement
- lower overhead
- improved utilization
- avoidance of future losses from excess capacity

### Costs
- severance / voluntary exit payments
- early retirement
- reconfiguration and transition costs
- retraining and internal transfer costs
- any investment needed to make the hybrid structure work

### Risks
- strike / industrial action delay
- implementation slippage
- governance conflict
- savings reversal after expiration of temporary measures
- lower-than-planned voluntary uptake

A package that looks good on gross savings but has weak delivery confidence may lose on **risk-adjusted NPV**.

## Bottom line
My technical judgment is:

- **Yes**, a deep hybrid no-closure package **can** be a substitute for consolidation.
- **No**, it will not be equivalent if it is mostly temporary or only modestly reduces workforce cost.
- To be credible, it generally needs to remove enough **fixed cost, capacity, and headcount** to get close to the closure case, usually around **70–90% of recurring savings on a risk-adjusted basis**.
