Management, owners, and investors are all trying to recast this as a simple competitiveness exercise. It is not. It is a decision about whether this company honours its workforce, its governance model, and its historic commitments — or whether it tears them up and calls that “strategy.”

Let me respond clearly.

## Where I agree
I agree that this company needs a durable future, not delay.
I agree that temporary fixes alone are not enough.
I agree that investment in future products, working-time flexibility, and operational improvements are legitimate topics.
And I agree that any solution must be economically credible.

## Where I disagree
I **do not** accept the conclusion management, owners, and investors keep pushing: that credibility requires plant closures or an open-ended threat of forced redundancies.

That is exactly the kind of one-sided logic that caused this confrontation in the first place.

The workforce has already delivered productivity, flexibility, and sacrifice for years. What we will not accept is being told that the only “serious” restructuring is one that destroys jobs, breaks the job-security framework, and weakens co-determination.

## On the “structural reality” argument
Yes, the market is difficult.
Yes, costs matter.
But “structural reality” is not a blank cheque to close German plants.

If management believes there is overcapacity, then the burden is on management to present a **no-closure package** that actually closes the gap through:
- capacity reductions without site closures,
- internal reallocations,
- working-time measures,
- fair, temporary workforce contributions,
- investment commitments,
- and product guarantees for the German plants.

That is the standard we are prepared to discuss. Not closures.

## On compulsory redundancies
We reject them outright.
Not “preferably avoid.”
Not “limit them.”
**Reject them.**

If the company wants savings, it must get them without forcing people out. That means attrition, natural turnover, early retirement, internal redeployment, and negotiated working-time and compensation measures — all within a binding framework.

If management says those instruments are not enough, then management has not done the work to design a proper alternative. That is not a reason to impose layoffs.

## On the demand for “durable” employee contributions
We hear this from the capital side constantly: employees must make “durable” contributions. Fine — but only if those contributions are:
- time-limited,
- fair,
- reciprocal,
- and tied to binding guarantees.

A workforce contribution without site guarantees is just a wage cut with nicer language.
A workforce contribution without protection against closures is just blackmail dressed up as restructuring.
A workforce contribution without a real say in implementation is a breach of co-determination in all but name.

## On the regional state
I welcome the state government’s line that closures and compulsory redundancies are not acceptable defaults. That is the correct political stance.
But the state should not be asked to bless a compromise that quietly abandons German jobs after the headlines fade.
If the state wants to be serious, then it should insist on:
- preservation of all German sites,
- no forced layoffs,
- binding investment,
- and full respect for co-determination.

## On the family holding and investors
You both keep saying “we are long-term oriented.” Then prove it.

Long-term ownership is not demonstrated by pushing through a restructuring that damages trust, triggers industrial conflict, and undermines future labour relations.
Long-term value is not protected by making the workforce absorb the adjustment while capital demands “competitiveness” but refuses to bind itself to employment security.

If you want the workforce to contribute, then you must accept:
- a genuine co-determination role in shaping the measures,
- investment and product commitments for every German site,
- and legally binding job security through 2030.

Without that, your talk of durability is just pressure.

## The real choice
There are two routes here:

### Route 1: Responsible restructuring
- all German sites remain open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding job and site guarantees to 2030,
- negotiated, temporary workforce contribution,
- capacity adjustments and working-time flexibility,
- investment commitments for German plants and future products,
- co-determination in shaping the plan.

This is the only route we will seriously engage with.

### Route 2: Confrontation
If management continues to insist on closures or forced layoffs, then there is no deal from our side.
We will resist through every legal and industrial means available to us.
We have already shown that the workforce is ready to act, and we will do so again.

## Bottom line
We are not here to help management choose which German plant to sacrifice.
We are here to defend jobs, sites, and co-determination.

So let me be absolutely unambiguous:
- **No plant closures in Germany.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No weakening of the historic job-security framework.**

If the other sides want a solution, bring forward a real no-closure plan with binding guarantees. If not, then do not expect the workforce to sign off on its own insecurity.
