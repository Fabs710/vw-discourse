I will respond plainly.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the analyst, and the other shareholder side on one central point: **the company needs a durable restructuring, not a cosmetic pause**.

I also agree that:
- the current cost base is too high,
- capacity has to be reduced,
- temporary fixes alone are insufficient,
- and a negotiated settlement is far better than a destructive confrontation.

And yes, I accept the technical conclusion that a **light** no-closure package will not be enough. If we want a no-closure path, it must be **deep, quantified, and enforceable**.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the leap from “light no-closure fails” to “therefore plant closures or compulsory redundancies must be on the table as default instruments.”

That is too quick. It is not yet proven that the only credible path is one that damages the regional industrial base.

From the state’s perspective, a restructuring is only acceptable if it:
- preserves jobs as far as possible,
- protects industrial presence in our region,
- respects co-determination,
- and remains economically credible over the long term.

A plan that destroys trust, weakens the social partnership, and risks lasting regional damage is not a responsible solution, even if it looks neat in a spreadsheet.

## To management
You are right that a temporary concession package is insufficient. But you still have **not** shown that every serious non-closure path has been exhausted.

What I expect from management is not a closure threat, but a real comparison of:
1. consolidation/closure,
2. a deep hybrid restructuring,
3. and a no-closure package with meaningful structural adjustments.

If you claim no-closure cannot work, then demonstrate it with numbers that include:
- strike risk,
- delay risk,
- governance conflict,
- transition costs,
- and the value of industrial peace.

Until then, closures are not “necessary”; they are merely one option.

## To the works council and union
I support your rejection of compulsory redundancies as a default mechanism. I also agree that the workforce must not be the sole shock absorber.

But I have to be equally direct with you: if the only acceptable answer is “all sites open, no forced layoffs, full guarantees through 2030,” then you are setting the bar very high.

That bar may be defensible only if the alternative package is truly strong:
- substantial capacity reduction,
- meaningful output concentration,
- durable labor-cost relief,
- overhead cuts,
- and verified implementation milestones.

A slogan is not enough. If you want broad shareholder and political support, the no-closure package must be serious enough to stand up against the consolidation case.

## On the analysts
The analysis and the finance modeler have actually clarified the real issue:

- a **light** no-closure package is unlikely to match the economics,
- a **deep hybrid** might,
- but only if it removes enough fixed cost, capacity, overhead, and headcount,
- and does so with high implementation certainty.

That is where the discussion should be. Not in abstract ultimatums, but in whether the hybrid can truly come close enough on risk-adjusted NPV.

## What would move me
I could support a restructuring if it includes all of the following:

1. **No immediate plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies as the first mechanism**
3. **Material permanent capacity reduction**
4. **Substantial voluntary workforce reduction**
5. **Durable, quantified labor-cost relief**
6. **Overhead and support-function cuts**
7. **Binding investment commitments for German sites**
8. **A credible product roadmap**
9. **Formal co-determination in implementation and monitoring**

That is the minimum for a balanced solution.

## What will not move me
I will not support:
- closures as a first resort,
- a restructuring that treats German sites as disposable,
- forced layoffs as a bargaining chip,
- or a “temporary contribution” that simply becomes a permanent insecurity regime.

And I will not accept any outcome that removes the state’s co-determination and veto role. That is part of the governance balance here.

## My bottom line
The right answer is **not** “no change.”  
But it is also **not** “closures by default.”

The right answer is a **deep, verified, hybrid restructuring** that:
- materially reduces cost,
- protects as many jobs and sites as possible,
- preserves the company as a regional economic anchor,
- and keeps co-determination and public-interest governance intact.

If management, owners, and investors want our support, bring a package that proves the economics **without** sacrificing the region’s industrial base unnecessarily. If you cannot, then do not expect the state to bless a solution that fails its social and governance test.
