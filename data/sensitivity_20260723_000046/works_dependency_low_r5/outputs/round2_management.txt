I’ll respond directly.

## First: where I agree
I agree with everyone on one point: **a cosmetic deal is useless**.  
If we leave the current cost base, capacity base, and labour-cost structure largely intact, then we do not fix the company — we postpone the problem and weaken our ability to invest in 2030 products and technology.

I also agree that **prolonged conflict is bad**. It damages output, customers, reputation, and ultimately the industrial base we are all trying to protect.

## Where I disagree — sharply

### To the works council
Your position is internally consistent, but it does **not** solve the economics.

You say:
- all German plants must stay open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding guarantees through 2030.

That is not a restructuring proposal. It is a **freeze**.

And a freeze is not compatible with:
- structurally high German factory costs,
- shrinking European demand,
- intense Chinese and low-cost competition,
- and a brand that is missing its return-on-sales target.

I do **not** accept that all sites can remain untouched regardless of economics. If some capacity is redundant, then that capacity has to go. If that means rationalisation of a plant footprint, I will not pretend otherwise.

I also do **not** accept that no compulsory reduction can ever be on the table. Voluntary exits are useful, but they will not be enough if we need a material, durable headcount reduction.

### To the union
You are right that the burden cannot fall one-sidedly on the workforce. But you are wrong if you think that means labour costs can remain largely protected while the company somehow becomes competitive.

A temporary contribution is not enough if the underlying structure stays too expensive. We need **real and durable labour-cost relief**, not just reversible gestures that expire before the savings matter.

And to be blunt: if the union’s answer to every hard adjustment is warning strikes, that will not strengthen the workforce’s position. It will accelerate the case for tougher measures.

### To the regional state government
I respect the political reality and the statutory veto position. But I have to be equally clear: **a political veto cannot be used to preserve uneconomic structure forever**.

If the state wants jobs protected, then it must engage with the economic reality that some capacity is excessive. That means not just defending every existing site, but helping enable a durable solution that restores competitiveness. Otherwise the company will keep losing ground, and that helps no one in the end.

### To the controlling family owners
I agree with you on one thing: a value-destructive industrial war is unacceptable. But value is also destroyed by **delay** and by pretending that competitiveness will recover without hard structural change.

So I will not support an approach that focuses only on avoiding headlines while leaving the cost base uncompetitive. That would be governance failure in another form.

### To institutional investors
You are correct that the market will not reward a fake deal. I agree with your emphasis on durability and execution.

But I want to be equally clear: a “non-closure alternative” is only acceptable if it produces **comparable economics**. That means not just smaller promises, but:
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- material headcount reduction,
- real labour-cost contribution,
- and a clear path to margin improvement.

If it does not achieve that, then it is not a serious alternative.

## The minimum I need
Let me put this in concrete terms.

### Scenario 1: Site-preserving plan
This only becomes acceptable if it delivers all of the following:
- substantial capacity reduction across the German footprint,
- materially lower labour costs,
- meaningful headcount reduction,
- changes to working time and legacy commitments,
- and a binding investment package tied to the restructuring.

But I need to be frank: if “site-preserving” means every plant stays open with no real footprint reduction, then it is **not sufficient**.

### Scenario 2: Mixed plan with limited rationalisation
This is the most realistic route if the parties want to avoid a maximal confrontation.

What I would consider:
- targeted rationalisation of capacity,
- consolidation of lines or functions,
- redeployment where possible,
- voluntary exits first,
- but with compulsory measures available if the scale required cannot be reached otherwise,
- and genuine labour-cost relief.

This could work **if** it actually closes the competitiveness gap.

### Scenario 3: Closure-inclusive plan
I will not rule this out.

If the employee side refuses to engage on a package that achieves the needed savings, then closures remain on the table. I am not using that as a threat for effect. It is a real consequence of the arithmetic.

## What I am not willing to accept
I will not accept:
- a blanket guarantee through 2030 that freezes the restructuring,
- a deal with no material capacity reduction,
- a package that relies only on voluntary exits and symbolic concessions,
- or a settlement that preserves every structure but leaves us uncompetitive.

That would be a cosmetic agreement, and I would rather have no deal than that.

## What I am prepared to discuss
I am prepared to discuss:
- a serious non-closure alternative **if** it delivers comparable savings,
- phased implementation,
- strong qualification and redeployment measures,
- voluntary programs,
- working-time flexibility,
- and investment commitments that follow from real savings.

But I need hard numbers and credible mechanisms, not general statements about social partnership.

## Bottom line
The company needs:
1. **lower capacity**,  
2. **lower labour costs**,  
3. **lower headcount**,  
4. **durable structural change**,  
5. **and investment capacity for the future**.

If the current proposals from labour and the state cannot deliver that, then they do not solve the problem. They only delay it.

So I am asking for something concrete in the next round:
- what exact capacity reduction do you accept,
- what exact labour-cost contribution do you accept,
- what exact headcount reduction is possible,
- and how do you guarantee that it is durable?

Without answers to those questions, there is no basis for a credible restructuring package.
