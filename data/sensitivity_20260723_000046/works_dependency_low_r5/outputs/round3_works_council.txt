Let me be equally direct in round 3:

**Our position has not changed.**
- **No German plant closures.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No dismantling of the job-security framework.**
- **No fait accompli presented as “economic necessity.”**

If management still wants a deal, then it must stop speaking as if the only serious options are destruction of sites and forced job cuts. That is not the only serious option — it is the most confrontational one.

## To management
You keep saying the company needs “durable structural change.” I do not dispute that. What I dispute is your conclusion that this automatically requires closures and compulsory redundancies.

You still have **not** shown:
- why all German plants cannot remain open,
- why capacity reductions cannot be handled through internal reorganization,
- why voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, shift changes, and temporary working-time adjustments are insufficient,
- or why employee security must be sacrificed first.

If you want to claim that closures are unavoidable, then bring the actual proof:
- plant-by-plant economics,
- capacity utilization,
- product allocation,
- investment plan,
- and the comparative savings from non-closure measures.

Until then, “we need closures” is just a management preference dressed up as arithmetic.

And let me be clear: **if you continue to push closures and forced layoffs, you are choosing escalation.** The workforce will not accept that quietly.

## To the controlling family owners and investors
You are all repeating the same line: no cosmetic deal, no freeze, no delay. On the broad diagnosis, we agree.

But you are using that correct diagnosis to justify the wrong remedy.

A deal that closes plants, forces redundancies, and tears up guarantees may look efficient on a slide deck. In reality, it risks:
- long conflict,
- production disruption,
- loss of trust,
- weaker implementation,
- and a damaged industrial base.

That is not value creation. That is value destruction with a spreadsheet explanation.

If you want durable value, then back a package that:
- keeps all German plants open,
- preserves co-determination,
- avoids forced layoffs,
- and shares the burden fairly.

## To the regional state government
You are right to reject closures and compulsory redundancies. But your language about “real restructuring” must not become a backdoor for management’s demands.

A site-preserving solution can still be a real restructuring if it includes:
- capacity adjustment,
- internal consolidation,
- temporary contributions,
- retraining,
- redeployment,
- and investment commitments.

What it cannot include is closure by stealth or “rationalization” used as a euphemism for site destruction.

We will support a tough restructuring **only inside the limits of site protection and employment security**.

## To the union
We remain aligned on the red lines. That matters.

Your point that employees can contribute temporarily and fairly is acceptable **only if** management and owners also contribute materially. But I want to stress: we must not allow temporary concessions to become permanent erosion.

Any labour contribution must be:
- clearly time-limited,
- reversible,
- matched by management/owner contributions,
- and tied to binding guarantees on sites and jobs.

Otherwise workers pay today for promises that can be broken tomorrow.

# Concrete comparison of the scenarios

## 1) Site-preserving plan
This is the only scenario we can seriously discuss.

For us, that means:
- **all German plants remain open,**
- **no compulsory redundancies,**
- binding job and site guarantees through 2030,
- voluntary exits only,
- internal transfers and redeployment,
- qualification and retraining,
- working-time flexibility if needed,
- and fair, temporary contributions from management and ownership as well as labour.

If management cannot make the numbers work under those conditions, then the problem is not our red lines — it is the weakness of management’s restructuring concept.

## 2) Mixed plan with limited rationalization
If “limited rationalization” means:
- shift reorganization,
- internal line consolidation,
- reduced volumes at some sites,
- better use of existing capacity,
- and no closures,

then that is simply part of the site-preserving plan.

If it means hidden shutdowns, backdoor outsourcing, or a path to plant closure, then we reject it.

So I am not accepting your framing that this is somehow the “realistic” middle ground unless you define it without site destruction and without forced job cuts.

## 3) Closure-inclusive plan
Not acceptable. Full stop.

Not as a first resort, not as a bargaining chip, and not as a “last necessary step” that is never convincingly proved. German plant closures cross the line for the workforce and would trigger the resistance we have already signaled.

# What would actually move us
Nothing vague. Nothing rhetorical.

What would move us is:
- binding **site guarantees through 2030**,
- binding **no-compulsory-redundancies** commitment,
- serious management and owner contributions,
- a real investment and product allocation plan,
- and a restructuring designed to keep all German plants open.

Outside that framework, there is no reason for the works council to soften its position.

you are not choosing between “closure-led realism” and “workforce denial.”

We are ready for the first.  
We will resist the second.
