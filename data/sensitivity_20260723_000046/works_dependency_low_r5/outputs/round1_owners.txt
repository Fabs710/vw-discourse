I’ll be direct.

We agree on one essential point: **the company needs a credible path back to competitiveness and margin strength**. That is not optional. If the cost base remains structurally too high and overcapacity is left untouched, the long-term value of the business will be eroded, and that helps no one — not the workforce, not the state, and certainly not the owners.

But I also need to be equally clear about what I do **not** accept.

### To management
Your diagnosis may be broadly right, but your current framing is too blunt if it is interpreted as “closures and forced cuts first, everything else later.”  
A value-destructive confrontation would be a serious failure of governance. A prolonged strike, reputational damage, and a breakdown of labor relations would destroy value faster than any temporary savings create it.

So I do **not** support a strategy that simply escalates toward closure as the default instrument. If closures are truly necessary, they must be justified by a demonstrably credible industrial plan, not used as a negotiating hammer. Management must show:
- where the savings come from,
- how durable they are,
- what investment follows,
- and how operational continuity is preserved.

“Structural savings” is the right goal. **A self-inflicted industrial war is not.**

### To works council and union
Your refusal of plant closures and compulsory redundancies is understandable, but I will not pretend that every red line can remain untouched if the business reality demands change.  
If the answer is “no closures, no redundancies, no meaningful labor-cost adjustment,” then you are not offering a solution either — you are describing a freeze.

That said, I do agree with you on one crucial point: **the burden cannot fall one-sidedly on employees**.  
A serious settlement would need:
- voluntary measures first,
- redeployment and qualification,
- working-time flexibility,
- and a real contribution from labor costs and legacy arrangements.

I would also expect management and the broader shareholder side to support the transition through:
- investment commitments,
- product allocation,
- and operational clarity about the future footprint.

### To the regional state government
Your role as an anchor shareholder and political stabilizer matters. I support preserving industrial capacity in Germany **where it is economically defensible**. But the state cannot simply veto every structural adjustment and expect the company to remain competitive by goodwill alone.

If the state wants to protect jobs and sites, then it should help secure a package that improves the company’s economics, not block necessary change outright.  
A blanket rejection of any plant closure is too rigid if the result is a weakening of the whole enterprise.

### My position
From the perspective of the controlling owners, the priority is simple:
1. **restore competitiveness**,  
2. **protect long-term enterprise value**,  
3. **avoid value-destructive instability**,  
4. **preserve governance control and the company’s strategic integrity**.

That means I am **open to a negotiated package**, but only if it delivers real structural improvement. I am **not** open to an arrangement that merely delays the problem, preserves all legacy structures, and leaves the company unfit for the next cycle.

So where do I stand?

- **Yes** to a durable restructuring.
- **Yes** to serious labor-side contributions if they are part of a fair, balanced package.
- **Yes** to investment and future-product commitments.
- **No** to a cosmetic deal.
- **No** to a prolonged conflict that destroys value.
- **No** to any outcome that permanently weakens the enterprise’s governance or strategic capacity.

If the parties want my support, they need to bring a package that actually closes the competitiveness gap. If they cannot, then the company will have to keep its options open, including more difficult structural measures.
