I’ll respond directly, and I’ll be equally clear.

## My overall position
The state is **not** prepared to support a restructuring that relies on **German plant closures** or **compulsory redundancies**. That remains outside the acceptable range. As a shareholder with a blocking minority and co-determination responsibility, I am not going to endorse a solution that guts regional employment or weakens the company’s role as an industrial anchor in the state.

At the same time, I do **not** support a frozen status quo. The company does need a real restructuring: lower costs, better capacity utilization, and credible investment in future products. But that restructuring must be achieved **without site destruction** and **without making workers the sole shock absorber**.

## Where I agree
### With management
I agree that:
- this is not a cosmetic problem,
- the cost gap is real,
- the company needs durable competitiveness,
- and prolonged conflict would be value-destructive.

Those points are correct.

### With the workforce side
I also agree that:
- the burden cannot fall only on employees,
- a deal needs fairness and trust,
- and temporary, negotiated contributions can be part of a solution.

That is all right.

### With the family owners and investors
I agree that:
- a weak, merely symbolic compromise would be irresponsible,
- delay is costly,
- and the company must restore margins.

So far, the diagnosis is broadly shared.

## Where I disagree
### To management
You still present closures and compulsory redundancies as if they are the natural conclusion of the economics. I do **not** accept that leap.

You have not shown that the only way to achieve the needed savings is to close German plants. Before you ask for closures, you owe the board a serious comparison of alternatives:
- working-time changes,
- internal consolidation,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- temporary labour-cost contributions,
- site specialization,
- and product allocation changes.

If those options are insufficient, prove it with plant-by-plant numbers. Until then, closures are not a necessity; they are a choice.

And I want to say this plainly: a closure-led path is not low-risk. It risks strikes, political escalation, and long-term damage to the company’s legitimacy in its home region. That is not decisive leadership; it may be self-inflicted instability.

### To the union and works council
I understand and support your red lines against:
- German plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- and dismantling of the job-security framework.

That said, I will also say this: a non-closure solution has to be **real restructuring**, not a freeze in softer language. So if the company can show that all German sites remain open and still the economics improve through capacity rationalization, productivity improvements, and fair temporary contributions, then that is the path we should pursue.

But that still requires the other side to prove the economics, not just demand sacrifice from the workforce.

### To the controlling family owners and investors
I agree that value matters, and I agree that an uncontrolled industrial conflict would be damaging. But I reject the idea that preserving German sites is somehow anti-economic by definition.

There is more than one way to restore competitiveness. A settlement that protects industrial capacity, avoids mass layoffs, and restores margins through negotiated change is not “soft”; it is often the only durable solution in a co-determined company with blocking minorities and a powerful workforce.

### To all capital-side actors
You keep asking for “durable economics.” Fair enough. But durability also means social and political viability. A deal that looks efficient on paper but detonates the workforce and alienates the region is not durable.

## My concrete position on the scenarios
### 1) Site-preserving plan
This is the only direction the state can seriously support.

But let me be precise: site-preserving does **not** mean static. It means:
- all German plants remain open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- voluntary exits and internal transfers first,
- qualification and retraining,
- working-time flexibility,
- targeted productivity measures,
- and a fair, temporary employee contribution if, and only if, management and owners also contribute materially.

It must also include a credible investment package for future products and technology.

That is a real restructuring. It is not a freeze.

### 2) Mixed plan with limited rationalization
This can be acceptable only if “rationalization” means:
- internal consolidation,
- shift reorganization,
- line specialization,
- better capacity utilization,
- and possibly lower volumes at some sites,

without German plant closures and without compulsory redundancies.

If “limited rationalization” is just a euphemism for a backdoor closure plan, then no.

### 3) Closure-inclusive plan
Not acceptable.

I want to be unambiguous: the state will not support closures as a first or default answer. If management insists on closure as the starting point, then you should expect the full use of the state’s governance rights. We are not here to bless industrial downsizing as a strategy of convenience.

## What I need to see next
If we want a settlement, I need the parties to put **actual numbers** on the table:
- How much savings are required?
- How much can be achieved without closures?
- What is the maximum acceptable temporary contribution?
- What capacity reduction can be done internally?
- What investment commitments are financeable and binding?

That is the proper next step.

## Bottom line
My position is simple:

- **No German plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Yes to real restructuring**
- **Yes to shared burden**
- **Yes to investment for the future**
- **No to a value-destructive industrial showdown**
- **No to a solution that sacrifices the state’s economic anchor**

If management wants my support, bring a serious non-closure package with credible economics. If that cannot be done, then the burden of escalation does not lie with the state.
