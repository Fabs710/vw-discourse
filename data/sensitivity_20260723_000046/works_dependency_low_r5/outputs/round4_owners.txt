I’ll respond directly.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, investors, and the state on the basic diagnosis: this company needs a **real** restructuring. A cosmetic deal or a short-term delay package would destroy value. The margin gap, the cost base, and the competitiveness problem are real.

I also agree with the works council and union on one important point: **prolonged conflict would be value-destructive**. A strike spiral is not a solution. It would damage cash flow, trust, and implementation.

And I agree that any burden-sharing must be **fairly distributed**. Employees cannot be the only shock absorber.

## Where I disagree
### To the works council and union
Your red lines are clear, but they are still **not an economics answer**.

You keep insisting:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of guarantees.

I understand the position. But I do **not** accept it as sufficient unless you can show, with hard numbers, that the company can still reach the required savings and capacity reduction. So far, I have not seen that.

What I need is not slogans about preserving everything. I need a credible package that demonstrates:
- how much capacity is reduced,
- how much headcount comes out,
- how much cost is removed,
- and how that is sustainable beyond 2030.

If the non-closure route can truly deliver the economics, I will support it. But that has not been proven.

### To the regional state government
I respect the state’s role and the political reality, but I do **not** accept a blanket veto posture as a substitute for restructuring economics.

If you rule out closures and compulsory redundancies entirely, then you must also be prepared to back a package that produces **comparable structural savings**. Otherwise, you are not solving the problem; you are postponing it.

### To management
I support your insistence on durable economics. But I also want to be precise: if you push closures and forced redundancies as the starting point, you may create the very instability that destroys value.

So I need management to show, in a quantified way, why the non-closure alternatives are insufficient:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- internal consolidation,
- working-time changes,
- specialization,
- and temporary burden-sharing.

If those measures cannot close the gap, say so clearly. If they can, then closures should not be the default answer.

## On the scenarios

### 1) Site-preserving plan
I will only consider this if it is **not** a freeze in disguise.

For me, it would need to include:
- substantial capacity reduction,
- meaningful headcount reduction,
- durable labor-cost relief,
- changes to legacy arrangements,
- and a binding investment package.

If “site-preserving” means preserving the current cost structure, then it is not acceptable.

### 2) Mixed plan with limited rationalization
This is the most promising route if it is real.

I can support:
- site specialization,
- line consolidation,
- lower volumes at some sites,
- internal redeployment,
- voluntary exits first,
- temporary and reversible employee contributions,

provided the package produces durable savings and a credible path back to target margins.

### 3) Closure-inclusive plan
I will not rule it out.

I am not saying closures are my preferred route. I am saying they remain on the table if the alternative does not deliver comparable savings. That is not a threat; it is the reality of the economics.

## What would move me
What would move me is a package with:
- quantified capacity reduction,
- quantified labor-cost relief,
- quantified headcount reduction,
- a credible implementation timetable,
- and binding investment commitments.

If the employee side can put a non-closure package on the table that genuinely closes the competitiveness gap, I will engage seriously. If it only preserves the current structure under a softer label, then I cannot support it.

## My bottom line
I am open to a negotiated solution. I am **not** open to a freeze.

So the choice remains:
- a restructuring that materially reduces cost and capacity and restores competitiveness, or
- a cosmetic arrangement that leaves the company uncompetitive and forces harder decisions later.

I will not support the second.

If the next round is serious, bring the numbers:
- exact capacity reduction,
- exact labor-cost contribution,
- exact headcount reduction,
- and the exact investment commitments tied to it.

Without that, we are not close to a durable settlement.
