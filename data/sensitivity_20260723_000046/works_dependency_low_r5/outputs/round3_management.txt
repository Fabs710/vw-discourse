I’ll be equally direct in round 3.

## First: what I agree with
I agree with everyone on one essential point: **a cosmetic deal is worthless**.  
If we do not reduce the cost base and the capacity base in a durable way, then we will not restore competitiveness, we will not hit the return-on-sales target, and we will not free up the funds needed for 2030 product and technology investment.

I also agree that **prolonged conflict destroys value**.  
A strike spiral helps no one — not the workforce, not owners, not investors, not the state, and certainly not the company’s future.

So the shared premise is clear:
- this company needs a real restructuring,
- it needs to be durable,
- and it must materially improve economics.

## Where I disagree — and sharply

### To the works council
Your position is still not a restructuring answer. It is a **preservation claim**.

You keep saying:
- all German plants stay open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding guarantees through 2030.

That may be emotionally understandable. It is not economically sufficient.

You still have **not** shown how that combination closes the gap created by:
- structurally high German factory costs,
- shrinking European demand,
- intense competition,
- and a brand that is missing its margin target.

You ask me to prove that closures are necessary. I am asking you to prove the opposite: that all sites can remain open **and** the economics still work. So far, I have not seen that proof.

And let me be clear: if the only answer is internal transfers, temporary working-time changes, and voluntary exits, then that is unlikely to deliver the scale of reduction we need. I will not pretend otherwise.

### To the union
You are right that the burden cannot fall only on workers. I agree with that.

But you are wrong if you think a **temporary** contribution and flexible arrangements can solve a **structural** cost problem on their own.  
We need **durable** labour-cost relief, not just reversible gestures that expire before the business is fixed.

And yes, if the response to every hard decision is warning strikes, that will not strengthen the workforce’s position. It will accelerate the case for more forceful measures. I do not say that lightly; I say it because time is running out.

### To the regional state government
I respect the state’s governance role and the political reality. But let’s be frank: a veto cannot be used to preserve uneconomic structure indefinitely.

Your position is internally coherent, but it risks turning “site protection” into “no change.” That is not acceptable if the business is to remain viable.

A socially acceptable solution is not the same thing as a viable one.  
We need both. Right now, you are still over-weighting the former and under-weighting the latter.

### To the controlling family owners
I agree that value destruction through an uncontrolled industrial war is unacceptable. I also agree that delay is destructive.

But I do **not** accept a position that treats every form of plant rationalisation as an optionality debate while the company keeps bleeding margin. If the non-closure path does not deliver the required economics, then we have to say so plainly.

If we hide behind language like “let’s keep all sites and somehow make it work,” we are avoiding the real decision.

### To investors
You are right to insist on measurable economics. But I want to be equally clear: if the employee side’s package does not produce substantial, durable savings, then it is not a credible alternative, no matter how much it is wrapped in social language.

## The hard reality
Let me put the economics plainly:

We need:
1. **lower capacity**,  
2. **lower headcount**,  
3. **lower labour costs**,  
4. **and more investment capacity for future products.**

Those are not slogans. They are the minimum conditions for restoring competitiveness.

A plan that leaves the footprint largely intact and only adds temporary concessions will not do the job.

## On the scenarios

### 1) Site-preserving plan
I will only consider this if it delivers **real structural change**, not just protection rhetoric.

For me, a site-preserving plan would still have to include:
- substantial capacity reduction across the German footprint,
- meaningful headcount reduction,
- durable labour-cost relief,
- changes to legacy arrangements,
- and a binding investment package tied to the restructuring.

If “site-preserving” means every plant stays untouched and every job guarantee is preserved, then it is not a serious restructuring proposal. Full stop.

### 2) Mixed plan with limited rationalization
This is the only route that looks potentially realistic **if** it actually means:
- line consolidation,
- site specialization,
- lower volumes at some sites,
- internal redeployment where possible,
- voluntary exits first,
- and compulsory measures available if the numbers do not come out otherwise.

If your definition of “limited rationalization” is simply a nicer word for avoiding hard decisions, then I reject it.

But if it is a serious package that produces durable cost and capacity savings, I am prepared to work with it.

### 3) Closure-inclusive plan
I will not rule this out.

I am not saying closures are my preferred answer. I am saying they remain on the table if the alternative does not deliver comparable savings. That is not a threat for effect; it is the reality of the arithmetic.

If the other sides want closures off the table, then they need to bring a package that actually fixes the economics. Otherwise, we are just postponing the problem.

## What would move me
Not slogans. Not appeals to tradition. Not general promises of future cooperation.

What would move me is a package with:
- quantified capacity reduction,
- quantified labour-cost relief,
- quantified headcount reduction,
- a credible implementation path,
- and binding investment commitments.

If the employee side can put a non-closure package on the table that genuinely closes the competitiveness gap, I will engage seriously. But if the package merely preserves the current structure under a different name, then it is not acceptable.

## My bottom line
I am open to a negotiated solution. I am **not** open to a freeze.

So the choice remains:

- **A restructuring that materially reduces cost and capacity and restores competitiveness**, or
- **a cosmetic arrangement that leaves the company uncompetitive and forces harder decisions later**.

I will not support the second.

If the next step is serious, then bring the numbers:
- exact capacity reduction,
- exact labour-cost contribution,
- exact headcount reduction,
- and the exact investment commitments tied to it.

Without that, we are not close to a durable settlement.
