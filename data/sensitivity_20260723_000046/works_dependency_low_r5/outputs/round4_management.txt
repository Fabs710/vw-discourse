- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding guarantees through 2030.

- far-too-high German factory costs,
- shrinking European demand,

- plant-by-plant capacity impact,
- headcount impact,

## On the compromise positions

### 1) Non-closure restructuring with quantified fallback trigger
This is the only route I see as potentially workable **if** it is structured correctly.

But let me be explicit: a fallback trigger cannot be a disguise for permanent delay. It must mean:
- phase one delivers measurable savings,
- phase one has a strict review date,
- and if targets are missed, the second phase must include additional measures that are not pre-ruled out.

If you want zero closures and zero compulsory redundancies forever, then you are not negotiating restructuring. You are negotiating immobility.

### 2) Site-preserving rationalization with differentiated plant roles
This is more promising, but only if it is real.

I can accept:
- site specialization,
- internal consolidation,
- lower volumes at some sites,
- product allocation shifts,
- and capacity reduction inside the German footprint.

But “specialization” cannot mean preserving everything and pretending the numbers improve. It must mean:
- some sites do less,
- some processes are removed,
- some overhead is eliminated,
- and the cost base falls materially.

### 3) Temporary burden-sharing with hard end-date and locked-in guarantees
I am open to that as part of a broader package.

But only under one condition: it must produce **durable** economics, not just temporary relief. If labor contributes temporarily, management and owners must contribute materially too. However, temporary concessions alone are insufficient. We need structural change.
