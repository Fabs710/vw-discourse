**Our position has not changed:**
- **No German plant closures.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No dismantling of the collective wage framework.**
- **No settlement where workers carry the main burden while management and owners stay protected.**

If management wants a deal, it has to stop treating closures and forced layoffs as the default answer. They are not a neutral “economic necessity” unless that claim is proven convincingly and transparently. So far, it has not been.

## To management
I agree with you on one point: a cosmetic deal is useless.  
I also agree that the company needs real restructuring, not delay.

But your argument still suffers from the same flaw: you say the company needs durable structural change, then immediately jump to closures and compulsory redundancies as if that were the only serious route. That is not proven.

You have still **not** shown:
- why all German plants cannot remain open,
- why capacity reductions cannot be achieved through internal reorganization,
- why voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, shift changes, and working-time flexibility are insufficient,
- or why employees must be the first and main source of pain.

If you want to claim closures are unavoidable, then bring the plant-by-plant numbers:
- utilization,
- product allocation,
- investment needs,
- cost comparison,
- and the savings from non-closure measures.

Until then, closures are a management preference, not a demonstrated necessity.

And let me be clear: if you continue to push closures and forced job cuts, you should expect renewed warning strikes and broader industrial action. That is not posturing. That is the consequence of breaking trust and shifting the crisis onto the workforce.

## To the works council
We are aligned on the red lines, and that matters.  
Your insistence on site protection and job security is fully legitimate.

I would only add one point: a site-preserving solution must not become a slogan. If we are to accept temporary contributions from employees, they must be:
- limited in time,
- reversible,
- tied to binding site and job guarantees,
- and matched by management and owner contributions.

That is the only way this becomes a fair package rather than a one-sided wage concession.

## To the regional state government
I support your clear rejection of closures and compulsory redundancies. That line is right.

But you also have to be careful not to let “real restructuring” become a backdoor for the same outcome under a softer name. If the company needs capacity adjustment, then do it through:
- internal consolidation,
- working-time changes,
- voluntary measures,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- and fair contributions from all sides.

A political statement is not enough; we need a settlement that makes the numbers work without site destruction.

## To the controlling family owners and investors
You keep speaking about long-term value, and I understand that. But value is not created by making the workforce absorb the entire crisis.

A deal that:
- closes plants,
- forces redundancies,
- tears up guarantees,
- and weakens the collective wage framework

may look efficient in a spreadsheet. In reality it risks:
- prolonged conflict,
- production disruption,
- lower trust,
- and a damaged industrial base.

That is not durable value creation. That is short-term financial logic at the expense of long-term stability.

If you want a serious settlement, then support:
- a fair burden-sharing package,
- lower but reversible capacity,
- internal redeployment,
- and binding investment commitments.

## Concrete position on the scenarios

### 1) Site-preserving plan
This is the only scenario we can seriously engage with.

For us, that means:
- all German plants remain open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding job and site guarantees through 2030,
- internal transfers and redeployment,
- qualification and retraining,
- voluntary exit options only,
- temporary working-time and pay-related contributions only if clearly limited and fairly shared,
- and binding investment in future products and technology.

If management can make the numbers work under those conditions, then we have a basis for agreement.

### 2) Mixed plan with limited rationalization
If this means:
- capacity adjustment,
- shift reorganization,
- internal line consolidation,
- reduced volumes at some sites,
- better use of existing capacity,

then that can be part of a site-preserving plan.

If it means hidden closures, outsourcing by stealth, or a backdoor to forced layoffs, then no. We will not sign up to that.

### 3) Closure-inclusive plan
Not acceptable. Full stop.

That crosses the line for the workforce and would trigger the resistance management already knows is available. We are not negotiating the elimination of German sites.

## What would actually move us
What would move us is not rhetoric about inevitability. It is a credible, shared-sacrifice package:
- binding site guarantees,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- management and owners contributing materially,
- a temporary and fair employee contribution,
- and a real investment and product plan.

That is the minimum basis for further talks.

## Final word
The workforce is willing to contribute to saving the company.  
The workforce is **not** willing to pay for the crisis alone.

So the choice is straightforward:
1. a negotiated restructuring that preserves jobs, sites, and collective standards, or  
2. escalation caused by management’s insistence on closures and forced layoffs.

We are ready for the first.  
We will fight the second.

If management wants a settlement, it should come back with concrete non-closure numbers and a fair burden-sharing package. Until then, there is no reason for us to retreat from our line.
