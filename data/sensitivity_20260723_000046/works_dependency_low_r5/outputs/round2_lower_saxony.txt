I will be direct.

Management’s latest position still goes too far in the wrong direction, and the employee side is still not giving enough room for a serious industrial compromise. From the state’s perspective, the task is not to preserve every status quo element at any cost, but it is also **not** to accept plant closures and forced redundancies as the default answer. That line remains firm.

## Where I agree

### With management
You are right that this company needs a **credible, durable** restructuring. A cosmetic deal would be irresponsible. The company does need:
- lower costs,
- a better capacity balance,
- and investment space for future products.

### With the workforce side
You are right that the burden must **not** fall one-sidedly on employees. A restructuring that simply destroys trust, breaks site commitments, and pushes the crisis onto workers will be socially and politically unacceptable, and it will likely be operationally unstable too.

### With the family owners and investors
You are right that a prolonged stalemate would destroy value. The company cannot afford endless conflict and drift.

## Where I disagree

### To management
I do **not** accept your framing that the only meaningful options are closures and compulsory redundancies.

You have not shown that a package with:
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary labour-cost contributions,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary exits,
- and targeted capacity adjustments

cannot deliver a serious part of the savings needed.

You are asking for structural change, which is legitimate. But your current line risks crossing into **industrial self-harm**:
- shutting German plants,
- provoking strikes,
- damaging regional economies,
- and undermining the very co-determination structure that has made this company stable for decades.

That is not a costless route to competitiveness. It is a high-risk one.

I also reject the implication that a political veto is somehow an illegitimate obstacle. The statute and co-determination are part of the company’s constitutional reality. If management wants durable support, it has to work **with** that governance model, not treat it as an inconvenience to be bypassed.

### To the works council and union
I support the red line against **German plant closures** and I support rejecting **compulsory redundancies** as a first resort. But I also need to be honest: simply saying “all sites stay open, no forced cuts” is not enough by itself if the company’s cost base remains structurally uncompetitive.

So I am not endorsing a freeze. I am saying the solution must be found **without destroying sites and jobs**.

That means the employee side should be prepared to discuss:
- temporary, negotiated labour-cost relief,
- shifts and working-time adaptations,
- internal mobility,
- productivity improvements,
- and voluntary schemes.

But those discussions only make sense if they remain within a framework of **site protection and no compulsory redundancies**.

## My position on the scenarios

### 1) Site-preserving plan
This is the **only** direction that can be politically and socially acceptable from the state’s side.

But I am not backing a blank cheque. A site-preserving plan must still be real restructuring. It should include:
- capacity rationalisation **without plant closures** where possible,
- better utilization of existing sites,
- internal consolidation,
- voluntary exits,
- qualification and redeployment,
- and a fair, temporary contribution from the workforce and owners.

What I will not support is a plan that simply preserves all current structures unchanged and hopes the problem disappears.

### 2) Mixed plan with limited rationalisation
This is the most realistic basis for a settlement **if** “rationalisation” means internal reorganization, not plant shutdown by another name.

I can see room for:
- line consolidation,
- shift adjustments,
- site specialization,
- and potentially reduced production volumes at some sites,

provided that:
- no German plant is closed,
- no compulsory redundancies are imposed,
- and there are binding commitments on investment and future allocation of products.

### 3) Closure-inclusive plan
This is where I must be explicit: **not acceptable** as the basis of a settlement.

A closure-inclusive path would:
- damage regional employment,
- deepen the political conflict,
- endanger the social partnership model,
- and run directly against the state’s role as anchor shareholder.

If that remains management’s preferred route, then you should expect the state to use every legitimate governance instrument available.

## What I need to see next
If we want a serious way forward, then stop arguing in abstractions and put **numbers and safeguards** on the table.

Specifically:
- What cost savings can be achieved **without** closures?
- What capacity reduction can be achieved through internal reorganization?
- What voluntary workforce reduction is realistic?
- What temporary labour-cost contribution are you asking for, and for how long?
- What binding investment and product commitments follow?

Management should answer those questions first, but the workforce side should also be prepared to state what it can responsibly accept **without** violating site protection and employment security.

## Bottom line
My position is this:

- **No plant closures in Germany**
- **No compulsory redundancies as the restructuring tool of first resort**
- **Yes to a negotiated restructuring that protects the state’s sites and the company’s long-term future**
- **Yes to temporary, fair contributions if they are shared and reversible**
- **No to a value-destructive confrontation or a governance override of the company’s social model**

So I am not joining management’s push toward closure-led restructuring.  
But I am also not endorsing a frozen status quo.  

A real compromise must protect **jobs, sites, and the company’s future competitiveness** together. If the next round cannot produce a serious non-closure package with credible economics, then the responsibility for escalation will not lie with the state.
