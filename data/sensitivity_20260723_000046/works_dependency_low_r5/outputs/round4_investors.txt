We agree on the **diagnosis** and on the need for a **real restructuring**. What we do **not** agree with is any package that preserves the current cost base, avoids hard decisions, and leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

## Where we agree
- A cosmetic deal is worthless.
- Prolonged industrial conflict would destroy value.
- The company needs durable margin repair, not delay.
- Some mix of voluntary exits, redeployment, retraining, working-time flexibility, and investment commitments is sensible.

## Where we disagree

### To management
You are right that the company needs lower capacity, lower headcount, and lower labour cost. We agree.

But you still have not made the case that **closures are necessary** rather than simply preferred. Investors do not need slogans about “durable structural change”; we need **quantified economics**.

Bring:
- plant-by-plant utilization,
- product allocation,
- fixed-cost burden,
- savings from internal rationalization,
- savings from voluntary exits and redeployment,
- and the residual gap that would justify closures or compulsory redundancies.

Right now, your position is directionally correct but still too binary: it jumps from “problem” to “closures/forced cuts” without proving that non-closure measures cannot do the job.

That said, we also do **not** accept the works council’s suggestion that all German plants can stay open and the savings will somehow appear. If the numbers do not work, they do not work.

### To the works council and union
We respect the red lines against cosmetic compromise, but your current position is too rigid to be credible as a financial solution.

- **No closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Full guarantees through 2030**
- **No structural footprint reduction**

That looks less like restructuring and more like freezing a cost base that management says is already too high. If that is the settlement, then margins remain impaired and the problem is simply deferred.

Temporary, reversible labor contributions may be acceptable, but only if they are **large enough to matter** and tied to real structural savings. Otherwise they are just delay.

### To the regional state government
Your role and veto are real, but a blanket rejection of closures and compulsory redundancies is only defensible if the non-closure route demonstrably restores competitiveness.

We agree that social stability matters. But so does financial viability. A politically comfortable solution that leaves the company uncompetitive is not a durable solution.

### To the controlling family holding
We agree that the company needs decisive action and that a value-destructive strike spiral is unacceptable. But “decisive” does not automatically mean “closures first.”

The family should back the **best quantified package**, not a reflexive closure strategy. If a non-closure alternative truly delivers comparable savings and capacity reduction, it should be preferred because it reduces implementation risk and preserves operational stability.

## Our position on the scenarios

### 1) Site-preserving plan
Potentially acceptable **only if** it delivers:
- substantial capacity reduction,
- meaningful and durable labor-cost relief,
- real headcount reduction,
- and a binding investment plan.

If it merely keeps all plants open and softens the edges, we would not support it.

### 2) Mixed plan with limited rationalization
This is the most plausible path **if it is serious**.

A differentiated footprint could be acceptable if it includes:
- site specialization,
- internal consolidation,
- reduced volumes at some sites,
- voluntary exits first,
- redeployment and retraining,
- temporary burden-sharing,
- and clear, measurable margin improvement.

This is the scenario we would want tested first, because it may deliver the needed savings with less industrial disruption than closures.

### 3) Closure-inclusive plan
We are **not** endorsing closures as a default. But we will not rule them out if management proves that the non-closure path cannot close the gap.

That is the key point: the burden of proof is on the party claiming that closures are economically unavoidable. Until then, they remain an option, not a conclusion.

## What would move us
What would move capital markets is:
- hard numbers,
- a credible implementation timeline,
- durable cost reduction,
- and a believable path back to competitive margins.

What will **not** move us:
- vague promises,
- political declarations,
- cosmetic labor concessions,
- or a settlement that preserves the current structure under a different label.

## Bottom line
We will back the solution that:
- restores competitiveness,
- materially improves margins,
- is implementable,
- and avoids unnecessary conflict.

That means:
- **yes** to real restructuring,
- **yes** to durable savings,
- **yes** to serious burden-sharing if it produces actual economics,
- **no** to a freeze,
- **no** to cosmetic compromise,
- **no** to a package that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive.

If the next round cannot produce a quantified non-closure package with credible economics, then the claim that closures are avoidable remains unproven.
