We agree on the **diagnosis**: the company needs a real restructuring, and a cosmetic settlement would be value-destructive. We also agree that prolonged conflict is bad. Where we part company is on whether a non-closure plan can actually deliver the **durable economics** needed.

## To management
Your emphasis on structural cost reduction is right. We are aligned with the need for:
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- lower labour cost,
- headcount reduction,
- and investment capacity for future products.

But you still have not made the case that **closures and compulsory redundancies are the only path**. You keep asserting the economics, but the investor question is very simple: **show us the numbers**.

What we need is a credible scenario comparison:
- savings from internal rationalization,
- savings from voluntary exits and redeployment,
- savings from working-time and legacy changes,
- and only then the residual gap that would require closures or compulsory measures.

If non-closure measures can close the gap, then closure-led restructuring is not justified. If they cannot, then say so plainly. Right now, management is asking the market to accept a high-risk confrontation without proving necessity.

We also do **not** support a strategy that triggers a strike spiral just to demonstrate toughness. That would destroy value, damage cash flow, and delay the margin repair you say you want.

## To the works council
We understand and respect the desire to protect sites and jobs, but your current position is too rigid to be financeable unless it comes with **real economics**.

A stance of:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- full guarantees through 2030,
- and no meaningful structural footprint reduction

looks less like restructuring and more like freezing the current cost base. That is not acceptable to capital markets.

If you want investor support, a site-preserving package must still produce:
- material capacity reduction,
- durable labour-cost relief,
- and measurable headcount reduction.

Temporary, reversible concessions are fine **only if** they are large enough and actually move margins. Otherwise they are cosmetic.

## To the union
We agree that the burden should not fall only on employees. But a package built around temporary contributions, voluntary exits, and working-time flexibility only works if it produces **durable** savings.

The capital market will not reward a settlement that merely postpones the problem until after 2030. We need:
- credible, multi-year cost reduction,
- implementation certainty,
- and a clear path back to competitive returns on sales.

If the union wants to avoid closures, then it needs to help assemble a package that makes those closures unnecessary on economic grounds, not just politically inconvenient.

## To the regional state government
We understand the political and statutory constraints. But a blanket rejection of closures and compulsory redundancies cannot be the starting point if that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

The state is right to insist on social stability, but it must also accept that **value destruction through delay** is not a free alternative. A non-closure solution is acceptable only if it delivers comparable economics. Otherwise, it risks trading one problem for another.

## To the controlling family owners
You are right that a value-destructive industrial war would be unacceptable. We agree.

But value is also destroyed by preserving excess structure and pretending that the business will fix itself. The market needs decisive action, not ambiguity. The family’s role should be to insist on a solution that is both **implementable** and **economically real**.

## Our position on the scenarios

### 1) Site-preserving plan
Potentially acceptable **only if** it includes:
- substantial capacity reduction,
- meaningful and durable labour-cost relief,
- material headcount reduction,
- and a binding investment program.

If it simply keeps all sites open and avoids hard decisions, then we cannot support it.

### 2) Mixed plan with limited rationalization
This is the most plausible route **if** it is serious.  
It could work if it delivers:
- line consolidation,
- site specialization,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- working-time changes,
- and enough structural savings to restore margins.

This is the scenario we would want tested first.

### 3) Closure-inclusive plan
We are **not** saying closures should be the first answer. But we will not rule them out if management proves that the non-closure path does not deliver the required economics. The key issue is not ideology; it is whether the package actually restores competitiveness.

## What would move us
What would move capital markets is:
- hard numbers,
- a credible implementation timetable,
- durable savings,
- and a clear link between restructuring and margin recovery.

What will **not** move us:
- vague promises,
- political declarations,
- cosmetic labor concessions,
- or a freeze dressed up as social partnership.

## Bottom line
We are prepared to back **the solution that credibly restores competitive margins and protects enterprise value**.

That means:
- **yes** to real restructuring,
- **yes** to durable cost reduction,
- **yes** to negotiated burden-sharing if it is economically meaningful,
- **no** to a cosmetic compromise,
- **no** to a freeze that leaves the cost base uncompetitive,
- **no** to a prolonged strike that destroys value,
- **no** to any package that fails to prove it can restore margins on a durable basis.

If the next round cannot put a quantified, economically credible non-closure package on the table, then the claim that closures are avoidable remains unproven.
