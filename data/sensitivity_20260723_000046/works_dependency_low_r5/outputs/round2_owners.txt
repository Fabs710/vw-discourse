I will respond plainly.

## Where I agree

I agree with almost everyone on the core diagnosis: **the company needs a durable competitiveness fix, not a cosmetic deal**.  
If the current cost base, capacity base, and labour-cost structure remain materially unchanged, then the group will continue to underperform and lose strategic room to invest. That would erode long-term value for everyone, including the workforce and the regional economy.

I also agree that **a prolonged industrial war would be value-destructive**. A strike spiral, reputational damage, and governance paralysis would weaken the company faster than any temporary savings could help.

So on the principle of **structural change**, I am aligned with management and with investors.

## Where I disagree

### To the works council and union
I understand your red lines, but I do **not** accept them as an answer to the economics.

A position of:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no meaningful labour-cost change,
- and full job guarantees through 2030

is not a restructuring. It is a freeze.

A freeze would leave the company with:
- excess capacity,
- structurally high German costs,
- weak margins,
- and less investment capacity for future products.

That is not a durable solution and I will not support it.

At the same time, I do **not** support management using closures as a blunt negotiating weapon if a credible alternative exists. The burden must be shared, and a settlement that needlessly destroys trust would also destroy value.

### To management
Your diagnosis is directionally right, but your framing is still too binary.  
If you present closures and compulsory redundancies as the default answer, you are very likely to trigger the exact instability that destroys value.

I will not back a course that creates a self-inflicted industrial crisis just to prove decisiveness.

What I need from management is not rhetoric about inevitability, but a **credible comparison of restructuring scenarios**:
- what can be achieved without closures,
- what requires rationalisation,
- and what savings truly depend on plant shutdowns or compulsory measures.

If the hard numbers show that closures are unavoidable, then that case must be made clearly and defensibly. If they do not, then management must not hide behind them.

### To the regional state government
I respect the political and statutory reality, and I do not dismiss the state’s role. But I cannot support using governance rights to preserve every current structure regardless of economics.

The company cannot be run as a social subsidy system with a car brand attached.  
If the business remains uncompetitive, the eventual damage to employment, investment, and regional strength will be worse.

So I support your aim of protecting Germany as an industrial base — **where that is economically defensible**. I do not support a blanket veto on necessary adjustment.

### To institutional investors
I agree with your emphasis on durable economics and execution certainty.  
But I also think you underestimate how much value can be destroyed by an uncontrolled confrontation.

A “technically optimal” restructuring that triggers years of labour conflict, political blockage, and reputational damage is not value-maximizing.  
The right package has to be **economically strong and governable**.

## My position on the scenarios

### 1) Site-preserving plan
This is only acceptable if it is more than a slogan.

For me, a site-preserving plan would need to deliver:
- substantial capacity reduction,
- meaningful labour-cost relief,
- a material headcount reduction,
- changes to working time and legacy arrangements,
- and a binding investment plan tied to future products.

If “site-preserving” means all current structures stay essentially intact, then I reject it. That would not restore competitiveness.

### 2) Mixed plan with limited rationalisation
This is the most realistic route, provided it is real and not cosmetic.

I would consider:
- line consolidation,
- site specialization,
- capacity reductions short of blanket closure where possible,
- voluntary exits first,
- redeployment and qualification,
- and temporary workforce contributions,

**if and only if** the package produces durable cost savings and a credible path back to margin targets.

That is the benchmark: not symbolic concessions, but measurable economics.

### 3) Closure-inclusive plan
I will not rule this out.

If the non-closure options do not close the competitiveness gap, then closures may be necessary. I am not endorsing them as a first choice, but I will not pretend they are off the table if they are required to protect the enterprise’s long-term value.

What I will not accept is a situation where closures are either:
- used lazily as the first answer, or
- excluded politically even if the economics require them.

## What I need next
We need to move from posturing to numbers.

I want management, the workforce side, and the state to answer these questions concretely:

1. **How much capacity reduction is actually needed?**  
2. **How much labour-cost relief is achievable without permanent damage to the business?**  
3. **How much headcount reduction can be achieved voluntarily, and where would compulsory measures become necessary if at all?**  
4. **What investment commitments are truly credible and financeable?**  
5. **Which scenario actually restores margins and competitiveness by 2030?**

Without those answers, we are not deciding — we are merely exchanging slogans.

## Bottom line

My position is straightforward:

- **Yes** to a real restructuring.
- **Yes** to a negotiated package if it delivers durable economics.
- **Yes** to shared burden, including management and owners.
- **No** to a freeze disguised as social responsibility.
- **No** to a value-destructive industrial war.
- **No** to any outcome that permanently weakens the company’s governance control or strategic integrity.

I am open to a non-closure solution **only if** it is genuinely capable of restoring competitiveness and margins.  
If it cannot do that, then the company must not be trapped by political or labour red lines that make the business unfit for the future.
