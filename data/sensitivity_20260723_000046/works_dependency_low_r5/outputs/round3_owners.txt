I will be direct.

## My position
The family holding supports a **real restructuring** and will not back a cosmetic deal or a delay tactic. The company needs durable margin repair, lower structural cost, and a credible path to competitiveness. That is the only way to protect the enterprise and the long-term value of the core asset.

At the same time, I do **not** want a self-inflicted industrial war. A drawn-out conflict would destroy value, damage the brand, and weaken the business for years. So I am not here to cheerlead closures for their own sake. I am here to back whatever package actually restores competitiveness **and** can be implemented without breaking the company.

## Where I agree
### With management
You are right that the current cost and capacity base is not sustainable. You are also right that temporary, symbolic measures are not enough.

I agree that we need:
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- lower labour costs,
- headcount reduction,
- and investment room for future products.

That part is non-negotiable from a value perspective.

### With investors
Your insistence on durable economics is correct. A weak compromise that merely postpones the problem is not acceptable.

### With the state
You are right that a deal must be socially and politically workable. A value-destructive confrontation would harm the company’s standing in Germany and beyond.

### With the workforce side
You are also right on one point: burden-sharing matters. A restructuring that puts everything on employees while management and owners do nothing would be unjust and unstable.

## Where I disagree
### To the works council and union
I do **not** accept the position that:
- all German plants must stay open,
- no compulsory redundancies are ever possible,
- and full job guarantees through 2030 are the default answer.

That is not a restructuring. It is a freeze. And a freeze would leave the company with structurally high costs and insufficient flexibility to compete.

I also do not accept the idea that closures are automatically “industrial destruction.” If a site is structurally redundant, keeping it open forever is not stewardship; it is denial.

That said, I am **not** endorsing closures as the first move. I want the numbers tested. If there is a serious non-closure package that truly delivers comparable savings and capacity reduction, I am open to it. But it must be economically real, not rhetorically comforting.

### To management
You are correct on the economics, but your framing is still too blunt.

If you push closures and compulsory redundancies as the default posture, you may create the very instability that destroys value. I need management to demonstrate, in hard numbers, why non-closure options are insufficient:
- voluntary exits,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time changes,
- site specialization,
- line consolidation,
- qualification,
- and temporary labour contributions.

If those do not close the gap, say so clearly. If they do, then closures should not be used as a negotiating shortcut.

### To the state
Your governance role is real, and I respect it. But it cannot become a permanent veto on necessary restructuring. If the company is to remain strong, governance must allow adjustment where economics require it.

## My view on the scenarios

### 1) Site-preserving plan
I would only consider this if it is **not** a freeze in disguise.

For me, it would need to include:
- substantial capacity reduction,
- meaningful headcount reduction,
- durable cost relief,
- productivity and work-rule changes,
- and a binding investment plan.

If “site-preserving” means all sites remain open with only light concessions, then I reject it. That will not restore margins.

### 2) Mixed plan with limited rationalisation
This is the most promising route **if** it is real.

I can support:
- line consolidation,
- site specialization,
- reduced volumes at some sites,
- internal transfers,
- voluntary exits first,
- temporary and reversible employee contributions,

provided the package produces durable savings and a credible path back to target margins.

This is the scenario I would want tested first, because it offers a chance to protect value without unnecessary escalation.

### 3) Closure-inclusive plan
I will not rule it out.

If the non-closure options do not deliver the required economics, then closures remain on the table. I am not saying they are desirable. I am saying they cannot be excluded if the business case demands them.

What I will not support is a fake compromise that preserves every structure, avoids every hard decision, and leaves the company uncompetitive.

## What I need next
We need concrete numbers, not slogans.

Each side should state:
- the minimum savings required,
- the maximum capacity reduction achievable without closures,
- the feasible headcount reduction from voluntary and internal measures,
- the size and duration of any labour contribution,
- and the investment commitments tied to each scenario.

Until then, the debate is incomplete.

## Bottom line
My position is straightforward:

- **Yes** to a real restructuring.
- **Yes** to shared burden.
- **Yes** to durable competitiveness.
- **No** to a freeze disguised as compromise.
- **No** to a value-destructive confrontation.
- **No** to any outcome that permanently weakens the company’s strategic position or governance integrity.

If a non-closure package can truly restore competitiveness, I will support it.  
If it cannot, then the company must not be trapped by political or labour red lines that make the business unfit for the future.
