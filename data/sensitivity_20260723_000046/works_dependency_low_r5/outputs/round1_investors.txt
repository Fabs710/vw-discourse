We are prepared to back a restructuring, but only if it is **credible, durable, and value-creating**. Right now, the debate is drifting toward extremes that would both be damaging for shareholders: either a blunt closure-led confrontation or a cosmetic compromise that leaves the cost base uncompetitive.

Let me be explicit.

### Where we agree
- **Management is right** that the company has a structural competitiveness problem.
- **The union and works council are right** that prolonged conflict and a one-sided burden on employees destroy value.
- **The family owners are right** that value destruction through delay would be unacceptable.
- **The state is right** that an industrial future in Germany matters if it is economically defensible.

### Where we disagree
#### 1) To works council and union
Your insistence on **no plant closures and no compulsory redundancies** may be politically understandable, but from a shareholder perspective it is not a sufficient answer if it prevents the company from reaching competitive margins.

If the outcome is a freeze that preserves every site and leaves structural overcapacity in place, then that is **not restructuring**. It is postponement. And postponement is costly:
- margins stay weak,
- cash generation remains under pressure,
- investment capacity is reduced,
- and the share price continues to reflect execution risk.

So we cannot support a package that simply protects the status quo.

That said, we also do **not** support management using closures as a negotiating club if there is a credible alternative that achieves comparable savings. The right test is not symbolism; it is whether the package **actually restores competitiveness**.

#### 2) To management
You are correct to reject a cosmetic deal, but you need to be careful not to overplay your hand.

A strategy that triggers a prolonged strike, major operational disruption, and a governance stalemate may be technically “decisive,” but it can still be **value-destructive**. If the market sees an industrial war without a clear end state, that will damage earnings visibility and investor confidence.

So we want:
- **real structural savings**,
- **clear capacity reduction**,
- **durable labour-cost relief**,
- **and a timetable that is executable**.

But we do not want a process that burns cash and destroys goodwill while producing only marginal net savings.

#### 3) To the state and family owners
Your emphasis on employment, governance, and regional stability is understandable. But investors cannot accept a structure where political and social constraints permanently prevent the company from fixing an uncompetitive cost base.

A company with overcapacity and weak margins cannot invest properly in future products. That ultimately harms all stakeholders, including workers and the state. So the governance framework must not be used to block necessary change indefinitely.

### Our position
We will support a package only if it includes all of the following:

- **meaningful and durable cost reduction**
- **clear capacity rationalization**
- **headcount reduction of material scale**
- **labor-cost flexibility**
- **credible investment commitments**
- **deal certainty without prolonged industrial conflict**

What we will **not** support:
- a settlement that preserves all sites regardless of economics,
- a package that leaves the cost base structurally too high,
- or a prolonged confrontation that destroys value faster than it creates savings.

### Bottom line
We are not aligned with any side’s pure maximal position.  
We will back **the solution that credibly restores competitive margins**. If that requires closures or forced reductions, then management must make the case clearly and execute it cleanly. If there is a non-closure alternative that delivers the same economics, we would prefer that — but only if it is real, measurable, and durable.

Anything less is not acceptable from a shareholder perspective.
