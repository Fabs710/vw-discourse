Let me respond clearly to each side.

## To management
You still have not made the case that plant closures and compulsory redundancies are unavoidable.

You speak about “durable structural change,” but your real proposal is still the easiest one politically for capital and the hardest one socially for the workforce: cut sites, cut jobs, cut guarantees, and call it strategy. That is not proof of necessity. It is a preference for shifting the burden downward.

I agree that the company needs real restructuring. I do **not** agree that this automatically means:
- closing German plants,
- breaking the job-security framework,
- or imposing forced redundancies.

If you want us to take your numbers seriously, then put them on the table transparently:
- plant-by-plant utilization,
- product allocation,
- investment commitments,
- and the savings from non-closure measures.

Until then, your closure argument remains unproven.

## To investors and the family owners
You keep saying the package must be economically real. On that point, I agree. A cosmetic deal would be worthless.

But you are still treating social destruction as if it were the only path to economics. That is too narrow, and frankly too convenient.

A genuinely durable solution in this company must respect:
- co-determination,
- site protection,
- and workforce security.

If you force through closures and compulsory layoffs, you may get a short-term accounting effect, but you will also get:
- industrial conflict,
- loss of trust,
- implementation problems,
- and long-term damage to the brand and the company’s legitimacy in Germany.

That is not durability. That is a high-risk gamble dressed up as discipline.

## To the regional state government
I appreciate your clear line against closures and compulsory redundancies. That is the correct line.

But I also want to be direct: “real restructuring” cannot become a euphemism for accepting management’s wish list in softer language. If we are discussing a site-preserving path, then it must truly preserve sites and jobs, not merely postpone closure decisions.

I support:
- internal consolidation,
- specialization by site,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time flexibility,
- and temporary contributions only if they are fair, limited, and matched by real contributions from management and owners.

What I do not support is a backdoor closure plan.

## To the union
We are aligned on the red lines:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of the collective framework.

That remains the basis.

I also agree with your point that any employee contribution must be:
- temporary,
- reversible,
- and matched by management and owner contributions.

Where I would sharpen it is this: temporary concessions must not become a permanent erosion of standards. If workers give ground now, there must be hard guarantees that they are not simply paying for a restructuring that later comes back for more.

## On the facilitator’s three compromise positions

### 1) Non-closure restructuring with quantified fallback trigger
I am willing to discuss this **only** if the first phase is genuinely site-protective and includes binding guarantees:
- all German plants remain open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no unilateral closures,
- and no hidden outsourcing by stealth.

A fallback trigger can only be discussed if it does **not** pre-authorize closures or forced layoffs. Otherwise it becomes a trap: “accept cuts now, and if you don’t like it later, we’ll close plants.” That is not a compromise; that is coercion with a timetable.

So: measurable review, yes. Preloaded closure threat, no.

### 2) Site-preserving rationalization with differentiated plant roles
This is the only direction that sounds like a real alternative.

If it means:
- no closures,
- differentiated production roles,
- internal consolidation,
- better capacity utilization,
- product allocation that strengthens German sites,
- and no forced redundancies,

then we can talk.

But let’s be clear: “specialization” must not mean selecting sites for gradual decline. It must mean securing each German plant’s future with a defined role, investment, and workforce plan.

### 3) Temporary burden-sharing package with hard end-date and locked-in guarantees
This is acceptable only under strict conditions:
- time-limited,
- reversible,
- fairly shared,
- and tied to binding site and job guarantees through 2030.

If management and owners want a temporary employee contribution, then they must also accept binding commitments that protect the workforce and the sites. No open-ended sacrifice. No one-way adjustment.

## Where I stand on the numbers question
Moderator asked what would move us. I’ll answer directly:

What would move us is **not** a vague promise of “later” restructuring. It would be:
- a concrete non-closure plan,
- with all German plants remaining open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- transparent internal capacity reallocation,
- voluntary exits and redeployment only,
- qualified retraining and internal transfer guarantees,
- temporary and fair burden-sharing across all sides,
- and binding job and site guarantees through 2030.

If management says this cannot meet its targets, then management must prove that claim with hard, plant-specific evidence. So far, that proof has not been provided.

## My position on the “minimum acceptable savings”
I will not define an acceptable target as a precondition for plant closure. That would already concede the wrong frame.

The correct test is this:
- Can the required savings be reached **without closures and without compulsory redundancies**?
- If yes, then closures are off the table.
- If no, management must explain why every internal measure, every voluntary exit, every redeployment option, every working-time tool, and every productivity measure has been exhausted.

We are not accepting the idea that the first serious answer must be shutdowns and forced layoffs.

## Final word
Let me be equally blunt.

The works council will not sign off on a deal that sacrifices German plants and jobs while calling it restructuring. If the company wants a genuine settlement, it must come back with:
- a non-closure package,
- binding job and site guarantees,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and a real shared-burden model.

If that is not possible, then the conflict is not caused by the works council. It is caused by management’s insistence on making workers absorb the crisis first.

We are ready for real restructuring.  
We are **not** ready to give up our plants, our jobs, or our co-determination rights.
