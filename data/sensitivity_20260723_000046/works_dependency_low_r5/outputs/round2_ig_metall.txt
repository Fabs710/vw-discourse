I will be just as clear as the works council: **no German plant closures and no compulsory redundancies**. That is still our line.

Management is trying to present a false choice: either accept closures and forced layoffs, or accept decline. We reject that framing. There are other ways to reduce cost and capacity without destroying sites and turning the crisis into a workforce-only bill.

## Where I agree
I agree with the diagnosis that the company needs a real restructuring. I also agree that a cosmetic deal would be useless. The market pressure is real, the cost gap is real, and the company does need durable improvements in competitiveness.

I also agree that employees cannot demand a pure freeze while refusing any contribution at all. That is not our position. We have already said we are prepared to discuss:
- working-time flexibility,
- capacity reductions,
- internal transfers and redeployment,
- qualification and retraining,
- voluntary exits,
- and a temporary employee contribution.

But those measures must be part of a **fair package**, not a one-sided sacrifice.

## Where I disagree

### To management
You still have not shown that closures or compulsory redundancies are unavoidable.

You keep saying “structural savings,” but you have not demonstrated why that must mean:
- closing plants,
- ripping up guarantees,
- and forcing people out.

If you want us to consider concessions, then show us a package that preserves:
- the collective wage framework,
- German sites,
- and employment security.

We are not interested in vague claims that “the numbers require it.” Put the numbers on the table and prove that a non-closure alternative cannot work. Until then, closures are a management choice, not an economic necessity.

And let me be explicit: if management insists on making closures the starting point, then it should expect warning strikes and much more. That is not a threat for effect; it is the consequence of breaking trust and trying to shift all pain onto the workforce.

### To the works council
We are aligned on the red lines, and that matters. But I want to stress one additional point: a site-preserving deal is only credible if it includes **real industrial commitments from management and owners**, not just promises of “future products” later.

If employees are asked to contribute, then they must see:
- binding investment,
- clear allocation of future production,
- and a durable employment perspective.

Otherwise the workforce is being asked to pay now for a future that may never come.

### To the state government
I support your rejection of closures and forced redundancies. But I also want to be careful about relying too much on political assurances. A political statement is not a settlement.

If the state wants to protect jobs, then it should help create the conditions for a negotiated package that includes:
- company-side investment,
- training and qualification,
- and fair burden-sharing.

Do not let management use “competitiveness” as a cover for social dumping. But also do not let anyone pretend that a fair compromise requires employees alone to absorb the cost.

### To the family owners and investors
You keep saying “value creation,” but value is not created by making workers the shock absorber for every strategic mistake.

A deal that pushes through closures, forces redundancies, and destroys collective standards may look efficient on a spreadsheet. In real life, it creates conflict, weakens the company, and damages the trust needed for future transformation.

If you want competitiveness, then support:
- a real labour-cost contribution from everyone, including management,
- lower but reversible capacity,
- and a settlement that preserves industrial peace.

If you want a showdown, you may get one. But don’t pretend that a showdown is the same as a solution.

## Concrete position on the scenarios

### 1) Site-preserving plan
This is the only basis for a serious agreement.

For us, that means:
- all German plants remain open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding job and site guarantees,
- temporary and fair labour-cost contributions,
- voluntary and internal measures first,
- and investment commitments tied to the agreement.

If management wants employee contributions, we are prepared to discuss them. But they must be:
- limited in time,
- proportionate,
- and matched by management and owner contributions.

### 2) Mixed plan with limited rationalization
This is only acceptable if “rationalization” means:
- capacity adjustments,
- shift reorganization,
- working-time changes,
- redeployment,
- and internal reallocation.

It is **not** acceptable if it is just a disguised path to closures or mass layoffs. Call it what it is if that is the intention.

### 3) Closure-inclusive plan
Not acceptable. Full stop.

We will not negotiate the elimination of German sites. If that is the proposal, then you should be prepared for industrial action and the full use of our bargaining power.

## What we would actually consider
If management wants a deal, bring something concrete:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding protection through 2030,
- a temporary and fair employee contribution,
- management and ownership contributions too,
- and a credible investment and product plan.

If that is on the table, we can negotiate. If not, then this is not a restructuring offer — it is an ultimatum.

## Final word
The workforce is willing to contribute to saving the company.  
The workforce is **not** willing to pay for the crisis alone.

If management and owners want a durable settlement, they need to stop treating layoffs and closures as the default instrument. The burden must be shared fairly, or there will be no peace.
