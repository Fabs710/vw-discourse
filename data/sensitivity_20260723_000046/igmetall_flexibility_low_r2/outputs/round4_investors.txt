We agree with the central diagnosis, but not with the way some parties are using it.

## Where we agree
- The company needs a **real structural restructuring**, not a symbolic gesture.
- A **cosmetic compromise** that preserves the current cost base would destroy value.
- A prolonged strike or open-ended confrontation would also destroy value.
- The expert assessments are directionally useful: **non-closure measures can contribute materially**, but only if they are **deep, quantified, and durable**.

## Where we disagree

### To the works council and union
We understand the red lines, but we do **not** accept “no closures” as a principle in itself.

That position only works for capital markets if it is paired with evidence that the company can still reach competitive margins. So far, we have heard:
- voluntary exits,
- working-time tools,
- productivity gains,
- limited labor-cost contributions,
- and internal capacity concentration.

That is a plausible start. But it is not yet a proven answer.

If the no-closure route leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive, then it is not acceptable to us, even if it protects sites in the short term. We will not back a solution that simply delays the same problem to 2027 or 2028.

### To management
We agree that you should not be forced into a weak compromise. But you still have not fully earned the right to claim that closures are unavoidable.

What is missing is the exact bridge:
- which plants are underutilized,
- what volume can be transferred,
- what fixed cost is truly removed under each scenario,
- and what the 2030 margin outcome is.

Without that, closures remain a strong option, but not yet a fully demonstrated necessity.

### To the state and family
We agree that a breakdown in social legitimacy would damage value. We also agree that co-determination and regional stability matter.

But they do not override the economic requirement. If the no-closure route is real, we would support it. If it is not real enough, then protecting every site becomes value-destructive.

## Our position now
Our standard is unchanged:

1. **Durable recurring savings**
2. **A credible path back to competitive margins**
3. **Deal certainty**
4. **No cosmetic settlement**
5. **No structurally uncompetitive cost base**

So we are open to a no-closure or hybrid package **only if** it produces savings and capacity reduction that are economically equivalent in substance, not just politically more comfortable.

## What would move us
We would be prepared to support a no-closure framework if it includes:
- verified recurring EBIT savings,
- meaningful capacity concentration,
- material workforce contribution,
- measurable headcount reduction through voluntary means,
- and a clear 2030 margin bridge.

What would **not** move us:
- promises without numbers,
- vague review clauses,
- deferred closure threats,
- or a deal that preserves too much cost and overcapacity.

## [SUMMON: independent plant-by-plant financial bridge and utilization model]
We need an independent, plant-by-plant bridge showing:
- current utilization,
- transferable volume,
- recurring savings from no-closure measures,
- one-time costs,
- and the 2030 margin impact under:
  1. no-closure consolidation,
  2. hybrid restructuring,
  3. closure-led restructuring.

That is the only basis on which we can judge whether avoiding closures protects or destroys shareholder value.

## Bottom line
We are not ideologically pro-closure.  
We are pro-competitiveness, pro-discipline, and pro-certainty.

If the no-closure path truly restores margins and removes enough structural cost, we can support it.  
If it does not, then closures remain on the table because preserving an uncompetitive footprint is not value protection — it is value erosion.
