Let me respond directly and without ambiguity.

### To the Works Council
I hear your refusal of closures and compulsory redundancies. I do **not** accept that as a starting condition for a serious restructuring.

The expert just confirmed what management has been saying all along: a no-closure path can only work if it delivers **large, recurring, quantified savings** through real headcount reduction, labor-cost relief, and hard capacity concentration. That is exactly the point. A vague promise to “keep every plant open” is not a restructuring plan. It is a constraint that still has to be made economically credible.

You keep asking us to prove why closures are necessary. I’ll turn that around: **show me a package that closes the competitiveness gap without closures, without compulsory redundancies, and without hollowing out the cost base.** Not slogans. Numbers. Recurring savings. Utilization gains. Headcount reductions. Margin impact by 2030.

If you can deliver that, we can talk. But if your answer is simply:
- every plant stays open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no material wage contribution,
- no binding structural relief,

then there is no basis for a solution. That would leave us with a company that is still too expensive, still overcapacity-laden, and still missing its return target.

### To the Industrial Union
You are right about one thing: a restructuring is necessary. But you are wrong to imply that the expert report weakens the case for closures. It does the opposite.

The report says clearly:
- non-closure measures can be material,
- but they must be **large and disciplined**,
- and a no-closure path usually needs a **much larger employee contribution** and/or volume concentration than people like to admit.

That is not a rejection of closures. It is a warning that if we avoid closures, then the alternative must be economically equivalent. So I am not moved by the argument that “closures are only an option.” Yes — and if the alternative cannot match the economics, then closures remain necessary.

I am prepared to discuss:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity measures,
- and a limited workforce contribution.

But I am **not** prepared to accept a package that protects all sites, avoids hard decisions, and leaves the company structurally weak. That would simply postpone the crisis and make the eventual correction more painful.

### To the Regional State Government
You continue to emphasize regional stability and co-determination. I respect that. But regional stability without competitiveness is not stability; it is managed decline.

I also want to be very clear: no one is trying to “sideline” co-determination. But co-determination does not mean management is obliged to preserve every legacy structure regardless of cost. The company’s duty is to the business as a going concern.

If you want to support a no-closure path, then you need to support the economics of it:
- lower labor cost,
- lower capacity,
- stronger flexibility,
- and a credible contribution that actually restores margins.

If you are only prepared to support a moratorium, then you are asking us to freeze the problem, not solve it.

### To the Controlling Family
You are right that a cosmetic settlement is unacceptable. But I think we should be explicit: a settlement without deep cost and capacity change is cosmetic whether or not it preserves headlines.

I appreciate that you are not endorsing closures as a reflex. Neither are we. But the current situation does not allow us to rule out closures if they are the only way to get the required structural savings. We need to be disciplined about what the business needs, not what is politically convenient.

If someone can present a quantified no-closure package that produces the same recurring improvement, I will consider it. But absent that, I will not let this company drift into a compromise that looks balanced but leaves us uncompetitive.

### To the Institutional Investors
You ask for quantified recurring savings, a clear path back to margins, and certainty for capital allocation. That is exactly what management is trying to secure.

But let’s be honest: capital markets should not demand a restructuring that avoids all pain if the cost base remains too high. Investors cannot have it both ways. Either they want a real competitiveness reset, or they want a slow erosion wrapped in industrial peace. We are not offering the second.

### What I need to see
Here is the minimum for a credible settlement from management’s side:

1. **Material recurring savings**
   - not one-off accounting effects,
   - not temporary deferrals,
   - but a durable reduction in fixed cost and labor cost.

2. **Real capacity adjustment**
   - consolidation of volumes,
   - elimination of redundant capacity,
   - higher utilization at the sites that remain central.

3. **A genuine workforce contribution**
   - wage restraint,
   - flexibility,
   - productivity gains,
   - and, where needed, voluntary exits.

4. **A binding timetable**
   - because we are not discussing abstract ambitions,
   - we are discussing competitiveness by 2030.

### What is not acceptable
Let me state the limit plainly:

- A no-closure arrangement with no hard savings is not acceptable.
- A deal that preserves every plant, every guarantee, and every cost structure is not acceptable.
- A settlement that leaves us unable to hit the brand’s return-on-sales target is not acceptable.

I am open on the **means**. I am not open on the **requirement**: the company must get materially cheaper and leaner.

### My position
So my position remains firm:
- we need durable structural change,
- we need a meaningful labor-cost contribution,
- we need capacity reduction,
- and we need a package that restores competitiveness to 2030.

If the other side wants to avoid closures, then bring a package that really does the job. If not, then we will have to keep all options on the table, including closures and deeper interventions, because I will not sign up to a compromise that fails the business.
