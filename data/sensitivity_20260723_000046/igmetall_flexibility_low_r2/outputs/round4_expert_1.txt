## Independent restructuring assessment

I can answer the plant-economics question that has now been surfaced by all sides:

> **Is a credible no-closure restructuring possible, or do the economics require at least some closures?**

### Short answer
**A no-closure path can be credible only if it removes enough recurring fixed cost and enough capacity to raise utilization materially.**  
If the German footprint is structurally overbuilt, then **keeping every plant open usually forces the savings burden onto labor, shifts, and overhead rather than footprint rationalization**. That can work in some cases, but it is often **harder to sustain through 2030** than a selective closure or strong consolidation path.

The key point is not “closure good” or “closure bad.” The key point is:

- **Closure is a tool for removing recurring fixed cost quickly and decisively.**
- **No-closure restructuring can work only if it achieves a comparable fixed-cost reduction through internal volume concentration, shift removal, product transfer, and labor-cost measures.**

---

# 1) What the economics usually look like

For a mature passenger-car manufacturing network, the savings stack typically comes from five buckets:

1. **Fixed manufacturing overhead**
   - plant leadership, maintenance, utilities base load, logistics, security, support functions
2. **Indirect labor and support labor**
   - line support, material handling, quality, planning
3. **Direct labor**
   - wages, premiums, bonuses, allowances, overtime
4. **Capacity utilization**
   - better utilization reduces cost per unit even without headcount cuts
5. **Capital and depreciation efficiency**
   - fewer underused lines and buildings means lower burden per car

### Closure-led scenario
A plant closure usually produces:
- **stronger recurring savings**
- **higher one-time costs**
- **greater labor and political conflict**
- **higher transition risk**

### No-closure consolidation scenario
This can produce:
- **lower one-time costs**
- **less conflict**
- **slower savings realization**
- **greater dependence on labor concessions and volume transfers**
- **risk of ending with still-excess capacity**

### Hybrid scenario
This is often the most realistic:
- close or downsize the weakest line/site,
- keep the legal footprint partly open if necessary,
- transfer volume into fewer sites,
- combine with wage/working-time/productivity concessions.

In practice, **hybrid solutions are frequently the best balance of feasibility and savings** if outright closure is politically or governance-wise blocked.

---

# 2) What has to be true for “no closure” to work

A no-closure path is credible only if it includes all of the following:

### A. Capacity concentration
You need to remove enough parallel capacity that utilization rises meaningfully.

As a rule of thumb:
- if a plant runs below roughly **60–65% utilization** on a sustained basis, it is usually a cost problem;
- below **50–55%**, it is often structurally uneconomic unless the site has special capabilities.

### B. Real volume transfers
If volumes stay fragmented across too many sites, no-closure savings will be shallow.

### C. Labor-cost relief
Without some combination of:
- wage restraint,
- lower bonuses,
- premium moderation,
- working-time flexibility,
- or contribution freezes,

the cost base often remains too high.

### D. Headcount reduction
A serious no-closure deal usually still requires:
- voluntary exits,
- natural attrition,
- redeployment,
- and possibly substantial reduction in indirect staff.

### E. Governance certainty
If management cannot be sure the savings will persist, it will discount them.  
If the deal is temporary or reversible, the market may not treat it as a durable restructuring.

---

# 3) What savings are typically available without closures

I cannot give site-specific numbers without the plant-by-plant data the parties keep asking for, but I can give realistic ranges for a German auto OEM restructuring.

## No-closure savings potential: indicative range
If the package is serious, recurring annual EBIT improvement might come from:

- **Workforce and working-time measures:** moderate
- **Productivity/indirect cost removal:** moderate
- **Capacity concentration:** moderate to significant
- **Labor-cost contribution:** moderate
- **Logistics and overhead simplification:** modest to moderate

### Typical magnitude
A deep no-closure program can sometimes generate roughly:
- **low hundreds of millions of euros per year** in recurring savings for a large premium OEM footprint,
- but only if the measures are broad, enforceable, and accompanied by real capacity reduction.

If the objective is a **large structural margin improvement by 2030**, a purely no-closure route often becomes difficult unless the company is willing to accept:
- major labor concessions,
- very large voluntary exits,
- and some de facto site downsizing even if formal closures are avoided.

---

# 4) What closures add economically

A closure can do three things the no-closure route struggles to do:

1. **Eliminate fixed cost at one stroke**
2. **Force volume concentration**
3. **Remove the risk of persistent underutilization**

### Typical financial effect
Compared with a no-closure solution, a closure-led plan usually delivers:
- **higher recurring annual savings**
- **higher one-time severance and transition costs**
- **higher risk of strike/reputational damage**
- **greater chance of immediate operational disruption**

### Important nuance
The real economic advantage of closure is not the headcount cut alone.  
It is the **removal of a fixed-cost structure that would otherwise continue absorbing overhead and underutilization losses**.

That is why management often prefers it when:
- utilization is weak,
- product allocation is insufficient,
- and labor-cost measures alone cannot close the gap.

---

# 5) Workforce and transition implications

## No-closure path
Usually involves:
- voluntary exits
- internal transfers
- retraining
- shift reductions
- working-time flexibility
- temporary wage concessions
- productivity commitments

### Benefits
- lower conflict
- lower political risk
- lower immediate legal/operational disruption
- easier to preserve supplier and regional stability

### Costs/risks
- may not go far enough
- may preserve excess capacity in disguise
- savings can erode after the agreement expires
- can defer rather than solve the problem

## Closure path
Usually involves:
- redundancy programs
- site shutdown logistics
- transfer of tooling and production
- asset write-downs
- supply-chain reconfiguration

### Benefits
- stronger recurring savings
- cleaner footprint rationalization
- better utilization at surviving plants

### Costs/risks
- high one-time cash cost
- strike and execution risk
- loss of know-how
- political and social backlash
- possible governance deadlock if veto rights exist

---

# 6) What I would look for in a plant-by-plant bridge

To determine whether the no-closure route is truly credible, you need a bridge showing for each German site:

- current and projected utilization through 2030
- direct labor cost per vehicle
- indirect labor and overhead per vehicle
- fixed cost base
- volume transfer capacity to other plants
- products/lines that can be removed
- one-time cost of consolidation
- recurring annual savings
- 2030 EBIT or margin impact

### Decision test
If a no-closure plan leaves:
- low utilization in one or more sites,
- limited cost removal,
- and a fragile margin bridge,

then it is likely **a delay strategy rather than a durable restructuring**.

---

# 7) Analytical judgment

Based on the facts given, my judgment is:

- **A no-closure deal is not impossible.**
- **But it is only credible if it includes significant capacity removal and recurring labor-cost relief.**
- If the company truly has structural overcapacity, then **keeping every German plant open makes the burden of adjustment much heavier on labor and productivity than on footprint rationalization**.
- That can be politically possible, but it is often **economically weaker** than a hybrid or selective closure path.

### Most likely outcome in comparable cases
Where governance gives employees and political stakeholders blocking rights, the most realistic settlement is often:
- **hybrid consolidation**
- with **no formal closure of the most politically sensitive site**
- but with **meaningful volume transfer, shift removal, and headcount reduction**

That usually lands between the two extremes.

If you want the key economic conclusion in one sentence:

> **A no-closure restructuring can work only if it still achieves deep, durable fixed-cost removal; otherwise, the company will likely need at least some form of closure, conversion, or decisive footprint reduction to restore competitiveness by 2030.**
