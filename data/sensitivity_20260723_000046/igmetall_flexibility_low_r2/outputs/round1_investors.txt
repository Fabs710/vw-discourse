From the perspective of dispersed capital-market shareholders, the discussion needs to be judged on one test only: **does it credibly restore the company to a competitive cost base and sustainable margins?** If yes, we can support it. If no, we will not.

### Where we agree
We agree with management and the controlling family on the core diagnosis:
- the German cost base is too high,
- the brand is under margin pressure,
- and incrementalism is not enough.

We also agree with the workforce representatives on one important point:
- a purely cosmetic settlement that avoids the real economics would just defer the problem and destroy more value later.

And we agree with the state government that a disorderly, value-destructive conflict is undesirable. Prolonged strikes, uncertainty, and operational disruption are all bad for shareholders.

### Where we disagree
We do **not** accept a position that says “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no guarantee changes” as an absolute starting point **if** that makes it impossible to get the required savings. That kind of rigidity may protect incumbents, but it does not protect shareholder value.

At the same time, we also do **not** accept management using closures as a reflex or as leverage without proving they are necessary. Plant closures are not a virtue in themselves; they are only acceptable if they are part of a credible, quantified plan that gets the company to competitive margins faster and more durably than the alternatives.

So our line is simple:
- **No support for a deal that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive.**
- **No support for a cosmetic compromise that preserves headline peace but not earnings power.**
- **Support for a hard, quantified restructuring if it genuinely restores competitiveness.**

### What we need to see
Before we would view any package as acceptable, it needs to show clearly:
1. **Where the savings come from**
   - labor cost,
   - capacity reduction,
   - overhead,
   - and operational simplification.

2. **Why the savings are durable**
   - not one-off accounting benefits,
   - not temporary deferrals,
   - but recurring margin improvement.

3. **What the trade-offs are**
   - if plant closures are avoided, then the alternative must deliver equivalent economics;
   - if job guarantees are extended, then there must be offsetting, credible cost action.

4. **How quickly the company gets back to target**
   - because near-term margin erosion matters to us,
   - and capital markets will not reward indefinite transition stories.

### On each party’s position

#### To management
You are right to insist that the status quo is not enough. But you need to be more precise. Saying “we need structural change” is not enough; show the numbers. If closures are truly required, demonstrate why. If they are not, then bring forward a package that matches their savings impact.

What we will not support is a plan that creates large disruption without delivering a measurable earnings uplift.

#### To the works council and union
Your red lines are understandable politically, but from an investor standpoint they are only acceptable if you can show a credible substitute that closes the competitiveness gap. A promise to discuss “fair contributions” is not enough on its own. We need to see real, durable economic effect.

If the alternative to closures is:
- lower labor cost,
- lower capacity,
- more flexibility,
- and no operational paralysis,

then we are open to that. But if the outcome is simply a protected status quo with minor concessions, we would oppose it.

#### To the state government
Your insistence on regional stability is relevant, but it cannot override the economics. A preserved footprint with an uncompetitive margin structure is not a solution; it is a delay. If you want to block closures, then you should also support a package that achieves the same financial result by other means.

#### To the controlling family
You are right that long-term value matters more than short-term optics. But long-term value also means being willing to take hard decisions if they are needed. We will support tough restructuring, but not ambiguity. If the plan is only “discipline” without quantified delivery, that is not enough.

### Our bottom line
We are prepared to back **a severe but credible restructuring**. We are **not** prepared to back:
- a settlement that leaves German operations structurally expensive,
- a strike-prone stalemate,
- or a compromise designed mainly to avoid difficult decisions.

If the parties want shareholder support, bring us a package with:
- quantified recurring savings,
- a clear path back to competitive margins,
- and enough certainty that capital can be allocated with confidence.

Absent that, we would prefer the company to take harder action now rather than preserve an uncompetitive structure and destroy more value later.
