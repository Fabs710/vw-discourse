Here is the industrial-restructuring assessment, focusing only on plant economics, capacity, savings, and competitiveness through 2030.

## 1) Bottom line

A **no-closure / no-compulsory-redundancy** path can sometimes deliver meaningful savings, but it usually **cannot fully replicate** the cost relief from closure of one major German plant **unless** the company also achieves:

- very large volume shifts to other plants,
- significant fixed-cost absorption improvement,
- sustained labor-cost concessions,
- and strong productivity gains.

In most European auto cases, the difference between:

- **“deep restructuring with closure”** and
- **“retention with non-closure measures”**

is often on the order of **low hundreds of millions to more than €1bn per year** in recurring EBIT impact, depending on the plant size, utilization, and whether the plant is producing ICE, EV, or mixed output.

So analytically:

- **Non-closure measures can be material and credible.**
- **But they must be large, quantified, and enforced.**
- If the company’s problem is truly structural overcapacity and high fixed labor/overhead cost, then **a no-closure path typically needs a much larger employee contribution and/or product allocation commitment** than parties often assume.
- A “keep every plant open” plan is only viable if it is paired with **hard capacity rationalization inside the footprint** and not merely cost deferral.

## 2) What usually drives savings in German auto restructuring

The main recurring levers are:

1. **Capacity reduction**
   - less fixed cost spread over fewer units;
   - higher plant utilization at remaining sites;
   - lower logistics and indirect labor.

2. **Workforce reduction**
   - voluntary exits, retirement windows, transfer packages, attrition;
   - direct payroll savings.

3. **Labor-cost adjustments**
   - lower bonus/outperformance payments;
   - temporary wage restraint;
   - reduction or suspension of special allowances;
   - changes to guaranteed hours or add-ons.

4. **Working-time and flexibility changes**
   - shorter weekly hours, shift redesign, variable hours;
   - but these often save less cash than they appear to on paper unless they also cut headcount or overtime.

5. **Product allocation / model transfer**
   - moving volume from one German site to another;
   - can preserve jobs but only if the receiving plant has spare capacity and the product mix fits.

6. **Procurement and overhead simplification**
   - not a substitute for structural plant action, but can contribute.

## 3) Non-closure savings: what is realistic?

Because you have not provided plant-level numbers, I have to use **industry-range estimates**. Those are not facts about this specific company; they are analytical benchmarks.

### A. Workforce-related savings
If the company can reduce domestic headcount through voluntary measures rather than closure, recurring savings can be substantial.

Typical all-in annual cost per industrial worker in Germany, depending on skill mix and burden, can be roughly:
- **€70k–€110k** loaded cost per employee per year in a high-cost auto setting.

So:

- **1,000 jobs removed** → roughly **€70m–€110m annual savings**
- **5,000 jobs removed** → roughly **€350m–€550m annual savings**
- **10,000 jobs removed** → roughly **€700m–€1.1bn annual savings**

But note:
- voluntary exit packages create **one-time restructuring charges**;
- savings ramp gradually;
- if replacement hiring continues, savings are smaller.

### B. Labor-cost contribution without headcount reduction
If employees accept wage restraint, shorter hours, or loss of allowances:

- A **1%–3% wage-bill reduction** in a large German auto workforce can produce meaningful savings, but usually not enough alone.
- On a very large domestic payroll, that may amount to **tens of millions to low hundreds of millions annually**, depending on baseline payroll.

This is helpful, but it rarely closes a major structural gap by itself.

### C. Working-time reduction
Working-time tools can reduce cash outflow, but if they are not accompanied by true headcount reduction or lower production volume, they can simply:
- shift fixed-cost absorption,
- create latent underutilization,
- or require more overtime later.

So working-time changes are most valuable when they **enable capacity alignment**.

### D. Indirect cost and complexity reduction
A complex German multi-site footprint carries indirect costs:
- planning,
- logistics,
- maintenance,
- management layers,
- changeover inefficiencies,
- duplicated support functions.

A non-closure program can still strip out a portion of this, often **€50m–€200m+ annually** in a large group context, but this is usually not enough to offset a major plant closure case by itself.

## 4) What a plant closure typically adds financially

A closure often delivers more than just payroll savings. It can remove:

- a full fixed-cost base,
- underutilized equipment,
- site overhead,
- local maintenance and logistics costs,
- and chronic inefficiency from low or declining utilization.

For a large plant, recurring annual savings from closure can plausibly be in the range of:

- **€150m–€400m per year** for a medium-sized site,
- and potentially more if the site is large, low-utilization, and highly fixed-cost-intensive.

But closure also brings:
- **one-time restructuring charges** that can be very large,
- severance, transfer, dismantling, remediation,
- supply-chain disruption,
- and possible loss of production flexibility.

So closures usually improve recurring economics **more decisively**, but at higher transition cost and operational risk.

## 5) Plant-by-plant capacity options: how to think about them

Without the company’s actual plant data, the correct way to assess each site is by four questions:

### 1. Current utilization
- Is the plant running at **<60%**, **60–80%**, or **>80%** of economic capacity?
- Below about **70%** in a fixed-cost-heavy auto plant, margin pressure tends to be severe.

### 2. Product complexity and flexibility
- Can it switch between ICE/EV, platforms, or variants?
- A flexible plant is easier to retain with volume reshuffling.

### 3. Investment requirement to stay competitive
- Does it need a large capex refresh for body shop, battery integration, paint, software, or logistics?
- If so, retention is only rational if future volume is durable.

### 4. Logistics and labor-cost advantage relative to alternatives
- Is it a low-cost high-productivity site or a strategically convenient one?
- If it is high-cost and low-utilization, the retention case is weak unless politically protected.

## 6) Viable non-closure path: what it would need to include

A genuine no-closure restructuring would need to be more than rhetoric. It would likely require:

### A. Hard volume concentration
- Move one or more models or major subassemblies to the best-performing German plants.
- Reduce overlap across plants.
- Eliminate “parallel capacity” rather than keeping every line alive.

### B. Headcount reduction without forced layoffs
- Voluntary exit windows.
- Early retirement.
- Internal transfers with limited backfill.
- Natural attrition.
- Hiring freeze in non-critical functions.

### C. Labor-cost contribution
- Temporary restraint on pay growth.
- Reduction in special payments.
- Flexibility changes that reduce overtime and premiums.
- Possibly a multi-year productivity bargain.

### D. Product and investment commitments
- In exchange, management must commit production, capex, and model allocation.
- Otherwise the workforce would be conceding permanently for only temporary relief.

### E. Governance and monitoring
- Trigger-based review on KPIs.
- Independent tracking of utilization, EBIT, cash, and market share.
- Defined fallback if the plan misses targets.

## 7) 2030 competitiveness scenarios

### Scenario 1: Deep restructuring with one or more closures
Likely effects:
- highest recurring cost improvement;
- better plant utilization at remaining sites;
- stronger odds of reaching target margins by 2030;
- but high one-time charges and industrial conflict risk.

This is the most direct route if the company’s current German fixed-cost base is materially above peers.

### Scenario 2: No closures, but aggressive internal consolidation
Likely effects:
- moderate to strong improvement if volume is reallocated and headcount falls materially;
- lower transition shock;
- still possibly insufficient if utilization remains weak in too many sites;
- depends heavily on employee contribution and management discipline.

This can work if the company is willing to do real consolidation inside the footprint, not just spread pain evenly.

### Scenario 3: No closures, limited concessions
Likely effects:
- temporary peace;
- weak structural savings;
- continued margin pressure;
- deferred conflict by 1–3 years;
- higher probability of a more severe restructuring later.

This is the least competitive path by 2030.

## 8) My judgment on the works council position

The works council’s statement is analytically understandable as a bargaining stance, but from an industrial-economics standpoint:

- **“Keep every German plant open” is not, by itself, a restructuring plan.**
- It is a **constraint**.
- A viable alternative must specify:
  - how much capacity is removed,
  - where volumes go,
  - how many jobs are removed voluntarily,
  - how much labor cost falls,
  - and how utilization improves.

If those numbers are not specified, then the no-closure position is **not yet financially testable**.

## 9) Practical threshold for credibility

A no-closure package becomes credible only if it can show, on a recurring basis:

- **material headcount reduction**,
- **multi-year labor-cost relief**,
- **clear utilization improvement**,
- **capex disciplined around surviving plants**,
- and **an EBIT/cash improvement large enough to close the gap to peers**.

As a rule of thumb, for a large OEM in this situation, I would expect a credible non-closure plan to demonstrate something like:

- **several hundred million euros annually at minimum**, and often closer to **€1bn/year** if the structural issue is severe,
- plus a clear path to higher utilization and better margin by 2030.

If it cannot, then management’s closure argument remains economically stronger.
