I’ll respond plainly.

### My position remains firm
- **No plant closures in Germany**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No dismantling of the collective wage framework**
- **No settlement where employees carry the full burden**

If those are the terms, then there is nothing to negotiate. If management wants a deal, it must be a **fair burden-sharing package** that protects jobs and sites.

---

## To management
You keep repeating “durable structural reset” as if that settles the matter. It does not.

What the expert reports actually showed is:
- a no-closure path is **possible**,
- but it must be **serious, quantified, and disciplined**,
- and it requires **real volume concentration, voluntary exits, working-time tools, and labor-cost contribution**.

That is precisely why the union has already signaled openness to:
- limited wage restraint,
- reduced or suspended bonuses/special payments,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exit programs,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- and productivity measures.

So let’s be clear: **we are not refusing restructuring. We are refusing a restructuring that destroys jobs and sites as the first resort.**

What you still have not proven is:
- why every non-closure option is insufficient,
- why all German plants must be put at risk,
- and why the workforce should accept compulsory redundancies when voluntary and internal measures have not even been exhausted.

If you want our cooperation, bring a **quantified no-closure package** with:
1. recurring savings by measure,
2. plant-by-plant capacity adjustments,
3. a binding employment framework,
4. and guaranteed investment/product commitments.

Without that, your “necessity” argument is just a preference for easier management action.

---

## To the works council
I agree with your core line: **no closures, no forced layoffs, no hollowing out of co-determination**.

Where I would sharpen the bargaining position is this: the workforce contribution must be **limited, temporary, and reciprocal**. If employees are asked to give, then management must give too:
- site security,
- job security,
- investment,
- and real production allocation.

A “contribution” without guarantees is not a contribution. It is a transfer of risk onto workers.

So yes, we can discuss flexibility and labor-cost relief. But only if it is part of a real settlement, not a disguised countdown to shutdowns later.

---

## To the state government
You are right that regional stability matters. You are also right that a strike or a destructive conflict would damage the company.

But I do not accept any framing that turns a moratorium into a trapdoor for later closures. If the state wants to support a durable solution, then it should support:
- **binding no-closure protection for a defined horizon**,
- **voluntary and internal adjustment tools**,
- **investment commitments**,
- and **joint monitoring of savings and utilization**.

What I cannot accept is a “review” that simply preserves the option to close plants once the workforce has conceded.

---

## To the controlling family
You say closures are a last resort. Fine. Then use them like one.

Right now, the burden is still too heavily placed on employees. The workforce is being asked to accept lower labor costs and greater flexibility, while management preserves the right to choose closures if the numbers do not quickly improve.

That is not fair burden-sharing.

If you want long-term value, then the company must prove it can restore competitiveness without sacrificing the social contract. In a co-determined company, trust is not a soft issue; it is an asset. Destroying it will cost money and capacity too.

---

## To institutional investors
You are right on one point: this cannot be a cosmetic compromise.

But you are wrong if you treat closures as the only credible way to discipline the business. The experts made clear that a no-closure plan can be economically meaningful if it includes:
- hard capacity concentration,
- meaningful voluntary exits,
- labor-cost contributions,
- and stronger utilization.

That is not a surrender to the status quo. It is a real restructuring with a different instrument mix.

What would be unacceptable to us is a deal where:
- plants remain technically open,
- but insecurity is permanent,
- workforce protections are weakened,
- and the burden still lands overwhelmingly on employees.

That is not a compromise; it is a slow erosion.

---

## On the expert reports
I want to be direct about what they mean for us.

### What they support
They support the view that:
- savings can come from non-closure instruments,
- but they must be large enough to matter,
- and they must be paired with a credible plan.

That is exactly the basis for our counter-position.

### What they do not support
They do **not** prove that:
- closures are unavoidable,
- or that compulsory redundancies are legitimate,
- or that the workforce should accept plant shutdowns simply because they may be financially efficient.

A financially efficient measure is not automatically a socially acceptable one, especially when there are alternatives that still deliver substantial savings.

---

## Our minimum acceptable framework
If management wants to move forward, then the package must include all of the following:

1. **No plant closures in Germany**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Preservation of the collective bargaining framework**
4. **Binding site and investment commitments**
5. **Capacity reduction through internal measures**
6. **A limited, time-bound employee contribution**
7. **Joint monitoring of utilization, savings, and delivery**

That is the minimum basis for a settlement.

---

## What we are prepared to discuss
We are prepared to discuss:
- temporary wage restraint,
- reduced bonus and special payment elements,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- internal redeployment,
- retraining,
- productivity measures,
- and capacity consolidation within the existing German footprint.

What we are **not** prepared to discuss:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- tearing up wage standards,
- or a structure where workers pay and owners/management do not.

We are willing to contribute.
