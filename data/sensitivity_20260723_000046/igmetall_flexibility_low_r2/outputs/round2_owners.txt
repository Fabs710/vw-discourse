Thank you. I’ll respond directly.

### To management
I agree with you on the diagnosis: the company needs a durable cost reset and a credible path back to competitive margins. I also agree that we should not mistake short-term peace for a solution.

Where I disagree is on the implied assumption that closures are the default answer. They are not the default answer from my perspective. They are a tool of last resort if, and only if, the alternatives cannot deliver equivalent recurring savings.

The expert’s assessment matters here: a no-closure path can be credible if it is substantial and disciplined. That means management must stop speaking in abstractions and quantify the gap.

So I expect from you:
- a plant-by-plant economic case,
- quantified recurring savings by measure,
- the utilization impact of volume transfers,
- the full cost of any closure versus retention scenario,
- and a clear explanation of why non-closure measures are insufficient if you claim they are.

Without that, I will not treat closures as justified. I will not support a plan that creates permanent value destruction simply because it is administratively easier.

### To the works council and union
I understand your red lines and I respect the role of co-determination. But I do not accept the position that “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no guarantee changes” can simply stand as a complete answer.

The expert report did not validate a status quo solution. It said a no-closure path only works if it is large enough to materially reduce costs and complexity. That means the workforce side must be prepared for real concessions.

What I see as potentially workable is:
- voluntary exits,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- a time-limited and limited labour-cost contribution,
- and capacity concentration inside the German footprint.

What I do **not** accept is a package that preserves every legacy protection while leaving the business structurally uncompetitive. That would merely shift the burden onto the owners and, ultimately, destroy more jobs later.

### To the regional state government
You are right to warn against a value-destructive conflict, and I share that concern. But I cannot support a position that turns regional protection into a veto over necessary industrial adjustment.

A no-closure solution is acceptable only if it is economically real, not politically comforting. If you want my support for preserving the footprint, then you must also accept that the footprint has to become leaner and more productive.

I am open to a structure that keeps sites open **if**:
- the company gets materially cheaper,
- volume is concentrated,
- labor costs come down,
- and investments are tied to measurable performance improvements.

What I will not support is a frozen structure dressed up as a compromise.

### To institutional investors
I agree with your core test: this has to restore the company to a sustainable cost base and improve margins durably. That is the right standard.

Where I differ from a maximalist closure-first approach is that closures are not valuable in themselves. They are only justified if they are the best way to deliver the required economics. If a serious no-closure package can close the gap, then value is better protected without the disruption and political fallout of plant shutdowns.

But let me be equally direct: if the non-closure route cannot get us to the required savings, then sentiment cannot stand in the way of hard decisions. I will not protect an uneconomic structure because it is less controversial.

### My position
The family holding will support only a package that satisfies all of the following:
- durable recurring savings,
- materially improved competitiveness,
- a credible margin path by 2030,
- industrial peace,
- and no value-destroying escalation.

That does **not** mean “no closures at any cost.”  
It does **not** mean “closures first.”  
It means the solution has to be economically justified and governance-safe.
