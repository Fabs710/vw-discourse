## Our view as capital-market shareholders

The two expert summaries confirm the central point: **this is not a cosmetic dispute**. The company needs a **durable structural reset**. That is the only basis on which we would support any settlement.

### Where we agree
We agree with management, the family holding, and the expert assessments that:
- the German cost base is too high,
- utilization and capacity are part of the problem,
- and incrementalism will not restore margins.

We also agree with the workforce and the state government on one important issue:
- a prolonged strike, disorderly conflict, or purely symbolic compromise would destroy value.

So yes, we are aligned on the diagnosis that **something material has to change**.

## Where we disagree

### 1) “No closures” is not acceptable as a principle
The works council and union are trying to turn plant closure into a moral taboo. We do **not** accept that framing.

If the company can prove that a no-closure path delivers equivalent recurring savings and margin recovery, fine. But if it cannot, then insisting on no closures becomes a demand to preserve an uncompetitive structure.

That is not acceptable to us.

### 2) But closures are not acceptable as a reflex either
We also do **not** accept management using closures as the default answer without proving necessity.

Plant closures are only justified if they are part of the **lowest-risk path to durable savings**. If non-closure measures can genuinely close most of the gap, then destroying capacity unnecessarily would be value-destructive.

So our position is not “close plants at any cost.”  
Our position is: **choose the option that restores recurring earnings most credibly and durably.**

### 3) A weak compromise is the worst outcome
The biggest risk here is a settlement that:
- avoids closures,
- avoids meaningful job reductions,
- avoids serious labor-cost change,
- and still claims to be a restructuring.

That would be a cosmetic deal. We would not support it.

Likewise, a closure package that creates high one-time costs and industrial disruption **without** clear recurring gains would also be unacceptable.

## What the experts actually imply
The external assessments are useful because they put numbers behind the politics.

### Our takeaway from the industrial expert
A no-closure path can work **only if** it includes:
- hard volume concentration,
- significant voluntary exits,
- real labor-cost concessions,
- better utilization,
- and disciplined product allocation.

That means the no-closure option is not “keep everything as is.”  
It is a serious restructuring with a different instrument mix.

### Our takeaway from the financial expert
The economics are clear:
- closures usually deliver stronger recurring EBIT improvement,
- but with higher one-time cash and execution costs;
- no-closure packages can be viable,
- but only if they still deliver **several hundred million euros per year** in recurring benefit, and more if the structural gap is large.

That is exactly the standard we apply.

## Our position on the current proposals

### To management
You have not yet made the economic case that closures are unavoidable. You have said they may be necessary, but that is not enough.

If you want shareholder support, provide:
- a plant-by-plant economic comparison,
- closure versus retention cost,
- recurring savings by measure,
- and a bridge to 2030 margins.

Until then, we are not endorsing closures as the default solution.

### To the works council and union
Your red lines are understandable politically, but they are not sufficient economically.

A deal that guarantees every German plant and preserves most existing protections is only acceptable if you can show:
- material headcount reduction,
- material capacity consolidation,
- real labor-cost contribution,
- and a credible margin path.

If you cannot produce those numbers, then your position is simply a blockade.

### To the state government
Your concern for jobs and regional stability is legitimate, but it cannot override economics. A protected footprint with structurally weak margins is not a sustainable outcome. It merely delays the correction.

### To the controlling family
You are right that value destruction from endless conflict is unacceptable. But the family should also resist the temptation to treat closures as a convenient lever. The question is not ideology; it is whether the chosen restructuring actually fixes the business.

## What we would support
We would support a package that meets all of these conditions:
1. **Durable recurring savings**
2. **A credible path back to competitive margins**
3. **Quantified capacity reduction**
4. **Material workforce contribution**
5. **Deal certainty and low execution risk**
6. **No cosmetic compromise**

## What we would not support
We would not support:
- a deal that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive,
- a settlement that merely postpones the crisis,
- or a strike-prone stalemate dressed up as compromise.

## Bottom line
We are **open to a no-closure solution**, but only if it is financially equivalent in substance, not just politically preferable.

If management can demonstrate that non-closure measures deliver enough recurring savings and margin improvement, then we can back that.

If not, then we will support harder restructuring, including closures, because preserving every site at the expense of competitiveness is not protecting shareholder value — it is eroding it.
