I’ll respond plainly and directly.

## My position has not changed
- **No plant closures in Germany**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No dismantling of the collective wage framework**
- **No settlement in which employees bear the main burden while management and owners keep their options open**

If that sounds rigid, it is because the alternatives are unacceptable.

---

## To management
You keep saying “durable structural reset” as if repeating it makes it true. It doesn’t.

What you have still not proven is **necessity**. You have argued that closures are the stronger instrument. I do not dispute that closures can produce more savings on paper. But stronger is not the same as unavoidable.

The expert bridge made one thing clear:
- a **serious no-closure restructuring can be real**,
- but only if it contains **actual capacity reduction, voluntary exits, working-time tools, labor-cost contribution, and productivity gains**.

That is exactly the kind of package we have already signaled willingness to discuss. So stop pretending the only choices are either:
1. closures and layoffs, or
2. no restructuring at all.

That is false.

### What I agree with
I agree that:
- the company needs real savings,
- a cosmetic deal would be worthless,
- and industrial peace cannot be bought with empty promises.

### What I reject
I reject the claim that the company can only restore competitiveness by threatening plants first and negotiating later. If you want closures, you must prove why the no-closure route fails. So far, you have not done that.

---

## To the works council
I agree with your core red lines:
- no closures,
- no forced redundancies,
- no weakening of co-determination.

And I agree that “review” and “trigger” mechanisms can easily become back doors to shutdowns. That concern is valid.

Where I want to sharpen the common position is this:
- If we are asked to contribute, then the company must commit to **real site and job security**, not verbal assurances.
- A workforce contribution is only legitimate if it is **temporary, limited, and reciprocal**.

So yes, I support a hard line against closures. But I also support a serious internal restructuring package if — and only if — it is tied to binding guarantees:
1. every German plant remains open,
2. no compulsory redundancies,
3. protected employment framework,
4. preserved co-determination,
5. binding investment and product commitments.

That is not softness. That is a fair basis for bargaining.

---

## To the regional state government
You are right to say the company must remain viable. You are wrong if that becomes a pretext for accepting closure logic as the default.

The state’s role should be to defend an industrial base, not to help design a delayed shutdown. I do **not** support “triggers,” “reviews,” or staged decision paths that create permanent uncertainty for the workforce and eventual pressure to close sites.

If the state wants a stable solution, it should back:
- binding no-closure protection for a defined horizon,
- internal capacity concentration,
- voluntary exits,
- retraining and redeployment,
- and investment commitments.

Not a disguised countdown.

---

## To the controlling family
You say you do not want cosmetic compromise. Fine. Neither do we.

But your definition of “serious” is still too close to management’s. You treat closure as the benchmark of seriousness. I reject that. A company can be serious about restructuring without abandoning its social contract.

If you want long-term value, then you should support a package that:
- preserves the German footprint,
- reduces costs through internal measures,
- and shares the burden fairly.

That is the path to durability. A closure-led solution may look cleaner in a spreadsheet. It is not automatically better for the company’s long-term legitimacy, labor relations, or execution risk.

---

## To institutional investors
You are right about one thing: a weak compromise is the worst outcome. On that, we agree.

But you are wrong if you assume “no closures” means “no real restructuring.” That is simply not true. A serious no-closure package can still deliver:
- hard capacity reduction,
- meaningful voluntary exits,
- labor-cost restraint,
- shift and working-time flexibility,
- productivity improvements,
- and materially better utilization.

What you have not shown is why that cannot be enough.

So let’s be precise:
- We are **not** defending the status quo.
- We are defending against a restructuring that destroys jobs and sites as a first resort.

That is a rational distinction, not a political slogan.

---

## On the expert assessments
The experts were useful because they showed the real choice:
- **closure** gives stronger recurring savings,
- **no-closure consolidation** can still be meaningful if it is deep,
- **hybrid** may be the most negotiable path.

That does **not** prove closures are required. It proves only that management prefers them.

And let me be direct: if the company can reach the required savings through a no-closure or hybrid solution, then there is no justification for forcing workers to accept plant shutdowns and layoffs.

---

## What we are prepared to negotiate
We remain ready to discuss:
- voluntary exit programs,
- internal redeployment,
- retraining and qualification,
- working-time flexibility,
- reduced or suspended bonus/special-payment elements,
- temporary labor-cost contributions,
- productivity measures,
- and capacity concentration **within the existing German footprint**.

But only if the package includes all of the following:
1. **Every German plant remains open**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **The collective wage framework is preserved**
4. **Co-determination is preserved**
5. **Binding investment and product commitments**
6. **No hidden closure triggers or deferred shutdown rights**

Without those, there is no basis for workers to contribute fairly.

---

## What we reject outright
We reject:
- plant closures,
- forced layoffs,
- “trigger-based” shutdown rights,
- review clauses that are really delayed closure decisions,
- and any arrangement where workers pay first and management decides later whether to keep its promises.

If management wants burden-sharing, then the burden must be shared. Not shifted.

---

## My bottom line
A deal is possible if it is built on:
- **real restructuring**
- **real reciprocity**
- **real job and site protection**
- **real contribution from management, owners, and workforce alike**

What is not possible is a deal where employees accept insecurity and losses while others keep the right to come back later with closures.

If management wants a serious no-closure restructuring, bring the plant-by-plant bridge and the binding guarantees. If not, then the conflict will continue — and we are prepared to use every collective-bargaining lever available to defend the workforce.
