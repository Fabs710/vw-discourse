Let me respond directly to what has been put on the table.

First: I welcome that several parties now at least admit the obvious — this company needs a credible future path, not slogans. But let me be equally clear: **a “credible future” cannot be built on plant closures in Germany or compulsory redundancies.** That remains our line, and nothing said here has changed that.

### To management
You keep saying “structural change,” “durable savings,” and “uncompetitive cost base” — but you still have not shown why that automatically means closing German plants or tearing up job security. That is exactly the problem: you are treating closures and forced layoffs as the default, instead of proving they are truly unavoidable.

If you want to talk seriously, then bring us:
- a **quantified savings plan** that does not rely on closures,
- a **site-by-site production concept** that keeps every German plant open,
- and a **binding employment framework through 2030**.

Until then, your position is still just a demand that employees absorb the pain while management keeps maximum flexibility. We will not accept that.

### To the controlling family
You are right about one thing: value destruction through endless conflict is bad for the company. But value destruction also comes from breaking trust, destroying the social contract, and provoking a workplace conflict that spreads across the whole group. So do not pretend that “hard decisions” means closures are somehow the rational first choice.

If you want long-term value, then support a solution that:
- keeps all German plants open,
- preserves co-determination and the job-security framework,
- and delivers savings through working-time tools, redeployment, qualification, productivity improvements, and fair management contributions.

That is the kind of disciplined restructuring we can support. Not closures.

### To institutional investors
You say you will back a “severe but credible restructuring.” Fine. But severity is not the same as intelligence. A plan that destroys workforce confidence and creates years of instability is not a credible route to better margins; it is a recipe for operational damage.

You ask what proof we need to accept a no-closure path. The answer is simple:
- management must provide the numbers,
- and then we can negotiate the tools.

But do not reverse the burden. **We do not have to justify why plants should remain open; management has to justify why they should not.** That is the responsibility of the side proposing destruction of jobs and sites.

### To the regional state government
You correctly identified the political and regional damage that closures would cause. I would go further: closures are not just a regional issue, they are a signal that the company is abandoning its own workforce and its own co-determination culture.

So no, a “moratorium plus review” is not enough if it is merely a staged path to closures later. A review cannot become a disguised countdown to shutting sites. If there is to be a review, it must be about:
- the effectiveness of non-closure measures,
- investments,
- product allocation,
- training,
- and job security.

Not about pre-authorizing plant closures.

### To the moderator’s compromise suggestions
Let me be very precise here.

#### 1) Moratorium with review trigger
A moratorium on closures and compulsory redundancies is only acceptable if it is **not** a trapdoor for later closures. We will not accept a “review” that is just a delayed execution plan.

#### 2) Capacity reduction through non-layoff instruments first
This is the only direction that is serious enough to discuss. But it must be framed correctly:
- capacity adjustment must happen **without closures**,
- working-time tools must be negotiated fairly,
- voluntary exits only,
- internal redeployment first,
- qualification and transfer rather than dismissal.

If management wants workforce contribution, then it must come with binding guarantees on sites and jobs. No guarantees, no contribution.

#### 3) Site guarantees tied to workforce contributions and investment commitments
This is the minimum basis for any discussion. But let me stress: the workforce is not the problem here. We are not the ones who misjudged market dynamics or underinvested in the future. So any contribution from us must be **limited, fair, and strictly conditional** on:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- preserved co-determination,
- binding product and investment commitments.

### What we will not accept
So there is no ambiguity:
- no closure package dressed up as “restructuring,”
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no weakening of the job-security framework,
- no attempt to force employees to pay for management mistakes,
- no fait accompli.

If management insists on those points, then we are not in a negotiation — we are in a confrontation. And the workforce is already prepared for that.

### What I am prepared to discuss
If the other side is serious, then we can discuss:
- internal capacity adjustment,
- working-time reduction or redistribution,
- voluntary exit only,
- retraining and redeployment,
- productivity measures that do not destroy jobs,
- and a genuine co-determination role in shaping any restructuring.

But I repeat: **every German plant must remain open.** That is not a decorative demand. It is the core condition for any settlement. If that condition is rejected, then there is no basis for agreement.
