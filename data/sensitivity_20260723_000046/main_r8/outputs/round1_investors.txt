From the perspective of dispersed capital-market shareholders, the key issue is not whether the outcome is socially comfortable, but whether it restores **credible, durable competitiveness** and protects long-term equity value.

So I will be blunt: I agree with management, and partly with the family owner, on the diagnosis. If the German cost base remains structurally uncompetitive, then the current margin problem will persist, capital will be wasted, and the stock will continue to underperform. We cannot support a settlement that merely postpones adjustment.

That said, I do **not** support a cosmetic compromise either. A deal that preserves every site, avoids meaningful capacity change, and substitutes temporary gestures for structural savings would be value-destructive. It would lock in weak returns and leave shareholders underwriting a slow erosion of the business.

Where I differ from the strongest versions of management’s position is on execution: **plant closures and forced redundancies are not valuable in themselves**. They are only justified if they are genuinely necessary to achieve the required cost reset. If equivalent durable savings can be achieved through capacity reductions, working-time flexibility, labour-cost contributions, redeployment, and other binding measures, then that is preferable — not for sentiment, but because it may deliver the same financial result with less disruption and less strike risk.

My position is therefore:

- **Yes** to a restructuring that delivers material, durable cost reduction.
- **No** to a settlement that leaves margins structurally impaired.
- **No** to a deal that is all guarantees and no numbers.
- **No** to prolonged conflict that destroys near-term value without changing the underlying economics.
- **Yes** to alternatives to closures, but only if they are quantified, enforceable, and sufficient.

To the works council and union: if you want investor support, you need to show more than resistance. You need to support a package that **actually closes the gap** on cost and capacity. “No closures, no layoffs” is not enough if it fails the economics. Shareholders will not back a structure that preserves every legacy entitlement while leaving returns depressed.

To management: if you want capital-market confidence, bring a plan that is **specific, measurable, and credible**. We need evidence that the package will return the company to competitive margins on a sustained basis. If closures are part of that, say so and justify them. If not, then present an alternative that is equally forceful and bankable.

To the state shareholder and controlling family: we understand the governance constraints, but those constraints do not create value. The market will judge the outcome on earnings power, cash flow, and returns on sales. If governance blocks a serious restructuring, the cost will be borne in valuation and access to capital.

In short: we support decisive restructuring, not political theater. If the final package restores margins and is credible over the medium term, we can support it. If it does not, we will not pretend otherwise.
