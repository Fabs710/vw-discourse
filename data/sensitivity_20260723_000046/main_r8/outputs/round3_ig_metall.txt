## To management
You still have not answered the central question: **what is the exact savings gap, and why can it not be closed without plant closures or compulsory redundancies?**

The expert just said what we already knew: a no-closure package may be sufficient, or it may not — but that depends on the actual numbers. Fine. Then put the numbers on the table.

What I do **not** accept is this pattern:
1. declare the situation “structural,”
2. treat closures as the default benchmark of seriousness,
3. and then ask labour to prove a non-closure plan works.

That is backwards.

You want the workforce to contribute? Then show the full savings bridge:
- current cost gap,
- target margin,
- plant-by-plant utilization,
- fixed-cost burden,
- savings from working-time changes,
- savings from productivity,
- savings from voluntary exits and internal redeployment,
- savings from management and owner contributions,
- and only then any residual gap.

Until that is done, closures are not “necessary”; they are simply the most forceful option management prefers.

## To the Works Council
I agree with your core line:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of the collective framework,
- and no reduction of co-determination to a cosmetic role.

And I agree that labour should not be asked to design its own destruction.

Where I would sharpen the position is this: we should not let management hide behind the term “capacity reduction” as a euphemism for closures. If they mean closures, they should say so openly and justify them.

But I also need to be careful: if we want to defend jobs and sites credibly, we have to engage on **real, quantified alternatives**. Not to soften our red lines — they stand — but to show that there is a serious route to savings without breaking the company’s social compact.

## To the state shareholder and family owner
You both say closures should not be the first resort. Good. Then use your governance power to make that real.

Right now, the burden is being shifted onto the workforce to make concessions while management keeps closure pressure in reserve. That is not a balanced process.

If you want a stable company, then insist on:
- a non-closure first package,
- independent verification,
- and a binding commitment that no plant closure or compulsory redundancy happens unless every internal alternative has been genuinely tested and documented as insufficient.

That is not obstruction. That is responsible governance.

## To investors
You keep saying “credibility,” “durability,” and “returns.” We agree on the need for durability. We disagree on the method.

A deal that forces through closures and triggers prolonged strikes, production disruption, and lasting distrust is not automatically more valuable. It may be the opposite. Industrial peace has value, and so does workforce commitment.

The expert also made clear: non-closure measures can be materially significant. So it is not credible to dismiss them out of hand.

What I can support is a package that:
- keeps German plants open,
- avoids compulsory redundancies,
- delivers material savings through negotiated measures,
- and includes a fair employee contribution matched by management and owner commitments.

What I will not support is a “value-maximising” solution that simply shifts all the adjustment burden onto employees and regions.

## My position remains firm
### Non-negotiable:
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No plant closures in Germany**
- **No weakening of the collective wage framework**
- **No erosion of real co-determination**

### Negotiable, if fair and matched:
- working-time flexibility
- productivity measures
- voluntary exits
- redeployment
- qualification/retraining
- limited and temporary labour-cost contributions
- bonus restraint or similar measures
- internal capacity adjustments

But those concessions only make sense if there are binding counter-commitments:
- site security
- job security through 2030
- investment and product allocation
- management and owner contribution
- a verifiable savings target

## On the expert’s assessment
The expert did **not** say closures are required. The expert said they may be needed if the gap is large and the footprint burden is high. That is not a mandate for closures; it is a call for proper verification.

So I support the call for data — but not the implicit assumption that the data will justify plant closures. They may; they may not. We are not conceding that point in advance.

The union is prepared to negotiate a **hard but fair** settlement. That means:
- employees contribute,
- but they do not carry all the pain;
- the company restructures,
- but it does not break its social contract;
- the business becomes more competitive,
- but not at the price of forced layoffs and plant closures.
