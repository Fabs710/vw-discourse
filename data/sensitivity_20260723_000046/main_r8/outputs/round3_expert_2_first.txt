### Independent automotive footprint and cost-structure assessment

I can assess the **technical plausibility** of a non-closure restructuring, but I cannot verify the case without the company’s actual numbers. On the information provided, my conclusion is:

> **A non-closure package can sometimes close a large share of a savings gap in an automotive OEM, but whether it can close the full gap without plant closures or compulsory redundancies depends entirely on the size of the overcapacity, the fixed-cost burden of the German sites, and the achievable labor-cost and utilization measures.**  
> If the required savings are very large relative to current plant utilization, a no-closure solution becomes materially harder and may be insufficient on economics alone.

## What is technically plausible without closures
A restructuring that avoids plant closures and compulsory redundancies can still generate meaningful savings through:

- **working-time reduction or flexibility**
- **shift-system changes**
- **wage moderation / temporary concessions**
- **bonus and allowance reductions**
- **voluntary exits, retirements, and attrition**
- **redeployment across plants**
- **productivity improvement**
- **logistics and internal complexity reduction**
- **sourcing changes and local content optimization**
- **delayed or rephased investment**
- **capacity rationalization inside plants** without closing the sites

In automotive manufacturing, these measures can be significant, especially if:
- utilization is low,
- there is duplicated overhead,
- labor agreements allow strong flexibility,
- and product mix can be simplified.

## What is harder to achieve without closures
The following savings are usually much harder to replace without plant closures:

- **removal of fixed overhead from a whole site**
- **elimination of plant-level maintenance, utilities, security, and management layers**
- **full depopulation of underutilized buildings**
- **structural reduction in depreciation/inefficiency from excess footprint**
- **major logistics simplification from concentrating production**

If the problem is primarily **excess fixed footprint** rather than just **high labor cost**, then no-closure alternatives often leave a substantial residual gap.

## Core economic test
The key question is not whether savings are possible in the abstract. It is:

> **How large is the annual gap, and how much of it is tied to footprint fixed costs versus workforce cost and process inefficiency?**

A defensible judgment requires at minimum:

1. **current and target production volumes by plant**
2. **capacity utilization by line and site**
3. **full fixed-cost stack by plant**
   - labor
   - depreciation
   - maintenance
   - energy
   - overhead
   - logistics
3. **incremental labor cost per vehicle**
4. **planned model allocation and platform strategy**
5. **expected savings from voluntary measures**
6. **cost of severance, transfer, retraining, and social plan measures**
7. **one-time restructuring costs versus recurring savings**
8. **timeline for implementation and ramp-up**

Without those data, nobody can honestly say that a no-closure package will or will not close the gap.

## On compulsory redundancies
From a footprint and cost-structure standpoint, compulsory redundancies are **not required in every restructuring**. They become relevant when:
- voluntary exits are insufficient,
- redeployment capacity is saturated,
- and the cost base still needs to fall materially.

So the absence of forced layoffs does **not** automatically make a restructuring impossible. But it can limit how fast and how far headcount reduction can go.
