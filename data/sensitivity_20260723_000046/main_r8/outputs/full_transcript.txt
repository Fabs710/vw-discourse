## Executive view

Without company-specific data, the answer is:

- **A pure non-closure package can close a meaningful part of the gap, but rarely all of it** if the issue is true structural overcapacity in a mature automotive footprint.

## 1) What each scenario can typically save

- wage moderation / temporary pay sacrifice,
- bonus or allowance reductions,
- productivity improvements,
- internal redeployment,
- voluntary exits only,

- Labor-cost savings: **moderate**
- Fixed-cost reduction: **limited**
- Capacity reduction: **limited to moderate**

- **5% to 15% reduction in controllable labor cost**

### Scenario B — Mixed package
Measures:
- one plant closure or major footprint consolidation,

- plus productivity measures,

**Typical effect**
- Labor-cost savings: **material**
- Fixed-cost reduction: **material**
- Capacity reduction: **material**
- Margin impact: usually the **best balance** between savings and implementation risk

**Indicative magnitude**

A mixed package can often deliver:

### Scenario C — Deep structural cuts with multiple closures
Measures:
- one or more plant closures,
- major job cuts,
- structural capacity reduction,
- labor cost reset,
- possible consolidation of platforms and product allocation.

**Typical effect**
- Largest absolute fixed-cost reduction
- Strongest capacity adjustment
- Highest implementation disruption

**Indicative magnitude**
This is the only scenario that reliably removes:
- direct labor,
- indirect labor,
- plant overhead,
- maintenance,
- logistics,
- and underabsorbed fixed cost

It is the most likely to produce a **durable, step-change margin improvement** if the existing footprint is truly oversized.

## 2) Why closures are financially powerful

In manufacturing, especially automotive, closure savings are not just “jobs saved or lost.” They remove multiple cost layers:

1. **Direct labor**
2. **Indirect plant labor**
3. **Maintenance and facilities**
4. **Energy and utilities**
5. **Factory-level overhead**
6. **Local logistics and internal transport**
7. **Allocated corporate overhead**
8. **Underabsorbed depreciation / fixed-cost burden**

That is why a closure can produce a savings multiple greater than the headcount reduction alone suggests.

A rough rule: if a plant is materially underutilized, the economics of closure can exceed a pure headcount reduction by **a wide margin**, because the biggest savings come from **eliminating idle fixed costs**, not just payroll.

## 3) Why non-closure measures often fall short

The non-closure toolbox is real, but it has limits:

### Working-time changes
Useful, but:
- savings are proportional to hours cut
- they may not fully reduce fixed overhead
- they can preserve excess plant capacity if volume does not fall

### Wage moderation / concessions
Useful, but:
- usually too small relative to the full cost base
- politically and industrially fragile
- often time-limited unless structurally embedded

### Voluntary exits
Useful, but:
- unpredictable in timing and mix
- often insufficient in scale for a large footprint correction
- can remove experienced staff in undesirable places

### Productivity gains
Useful, but:
- usually partly offset by learning curve, capex needs, and implementation lag
- often already embedded in management plans, so not all incremental

### Internal redeployment
Useful, but:
- does not reduce company-wide labor cost unless it eliminates a role elsewhere
- can just move the cost, not remove it

So a non-closure package can be economically meaningful, but it tends to be strongest on **variable labor cost** and weakest on **structural fixed-cost removal**.

## 1. Decision context
The company faces a structural competitiveness crisis: shrinking European demand, intense low-cost competition, high German cost levels, and missing profitability targets. The decision is not whether restructuring is needed, but whether it should proceed through plant closures and compulsory redundancies, or through a negotiated non-closure package that achieves comparable savings with greater employment protection.

Governance makes the issue unusually constrained. Co-determination gives labour an effective veto over major site decisions; the family holding and the regional state both have blocking power and political legitimacy; investors demand credible economic correction but do not control the process alone. The live issue is therefore a negotiated settlement under mutual veto, not a simple management decision.

## 2. Stakeholder assembly
- **Group & Brand Management**: operational lead for the restructuring; high economic authority, moderate legitimacy.
- **Works Council**: plant-level workforce representation with co-determination power; high legitimacy and blocking power.
- **Trade Union**: bargaining authority over wages and strike power; high leverage, especially industrially.
- **Regional State Government**: anchor shareholder and political actor; high legitimacy, blocking power.
- **Controlling Family Holding**: governance controller with major voting power; very high standing.
- **Institutional Investors**: capital-markets constituency; meaningful economic weight, lower direct control.
- **Independent Industrial Restructuring Expert/Auditor**: no decision power, but high informational legitimacy.

Weighted by standing, the family, state, management, and labour-side institutions dominate the practical settlement space; investors matter mainly as a value discipline voice.

## 3. Final positions

### Group & Brand Management
Management insists the company needs a durable, quantified cost and capacity reset. It will consider non-closure measures, but only if they are proven to close the recurring savings gap; otherwise closures and workforce reductions remain on the table. It rejects an absolute no-closure principle in advance.

### Works Council
The Works Council holds a hard line: German plant closures and compulsory redundancies are unacceptable starting points. It supports a full savings bridge and independent verification only if the process is explicitly “non-closure first” and includes management/owner burden-sharing, job security through 2030, and real co-determination.

### Trade Union
The Union aligns closely with the Works Council on no forced layoffs and no closure-first framing. It is prepared to discuss flexibility, productivity, redeployment, and limited labour-cost moderation, but only in exchange for binding job security, investment commitments, and a fair sharing of sacrifice. It wants numbers, but not as a procedural route to legitimising closure.

### Regional State Government
The state shareholder agrees the company needs a real restructuring, but rejects closures and compulsory redundancies as default instruments. It supports independent verification and a phased comparison of non-closure, mixed, and residual-closure scenarios, while insisting that any solution preserve regional employment, co-determination, and the state’s blocking role.

### Controlling Family Holding
The family agrees with the diagnosis of structural weakness and wants a value-preserving solution, not symbolism. It will back the economically sufficient outcome, but only after an audited plant-by-plant savings bridge shows whether non-closure measures are enough; it does not want to pre-commit to closures, but also does not accept an absolute no-closure veto without proof.

### Institutional Investors
Investors demand durable recurring savings, clear implementation, and a route back to competitive margins. They prefer a solution that avoids conflict and closures if it is economically equivalent, but they will not support a package that preserves the footprint while leaving the company structurally uncompetitive.

### Independent Industrial Restructuring Expert/Auditor
The expert’s role is not to choose the outcome, but to define the evidence standard. It says a non-closure package could be sufficient in principle, but only if plant-by-plant costs, capacity, utilisation, and measure-by-measure savings are disclosed and verified. Without that, no side can credibly claim that closures are necessary or unnecessary.

## 4. Genuine conflicts (structurally incompatible positions)
1. **Closure as open option vs. closure as excluded starting point**  
   Management and investors treat closures as potentially necessary; labour and the state treat them as unacceptable unless proven indispensable.

2. **Proof burden**  
   Management wants labour to demonstrate a viable alternative; labour insists management must first prove non-closure insufficiency.

3. **Forced redundancies**  
   Labour rejects compulsory redundancies outright; management and investors leave them open if required.

4. **Job/site guarantees vs. economic flexibility**  
   Labour seeks strong guarantees through 2030; management and shareholders want guarantees conditional on economic viability.

5. **Process meaning of verification**  
   Labour wants verification to test non-closure viability; management fears it becomes a device to delay hard decisions while preserving the option to cut later.

## 5. Areas of convergence
- All parties accept that the crisis is structural, not cosmetic.
- All parties accept that a negotiated settlement is preferable to unilateral escalation.
- All parties see value in an independently verified savings bridge.
- All parties accept that management, owners, and employees must all contribute.
- There is broad support for non-closure measures: working-time flexibility, productivity, redeployment, retraining, voluntary exits, and overhead reductions.
- Everyone agrees that a weak, temporary deal would simply postpone the conflict.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker
1. **Speed vs. proof**  
   Immediate hard restructuring may deliver faster cost correction, but without verification it risks industrial conflict and governance blockage.

2. **Competitiveness vs. social legitimacy**  
   Deeper footprint cuts may improve margins, but they carry political, legal, and labour-relations costs that can destroy execution credibility.

3. **Certainty vs. flexibility**  
   Binding job/security guarantees improve trust, but reduce management’s room to adapt if demand worsens.

4. **Short-term peace vs. long-term restructuring**  
   A no-closure deal may buy peace, but only if it truly closes the recurring gap; otherwise it merely delays a worse confrontation.

5. **Value preservation vs. legacy protection**  
   Investors and management prioritize structural competitiveness; labour and the state prioritize preservation of sites, jobs, and co-determination.

## 7. Integrative pathways
### Pathway A: Sequenced “non-closure first” test
Agree a time-limited process in which a fully populated, independently verified non-closure package is tested first. Only if it leaves a material residual gap does the parties reopen footprint questions.

### Pathway B: Conditional no-closure default
Commit that German closures and forced redundancies are off the table unless an auditor confirms that non-closure measures, plus management/owner contributions, still fail to close a defined residual gap.

### Pathway C: Mixed package with protected sites
Combine employee flexibility, voluntary exits, redeployment, productivity, and management/owner contributions with protected strategic sites, while reserving a narrowly defined fallback for any residual structural gap.

### Pathway D: Governance-for-trust package
Pair the economic package with enforceable monitoring: independent auditor, milestone reporting, investment/product commitments, and a review gate tied to verified savings rather than political declarations.
