I’ll respond directly and plainly.

## To the Works Council and Union

I hear your red lines: no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no weakening of co-determination. I also hear the offer of working-time changes, voluntary measures, redeployment, and productivity improvements.

Where I agree:
- We do need a **durable** settlement, not a cosmetic one.
- Temporary fixes alone are not enough.
- A negotiated package is better than uncontrolled conflict.
- Employees should contribute if the company contributes as well.

Where I disagree:
- You are still treating **no closures** and **no forced reductions** as if they were absolute principles, regardless of the economics. They are not.
- You are still asking management to prove a negative while not yet offering a package that closes the cost gap in a measurable way.
- “Capacity reduction without closures” is not a plan by itself. If the numbers do not materially improve, then it is just a semantic shield for the status quo.

Let me be clear: I am not here to manage decline. If we preserve every site, keep every historic guarantee, and avoid meaningful labour-cost change, then we will keep an uncompetitive structure and simply run out of room to invest.

## The core issue remains unchanged

We face:
- a shrinking European market,
- intense low-cost competition,
- pressure from China,
- and a German cost base that is above target.

That means the solution must deliver:
1. **real capacity reduction**,  
2. **real labour-cost relief**,  
3. **real productivity gains**, and  
4. **real investment headroom** for 2030.

If your package cannot do that, then it is not sufficient.

## To the regional state government

I appreciate that you do not want reckless social damage. Neither do I want that. But your insistence that closures are politically unacceptable does not change the economics.

If the state wants to support the company, then it should support a settlement that is economically credible, not one that merely postpones adjustment. I do not accept a position where governance constraints become a permanent veto over necessary restructuring.

## To the controlling family

You say you support value protection. Then support a package that actually protects value. That means accepting that:
- overcapacity must be reduced,
- labour costs must come down structurally,
- and some locations may no longer fit the future footprint.

If the answer is only “find alternatives,” without accepting that the footprint itself has to change, then we are not talking about a restructuring; we are talking about wishful thinking.

## On alternatives

Yes, I am prepared to examine non-closure measures. But they must be quantified and sufficient. So here is what I need to see in concrete terms:

- How much cost comes out of working-time measures?
- How much from wage moderation or lower bonus components?
- How much from voluntary exits and redeployment?
- How much from productivity measures?
- How much from line and plant footprint changes?
- And critically: how does the total close the gap to a competitive return-on-sales level?

If the answer is not enough, then we must be honest about that.

## What I am prepared to discuss

I remain open to a package that includes:
- voluntary workforce reductions,
- internal transfers and retraining,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity commitments,
- labour-cost contributions,
- and capacity adjustments.

But I am not open to a package that leaves the company structurally overmanned and under-margin. That would be a delay tactic, not a solution.

## Bottom line

I will not pretend there is a comfortable compromise here.

- If you want **no closures at all**, then show me a package with equivalent durable savings.
- If you want **no compulsory redundancies**, then show me how the workforce contribution becomes material enough to restore competitiveness.
- If you want **binding guarantees through 2030**, then those guarantees must be compatible with a viable business model.

Otherwise, we are back to the same problem: a protected cost base, declining competitiveness, and no funds for the future.

So my position is unchanged:  
**We need a structurally sufficient restructuring, not a symbolic one.**  
If that means difficult site decisions, I will not rule them out.
