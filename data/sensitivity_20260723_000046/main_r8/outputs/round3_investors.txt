We agree on the diagnosis: this company has a structural competitiveness problem, and a cosmetic settlement would destroy value. We also agree that employees should not be asked to absorb the entire burden. That is where agreement ends if the package does **not** restore margins.

From the capital markets’ side, the only question that matters is whether the final deal delivers:

- **durable annual savings**
- **real capacity rationalisation**
- **credible implementation certainty**
- **acceptable industrial risk**
- and a **clear path back to competitive returns on sales**

If it does not, we will not support it.

## To management
You are right that the business cannot be carried indefinitely with an uncompetitive cost base. But you are still asking the market to accept a conclusion before the full case is shown. Repeating that closures may be necessary is not the same as proving they are necessary.

I support the call for a **plant-by-plant savings bridge**. Show:

- current cost gap
- savings from working-time changes
- savings from productivity and redeployment
- savings from voluntary exits
- savings from labour-cost measures
- management and owner contribution
- one-time restructuring costs
- residual gap under each scenario

Without that, the market cannot distinguish between a necessary restructuring and the most disruptive one.

## To the Works Council and Union
Your refusal of plant closures and compulsory redundancies is understandable, but it is **not sufficient** if it leaves the company structurally overcosted. “No closures” is not a financial strategy. It is a veto position unless you can prove an economically equivalent package.

The expert was right: non-closure measures can be meaningful, but whether they are sufficient depends entirely on the scale of the overcapacity and fixed-cost burden. So I need numbers, not principle alone.

If you want the market to back a no-closure solution, it must be **materially equivalent** in recurring savings and capacity correction. So far, that has not been demonstrated.

## To the state shareholder and family owner
You are both right to resist a reckless, socially destructive break. But governance constraints do not create value. If the non-closure package is truly sufficient, support it. If it is not, then do not hide behind political discomfort to preserve an uncompetitive structure.

I do not want closures for their own sake. I do want the economically correct footprint. The market will punish denial faster than it punishes pain.

## What would move us
We would support a settlement if it is:

1. **Quantified**
2. **Durable**
3. **Enforceable**
4. **Margin-accretive**
5. **Not dependent on permanent goodwill**
6. **Not a temporary bridge to another crisis**

That can include:
- working-time flexibility
- voluntary workforce measures
- redeployment
- qualification/retraining
- limited labour-cost contributions
- productivity improvements
- and, if proven necessary, footprint changes

But if the outcome merely preserves every site while leaving margins structurally impaired, then we will not endorse it.

## My bottom line
I am open to alternatives to closures. I am **not** open to alternatives to competitiveness.

If a non-closure or mixed package really closes the gap, fine — support it.  
If it does not, then management should stop presenting a protected cost base as a solution.
