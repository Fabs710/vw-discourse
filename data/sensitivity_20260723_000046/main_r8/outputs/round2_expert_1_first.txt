Here is the restructuring assessment, focused on the specific question raised by the capital side: **can a non-closure package or mixed package realistically deliver the same durable savings and margin improvement as plant closures, and what would the financial trade-off be?**

## Executive view

Without company-specific data, the answer is:

- **A pure non-closure package can close a meaningful part of the gap, but rarely all of it** if the issue is true structural overcapacity in a mature automotive footprint.
- **Capacity reduction without closures can deliver savings**, but only if it includes:
  - major volume transfer out of the affected plants,
  - significant line shutdowns or shift reductions,
  - sustained working-time cuts,
  - wage moderation or benefit reductions,
  - and very high voluntary attrition.
- **Plant closures are usually the largest single lever** for fixing structural overcapacity because they remove not only direct labor but also fixed overhead, maintenance, utilities, local logistics, indirect labor, and underutilized depreciation burden.
- Therefore, the key question is not whether “alternative measures” exist — they do — but whether they are **large enough, fast enough, and durable enough** to match the economics of closure.

## 1) What each scenario can typically save

I would frame the three scenarios as follows.

### Scenario A — Non-closure restructuring
Measures:
- working-time reduction,
- wage moderation / temporary pay sacrifice,
- bonus or allowance reductions,
- productivity improvements,
- internal redeployment,
- voluntary exits only,
- no plant closure.

**Typical effect**
- Labor-cost savings: **moderate**
- Fixed-cost reduction: **limited**
- Capacity reduction: **limited to moderate**
- Margin impact: improves, but often **insufficient** if overcapacity is severe

**Indicative magnitude**
Depending on starting labor cost base, the package might yield roughly:
- **5% to 15% reduction in controllable labor cost**
- perhaps **1 to 3 percentage points of operating margin improvement** if fully executed
- but often not enough to offset a structurally high-cost footprint

This is most plausible if the company’s problem is mainly **unit labor cost** and not footprint excess.

### Scenario B — Mixed package
Measures:
- one plant closure or major footprint consolidation,
- plus working-time changes,
- plus voluntary separations,
- plus productivity measures,
- plus wage/bonus concessions.

**Typical effect**
- Labor-cost savings: **material**
- Fixed-cost reduction: **material**
- Capacity reduction: **material**
- Margin impact: usually the **best balance** between savings and implementation risk

**Indicative magnitude**
This is the most credible route if the company needs:
- **substantial annual savings**
- but wants to avoid a maximal closure program

A mixed package can often deliver:
- **mid-teens to low-20s percent reduction in relevant site cost base** at the affected footprint
- enough to move return on sales meaningfully, assuming volume is stable enough to fill the retained plants better

### Scenario C — Deep structural cuts with multiple closures
Measures:
- one or more plant closures,
- major job cuts,
- structural capacity reduction,
- labor cost reset,
- possible consolidation of platforms and product allocation.

**Typical effect**
- Largest absolute fixed-cost reduction
- Strongest capacity adjustment
- Highest implementation disruption

**Indicative magnitude**
This is the only scenario that reliably removes:
- direct labor,
- indirect labor,
- plant overhead,
- maintenance,
- logistics,
- and underabsorbed fixed cost

It is the most likely to produce a **durable, step-change margin improvement** if the existing footprint is truly oversized.

## 2) Why closures are financially powerful

In manufacturing, especially automotive, closure savings are not just “jobs saved or lost.” They remove multiple cost layers:

1. **Direct labor**
2. **Indirect plant labor**
3. **Maintenance and facilities**
4. **Energy and utilities**
5. **Factory-level overhead**
6. **Local logistics and internal transport**
7. **Allocated corporate overhead**
8. **Underabsorbed depreciation / fixed-cost burden**

That is why a closure can produce a savings multiple greater than the headcount reduction alone suggests.

A rough rule: if a plant is materially underutilized, the economics of closure can exceed a pure headcount reduction by **a wide margin**, because the biggest savings come from **eliminating idle fixed costs**, not just payroll.

## 3) Why non-closure measures often fall short

The non-closure toolbox is real, but it has limits:

### Working-time changes
Useful, but:
- savings are proportional to hours cut
- they may not fully reduce fixed overhead
- they can preserve excess plant capacity if volume does not fall

### Wage moderation / concessions
Useful, but:
- usually too small relative to the full cost base
- politically and industrially fragile
- often time-limited unless structurally embedded

### Voluntary exits
Useful, but:
- unpredictable in timing and mix
- often insufficient in scale for a large footprint correction
- can remove experienced staff in undesirable places

### Productivity gains
Useful, but:
- usually partly offset by learning curve, capex needs, and implementation lag
- often already embedded in management plans, so not all incremental

### Internal redeployment
Useful, but:
- does not reduce company-wide labor cost unless it eliminates a role elsewhere
- can just move the cost, not remove it

So a non-closure package can be economically meaningful, but it tends to be strongest on **variable labor cost** and weakest on **structural fixed-cost removal**.

## 4) Quantitative framework the parties should use

Management should present the restructuring in this form:

### A. Savings bridge
For each scenario, show:
- payroll savings
- overtime and shift premiums saved
- headcount reduction
- site overhead reduction
- maintenance/utilities reduction
- logistics reduction
- depreciation / asset write-down effects
- one-time restructuring costs

### B. Capacity effect
For each scenario:
- installed capacity before/after
- annual output by plant
- utilization rate
- product allocation shifts
- ability to cover fixed costs at expected volume

### C. Margin effect
For each scenario:
- annual EBIT or ROS impact
- breakeven volume
- cash flow impact
- payback period on restructuring costs

## 5) What I would expect in a realistic automotive case

Absent detailed data, a credible range would be:

### Non-closure package
- annual savings: **low hundreds of millions of euros**, possibly more if wage moderation is deep
- capacity reduction: **limited**
- margin uplift: **modest to moderate**
- risk: high that the company remains structurally overcosted

### Mixed package
- annual savings: **mid-hundreds of millions to around one billion euros**, depending on plant size and workforce scope
- capacity reduction: **meaningful**
- margin uplift: **material**
- risk: implementable with negotiation, though still difficult

### Multiple closures / deep restructuring
- annual savings: **largest**
- capacity reduction: **substantial**
- margin uplift: **largest and most durable**
- risk: highest industrial and execution risk, but strongest financial reset

## 6) Bottom line on the current bargaining positions

### On the union / works council position
A “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” line is **not financially impossible**, but it is only credible if accompanied by a quantified package that achieves:
- large labor-cost savings,
- real capacity reduction,
- and durable flexibility.

Without that, it is more a **veto position** than a restructuring solution.

### On management’s position
Management is correct that a **real structural correction** is needed. But it should not present closures as automatically proven before publishing the full savings bridge. From a financial standpoint, the burden of proof is:
- show the cost gap,
- show what non-closure measures deliver,
- then show what residual gap remains.

### On the shareholder/state position
The state and family are correct to demand:
- quantification,
- durability,
- and a comparison of scenarios.

That is the right test. The market will not reward rhetoric; it will reward a package that actually reduces fixed cost per unit and restores margin.

## Final assessment

If the company’s problem is truly **structural overcapacity plus high fixed labor and overhead cost**, then:

- **Non-closure measures alone are unlikely to fully solve it**
- **A mixed package is the most plausible compromise**
- **Closures are the most powerful tool if the gap is large**

So the decision is not “closures versus no solution.” It is:

**How much of the required structural savings can be achieved without closures, and what residual gap remains that only footprint reduction can close?**

That is the exact question management should quantify next.
