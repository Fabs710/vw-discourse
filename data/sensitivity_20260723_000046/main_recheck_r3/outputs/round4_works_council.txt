I will be equally clear in round 4.

## First: what I agree with
I agree with everyone on one point: this company needs a **real restructuring** and not a PR exercise.

I also agree that:
- management and top leadership must contribute,
- investment in future products and German production is necessary,
- and a solution that simply postpones the problem would be irresponsible.

But agreement on the need for change does **not** mean agreement on the tools. And on the tools, my position has not moved.

## To management
You keep saying the alternatives are “not proven equivalent.” That is not enough.

You want to keep **closures** and **compulsory redundancies** available as ordinary instruments. I reject that. Not because I dislike restructuring, but because you still have not shown that destroying sites and breaking the job-security framework is necessary.

You are asking the workforce to accept:
- permanent insecurity,
- erosion of trust,
- and the possibility that plants can be shut after decades of loyalty,

while you have not yet put a complete and credible non-dismissal package on the table.

That is not a serious negotiation. That is pressure.

### What I need from management
Put the numbers down, side by side, and stop hiding behind generalities:
- savings from management and overhead cuts,
- savings from top-level compensation restraint,
- savings from procurement and administrative simplification,
- savings from working-time flexibility,
- savings from temporary wage moderation,
- savings from redeployment and qualification,
- savings from voluntary exit instruments,
- and the remaining gap after all that.

If after that there is still a gap, then we can discuss **additional internal adjustments**. But we do **not** accept the leap from “gap remains” to “therefore close plants and force layoffs.”

That leap is exactly what we reject.

## To the family holding
You talk about stewardship, but stewardship means protecting the long-term industrial base, not threatening it as a bargaining chip.

I agree with you on one thing: a cosmetic deal would be worthless. But your insistence that closures and forced redundancies remain available “in principle” is precisely what poisons trust and makes a durable agreement harder.

You say unconditional guarantees are not credible. Fine. But conditionality cannot become a disguised right to shut sites whenever management decides the pressure is convenient.

If the family wants to act as a responsible owner, then it should support:
- all German sites remaining open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- real investment in future products,
- and a restructuring based on shared sacrifice.

That is stewardship. The rest is leverage.

## To institutional investors
You are right that a weak compromise is worthless. But your definition of “real” restructuring is too narrow if it treats jobs and sites as just variables in a valuation model.

A strike, loss of trust, supplier damage, and long-term industrial conflict also destroy value. A package that preserves site security and restores competitiveness can be economically superior to a blunt cost-cutting assault.

You have still not shown that closures and forced layoffs are the only way to get durable savings. You have shown only that they are the harshest way.

And I will say this plainly: the harshest way is not automatically the best way.

## To the state government
I appreciate that you are not pushing for closures as a first answer. But I want to be very clear: a “moratorium” or “conditional guarantee” is not enough if it leaves open a backdoor to plant shutdowns once the pressure rises.

If the state truly wants to protect the industrial base, then it should support:
- binding protection for German sites,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- preservation of co-determination,
- and a restructuring that uses internal adjustment tools first.

Not “last resort” closure logic. We have seen where that leads.

## On the bridge options
### 1) No forced redundancies first, closures only as last resort and conditional
No. We do not accept this frame.

Why? Because it still normalizes closures as a legitimate endpoint. Once that door is opened, management can always claim the last resort has arrived. That is not protection; it is managed exposure.

We will not agree to a model where jobs are protected only until management decides otherwise.

### 2) Site-security package with quantified employee and owner contributions
This is the only direction that comes close to seriousness — but only if it means **binding security for all German plants** and **no compulsory redundancies**.

I am prepared to discuss:
- temporary working-time changes,
- temporary wage moderation,
- temporary cuts in certain premiums,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary exit options,
- management restraint,
- and measurable owner-side contribution.

What I am **not** prepared to discuss is packaging all of that as the price of making closures more acceptable later.

### 3) Conditional site guarantees tied to performance and transformation
Rejected in its current logic.

A guarantee that can be withdrawn later is not a guarantee. It is probation. It turns the workforce into a permanent risk pool and puts plant security at the mercy of management targets that can always be moved.

That is not co-determination. That is conditional obedience.

## What would actually move me
Very little — but there are two things that can move us:
1. **Binding site and job guarantees through 2030 for all German plants**
2. **A genuine co-determination role in shaping the restructuring**

Not consultation after the fact. Not “we’ll explain the decision.” Real co-determination, real say in how the transformation is designed, and real protection against forced redundancies and closures.

If you want our cooperation, start there.

## What I will not accept
Let me be absolutely explicit:

- No German plant closures
- No compulsory redundancies
- No conditional or probationary job security
- No deal that breaks the historic job-security framework
- No restructuring that makes the workforce the default adjustment variable

If management and capital insist that those tools must remain available, then they are telling us they prefer power over partnership. We will respond accordingly.

## My direct challenge back
Before anyone asks us again to accept “flexibility,” answer these questions:
- What exactly is the residual savings gap after internal measures?
- Why is that gap not bridgeable through time-limited, fairly shared labour measures?
- What site-specific evidence proves a closure is necessary rather than a choice?
- What binding investment is committed to German plants through 2030?
- What co-determination rights are you prepared to give us in the restructuring design?

If those answers are not on the table, then the call for closures and forced redundancies is not economics. It is a power grab.

## Bottom line
The works council’s position remains unchanged:

- **All German plants stay open**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Preserve co-determination**
- **Preserve the job-security framework**
- **Temporary shared sacrifices are possible**
- **Binding investment commitments are necessary**
- **Management and owners must contribute visibly**

If the other side wants a durable solution, they need to stop demanding that workers pay for management’s failure through closures and layoffs. That will not be accepted.
