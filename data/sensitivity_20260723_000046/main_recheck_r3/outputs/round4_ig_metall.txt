I’ll be direct.

## To the Works Council
I agree with your core line: **no compulsory redundancies**, no one-sided burden on the workforce, and no pretending that workers should pay for management’s mistakes alone.

I also agree that a cosmetic compromise would be worthless. If management wants a settlement, it has to be real, measurable, and fair.

Where I differ slightly is that we should keep the bargaining focus on the **collective agreement and shared-sacrifice package** first, because that is where we can still shape a durable solution. But on the core red lines, we are aligned.

## To management
You still have not done what is required if you want us to move: **prove that closures and forced layoffs are necessary**.

Saying alternatives are “not proven equivalent” is not enough. That argument cuts both ways. You have not proven that closures and compulsory redundancies are the only serious route either.

What we expect is a side-by-side comparison with numbers:
- savings from management restraint,
- savings from overhead and procurement cuts,
- savings from working-time flexibility,
- savings from temporary wage moderation,
- savings from redeployment and qualification,
- savings from voluntary exit and early retirement,
- savings from productivity measures,
- and the remaining residual gap.

If, after that, there is still a gap, then we can discuss **additional internal adjustments**. But we do **not** accept the leap from “gap remains” to “therefore close plants and force layoffs.”

That leap is exactly what we reject.

And let me be clear: if you keep insisting that closures and compulsory redundancies remain ordinary tools, then you are not negotiating in good faith with the workforce. You are asking us to accept that employees bear the structural risk while management preserves maximum freedom. That will trigger resistance, including industrial action if necessary.

## To the family holding
You talk about stewardship, and I’ll acknowledge one thing: you are right that a weak, symbolic deal would be bad for the company.

But stewardship is not served by threatening plants and jobs as leverage. It is served by building a durable industrial base.

So no, I do not accept the idea that closures and forced redundancies must remain “available in principle” as a normal bargaining backdrop. That is exactly what poisons trust and makes a stable settlement harder.

If the family wants to be taken seriously as a responsible owner, then support:
- a binding job-security framework,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- serious investment in German plants,
- and a restructuring based on shared sacrifice rather than punishment.

## To institutional investors
You are right on one point: a deal must be economically serious.

But your position still treats jobs and sites as variables in a valuation model without fully accounting for the cost of conflict. A prolonged strike, reputational damage, supplier disruption, and loss of trust are not side effects; they are major economic risks.

You have still not demonstrated that closures and forced layoffs are the only way to get durable savings. You have shown only that they are the harshest way.

And I will say this plainly: the harshest way is not automatically the best way.

## To the state government
I appreciate that you are pushing for a socially managed restructuring and not for blind closure logic.

But I’ll be equally clear: a “moratorium” or “conditional guarantee” is only useful if it actually protects jobs and sites, not if it merely delays the closure threat.

If the state wants to preserve the industrial base, it should back:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- protection of German sites,
- binding investment commitments,
- and a restructuring that uses internal adjustment tools first.

## On the bridge options
### 1) No forced redundancies first, closures only as last resort and conditional
This is not enough for us as stated.

Why? Because it still normalizes closures as a legitimate endpoint. Once that door is open, management can always claim the threshold has been reached. That is not security. That is managed exposure.

We will not agree to a model where the workforce is protected only until management decides otherwise.

### 2) Site-security package with quantified employee and owner contributions
This is the only direction that is serious — but only if it means **binding security for all German plants** and **no compulsory redundancies**.

We are prepared to discuss:
- temporary working-time changes,
- temporary wage moderation,
- temporary cuts in certain premiums,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary exit options,
- management restraint,
- and measurable owner-side contribution.

That is a real shared-sacrifice package.

What we will not accept is using those concessions as the price of making closures more acceptable later.

### 3) Conditional site guarantees tied to performance and transformation
Rejected in its current logic.

A guarantee that can be withdrawn later is not a guarantee. It is probation. It turns job security into a performance trap, with management defining the metrics and moving the goalposts whenever convenient.

That is not co-determination. It is conditional obedience.

## What would actually move me
Very little unless the other side changes course. But there are two things that can move us:

1. **Binding site and job guarantees through 2030 for all German plants**
2. **A genuine role for labour in shaping the restructuring, not just being informed about it**

Not consultation after the fact. Real co-determination in how the transformation is designed.

If management wants our cooperation, that is where it starts.

## What I will support
I can support a package with:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no German plant closures,
- temporary shared labour contributions,
- internal redeployment and qualification,
- working-time flexibility,
- management and owner restraint,
- binding investment commitments,
- preservation of the collective wage framework.

I will not support:
- open-ended insecurity,
- a permanent wage reset imposed only on employees,
- or any deal that makes the workforce the default adjustment variable.

## Bottom line
Our position remains unchanged:

- **No German plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Preserve the collective wage framework**
- **Temporary, fairly shared contributions are possible**
- **Binding investment commitments are necessary**
- **Management and owners must contribute visibly**

If the other side continues to insist that closures and forced layoffs remain normal tools, then they are saying they prefer power over partnership. We will respond accordingly, including with industrial action if needed.
