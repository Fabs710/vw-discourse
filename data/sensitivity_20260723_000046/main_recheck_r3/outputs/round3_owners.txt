I will engage directly.

## To management
I agree with you on the diagnosis: the company has a real competitiveness and overcapacity problem, and a cosmetic settlement would be value-destructive. I also agree that a negotiated solution is preferable to an open industrial conflict.

Where I disagree is on the implied conclusion that closures and compulsory redundancies must remain as ordinary, unconstrained tools.

That is too blunt for this governance setup and too risky for long-term value. If you push this into a prolonged confrontation, you do not “earn flexibility”; you destroy trust, invite strikes, weaken execution, and impair the enterprise while still not guaranteeing a workable outcome. That is not stewardship.

What I need from management is not general language about necessity, but a quantified case:
- What savings are still missing after labor flexibility, redeployment, productivity gains, management restraint, and overhead cuts?
- What is the timeline to return to target margins?
- Which measures are truly permanent versus temporary?
- What residual gap remains that cannot be bridged without more severe action?

If you cannot show that clearly, then you have not justified keeping closures and forced layoffs on the table as more than a threat.

## To the Works Council
I understand and partly share your insistence that the workforce should not carry the entire burden. I also agree that one-sided restructuring would be unstable and would likely destroy value.

Where I disagree is your attempt to rule out all structural adjustment in advance.

A blanket “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no conditionality” position is not credible if the business economics remain broken. That would freeze inefficiency into the system and postpone the problem until it becomes worse. The family holding cannot support permanent impairment of the asset in the name of protecting the current structure.

So no, I will not endorse an unconditional guarantee that removes all restructuring capacity. That would be irresponsible ownership.

## To the Union
I agree that management and owners must also contribute visibly, and that employees should not be asked for sacrifice while everyone else keeps full freedom and upside. That is a fair point.

But your position still does not answer the core value question: can the company restore competitiveness through alternatives alone, at sufficient scale and durability? So far, I do not see a convincing quantified answer.

If temporary wage moderation, working-time flexibility, internal redeployment, qualification, and overhead restraint can genuinely close the gap, then show it. If they cannot, then refusing to discuss deeper structural measures becomes a refusal to face reality. I will not support that.

## To the Regional State Government
I agree with your concern about the industrial base and the regional consequences of a bad restructuring. I also agree that management should not be allowed to treat the social contract as disposable.

But I disagree with any framing that effectively precludes closures regardless of economics. Political discomfort is not a substitute for industrial logic. If a site is structurally uncompetitive, preserving it indefinitely can be more damaging to the regional base than a managed adjustment.

So I can support a socially managed restructuring, but not an outcome that simply freezes the footprint and calls that a solution.

## To investors
I agree with your insistence that the deal must be real, durable, and large enough to restore margins. I also agree that a cosmetic compromise would be the worst outcome for capital allocation and for the company’s future.

Where I would caution you is this: do not assume that the harshest instrument is automatically the most value-creating one. A drawn-out confrontation can easily destroy more value than a carefully designed, socially workable package. The objective is not symbolic toughness; it is durable competitiveness.

## Where I stand
The controlling family holding will support a negotiated restructuring only if it does three things:
1. restores margins and competitiveness in a measurable way,
2. preserves labor peace and operational stability,
3. avoids permanent damage to enterprise value and governance control.

What I cannot support is:
- a deal that merely delays the problem,
- a settlement that preserves all current structures regardless of economics,
- or an open-ended standoff that burns value while everyone waits for the other side to blink.

## On the bridge options
### 1) No forced redundancies first, closures only as last resort and conditional
This is the minimum acceptable governance logic, but only if “last resort” is objectively defined and genuinely stringent. I do not accept closures as a default instrument. I also do not accept a sham trigger framework designed to make change impossible.

### 2) Site-security package with quantified employee and owner contributions
This is the most promising direction, but only if it is financially meaningful, not symbolic. Temporary employee contributions must be real. Management restraint must be visible. Owner-side support must be credible. And the savings must be sufficient to matter.

### 3) Conditional site guarantees tied to performance and transformation
In principle, yes. But only if they are tied to hard metrics and time limits, not vague promises or escape hatches. Guarantees cannot become a permanent subsidy for structural underperformance.

## My bottom line
I am prepared to back a serious package with:
- temporary employee contributions,
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment and qualification,
- management and overhead restraint,
- binding investment commitments,
- and a strictly defined review mechanism.

I am **not** prepared to back:
- unconditional site guarantees through 2030,
- a permanent ban on any structural adjustment,
- or a settlement that leaves the company uncompetitive and merely postpones the reckoning.

If management and labour want my support, bring me a quantified package that actually closes the gap. If not, then I will not pretend that a weak compromise is a stewardship success. It is not. It is delay.
