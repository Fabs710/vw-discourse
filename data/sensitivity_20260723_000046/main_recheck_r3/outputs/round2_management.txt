Let me respond directly.

I agree with one part of what labour and the state are saying: **this must be durable, not cosmetic**. A short-lived patch that leaves the cost base untouched would be a mistake. And I agree that a purely unilateral approach would be the wrong path in this governance structure.

But I need to be equally clear on the central point: **a package that forbids plant closures and compulsory redundancies in advance does not solve the structural problem**.

## Where I agree
- **A negotiated solution is necessary.**
- **The company needs a future in Germany.**
- **Investment commitments matter.**
- **A one-sided burden on employees would be unstable and unacceptable.**
- **Management and top leadership should also contribute.**

## Where I disagree
### 1) “No closures, no layoffs” cannot be a fixed rule
The works council and union are asking me to rule out the very instruments that may be required to restore competitiveness. I cannot do that.

If every German site is guaranteed regardless of economics, and if compulsory redundancies are off the table no matter what, then we are left with symbolic adjustments only. That would not close the cost gap, would not fix underutilization, and would not restore the brand’s return on sales.

That is not restructuring. That is delay.

### 2) Capacity reduction must be real, not just rearranged
You keep talking about working-time flexibility, redeployment, voluntary programs, and productivity gains. Those can contribute, and I am open to them. But I need to be blunt: **they are unlikely to be sufficient on their own**.

If the company has structural overcapacity, then the capacity has to come out somewhere. If you refuse closures and refuse compulsory redundancies, then show me how you achieve the same permanent savings in a way that is economically equivalent, operationally credible, and durable through 2030.

Not slogans. Numbers.

### 3) “Shared sacrifice” has to produce measurable savings
I hear the call for management restraint and owner contribution. Fine. We can discuss overhead reductions, leadership cost discipline, and deferrals where appropriate. But those measures will not substitute for the core restructuring of the industrial base.

The question is not whether everyone contributes a little. The question is whether the package is large enough to make the business competitive again.

## On the quantified options
You asked for numbers and concrete alternatives. Good — that is the right discussion.

What I need to see from the employee side is a package that credibly answers:
- How much labour-cost relief is delivered?
- For how long?
- What capacity reduction is achieved in practice?
- What utilization improvement follows?
- How does the company regain margin and fund future investment?

If the answer is only temporary wage moderation plus voluntary measures, that is not enough.

## On site guarantees
I cannot agree to unconditional site guarantees through 2030 for every German plant. That would remove any ability to adapt if a site remains structurally uncompetitive. We need **conditional commitments**, not blank cheques:
- sites that are competitive and contribute to the transformation can receive investment and security;
- sites that do not deliver must be part of the adjustment.

That is the only responsible way to manage the business.

## On compulsory redundancies
I am prepared to discuss alternatives to forced layoffs as a first preference:
- voluntary severance,
- internal redeployment,
- early retirement,
- working-time measures,
- qualification and transfer programs.

But I will **not** accept a blanket ban that permanently removes redundancy as a management tool. If the numbers require it, that option has to remain available.

## On labour and industrial action
Let me also say this plainly: prolonged strikes will damage output, reputation, and trust — and they will not change the economic reality. We are not going to be intimidated into a weak agreement. If the response to a structural cost problem is industrial confrontation instead of solutions, that will only accelerate the need for harder measures.

## My current position
I am still open to a negotiated package, but only if it contains:
1. **material, durable capacity reduction**
2. **measurable labour-cost relief**
3. **credible site-level changes where economics require them**
4. **investment commitments tied to competitiveness and performance**
5. **a route to restore margins by 2030**

What I cannot support is:
- a deal that preserves all structures unchanged,
- a blanket prohibition on closures and layoffs,
- or a compromise that postpones the crisis and weakens investor confidence.

If the workforce side wants me to move, bring a package that actually closes the competitiveness gap. Otherwise, I will continue to insist that all structural options remain on the table.
