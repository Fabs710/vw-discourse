Let me respond plainly, because we are now going in circles.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on one fundamental point: **this company needs a durable restructuring, not a cosmetic patch**.

I also agree that:
- a negotiated solution is preferable to a unilateral escalation,
- investment in future products and technology is essential,
- management and top leadership must contribute,
- and a one-sided burden on employees would not be stable.

So yes: there is common ground on the need for change.

## Where I disagree — and this is the core issue
The labour side keeps repeating: **no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no weakening of guarantees**.

I have to say clearly: **that is not a restructuring plan**. It is a constraint that may make real restructuring impossible.

You are asking management to restore competitiveness, reduce overcapacity, improve margins, and free funds for the next product cycle — while ruling out the very instruments that can permanently remove excess cost and capacity. That does not add up.

### 1) The social contract does not override economic reality
I understand the significance of job security and the German industrial footprint. But if the company remains structurally too expensive, then preserving every current structure unchanged simply transfers the problem into the future. That would eventually put even more jobs and investment at risk.

We are not talking about “preferences.” We are talking about whether the business can compete through 2030.

### 2) Alternative measures may help — but they are not proven equivalent
You both list:
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary wage moderation,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- management restraint,
- procurement and overhead cuts,
- productivity improvements.

Fine. I am not dismissing those. They belong in the package.

But I have still not heard a credible, quantified case that these measures alone can deliver the **permanent** savings and capacity reduction the business needs. Temporary relief is not enough if the cost base remains structurally uncompetitive.

### 3) “No closures ever” is too rigid
The works council and union want to rule out plant closures entirely. I cannot accept that.

If a site is structurally uncompetitive, chronically underutilized, and unable to contribute to the transformation, then management must retain the ability to act. A blanket prohibition would lock in inefficiency and undermine the entire restructuring.

That does not mean closures are our first choice. It means they cannot be excluded in advance.

### 4) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be an absolute permanent veto
I hear your refusal of forced layoffs. It is understandable. But from a management and owner perspective, a permanent and absolute ban is not responsible if the numbers do not work otherwise.

We prefer voluntary instruments first:
- voluntary severance,
- early retirement,
- internal redeployment,
- transfer and qualification programs,
- working-time solutions.

But I will not accept a permanent management handcuff that removes redundancy as a last-resort tool. If the company needs structural adjustment, that option has to remain available.

## To the works council
You keep demanding binding site guarantees through 2030 for all German plants. I cannot support unconditional guarantees across the board.

Why? Because then we would be promising security even for sites that may remain structurally uncompetitive. That is not stewardship; that is postponement.

If you want security, then it must be linked to:
- competitiveness,
- productivity,
- transformation milestones,
- and investment delivery.

A blanket guarantee without conditions would not be credible to owners, investors, or the market.

## To the union
You are right that labour should not carry the full burden. I agree.

But you cannot on the one hand demand shared sacrifice and on the other hand preclude the tools that may be necessary if the shared sacrifice still falls short. That is inconsistent.

If you want me to lean harder toward non-closure measures, then I need a package with real numbers:
- how much savings from wage moderation,
- how much from working-time changes,
- how much from overhead cuts,
- how much from productivity gains,
- and how much from capacity reduction achieved without closures.

Not principles. Numbers.

## To the state government
I respect your concern about the regional industrial base. But “preserve the footprint” is not a solution if the footprint remains uncompetitive.

The state can help shape a socially managed transition, but it cannot legislate away the cost problem. If you want a viable German industrial base in 2030, then the company must be allowed to adapt materially.

## To the family shareholder and investors
I agree with you that a cosmetic deal is worthless. We need something durable and economically real.

But I also need to be clear: if the employee side insists on ruling out closures and forced redundancies entirely, then we are left with insufficient tools. In that case, the risk is not just conflict — it is prolonged decline.

## On the facilitator’s bridge options
### 1) “No forced redundancies first, closures only as last resort and conditional”
This is closer to reality than a total ban, but only if the trigger framework is genuinely objective and not designed to make restructuring impossible. I can work with conditionality. I cannot work with a disguised veto.

### 2) “Site-security package with quantified employee and owner contributions”
This is the most promising direction, but only if the savings are large enough and the commitments are credible. Temporary employee contributions must be meaningful, not symbolic. Owner and management restraint must be visible, not cosmetic.

### 3) “Conditional site guarantees tied to performance and transformation”
This is acceptable in principle. It is the only way to reconcile social protection with economic responsibility. Guarantees must be earned through performance and investment, not granted unconditionally regardless of business reality.

## What I am prepared to support
I am prepared to support a package with:
- material temporary employee contributions,
- working-time flexibility,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and transfer,
- management and overhead restraint,
- targeted investment commitments,
- and a serious effort to reduce capacity without unnecessary disruption.

## What I am not prepared to support
I will not support:
- a blanket ban on plant closures,
- a permanent prohibition on compulsory redundancies,
- unconditional site guarantees through 2030,
- or any settlement that preserves the current cost base and calls that competitiveness.

## My bottom line
I am still open to a negotiated solution. But it must be one that:
1. **delivers durable, measurable cost reduction**
2. **reduces structural overcapacity**
3. **restores margins**
4. **protects the company’s investment capacity**
5. **preserves Germany as an industrial base by making it viable, not by freezing it in place**

If the employee side wants me to move further away from closures and forced redundancies, then bring a quantified package that actually closes the gap.

Until then, I will continue to say the same thing: **all structural options remain on the table, because the economics require it.**
