I will be direct.

The state shareholder recognizes the competitive pressure management is describing. I agree that the company needs a durable restructuring, not a cosmetic patch. I also agree that any solution must be economically credible and must support future investment in technology and products.

But I **do not agree** with the repeated framing that German plant closures and compulsory redundancies must remain on the table as ordinary tools. In this company, and in this region, that is not a neutral technical choice. It would be a major break in trust, with severe consequences for employment, suppliers, and the industrial base.

### Where I agree
- The company has a real cost and capacity problem.
- A negotiated solution is preferable to open conflict.
- Temporary employee contributions, working-time flexibility, redeployment, qualification, and targeted productivity measures are legitimate parts of the toolbox.
- Management and owners should also contribute visibly.

### Where I disagree
#### To management
You have still not demonstrated that closures are the only way to achieve the required savings. “Keep all structural options open” is not the same as proving they are needed. In particular:

- **I cannot support a closure-first logic.**
- **I cannot support forcing the workforce into a position where jobs and sites are treated as expendable from the outset.**
- **I cannot support unconditional site insecurity as the basis of a settlement.**

If you want state support, you need to show a package that is **credible without breaking the regional industrial footprint**. That means quantified alternatives, not just statements of necessity.

#### To the Works Council and Union
I support your insistence that the burden cannot fall only on employees. That is legitimate, and I share your rejection of a one-sided demolition of job security.

But I also need to be honest: a hard “no” to every structural adjustment is not a workable answer if the company’s economics do not improve. The state will not sign up to a solution that simply defers the problem and leaves the company weaker in 12 months. So I am asking you to remain open to **real** capacity and productivity measures — but only on the condition that they are paired with binding commitments and social partnership.

### What the state can support
We can support a package that contains:
- real but socially managed capacity reduction,
- working-time flexibility,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and transfer programs,
- temporary and fairly shared labour-cost relief,
- management restraint and top-level contribution,
- binding investment commitments for German sites,
- and strong protection of co-determination.

### What we cannot support
We cannot support:
- plant closures as the default answer,
- mass layoffs imposed on the workforce,
- a restructuring that hollows out the regional economy,
- or any outcome that sidelines the state’s co-determination and veto role.

That limit is clear.

### On quantified options
If management wants seriousness, then put numbers on the alternatives:
- How much saving comes from flexible working time?
- How much from voluntary instruments and redeployment?
- How much from overhead and management cost cuts?
- What investment volume is committed to German production?
- What capacity reduction can be achieved **without** closures?

If the answer is that only plant shutdowns can deliver savings, then I would want to see a very strict justification — because the social and regional costs would be enormous, and I am not prepared to treat those costs as secondary.

### My position going into the next round
The state shareholder is prepared to back a **negotiated restructuring that preserves sites and jobs as far as possible while restoring competitiveness**. But I will not move to an outcome that guts regional employment or makes closures the starting point.

If management and the capital side want my support, they need to come back with a package that:
1. keeps the German industrial base intact wherever possible,
2. shares the burden fairly,
3. protects co-determination,
4. and is durable enough to actually restore competitiveness.

Absent that, they should not expect the state to wave through an outcome that is economically shallow and politically destructive.
