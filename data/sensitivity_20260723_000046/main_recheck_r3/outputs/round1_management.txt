We hear the concern about trust and the workforce’s burden. Let me be just as clear in return: **a cosmetic restructuring that preserves the current cost base is not an option**.

The company is facing a structural competitiveness problem, not a temporary dip. German manufacturing costs are too high, the European market is shrinking, and we are missing our return-on-sales target. If we do not reduce overcapacity and labour costs in a durable way, we will not have the resources to invest in the next product cycle, software, batteries, and technology to 2030.

So:

- **I agree** that this must be a negotiated solution, not a unilateral diktat.
- **I agree** that the company needs a future in Germany and that investment commitments matter.
- **I disagree** that every site can be guaranteed regardless of economics.
- **I disagree** that compulsory redundancies and closures can be ruled out in advance.

Those red lines remove the very tools needed to make the business viable. If the workforce side comes to the table with alternatives that deliver **comparable, permanent savings and capacity reduction**, I am open to them. But they must be credible, measurable, and sufficient.

What I need to see is:

1. **A structural reduction in capacity**
2. **A genuine labour-cost contribution**
3. **A plan that improves margins durably, not just for one budget year**
4. **Freedom to make site-level changes where economics require it**

If the answer is only “no closures, no layoffs, no guarantees are revisable,” then we are simply postponing the crisis.

I am prepared to discuss:
- working-time flexibility
- wage moderation or temporary labour-cost relief
- internal redeployment and qualification
- targeted investment commitments where sites are competitive

But I will not pretend that this can work without meaningful capacity adjustment. The business has to be fit for 2030, not merely protected in its current form.
