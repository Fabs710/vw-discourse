Let me respond directly, because we are still circling the same issue.

## Where I agree
I agree with all parties on three points:

1. **This company needs a durable restructuring.**  
   No one in this room has credibly argued that the current cost base is sustainable.

2. **Management and leadership must contribute.**  
   I accept that top-level restraint, overhead cuts, and a visible management contribution are part of the answer.

3. **A negotiated solution is preferable to uncontrolled conflict.**  
   Prolonged strikes, reputational damage, and supplier disruption would hurt everyone, including us.

So yes, there is common ground on the diagnosis and on the need for shared sacrifice.

## Where I disagree — and this is the core
The labour side keeps repeating: **no closures, no compulsory redundancies, all German sites protected, no conditionality.**

I have to say plainly: **that is not a restructuring plan**. It is a refusal to engage with the economic reality.

We are not discussing a marginal improvement exercise. We are dealing with:
- a structurally uncompetitive cost base,
- overcapacity in Germany,
- a shrinking European market,
- and a brand that is still missing its return-on-sales target.

If we do not remove structural cost and capacity, we do not solve the problem. We postpone it — and probably make it worse.

## On the numbers
Both the Works Council and the Union keep asking us to prove that closures or compulsory redundancies are necessary. Fair enough. But you still have not shown that the alternatives are sufficient either.

So here is the actual challenge:

- How much durable savings comes from management restraint?
- How much from overhead and procurement cuts?
- How much from temporary wage moderation?
- How much from working-time flexibility?
- How much from redeployment and qualification?
- How much from voluntary exit and early retirement?
- How much from productivity measures?

And then: **what residual gap remains?**

That residual gap is the real issue. If it is meaningful, then we cannot rule out structural tools in advance. If you cannot close the gap with alternatives, then your “no closures, no layoffs” position is simply a veto on restructuring.

## To the Works Council
You say closures normalize job destruction and break trust. I understand that concern. But trust is already damaged if the company remains uncompetitive and slowly loses investment capacity.

I do **not** accept:
- a blanket ban on closures,
- a permanent prohibition on compulsory redundancies,
- or unconditional site guarantees through 2030.

Why? Because that would lock in the current structure regardless of whether it works. That is not stewardship; that is freezing the problem in place.

If you want me to move further toward site security, then you need to engage with **conditionality**:
- measurable productivity gains,
- transformation milestones,
- investment delivery,
- and objective triggers.

A guarantee with no conditions is not a solution if the business underperforms. It becomes a permanent cost overhang.

## To the Industrial Union
You say the alternatives have not been disproven. That is true. But the reverse is also true: you have not proven they are enough.

I appreciate that you are prepared to discuss temporary labour measures, productivity improvements, and shared sacrifice. That is useful.

But temporary measures are not enough if the company needs **durable** structural savings. Temporary wage moderation or working-time changes do not automatically solve overcapacity. They may buy time; they do not necessarily fix the business.

So I am prepared to discuss:
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary labour-cost relief,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary exit,
- and management restraint.

I am **not** prepared to accept these as automatic substitutes for all structural change.

## To the family holding
I agree with you that a cosmetic deal would destroy value. You are right to insist on seriousness.

Where I disagree is your caution against using closures and forced redundancies as tools at all. In this governance setup, it is not credible to remove them from the table before we know whether the alternatives actually work.

If we end up with a package that materially closes the savings gap, fine — that is exactly what I want. But if we do not, then it would be irresponsible to forbid the company from acting.

## To the state government
You are right to stress the industrial base and the regional consequences. I take that seriously.

But I will be blunt: preserving the footprint is not the same as preserving competitiveness. If the German footprint remains structurally too expensive, then the company eventually loses room to invest everywhere — including in Germany.

I can support a socially managed restructuring. I cannot support a settlement that freezes the cost structure and calls that regional responsibility.

## On the bridge options
### 1) No forced redundancies first, closures only as last resort and conditional
This is the closest to a workable framework, but only if “last resort” is real and objective.

I am prepared to suspend compulsory redundancies for a defined period **if** we have:
- measurable savings,
- clear productivity commitments,
- binding implementation steps,
- and a jointly defined trigger process if targets are missed.

What I will not accept is a disguised veto that makes restructuring impossible.

### 2) Site-security package with quantified employee and owner contributions
This is the most promising route, but only if the contributions are meaningful and the package is large enough to matter.

That means:
- temporary employee contributions,
- management restraint,
- overhead reductions,
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary exit instruments,
- and binding investment commitments.

If the combined result closes the gap, then we have a viable alternative to hard closures. If it does not, then it is just delay.

### 3) Conditional site guarantees tied to performance and transformation
In principle, yes — but only if the conditions are objective, transparent, and enforceable.

I will not support open-ended promises that cannot be monitored. I will not support unconditional guarantees that eliminate management’s ability to respond if sites miss agreed targets.

## What I can support
I can support a package with:
- temporary labour-cost relief,
- working-time flexibility,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and transfer,
- management and overhead restraint,
- targeted investment commitments,
- and a structured review process.

## What I cannot support
I cannot support:
- a blanket ban on plant closures,
- a permanent ban on compulsory redundancies,
- unconditional site guarantees through 2030,
- or a settlement that leaves the company uncompetitive.

## Bottom line
I am still open to a negotiated solution. But it must do all of the following:

1. deliver **durable and measurable cost reduction**
2. reduce **structural overcapacity**
3. restore **medium-term margins**
4. protect the company’s **investment capacity to 2030**
5. preserve the German industrial base by making it **viable**, not by freezing it

If the employee side wants me to move further away from closures and compulsory redundancies, then bring a quantified package that actually closes the gap.

Until then, I will continue to say the same thing: **all structural options remain on the table, because the economics require it.**
