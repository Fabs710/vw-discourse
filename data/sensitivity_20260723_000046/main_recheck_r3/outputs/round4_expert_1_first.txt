Yes — in industrial-economics terms, **non-dismissal measures can often close a meaningful part of a German OEM’s cost gap, but they rarely close a large structural gap on their own unless the gap is moderate and the company is already close to best-in-class productivity**.

## Bottom line
For a large German carmaker with:
- high German labor costs,
- overcapacity,
- weak European demand,
- EV transition investment burden,
- and margin below target,

**non-dismissal measures alone are usually capable of closing perhaps 30–60% of the residual gap**, depending on how aggressively they are executed.  
If the remaining gap is still material after that, **plant closures, product reassignment, or headcount reduction typically become necessary** to restore competitive economics.

## What non-dismissal measures can realistically deliver
Below are realistic, order-of-magnitude ranges for a large German passenger-car operation.

### 1) Working-time flexibility / shorter hours / shift redesign
- Savings: **low to mid single-digit % of direct labor cost**
- More important for: matching output to demand and avoiding overtime/underutilization
- Typical effect on total manufacturing cost: **~1–2 percentage points**

This helps, but it does not remove structural overcapacity.

### 2) Temporary wage moderation / bonus suspension / premium reduction
- Savings: **~1–3% of total labor cost**, sometimes more in a crisis settlement
- On total unit cost: usually **sub-1 to 1.5 percentage points**

This is real money, but temporary unless embedded in a longer settlement. It is not a permanent competitiveness fix unless base pay and job architecture are also changed.

### 3) Management and overhead cuts
- SG&A / administrative overhead reductions: **~5–15%** is often achievable in a serious restructuring
- On total company cost base: often **~1–3 percentage points**
- If the company is bloated in functions, this can be material

However, overhead cuts alone rarely solve a plant-level cost problem.

### 4) Procurement savings / simplification / supplier renegotiation
- In automotive, this can be large if sourcing is fragmented or complexity is high
- Realistic durable savings: **~1–3% of COGS** in a focused program
- On total unit economics: often **~1–2 percentage points**

This is one of the strongest non-dismissal levers, but it depends on platform simplification and disciplined specification control.

### 5) Internal redeployment / qualification / attrition
- This can avoid forced layoffs and reduce payroll through natural attrition
- Savings depend on vacancy discipline and retirement profile
- Usually a **slow-burn measure**, not a fast fix

Useful for social manageability; weaker as a rapid cost-gap closer.

### 6) Voluntary exit / early retirement / severance
- Can reduce labor cost materially
- But the cost is front-loaded
- Often essential if the workforce is oversized

This is not a “non-cost” solution; it is a restructuring tool that avoids compulsory redundancy but still reduces headcount.

## What non-dismissal measures usually cannot do
They generally **cannot fully offset**:
- a structurally expensive German labor model,
- low plant utilization,
- duplicated capacity across multiple sites,
- and product line under-absorption.

That is because:
- fixed costs remain fixed,
- overhead absorption worsens when volumes fall,
- and temporary labor concessions do not eliminate permanently excess capacity.

## Competitiveness benchmark
Relative to peer automakers and industrial comparators:

### German OEM vs.
- **French / Southern European plants**: German sites are often structurally more expensive on labor and overhead.
- **Central/Eastern European plants**: German sites can be far more expensive, especially in assembly and labor-intensive subassemblies.
- **Japanese benchmark**: typically superior on lean labor productivity, fewer variants, tighter standardization.
- **US transplants**: can be competitive if scale and simplicity are high, but usually not as complex as legacy German OEM footprints.
- **Chinese manufacturers**: materially lower labor and often lower battery/electronics system cost, plus faster industrial learning curves.

For a German premium OEM, being competitive **does not require matching Chinese labor cost**, but it **does require**:
- high labor productivity,
- high capacity utilization,
- low complexity,
- strong automation where justified,
- and lean overhead.

If any of those are missing, the cost gap can become too large for labor concessions alone to close.

## Practical estimate of the residual gap
If management claims a “large, durable reduction” is needed, the residual gap after non-dismissal measures is likely still in the range of:

- **~2–5 percentage points of revenue** in a stressed premium-car operation, or
- **~€1,000–€3,000 per vehicle** depending on volume, mix, and plant utilization.

That is a broad range, but it reflects how much overcapacity and labor-cost disadvantage can matter in German automotive manufacturing.

If the residual gap is at the **lower end**, a no-closure package may work.  
If it is at the **upper end**, it becomes very difficult to close without:
- capacity reduction,
- site rationalization,
- or permanent workforce reduction.

## What would need to be true for non-dismissal measures to be enough
A no-closure / no-forced-redundancy package could be economically credible if all of the following are true:

1. **The cost gap is moderate, not severe**
2. **Plant utilization can be raised materially**
3. **There is excess overhead that can be removed**
4. **Product complexity can be simplified**
5. **Employee concessions are sizable and durable**
6. **Supplier and footprint rationalization can occur without closures**
7. **Investment commitment is tied to measurable productivity gains**

If those conditions are not present, then the package is likely insufficient.

## My assessment of the negotiation logic
The labour side is right that **closures and layoffs are not the only conceivable instruments**.  
Management and capital are right that **non-dismissal measures are often not equivalent** to structural capacity reduction.

So the correct industrial-economic position is:

- **Try non-dismissal measures first**
- **Quantify the residual gap**
- **If the residual gap remains material, do not pretend that labor concessions alone solve it**

In a serious restructuring, the key question is not ideological. It is arithmetic:
**Does the package reduce unit cost enough to restore margin and fund future investment?**

**Non-dismissal measures can close part of the gap, sometimes a substantial part, but they are unlikely to fully close a large structural gap at a high-cost German OEM unless the gap is already moderate and the footprint is close to right-sized.**
