## 1. Decision context
The company faces a genuine competitiveness and overcapacity problem in Germany, with management seeking a durable restructuring to restore margins and fund future investment. The live issue is not whether restructuring is needed, but whether it can be achieved primarily through non-dismissal measures or whether structural tools such as plant closures and compulsory redundancies must remain available as fallback options.

Governance makes this unusually hard. Co-determination gives labour an effective veto, the family holding and state shareholder have blocking power, and institutional investors can pressure but not impose. The interaction revealed that the bargaining problem is less about diagnosis than about proof, sequencing, and credibility of fallback mechanisms.

## 2. Stakeholder assembly
- **Group & Brand Management**: highest operational standing; driving the restructuring case.
- **Controlling Family Holding**: very high governance standing; wants competitiveness restored without value destruction.
- **Regional State Government**: high standing; political and shareholder actor with blocking leverage.
- **Works Council**: high legitimacy and formal power via co-determination; rejects closures/forced layoffs.
- **Industrial Union**: high bargaining power over labour peace and strikes; aligned with works council on core red lines.
- **Institutional Investors**: moderate-to-high financial influence, but weaker formal governance power; support economically credible restructuring.

## 3. Final positions
### Group & Brand Management
Management insists the company needs durable structural savings and cannot pre-commit to ruling out closures or compulsory redundancies. It is open to labour flexibility, overhead cuts, redeployment, and investment commitments, but says these may not close the residual gap.

### Works Council
The works council rejects plant closures, compulsory redundancies, and conditional site security. It supports shared sacrifice, management restraint, and investment commitments, but only under binding site and job guarantees through 2030 and real co-determination in restructuring design.

### Industrial Union
The union aligns closely with the works council: no compulsory redundancies, no one-sided burden on workers, and no “cosmetic” deal. It is willing to discuss temporary labour measures, productivity improvements, and voluntary exits, but only within a shared-sacrifice package that preserves collective bargaining protections.

### Regional State Government
The state wants restructuring without hollowing out the regional industrial base and opposes closures as a first or default instrument. It favors a time-limited, socially managed compromise with investment commitments and strong job protections, while acknowledging the need for durable savings.

### Controlling Family Holding
The family holding wants a serious, value-preserving restructuring and does not accept blanket protection of all German sites regardless of economics. It is open to a moratorium and non-dismissal measures first, but wants structural tools available if the residual gap remains material.

### Institutional Investors
Investors support a negotiated restructuring only if it materially and durably restores margins and capacity balance. They reject a blanket no-closure/no-layoff stance unless it can be shown, with numbers, to deliver equivalent permanent savings.

## 4. Genuine conflicts (structurally incompatible positions)
1. **Absolute site/job guarantees vs. retained restructuring flexibility**  
   Labour wants all German sites protected and no compulsory redundancies; management, family, and investors insist structural tools must remain available if needed.

2. **Conditionality vs. certainty**  
   Labour sees conditional guarantees as disguised threats; capital-side actors see unconditional guarantees as economically non-credible.

3. **Timing of fallback measures**  
   Labour wants closures and layoffs excluded unless all alternatives are proven exhausted; management and capital want the option available before a guaranteed proof threshold becomes an effective veto.

4. **Definition of shared sacrifice**  
   Labour sees concessions only as part of a protected, negotiated framework; management and investors see them as potentially insufficient unless they materially close the gap.

## 5. Areas of convergence
- All parties accept the company has a real competitiveness and cost-base problem.
- All parties prefer a negotiated solution to open conflict.
- All parties accept management/top leadership must contribute visibly.
- All parties agree investment in future products and German production is necessary.
- All parties now accept that non-dismissal measures may close a substantial part of the gap, even if not necessarily all of it.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker
- **Job and site security vs. structural competitiveness**
- **Immediate labour peace vs. preservation of management flexibility**
- **Political legitimacy and co-determination stability vs. margin restoration**
- **Temporary sacrifices now vs. permanent workforce reduction later**
- **A binding no-closure commitment vs. a staged process with verifiable fallback triggers**

## 7. Integrative pathways
### Pathway A: Time-limited non-dismissal first, with independent verification
Implement management restraint, overhead cuts, procurement savings, working-time flexibility, temporary labour relief, redeployment, qualification, and voluntary exits first. Then use an independent audit to verify achieved savings and the residual gap before any structural tools are considered.

### Pathway B: Conditional site-security pact with hard moratorium during the test phase
Agree no closures and no compulsory redundancies during a defined test period, in exchange for measurable labour contributions, management restraint, owner-side support, and binding investment commitments. After the test phase, a jointly governed second-stage review addresses any remaining gap.

### Pathway C: Two-tier agreement
Protect a core set of German sites with binding guarantees, while creating a separate, jointly governed fallback process for the remaining footprint. This may preserve political and labour legitimacy while leaving a controlled path for further adjustment.

## 8. Open questions
- What is the exact residual savings gap after all non-dismissal measures?
- What savings are durable versus temporary?
- Which sites, if any, are genuinely at issue?
- What objective trigger would justify moving to fallback structural tools?
- What investment commitments can be credibly bound through 2030?
- How much owner-side financial support is on offer?
- What level of labour contribution is sufficient to make the package economically credible?

## 9. Recommended next step
Commission a joint, independent industrial-cost and restructuring audit to quantify the residual gap after non-dismissal measures, plant by plant and function by function. Use that audit as the basis for a time-limited restructuring pact that includes a no-dismissal test phase, quantified employee and owner contributions, and a pre-agreed second-stage review mechanism.

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction
- The real conflict is not over whether restructuring is needed, but over **whether management must prove the necessity of closures before retaining them as options**, or whether retaining them in principle is itself essential governance discipline.
- Labour’s position hardened into a clear distinction between **temporary shared sacrifice** and **structural threat**: it will accept the first but not the second.
- Management, investors, and the family converged on a stronger view that **non-dismissal measures may be substantial but are unlikely to fully close a large structural gap**.
- The expert input changed the discussion from ideology to arithmetic: non-dismissal measures may close **a meaningful share** of the gap, but not necessarily all of it, making sequencing and trigger design the decisive issues.
- The state’s role emerged as more than symbolic: it is effectively a **governance broker** seeking a politically defensible compromise that preserves the regional base without freezing the company’s economics.
- A key hidden issue is **trust in conditionality**: labour interprets conditional site guarantees as latent closure threats, while capital interprets unconditional guarantees as a disguised veto. This makes the design of verification and review mechanisms central, not peripheral.
