From the capital-markets side, our position is straightforward: **we support restructuring only if it credibly restores competitiveness and margin performance**. We are not attached to any particular method. Plant closures, capacity cuts, wage moderation, redeployment, or working-time changes are all tools — but the outcome must be real, durable, and economically sufficient.

### Where we agree
- **With management:** the cost base is too high, overcapacity is real, and the current return-on-sales profile is not acceptable.
- **With the family shareholder:** a cosmetic deal is worthless. The market will not reward delay, and prolonged conflict destroys value.
- **With the state:** preserving long-term industrial capability matters, but only if the business remains viable.
- **With labour:** a one-sided solution imposed only on workers is unlikely to be stable and could be value-destructive.

### Where we disagree
#### To the Works Council and Union
Your “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” line is understandable politically, but from an investor perspective it is **not sufficient** unless you can show an alternative that delivers **equivalent structural savings**. If that line means the company is left with an uncompetitive cost base, then we would regard that as unacceptable.

We are not asking for symbolic sacrifice. We are asking for:
- **measurable capacity reduction**
- **durable labour-cost relief**
- **credible productivity gains**
- **a realistic path back to target margins**

If those cannot be achieved without closures, then closures cannot be ruled out simply for principle’s sake. Likewise, if job protections block the necessary adjustment, that becomes a shareholder-value problem.

#### To Management
We support decisiveness, but not open-ended confrontation. A prolonged strike, legal standoff, or half-baked restructuring would be bad for earnings, bad for valuation, and bad for investor confidence. If you want the market to back you, the package must be:
- **specific**
- **costed**
- **implementable**
- **timed**
- and **large enough to matter**

“Freedom to make site-level changes” is not enough unless you can show the savings will actually land.

### What we need to see
Our support depends on a package that clearly answers:
1. **How much cost is removed, and by when?**
2. **What capacity reduction occurs, and what is the resulting utilization improvement?**
3. **How durable are the labour-cost measures?**
4. **How is the investment plan funded once margins improve?**
5. **What is the downside if negotiations fail — and how quickly does management act?**

### Bottom line
We are **not** aligned with any outcome that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive or margins impaired. A deal that merely preserves all sites and all guarantees while pushing the economics into the future would not be supportable.

But we are also not cheering for disruption for its own sake. If a negotiated package can deliver **credible, durable savings** and put the company back on a competitive trajectory, we would support it. If not, we would be inclined to press for harder structural action and would reassess our capital allocation accordingly.
