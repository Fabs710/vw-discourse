Let me respond directly, because we are now repeating one central move from labour and the state: **treating “no closures” as a principle, and then asking management to somehow make the economics disappear.** That is not a restructuring strategy.

## First, what I agree with
I agree on three things:
- **A cosmetic deal is worthless.**
- **Temporary concessions alone are not enough.**
- **Investment and future product allocation must be part of the package.**

I also agree that a deal which detonates industrial peace without solving the cost problem would destroy value. That is exactly why I am not asking for theatre. I am asking for **durable structural change**.

## Where I disagree
I disagree with the central labour-side claim that:
> “No plant closures” plus voluntary exits, working-time changes, and limited employee contributions can be assumed to be enough.

The expert views were not ambiguous on this point:
- the company likely needs **material, durable capacity reduction**;
- a **no-closure package is the weakest option economically** unless the needed savings are modest;
- and if the footprint is genuinely overbuilt, then **one or more plants must be closed, repurposed, or dramatically downsized**.

That is the economics. Not a management preference. Not a rhetorical choice.

## To the Works Council
You say we have not proven that closures are necessary. I disagree.

We have already established the key facts:
- German factory costs are far above target;
- the European market is shrinking;
- competition is intense, including from lower-cost and Chinese producers;
- the brand is missing its return-on-sales target;
- and the company needs funds for future products and technology.

Given that backdrop, your proposal is not a restructuring package. It is essentially:
- keep all German plants open,
- avoid compulsory redundancies,
- preserve the job-security framework through 2030,
- and hope that voluntary measures and flexibility somehow close a structural cost gap.

That does not match the scale of the problem.

You keep saying closures are only a “management shortcut.” No. In the current situation, closures are a **structural lever**. If one plant is chronically underutilised or subscale, keeping it open for symbolic reasons is not stewardship. It is cost preservation.

I am not saying every plant is doomed. I am saying **we cannot start from an absolute ban on closures**. That would pre-decide the economics against us.

## To the Union
You are right that permanent wage dumping is unacceptable. I am not asking for that.

But I reject the idea that “protecting the collective bargaining framework” means protecting every cost item and every guarantee regardless of market reality. If the current framework blocks durable competitiveness, then yes, it must change.

Your menu of measures:
- temporary contribution,
- working-time flexibility,
- bonus restraint,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- productivity improvements,

is not wrong as far as it goes. But it still does **not** answer the central question:
**how do we remove enough fixed cost and installed capacity to restore margins by 2030?**

If you cannot answer that without closures, then your package is incomplete.

And I will be blunt: a settlement that keeps the current footprint but calls it “shared sacrifice” is not enough if the savings are too small or too temporary to matter.

## To the Regional State Government
You asked for plant-by-plant scenarios. That is reasonable, and I welcome it. But I need to be equally direct: **the burden of proof cannot be one-sided**.

If the state wants to keep every German plant open, then it must accept that the company still has to hit:
- lower fixed costs,
- lower labour costs,
- and better utilisation.

That means either:
1. a no-closure package that delivers savings comparable to closure-based restructuring, or
2. acceptance that some sites cannot remain in their current form.

The state cannot simultaneously insist on no closures and refuse the economic consequences of that position.

You say closures are not the automatic answer. Fine. I never said automatic. But they are very much a legitimate answer when the economics require them.

## To the Controlling Family
I agree with you more than the others on one point: **a deal that is politically neat but economically weak is not stewardship**.

You are right that closures can trigger political and industrial damage. I do not dismiss that. But I also need you to recognize the other side of the equation: a settlement that preserves too much of the current structure may simply postpone the crisis and make the eventual adjustment harsher.

So I need the ownership side to be clear:
- if a site is not viable, will you support closure?
- or will you insist on preserving every site regardless of economics?

That is not a rhetorical question. It determines whether the group can actually be turned around.

## To Investors
You are right to insist on durable savings and deal certainty. I share that.

What I do not accept is the implication that any non-closure plan is automatically better if it is politically easier to sell. The investor test must be economic reality:
- Does it materially reduce fixed costs?
- Does it materially reduce capacity?
- Does it improve utilisation?
- Does it restore margins by 2030?

If the answer is no, then it is not a good settlement no matter how peaceable it sounds.

## My position after this round
I will be very clear.

I am **not** moving off these core points:
- **real structural capacity reduction is required**
- **labour-cost relief must be material and durable**
- **temporary or cosmetic measures are not enough**
- **closures remain on the table**
- **compulsory redundancies cannot be ruled out in principle if they become unavoidable to secure the restructuring**

I am open on the **mechanism**:
- closures,
- consolidation,
- line shutdowns,
- shift reductions,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- flexibility,
- and negotiated employee contributions.

But I am **not** open to an outcome that leaves the cost base uncompetitive.

## What would move me
I would shift only if the labour/state side could show a package that credibly delivers:
1. **the same or near-same recurring annual savings as a closure-based package**
2. **durable capacity reduction, not just temporary relief**
3. **a lower fixed-cost base through 2030**
4. **a credible path to return on sales**
5. **enough certainty that the deal will actually hold**

If that can be shown, I will look at it seriously.

If not, then the opposition to closures is not a solution. It is a refusal to confront the scale of the problem.

## My direct ask
I want the next round to focus on numbers, not slogans:
- plant-by-plant economics,
- recurring annual savings,
- utilisation impact,
- implementation risk,
- and what combination of measures can actually restore competitiveness.

If the no-closure camp cannot produce a package that matches the economics, then we should stop pretending it is equivalent to real restructuring.

That is where I stand.
