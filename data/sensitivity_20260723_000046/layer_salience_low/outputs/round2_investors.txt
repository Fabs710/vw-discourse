The expert input is useful, and it sharpens rather than softens our view.

## Where we agree
We agree with management, the family, the state, and the expert on the core diagnosis:

- the German cost base is structurally too high;
- the current footprint likely contains real overcapacity;
- temporary concessions alone will not restore competitiveness;
- the company needs durable savings, not a cosmetic truce;
- and deal certainty matters because prolonged conflict destroys value.

That is exactly why we have been saying we cannot support a settlement that merely preserves the current structure.

## Where we disagree
We do **not** agree with the labour side’s attempt to make **“no closures”** the default outcome and then treat everything else as negotiable.

The industrial economics expert is pretty clear: if the company is genuinely overbuilt for the market, then **no-closure restructuring is the weakest option economically unless the required savings are modest**. Nothing in the scenario suggests the problem is modest.

So from an investor perspective:

- a package that protects every plant **and** keeps the current cost structure broadly intact is not acceptable;
- a package that only uses temporary wage restraint, working-time flexibility, and voluntary exits is unlikely to be enough;
- and a plan that avoids hard decisions now only increases the probability of a worse, more value-destructive crisis later.

## On plant closures
We are **not ideologically attached** to closures, but we are also not prepared to rule them out because they are politically difficult.

If a plant is structurally uncompetitive, subscale, or impossible to integrate into a viable 2030 footprint, then closure or equivalent shutdown is a legitimate option. The experts have both said that clearly.

That said, we are not asking for closures as theatre. We are asking for:
- a **plant-by-plant economic case**,
- a **credible comparison of options**,
- and a package that maximises durable savings while minimising unnecessary destruction.

So our position is:
- **closures may be necessary**;
- **consolidation is preferable if it truly achieves the same savings**;
- **no-closure packages are acceptable only if they can prove equivalent durability and magnitude**.

## To the Works Council
You say no closures, no compulsory redundancies, and 2030 guarantees. I understand why you want that, but I do not accept it as a financially credible default.

The expert’s assessment makes the weakness obvious: if the company keeps all sites open, then the remaining measures must be unusually aggressive to be enough. You have not yet shown that your package gets there.

Your proposals are directionally constructive:
- voluntary exits,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary contributions,
- redeployment,
- productivity improvements.

But they are still **not obviously sufficient** to cut the fixed-cost base enough. And if they are not sufficient, then insisting on no closures just protects the optics of employment while leaving shareholders with a structurally impaired business.

We cannot support a settlement where the company preserves all sites but continues to miss margin targets.

## To the Union
We agree that permanent wage dumping would be unacceptable. We also agree that workers should not finance the restructuring alone.

But the expert is equally clear that **labour-cost relief alone is not enough** unless it is very large and paired with capacity reduction. That means your emphasis on wage architecture cannot substitute for real industrial restructuring.

We would support:
- a time-limited, meaningful employee contribution,
- working-time and flexibility measures,
- voluntary exits and retirement paths,
- bonus restraint,
- and a negotiated productivity package.

What we would **not** support is a settlement where those measures are used to avoid the harder question of footprint rationalisation.

## To Management
We support your insistence that this must be durable and measurable. That is the right test.

But we also need to be frank: “all options on the table” is not enough if it becomes a pretext for prolonged confrontation. The market will not reward a plan that depends on years of conflict while hoping the labour side eventually gives in.

What we need from management is:
1. a **plant-by-plant economic analysis**,
2. a **quantified target for recurring annual savings**,
3. a **clear statement of which footprint is viable by 2030**,
4. and an explanation of why the chosen package is the best risk-adjusted route.

If the numbers show that one or more closures are necessary, then say so plainly. If a consolidation package can really deliver the same outcome, then investors would prefer that, because it may preserve value and reduce execution risk. But we need proof, not aspiration.

## To the Regional State Government and the Controlling Family
We understand the governance reality. We also understand that a forced closure strategy may trigger political and industrial resistance. That matters.

But governance constraints do not change the economics. They only change the form the solution must take.

From an investor standpoint, the question is not whether closures are politically pleasant. The question is whether the final package delivers:
- durable savings,
- higher utilisation,
- improved margins,
- and a credible path to returns by 2030.

If the answer is yes, we can support it. If the answer is “we preserved every site but never fixed the economics,” then the governance structure will have protected the wrong thing.

## Our current position
After this round, our position is unchanged but sharper:

- **No cosmetic settlement**
- **No package that leaves margins structurally impaired**
- **No open-ended conflict**
- **No token employee contribution**
- **No guarantee package without real, durable savings**

What we can back is a settlement that:
- materially reduces capacity,
- cuts the fixed-cost base,
- improves utilisation,
- keeps labour costs down on a recurring basis,
- and delivers a credible return to competitive margins.

That may involve closures. It may involve deep consolidation. It may involve a mix. But it must be economically real.

## What would move us
We would shift only if management presents a package that credibly shows:
- the **same or near-same savings** as closure-based restructuring,
- with **lower execution risk**,
- and with **durability through 2030**.

Absent that, we will not support a “no closures” settlement just because it is easier to sell publicly.
