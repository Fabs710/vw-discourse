Let me be equally clear in Round 4: I do **not** accept the repeated attempt to turn “no closures” into a veto on real restructuring.

I agree with all parties on one point: **a cosmetic deal is worthless**. We also agree that **temporary measures alone are not enough**. But beyond that, the labour side and the state keep asking me to accept a solution that preserves the current footprint, preserves too much of the current cost base, and still somehow expects me to restore competitiveness by 2030. That is not credible.

## To the Works Council
You say I have proven only that closures are the “most aggressive” lever, not that they are necessary. I disagree.

What I have established is this:
- German factory costs are materially above target.
- The European market is shrinking.
- Competition is intensifying.
- The brand is missing its return-on-sales target.
- We need durable savings, not temporary relief.
- We need funds for future products and technology.

Given those facts, a plan that relies on voluntary exits, working-time changes, product transfers, and limited employee contributions **while keeping the current footprint essentially intact** is not a restructuring plan. It is delay.

You keep asking “which plant is non-viable?” Fine. That is exactly why I support a plant-by-plant review. But do not confuse that with a blanket promise that every site can be preserved. If the numbers show a site is structurally subscale or uneconomic, then I will not pretend otherwise for the sake of symbolism.

And let me say this bluntly: **a no-closure pledge through 2030 is not a serious answer if it blocks the cost and capacity adjustment needed to survive to 2030.**

## To the Union
I hear your insistence on no forced redundancies, no wage dumping, and no weakening of collective bargaining. I am not here to dismantle the framework for its own sake.

But you are still avoiding the central issue: **how do you remove enough recurring cost and capacity?**

Your menu:
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary contributions,
- productivity improvements,

may help, but I have not yet heard a combination that credibly gets us to the necessary savings scale. If you cannot deliver that scale, then you are not offering an alternative — you are offering a softer path to the same unsolved problem.

So I will repeat: **temporary, limited contributions are discussable; permanent preservation of the current cost structure is not.**

## To the Regional State Government
I appreciate that you support a plant-by-plant review. Good. But let us be honest about what that means: it can lead to closures if the economics justify them.

That is not a threat. It is governance.

You cannot simultaneously:
- insist on no closures as a default,
- demand durable competitiveness,
- and reject the possibility that some sites may need to be rationalised.

If the state uses its blocking position to freeze the footprint regardless of economics, then it is not protecting the company. It is protecting inertia.

## To the Controlling Family
You say you do not support a closure-first script if it triggers avoidable value destruction. I agree with the principle. I am not asking for a closure-first script. I am asking for **the right restructuring tool where the numbers require it**.

What I need from ownership is a clear answer:
- If a site is demonstrably non-viable, will you back closure or equivalent shutdown?
- Or will you protect every site regardless of economics?

Because those are not the same thing. And if your answer is the latter, then we do not have a restructuring mandate — we have a preservation mandate dressed up as stewardship.

## To Institutional Investors
You are right to reject a cosmetic settlement. Where I part company with you is when “economic realism” becomes shorthand for closure as a default rather than as a proven necessity.

I have not argued for closure as ideology. I have argued that **the savings must be real, recurring, and large enough**. If deep consolidation can do that, then I am open to it. But if the no-closure package only produces modest or temporary savings, then it fails the economic test.

That is the difference between us and the labour side:
- they want a guarantee first and proof later;
- I want proof first and then the structure that follows from it.

## On the expert view
The expert input is helpful, and it supports my core position more than theirs:
- the problem is structural,
- temporary fixes are not enough,
- labour-cost relief alone is unlikely to be sufficient,
- and meaningful capacity reduction is required.

That does **not** mean every path must involve formal closures. But it does mean that any package that keeps the footprint essentially unchanged is inadequate.

So let us be precise:
- If “consolidation without formal closure” means **real** line shutdowns, shift reductions, product concentration, and fixed-cost removal at scale, then it may be acceptable.
- If it means “no closure” while the plant network remains broadly intact, then it is not.

The label does not matter. The economics do.

## What I will not accept
I will not accept:
- a no-closure promise that prevents structural change,
- temporary concessions sold as lasting solutions,
- burden-sharing that is too small to matter,
- or a deal that preserves every site while leaving us structurally uncompetitive.

## What would move me
I would shift only if one of two things is shown in hard numbers:
1. A deep consolidation package without formal closures that delivers **equivalent recurring annual savings**, real capacity reduction, and a lower fixed-cost base through 2030; or
2. Evidence that a specific site is truly viable and can remain open **without** undermining the required cost transformation.

Until then, closures remain on the table because they are part of the credible toolset for a serious restructuring.

## My position, plainly stated
- **Yes** to plant-by-plant economics.
- **Yes** to a serious comparison of scenarios.
- **Yes** to labour contributions if they are limited and durable.
- **Yes** to investment and product commitments.
- **No** to a blanket no-closure veto.
- **No** to compulsory redundancies as a first instinct, but **yes** to them if they become unavoidable in a proven restructuring case.
- **No** to a settlement that leaves the cost base uncompetitive.

If the other side wants to avoid closures, then bring me a package that actually matches the economics. Not slogans. Not guarantees. Numbers. Durable numbers.

That is the standard I will hold.
