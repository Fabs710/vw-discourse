I will be direct.

## My position
I support a **serious restructuring** because the company’s competitiveness and margins must be restored. I do **not** support a settlement that preserves the current cost structure, keeps the company structurally uncompetitive, and calls that stewardship.

At the same time, I do **not** support a closure-first approach if it creates avoidable value destruction through protracted conflict, political escalation, and permanent damage to industrial relations. The family holding’s interest is not in “winning” a bargaining round; it is in protecting the enterprise’s long-term value.

So my test is simple:

- Does the package **durably improve competitiveness**?
- Does it **actually reduce fixed costs and capacity** enough to matter?
- Does it **avoid destabilising the enterprise**?
- And does it **hold through 2030 and beyond**?

If the answer is no, I will not support it.

---

## To management
You are right about the diagnosis: the German footprint is structurally too expensive, and temporary measures alone are not enough.

Where I agree:
- the company needs durable savings;
- investment in future products must be protected;
- and a cosmetic compromise would be a mistake.

Where I disagree:
- I do **not** accept that closures are the only serious route.
- I do **not** accept that management can treat closures as the default and then ask others to prove a negative.
- I do **not** accept a plan that overstates the ease of implementation and understates the cost of conflict.

You have not yet shown me a **plant-by-plant economic case** that proves which sites are truly non-viable and what the alternatives would save. Until you do, your preference for closures is still a preference, not a demonstrated necessity.

That said, I also reject the notion that “no closures” is automatically a viable answer. If the no-closure package cannot produce **durable, recurring savings at scale**, then it is not enough. Full stop.

---

## To the works council
I understand why you insist on:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and employment security through 2030.

I agree that a deal that simply dumps the burden onto workers would be unstable and unfair.

But I have to be equally clear: if your position is effectively “no closures, no forced redundancies, no meaningful capacity reduction,” then that is not a restructuring path. That is a blockade.

The expert assessments have already made the central point: this looks like a **capacity and fixed-cost problem**, not a temporary dip. So some **real industrial adjustment** is unavoidable. The question is whether it comes through:
- deep consolidation,
- line rationalisation,
- shift reduction,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- and a negotiated labour contribution,

or through a later crisis that is much worse for everyone.

I am open to solutions that preserve sites where possible, but I will not back an arrangement that keeps the footprint intact while the business continues to miss targets.

---

## To the union
I agree with your insistence that the collective bargaining framework must not be hollowed out. I also agree that employee contributions must be:
- time-limited,
- capped,
- and matched by real commitments from the company and owners.

Where I disagree is with the implication that collective standards can be preserved in full while the company’s economics are reset mainly through goodwill and temporary concessions.

The restructuring needs **real recurring savings**, not symbolic sacrifice. If labour contributions are too small, too temporary, or too reversible, they will not solve the problem. If they are too large and not paired with operational change, they will provoke resistance and fail to hold.

So I would view as credible:
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary bonus restraint,
- productivity measures,
- and a negotiated contribution from labour,

**but only if** they are tied to a footprint reduction that makes the remaining operations genuinely competitive.

---

## To the regional state government
You are right to insist that the solution must survive the governance reality. A plan that cannot pass the supervisory board, or that triggers a political backlash large enough to paralyse implementation, is not a real plan.

Where I agree:
- no cosmetic compromise;
- no blind acceptance of closures;
- no ignoring the social and political consequences.

Where I disagree:
- the state cannot simply say “no closures” and then leave management to solve the economic gap by magic.
- if the state uses its blocking position, it also has to accept that the company still needs a viable footprint.

So I support your demand for **plant-by-plant scenarios**. That is the right next step. But I do not accept using that request as a way to defer difficult decisions indefinitely.

If a site is truly non-viable, I expect that to be shown honestly. If a deep consolidation package can genuinely match the savings with less disruption, I am prepared to consider it. That is the proper stewardship test.

---

## To institutional investors
You are right to reject a token settlement. You are right that the company needs durable savings and a credible path to restored margins.

Where I agree:
- no cosmetic deal;
- no open-ended uncertainty;
- no token employee contribution.

Where I disagree:
- I do **not** accept that closures are automatically the best value solution.
- I do **not** accept a settlement that maximises the accounting short term but destroys long-term industrial peace.
- I do **not** accept that the investor answer is simply “cut deeper,” without regard to implementation risk.

You should want the same thing I want: a package that restores competitiveness **without permanently impairing the enterprise**. If consolidation can deliver equivalent savings with lower execution risk, that is preferable. If it cannot, then closures may be justified. But I want proof, not ideology.

---

## What would move me
I will move only if the evidence shows one of two things:

1. **A deep consolidation package without formal closures** can deliver
   - recurring annual savings at the required scale,
   - materially higher utilisation,
   - a lower fixed-cost base,
   - and a credible margin improvement by 2030,

**or**

2. A specific closure or shutdown is shown to be **truly unavoidable**, because the alternative packages cannot meet the target and would simply delay the crisis.

That is the standard.

What will **not** move me:
- slogans about fairness without numbers;
- refusal to acknowledge overcapacity;
- temporary concessions presented as structural solutions;
- or a deal that protects every current arrangement while the business continues to erode.

---

## My position on the compromise paths
### 1) Plant-by-plant proof process
I support this.

Not because it guarantees no closures, but because it forces the decision onto facts rather than assumptions. If management believes a site is non-viable, prove it. If alternatives can match the economics, prove that too.

### 2) Consolidation without formal closure
I am open to this **only if** it removes real capacity and fixed cost, not just cosmetic volume. If it is simply closure by another name with no real savings, I would not support it.

### 3) Time-bound restructuring compact with deferred closure option
This can be useful, but only if the triggers are rigorous and independent. I will not support a vague “review later” arrangement that just postpones the decision and keeps the company in permanent uncertainty.

---

## My bottom line
I am prepared to support a **hard but durable restructuring**.

I am **not** prepared to support:
- a purely temporary bridge,
- a no-closure settlement that does not match the required economics,
- or any arrangement that permanently weakens enterprise value or governance stability.

So my position is:

- **Yes** to real capacity reduction.
- **Yes** to material labour-cost relief.
- **Yes** to binding investment and product commitments.
- **Yes** to plant-by-plant economic proof.
- **No** to cosmetic peace.
- **No** to permanent value destruction.
- **No** to closure as a first principle, but **yes** to closure if the numbers truly require it.

That is where the family holding stands.
