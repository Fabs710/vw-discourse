I will respond plainly, because this is now the point where slogans have to give way to substance.

## My position

I **agree** with the diagnosis that the company has a structural competitiveness problem. I also agree that a purely temporary bridge, or a cosmetic compromise, would be irresponsible.

But I **do not accept** that plant closures are the default answer, and I **do not accept** a restructuring that destroys regional employment or sidelines the state’s co-determination and veto role. Those are not negotiable from my side.

The expert’s assessment is useful, and I take it seriously: yes, the company likely needs **durable capacity reduction** and **material labour-cost relief**. But “durable capacity reduction” does **not automatically mean closures**. It can also mean:
- shift reductions,
- line rationalisation,
- repurposing,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- early retirement,
- and a negotiated lowering of fixed-cost burden.

What I am prepared to support is a package that **preserves the German industrial base as far as possible**, protects jobs, and still restores competitiveness.

## To management

You have made the strongest case for why change is needed, but not yet the strongest case for why **closures must come first**.

The expert says closures are the most straightforward route, not the only conceivable one. That matters. Because in this governance structure, a solution that is technically neat but politically impossible is not a solution at all.

So I challenge you on three points:

1. **Show the numbers plant by plant**
   If a site is truly beyond saving, say why in transparent operational terms. Not generalities about “overcapacity.”

2. **Prove that alternatives cannot reach the same savings**
   Before closures, you need to demonstrate why a package of capacity rationalisation, voluntary exits, working-time measures, and productivity gains cannot deliver a comparable durable effect.

3. **Accept that burden-sharing must be real on all sides**
   If you ask for worker sacrifice, then you must also commit management and owners to a credible investment and industrial roadmap. Not just pressure on labour.

Right now, your position still reads like: “accept structural cuts first, and we’ll talk about safeguards later.” I cannot support that.

## To the Works Council

I support your red lines on **no compulsory redundancies** and I strongly share your concern about keeping German plants open. But I need to be equally frank with you: if your position becomes “no closures, no forced layoffs, no meaningful capacity reduction,” then you are not offering a restructuring path, you are offering a blockade.

The expert’s view confirms that the current footprint may be too large for the market. That means some reduction in installed capacity is probably unavoidable. The question is **how** that happens and **who bears the cost**.

So I can support your insistence on:
- no closures as the starting point,
- no forced redundancies,
- binding site and employment security,
- and negotiated, fair employee contributions.

But I will not support a position that rules out **all** meaningful structural adjustment. If jobs are to be protected long term, then the company must become viable long term.

That means you should be prepared to discuss:
- shift elimination,
- partial line consolidation,
- internal redeployment across sites,
- voluntary programmes,
- and temporary working-time arrangements.

Not because I want to weaken the workforce, but because I want to avoid the more destructive outcome of closure through deadlock.

## To the Union

You are right to insist that the collective bargaining framework must not be dismantled under the banner of restructuring. I agree with that.

I also agree that worker contributions must be:
- limited,
- negotiated,
- time-bound,
- and matched by commitments from management and ownership.

Where I disagree is with any framing that suggests the company can preserve the current structure and simply finance the gap through wage restraint. The expert has made clear that labour cost relief alone is unlikely to be enough unless it is unusually large and paired with capacity adjustment.

So the union should not treat this as a fight over wage principles only. It is also a question of industrial footprint and future product allocation.

A fair settlement would need to include:
- a measurable labour contribution,
- but also a credible operational restructuring,
- and binding investment for German sites.

That is the only way I could defend worker concessions politically.

## To the controlling family

You are right that conflict destroys value. But I also need to say: value is not preserved by forcing a deal that tears apart the regional industrial base and then expecting social peace to hold.

You should not assume that closures are the cleanest ownership solution. In this company, they may trigger a much larger political and industrial cost than the accounting model shows. That is especially true when the regional state has a blocking stake and the co-determination structure gives labour a real veto.

If you want a durable solution, then support a package that:
- preserves core sites,
- reduces capacity in a socially managed way,
- and gives the workforce a reason to stay in the deal.

A solution that is financially elegant but socially ungovernable is not stewardship.

## To investors

I hear the insistence on durable savings, and I do not dismiss it. But “durable” does not have to mean “maximal closure.” It means the business must be able to compete in 2030 and beyond.

The right test is not whether the company can produce a one-off cost cut. The test is whether it can:
- lower its fixed-cost base,
- protect future investment,
- and avoid a permanent erosion of the German industrial platform.

If the package can do that without closures, it deserves serious support. If it cannot, then we are not there yet.

## Where I am moving, and where I am not

I am **not** moving on these points:
- no German plant closures as the starting point,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no sidelining of the state’s co-determination role,
- no one-sided restructuring that makes workers absorb the full burden.

I **am** prepared to discuss:
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary employee contributions,
- and a negotiated productivity package.

But those must be part of a package with:
- binding investment commitments,
- site protection where feasible,
- and a realistic industrial roadmap.

## My concrete demand for the next round

If management wants support from the state side, I need a **plant-by-plant restructuring scenario** with at least three options:

1. **No closure, but deep internal restructuring**
2. **Partial consolidation / line reduction**
3. **Closure only where absolutely unavoidable**

For each option, I want to see:
- expected savings,
- employment impact,
- implementation risk,
- political and social consequences,
- and what investment commitment is tied to it.

That is the only serious basis for deciding whether closures are genuinely unavoidable or merely preferred.

So my bottom line remains:

- **Yes** to a tough restructuring.
- **No** to plant closures as the automatic first choice.
- **No** to compulsory redundancies.
- **Yes** to a negotiated, socially responsible package that protects the company as a regional anchor.

If the expert’s economics truly require closures, that burden of proof is on management, not on the workforce or the state to disprove by default.
