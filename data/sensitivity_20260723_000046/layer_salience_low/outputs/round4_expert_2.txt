Yes. From a plant-level industrial economics perspective, the key issue is whether the company can remove enough **recurring fixed cost and excess capacity** without formal closures to restore competitiveness by 2030.

## Short answer
A **credible no-closure package** is possible only if it achieves something close to **closure-like economics** through:
- major and permanent **capacity reduction**
- **shift eliminations / line shutdowns**
- transfer of volume to fewer plants
- substantial **fixed-cost removal**
- meaningful, recurring **labour-cost relief**
- and a materially higher **utilisation rate** at the retained sites

If it does **not** do those things, then it is unlikely to be sufficient.

## My minimum economic standard
For a mature German OEM facing shrinking European demand and above-peer factory costs, I would generally expect a viable 2030 restructuring to deliver:

- **effective installed-capacity reduction** of roughly **10–20%** in the domestic footprint, or an economically equivalent reduction in output capability
- **mid-single-digit to low-double-digit % recurring labour-cost relief per vehicle**
- a clear **lift in utilisation** at the retained plants
- removal of enough support and overhead cost that the savings are **durable**, not one-off

That is not a precise forecast; it is a turnaround benchmark.

## What works best economically
### 1) Deep consolidation without formal closure
This can work if it includes:
- one or more sites losing major volume
- permanent removal of shifts
- line rationalisation
- product concentration onto fewer plants
- substantial voluntary exits and retirement bridges
- working-time flexibility and premium-pay restraint
- overhead/support-function reduction

This can approach closure-level savings **if** the footprint really shrinks in practice.

### 2) Closure-led restructuring
This is usually the cleanest way to remove excess capacity and fixed cost. It tends to provide the strongest economic result if a site is structurally subscale or expensive to run.

### 3) Time-bound compact with escalation
Useful as a bridge, but only if the trigger points are objective and if missed milestones lead to real escalation. By itself, it is not a substitute for capacity reduction.

## What is unlikely to be enough
I would be skeptical of any package based mainly on:
- wage moderation alone
- temporary labour concessions
- voluntary exits without footprint reduction
- redeployment without line shutdowns
- vague productivity promises without quantified fixed-cost removal

Those measures can help, but usually not enough for a structural overcapacity problem.

## Plant-level economics: what matters most
At each site, the decisive variables are:
- **utilisation**
- **fixed cost per unit**
- **break-even volume**
- logistics/tooling constraints
- product mix and future allocation
- execution risk and transition timing

A site with low utilisation and high fixed cost per vehicle can sometimes be saved through consolidation, but only if volume is shifted elsewhere and the site’s cost base falls sharply.

## Bottom line
A **no-closure, no-forced-redundancy** settlement can be economically credible **only if** it removes real capacity and fixed cost at scale — not if it merely preserves the footprint with softer labour terms.
