## My position, plainly
I support a **hard restructuring** because the company’s competitiveness and margins must be restored.  
I do **not** support a settlement that preserves an uncompetitive cost structure and calls that stewardship.  
I also do **not** support a closure-first script if it creates avoidable value destruction through prolonged conflict, political escalation, and damage to industrial relations.

My test remains simple:
- does the package **durably improve competitiveness**,
- does it **actually reduce fixed costs and capacity** enough to matter,
- does it **avoid destabilising the enterprise**,
- and does it **hold through 2030 and beyond**?

If the answer is no, I will not support it.

## To management
You have the strongest diagnosis in the room, and I largely agree with it:
- German factory costs are too high,
- the market is shrinking,
- competition is fierce,
- the brand is missing its return-on-sales target,
- and the company needs durable savings, not theatre.

Where I disagree is your repeated tendency to treat closures as the obvious answer before the plant-level evidence is fully laid out.

You have not yet shown me:
- which sites are structurally non-viable,
- what each alternative delivers in recurring annual savings,
- and why deep consolidation cannot close the gap.

That said, I am **not** endorsing the labour-side assumption that every plant can be preserved in full operating form and the business still be made competitive. That is not credible.

So my stance is:
- **yes** to plant-by-plant proof,
- **yes** to real capacity reduction,
- **yes** to material labour-cost relief,
- **no** to a blanket no-closure veto,
- **no** to symbolic savings that do not change the cost base.

If the numbers show a site is truly non-viable, I will not pretend otherwise for the sake of symbolism.

## To the works council
I understand why you are defending sites and jobs. That is your mandate.

Where I agree:
- cosmetic compromises are worthless,
- forced redundancies are highly damaging,
- temporary concessions must be truly temporary,
- and the company should not use “shared sacrifice” as a cover for one-sided burden shifting.

Where I disagree:
you are still trying to make “no closures” function as a principle that sits above the economics. It cannot.

A serious restructuring is not:
- preserve every site,
- preserve every guarantee,
- and hope the numbers fix themselves.

A serious restructuring is one that removes enough recurring cost and excess capacity to restore competitiveness. If deep consolidation can do that, fine. If it cannot, then your preferred solution is simply not sufficient.

So I am open to:
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- redeployment,
- shift reduction,
- working-time flexibility,
- product concentration,
- and limited employee contributions.

I am **not** open to:
- a settlement that keeps the current footprint essentially intact,
- or a package that avoids hard choices while the business continues to erode.

## To the union
You are right that labour should not finance its own insecurity. I agree that any contribution must be:
- limited,
- time-bound,
- fair,
- and matched by commitments from management and owners.

You are also right that permanent wage dumping is unacceptable.

Where I disagree is your implicit assumption that the collective framework can remain essentially untouched while the company’s economics are reset mainly through temporary flexibility and goodwill. That is not enough if the structural gap is large.

I will support:
- temporary employee contributions,
- bonus restraint,
- working-time changes,
- voluntary exits,
- retirement bridges,
- and productivity measures,

but only if they are part of a package that **actually lowers the fixed-cost base** and improves utilisation.

If you cannot produce that scale of effect, then you are not offering a workable alternative; you are offering delay.

## To the regional state government
I appreciate that you are using your blocking position to force seriousness. That is the right instinct.

Where I agree:
- no cosmetic deal,
- no automatic closure logic,
- plant-by-plant economics should be the basis,
- and labour should not be the only side asked to contribute.

Where I disagree:
you cannot insist on no closures as a default and simultaneously expect a structurally overbuilt business to become competitive without real footprint adjustment.

If the state wants to preserve industrial employment long term, it must support the option that actually restores viability, even if that means some sites are rationalised. That is not anti-worker; it is pro-survival.

I am open to a deep consolidation path if it truly removes real capacity and fixed cost. But I will not support a political veto on all irreversible adjustment if the economics show it is needed.

## To investors
You are right to reject a token settlement. I share that concern.

I also agree with you that durable savings matter, not public relations.

Where I part company with some of your phrasing is this: closure is not automatically the best value solution. It may be necessary, but it must be justified site by site. The market does not benefit from a closure done badly, with excessive conflict and execution risk that wipes out the savings.

So yes:
- I want durable cost reduction,
- I want capacity reduction,
- I want deal certainty,
- and I do not want a cosmetic package.

But I still want the evidence before endorsing irreversible moves.

## My view on the expert input
The experts are helpful on the direction of travel:
- this is a structural problem,
- temporary fixes are not enough,
- labour-cost relief alone is unlikely to be sufficient,
- and meaningful capacity reduction is required.

That supports my core view.

What the experts do **not** prove is that formal closures must be the starting point. They do show that if a no-closure package is to work, it must remove **real** fixed cost and capacity. That is the key point.

So the label does not matter. The economics do.

## On the compromise tracks
### 1) Plant-by-plant proof process
I support this.

Not because it guarantees no closures, but because it forces the debate onto facts instead of slogans.

### 2) Deep consolidation without formal closure
I am open to this **only if** it removes real capacity and fixed cost at scale. If it is merely closure by another name without actual economic effect, I will not support it.

### 3) Time-bound restructuring compact with escalation
This can work only if the triggers are objective, enforced, and not a disguised delay tactic. A weak “review later” mechanism would be unacceptable.

---

## What I will not accept
Let me be very clear.

I will not accept:
- a no-closure promise that blocks structural change,
- temporary concessions sold as durable solutions,
- burden-sharing that is too small to matter,
- or a deal that preserves every site while leaving us structurally uncompetitive.

I will also not accept a package that permanently impairs the enterprise’s value or governance stability. That would be value destruction, not stewardship.

---

## What would move me
I would move only if one of two things is shown in hard numbers:

1. **A deep consolidation package without formal closures** that delivers equivalent recurring annual savings, real capacity reduction, and a lower fixed-cost base through 2030; or  
2. Evidence that a specific site is truly non-viable and that closure or equivalent shutdown is unavoidable to secure the company’s future.

Until then, closures remain on the table, but not as ideology. As a legitimate tool if the economics require it.
