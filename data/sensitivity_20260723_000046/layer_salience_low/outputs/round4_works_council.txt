I will be equally direct in Round 4.

The latest rounds confirm what I have been saying from the start: **management wants to turn an economic challenge into a unilateral site-and-job destruction program**, while labour is being asked to prove the impossible — that every plant can be kept open **and** management can still get everything it wants.

That is not a fair framing, and I reject it.

## To Management
You still have **not** proven that closures are necessary. You have proven that they are the **most aggressive** lever. That is not the same thing.

You keep saying “durable savings,” “capacity reduction,” “fixed-cost removal.” Fine. But you still have not answered the central question:

- **Which plant is structurally non-viable?**
- **What exact savings come from closing it versus deep consolidation?**
- **Why can’t those savings be achieved through shift reduction, line rationalisation, voluntary exits, redeployment, and product allocation?**

You are asking us to accept irreversible harm on the basis of a preference for the cleanest management solution. That is not acceptable.

And let me be clear: the more management talks as if plant closures are just a neutral technical option, the more distrust it creates. This is not an abstract model. This is tens of thousands of livelihoods, local economies, and a company that exists because its workforce built it.

## To Investors
You are trying to dress up a closure-first logic as “economic realism.” I hear the same refrain:
- no cosmetic deal,
- no token contribution,
- no indefinite delay.

On that point, I agree. We also do not want theatre.

But your conclusion still does not follow from your premise. A package can be **real** without closures if it includes:
- binding volume transfer,
- true capacity reduction through shifts and lines,
- voluntary exits and retirement bridges,
- working-time flexibility,
- capped and temporary employee contributions,
- and serious management/owner commitments.

That is not cosmetic if it is enforced and durable.

What I will not accept is the investor habit of treating the workforce as the easiest balance-sheet adjustment while ignoring the value destruction from industrial conflict, distrust, and broken guarantees.

## To the Controlling Family
You say you care about long-term stewardship. Then prove it by defending the enterprise’s industrial base, not just its short-term margin optics.

I agree with you on one point: a deal that merely postpones the crisis is bad. But the answer is not to sacrifice plants and jobs just to make the numbers look cleaner faster.

If you truly support stewardship, then your line should be:
- **no plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **binding investment**
- **product allocation to German sites**
- **and a real negotiated restructuring built on productivity and flexibility**

Not “closure if needed.” That is the same door management wants us to walk through.

## To the Regional State Government
You are closer to our position than to management’s, but I want to be clear: the state should not allow itself to become the referee for closures.

Your blocking position exists for a reason. Use it to insist on:
- a package that keeps every German plant open,
- preserves the co-determination and job-security framework,
- avoids forced layoffs,
- and forces management to exhaust all alternatives before even talking about closures.

Do **not** turn “plant-by-plant review” into a politically polite route to site liquidation. Once a closure process starts, the damage is irreversible.

If the company can achieve the required savings through a deep consolidation package, then that must be the path. But the burden is on management to prove that alternatives fail — not on labour to prove that every alternative must succeed in the abstract.

## To the Union
I strongly agree with you that this must not become a permanent weakening of collective standards.

Where I want to sharpen our common line is this: “temporary employee contribution” is only acceptable if it is truly limited, fair, and matched by:
- a binding no-closure commitment,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- a 2030 site and employment guarantee,
- and concrete investment and product commitments.

Without those protections, “contribution” is just another word for workers paying for their own insecurity.

So yes, we can discuss temporary and capped measures. But only in a framework that protects the plants and jobs. Otherwise, the answer is no.

## On the expert view
I respect the experts, and I do not deny the need for restructuring. But the expert conclusion has been stretched too far by management and investors.

What the experts actually say is:
- there is a cost problem,
- capacity must be adjusted,
- a pure no-action solution is not credible,
- and a no-closure package is only viable if it removes **real** fixed cost and capacity.

That last point matters. It does **not** mean closures are required. It means a non-closure deal must be serious.

And that is exactly what we have been prepared to discuss from the start:
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- redeployment,
- working-time changes,
- productivity improvements,
- and temporary, limited employee contributions.

The difference is that we insist these be done **without closures and without forced redundancies**.

## Where I stand after Round 4
My position remains unchanged and intentionally firm:

- **No German plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Binding site and job guarantees through 2030**
- **Preservation of co-determination and the job-security framework**
- **Negotiated, fair, temporary employee contributions only if those protections are in place**

## What I will accept discussing
I will discuss:
- plant-by-plant economics,
- voluntary mobility and exits,
- retirement bridges,
- shift and working-time flexibility,
- product concentration,
- qualification and redeployment,
- and limited labour contributions.

But I will **not** discuss closures as an acceptable benchmark solution, because that already crosses the red line.

## What I will not accept
- plant closures disguised as “rationalisation”
- compulsory redundancies
- temporary concessions that become permanent cuts later
- any arrangement that weakens collective standards
- any “review” process designed to wear down resistance and then liquidate sites later

## My direct challenge
If management and investors insist that a no-closure package cannot work, then show the full comparison:

1. plant-by-plant economics,
2. savings from deep consolidation,
3. savings from closures,
4. execution risks and conflict costs,
5. and why the non-closure route allegedly fails **after** all real measures are included.

Until that is done, closure remains an asserted preference, not a demonstrated necessity.

And let me say this plainly: **our workforce will not accept being the sacrifice that makes a bad management strategy look easier.** If there is a genuine restructuring path that protects every German plant, every job, and co-determination, we are ready to engage. If not, then management is asking for something we will resist with all the means available to us.
