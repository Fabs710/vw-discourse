Management wants us to believe that closures and forced job cuts are the only serious answer. I reject that.

Let me be very clear: **we will not accept German plant closures, and we will not accept compulsory redundancies.** Those are not bargaining chips for us; they are red lines.

## Where I agree
I do agree on one point: the company needs a **credible, durable restructuring**. A temporary patch will not solve the problem, and nobody benefits if the company is weaker in 2030. I also agree that investment and future product allocation have to be part of any serious package.

## Where I disagree
But I fundamentally disagree with the idea that “durable” automatically means:
- closing plants,
- breaking the job-security framework,
- and forcing workers to pay for management’s strategic errors.

That is not burden-sharing. That is shifting the crisis onto the workforce.

Management says all options must be on the table. Fine. Then let me be equally clear: **the option of keeping all German plants open, preserving employment, and restructuring through alternatives must also be treated seriously**. You have not shown that closures are necessary — only that they are convenient from a cost-cutting perspective.

## To management
You asked for a concrete package. Here is the starting point for a real negotiation:

1. **No plant closures in Germany**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Binding employment and site guarantees through 2030**
4. **Capacity adjustment only through voluntary and socially responsible measures**
5. **Working-time flexibility and temporary contributions instead of permanent cuts**
6. **Meaningful investment commitments for German plants**
7. **A real co-determination role in shaping the restructuring path**

If you want employee contributions, then they must be:
- time-limited,
- fair,
- and matched by binding guarantees on jobs, sites, and future products.

We will not agree to a deal where workers give up security first and are promised vague “competitiveness” later.

## To the union
I agree with your stance that the burden must be shared and that collective standards must not be destroyed. That is exactly right. The workforce is prepared to contribute, but not to surrender its future. Any contribution must be part of a negotiated package that protects sites and jobs.

## To the state government and owners
You both speak about responsibility and long-term value. Then show that responsibility by rejecting a restructuring that destroys industrial capacity in Germany. If the company’s governance structure gives you blocking power — and it does — then use it to prevent closures and forced layoffs, not to pressure labour into accepting them.

## To investors
You want durable savings and deal certainty. I understand that. But there will be no durable settlement if the company breaks its social contract with the workforce. A “solution” that creates industrial conflict, strikes, political backlash, and loss of trust is not certainty — it is deferred crisis.

## The real question
Management keeps saying the German cost base is too high. That may be true. But the question is not whether there is pressure. The question is **how** it is addressed.

We are willing to talk about:
- productivity improvements,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- temporary working-time measures,
- limited wage-related contributions,
- and operational flexibility.

We are **not** willing to talk about:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or the dismantling of the employment-security framework.

## Bottom line
If the proposal depends on closures or forced layoffs, it is unacceptable.

If the proposal is a genuine negotiated restructuring that:
- keeps every German plant open,
- avoids compulsory redundancies,
- preserves co-determination,
- and secures investment and employment through 2030,

then we can discuss the burden-sharing details.

Until then, the works council will continue to resist this plan with all the means available to us.
