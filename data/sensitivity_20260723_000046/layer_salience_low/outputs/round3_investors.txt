The latest round sharpens the issue nicely: this is no longer about whether restructuring is needed — it clearly is — but about whether the package is **economically real** or just **socially palatable**.

## Where we agree
We agree with management, the family, the state, and the experts on the core diagnosis:

- the German cost base is structurally too high;
- overcapacity is real or at least highly likely;
- temporary concessions alone will not restore competitiveness;
- a cosmetic settlement would destroy value;
- and the company needs durable savings that hold through 2030.

That is the right frame. We are not interested in a deal that buys a few quarters of peace and then leaves margins structurally impaired.

## Where we disagree with labour and the state
We do **not** accept the repeated attempt to make **“no closures”** the default principle and then force everyone else to work around it.

That position is not yet a restructuring plan. It is a **constraint**. A valid alternative has to be judged by whether it can deliver the required recurring savings and capacity reduction, not by whether it is easier to defend publicly.

The experts were clear on one thing investors care about: if the footprint is genuinely overbuilt, then a pure no-closure package is usually the weakest option economically unless the needed savings are modest. Nothing in the scenario suggests the problem is modest.

So from our side:

- **No cosmetic no-closure settlement**
- **No token employee contribution**
- **No deal that preserves excess cost and hopes productivity alone will save it**
- **No indefinite delay disguised as social responsibility**

## On plant closures
We are **not ideologically attached** to closures. But we are equally not prepared to rule them out because they are politically difficult.

If a plant is structurally uncompetitive, subscale, or impossible to integrate into a viable 2030 footprint, then closure or equivalent shutdown is a legitimate option. The question is not whether closures are pleasant; it is whether they are necessary.

At the same time, we do agree with the family and the state on one important point: if closures are chosen, they should be based on a **plant-by-plant economic case**, not used as a blunt bargaining tactic.

So our position is:

- **closures may be necessary**;
- **consolidation is preferable if it truly achieves the same durable savings**;
- **no-closure packages are acceptable only if they can prove equivalent magnitude and durability**.

## To Management
You have made the strongest case for why action is required. But you still owe the market a clearer, quantified answer.

We want to see:
1. a **plant-by-plant economic analysis**;
2. the **recurring annual savings required by 2030**;
3. the **minimum capacity reduction needed** to hit those savings;
4. the **trade-off between closure, consolidation, and no-closure measures**;
5. and the **execution risk** attached to each path.

Right now, management is correct on the diagnosis, but not yet fully persuasive on the exact solution. “All options on the table” is not enough. Investors need to know which options actually work.

If the numbers show that one or more closures are necessary, then say so plainly. If a consolidation package can really deliver the same economics with less disruption, that is attractive. But it has to be proven, not asserted.

## To the Works Council
We understand why you resist closures and compulsory redundancies. No investor wants chaos or value destruction either.

But I do **not** accept your attempt to treat:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and 2030 job/site guarantees

as if those were the starting assumptions of a viable turnaround.

If the company is overbuilt, then some real capacity reduction is unavoidable. The question is whether that happens through managed restructuring or through a worse crisis later.

Your proposals — voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time flexibility, temporary contributions — are not unreasonable. But on the facts given, they are **not obviously enough** unless they are unusually deep and durable.

We would support them if they are part of a package that:
- materially lowers fixed costs,
- genuinely reduces capacity,
- and restores margins.

We would not support them as a way to avoid hard decisions altogether.

## To the Union
We agree that permanent wage dumping would be unacceptable and destabilising. We also agree that worker contributions must be limited, negotiated, and time-bound.

Where we disagree is with the idea that collective bargaining protections can be preserved in full while the company is reset mainly through goodwill, flexibility, and short-term concessions.

The expert assessment is important here: labour-cost relief alone is unlikely to be enough unless it is very large and paired with capacity reduction.

So yes, we can support:
- temporary employee contributions,
- bonus restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exit programs,
- retirement bridges,
- and productivity measures.

But only if they are tied to a footprint that can actually compete.

## To the Regional State Government
We respect the state’s concern about regional employment and governance stability. That matters.

But the state cannot have it both ways:
- insist on no closures as a default,
- while also expecting the company to absorb the full burden of structural overcapacity,
- and still deliver market returns.

If the state wants to preserve industrial employment, it should support the option that best restores the business to profitability. Sometimes that will be consolidation; sometimes, if the economics are clear, it may be closure.

What we do support is your demand for:
- plant-by-plant economics,
- comparison of scenarios,
- and proof before irreversible decisions.

That is the right process. But it should not become a procedural veto on tough decisions.

## To the Controlling Family
We agree with your stewardship logic more than with the labour-side framing.

You are right that a deal that preserves too much of the current structure can just postpone the crisis. You are also right that the company needs durable competitiveness, not a symbolic peace treaty.

What we want from you is clarity:
- If management shows a site is structurally non-viable, will you back closure?
- Or will you insist on preserving every site regardless of economics?

That answer matters because it determines whether the market can believe the restructuring will actually be executed.

We are aligned with you on rejecting cosmetic deals. Where we part company is if “socially survivable” becomes a reason to accept a package that is economically insufficient.

## Our current investor position
We will back a settlement only if it delivers:

- **material and recurring cost reduction**
- **durable capacity reduction**
- **higher utilisation**
- **a credible path to competitive margins**
- **deal certainty without endless delay**

That may involve closures. It may involve deep consolidation. It may involve a mix. But it must be economically real.

We will not support:

- a no-closure settlement that leaves the cost base uncompetitive;
- a token labour contribution package;
- or a plan that substitutes political comfort for structural improvement.

## What would move us
We would shift only if management presents a package that credibly demonstrates one of these:

1. **A deep consolidation / no-formal-closure package** that matches closure-based savings within an acceptable risk range and with durability through 2030; or
2. **A closure-based package** that is clearly justified by plant economics and is executable without creating a larger value destruction than the savings it produces.

That is the standard.

## Final bottom line
We are willing to support tough restructuring. We are not willing to support structural denial.

- **No cosmetic compromise**
- **No structurally impaired margins**
- **No indefinite conflict**
- **No token concessions**
- **No blanket ban on closures**

The company needs a credible return to competitiveness. If the numbers say closures are necessary, then the board should have the courage to say so. If consolidation can truly achieve the same result, that is better. But either way, the answer must be driven by economics, not by slogans.
