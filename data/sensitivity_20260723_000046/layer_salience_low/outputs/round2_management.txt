Let me respond plainly.

I appreciate that both the works council and the union are willing to talk about alternatives to immediate closures. I also note that the regional state government and the controlling family do not want a destructive conflict. That is useful. But I need to be equally clear: **none of the replies so far solve the core problem**.

The core problem is not whether we can all say “no closures” in different words. The core problem is that **our German cost and capacity base is structurally too high for the market we are now facing**. If we do not fix that, we will continue to underperform, and then all the guarantees in the world become hollow because the business will not generate the cash to sustain them.

## To the Works Council
You keep saying closures and compulsory redundancies are red lines. I understand that politically. But from a management perspective, a red line that prevents structural adjustment is not a bargaining position I can accept as a starting point.

You propose:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- 2030 site guarantees,
- capacity adjustment only through voluntary measures,
- and binding investment commitments.

That is not a restructuring package yet. That is a request to preserve the existing footprint and hope flexibility and goodwill will solve a hard competitiveness problem.

That will not be enough.

If you want me to consider avoiding closures, then you need to show how the package delivers:
- **material and durable cost reduction**,
- **real capacity reduction**,
- **a lower fixed cost base**,
- and **enough labor-cost relief to restore margins**.

Temporary measures alone do not change the underlying cost position. Working-time flexibility, redeployment, and qualification are useful tools, but they are not substitutes for structural change if the plants remain too numerous and too expensive for demand.

So I am rejecting your assumption that “no closures” can be the default and everything else can be built around it. If an alternative package can genuinely match the savings, I will look at it. But it has to be credible on the numbers, not just on principle.

## To the Union
You are right about one thing: a settlement that simply dumps the whole burden on the workforce would be unstable and unfair, and it would provoke exactly the conflict we all want to avoid.

But I disagree with your framing that management is trying to use restructuring to weaken collective standards for their own sake. That is not the objective. The objective is to restore competitiveness so the brand can survive and invest through 2030.

I also need to be clear: protecting the collective bargaining framework cannot mean preserving every cost item and every guarantee regardless of economics. If the framework blocks durable adjustment, then the framework itself becomes part of the problem.

Your ideas on:
- temporary employee contribution,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary mechanisms,
- redeployment and qualification,
- bonus waivers or solidarity contributions,

are not unreasonable as components. But they only help if they are **large enough to matter** and paired with **real structural savings**. A capped, time-limited contribution that barely moves the cost base is not sufficient.

And I will not agree that management and owners must first “show sacrifice” in abstract terms while the company remains stuck with too much capacity. We need a package that changes economics, not a moral exchange of gestures.

## To the Regional State Government
You are right to say that this cannot be treated as a simple financial optimization exercise. The regional and political consequences matter. I accept that.

But I need to push back on the idea that political difficulty should shape the solution more than economic necessity. If governance reality makes necessary restructuring impossible, then the company will continue to lose competitiveness and eventually the regional base you want to protect will be weaker anyway.

You say no closures as a starting point. I can accept that as a negotiation posture. I cannot accept it as a veto on structural change.

If you want to help secure German sites, then use your influence to back a package that actually restores profitability. That means:
- lower structural costs,
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- and a labor contribution that is real, not symbolic.

Otherwise we are just delaying the same crisis.

## To the Controlling Family
You are correct that a plan that cannot survive co-determination and political reality is not a real plan. But the reverse is also true: a plan that satisfies every veto point by weakening the economic content is also not a real plan.

You say you do not want conflict that destroys value. Nor do I. But prolonged uncertainty is also value destruction. That is why management needs a mandate for tough restructuring, not just a call to preserve harmony.

I agree with you that cosmetic solutions are unacceptable. Where I disagree is on the implicit suggestion that closures are merely a theoretical instrument. In my view, they remain on the table because without a credible threat of structural change, there is no incentive for the other side to accept the scale of adjustment required.

That does not mean closures are my preferred outcome. It means they are part of the leverage needed to get a durable settlement.

## To Institutional Investors
You are right to reject a cosmetic settlement. You are also right that deal certainty matters. But certainty without sufficient restructuring is worthless.

You ask for quantified, durable cost reduction and capacity adjustment. I agree. What I do not accept is the implication that this can only happen if management gives up the ability to close plants or reduce headcount materially.

If the employee side is serious about avoiding closures, then it must present a package that achieves:
- comparable savings,
- permanently lower fixed costs,
- and credible margin improvement.

If not, then investors should not pretend that protecting every site is an economically neutral choice. It has a cost, and that cost must be paid somewhere.

## My bottom line
Here is where I stand after listening to everyone:

- **I am open to an alternative package**
- **I am not open to preserving the current structure**
- **I am not open to a purely temporary bridge**
- **I am not open to a deal that leaves the cost base uncompetitive**
- **I am not open to guarantees that block real restructuring**

What would move me is a package with all of the following:
1. **A measurable and durable reduction in capacity**
2. **A material employee contribution**
3. **A lower structural labor-cost base**
4. **A credible path to improved return on sales**
5. **Freedom to reallocate investment to future products and technology**
6. **Enough flexibility to implement the change without endless delay**

If the works council and union want to keep closures off the table, then they need to replace them with something that delivers the same economic result. Not a softer version. Not a temporary version. The same result.

If that cannot be done, then I will continue to argue that closures and stronger headcount reduction remain necessary.
