From a restructuring and turnaround perspective, the core question is not whether some form of adjustment is needed — it clearly is — but **how much capacity must leave the system and how much labour-cost relief is required for the German footprint to be competitive by 2030**.

## Bottom-line assessment

Based on the facts presented, I would treat the following as the **minimum credible restructuring logic**:

- **real capacity reduction**, not just temporary underutilisation;
- **durable fixed-cost removal**;
- **meaningful labour-cost relief** through wage moderation, working-time flexibility, and benefits/guarantee changes;
- **headcount reduction predominantly via voluntary and natural attrition channels where possible**, but with the possibility that voluntary-only measures may be insufficient;
- **product and volume concentration onto fewer German lines/sites** to lift utilisation;
- **binding productivity and operating-flexibility measures** tied to 2030 milestones.

## What is demonstrated by the facts

The facts support these conclusions:

1. **The company has a structural competitiveness problem.**  
   That is explicit: shrinking European demand, Chinese/lower-cost competition, and German factory costs above target and above peers.

2. **Temporary fixes are unlikely to be enough.**  
   The company needs a durable lower cost base, not a one-year bridge.

3. **Labour cost alone will not solve the issue.**  
   Even if wages are adjusted, the problem also includes overcapacity and low utilisation.

4. **A pure “no closures, no redundancies” position is hard to square with a deep turnaround.**  
   If the current footprint is genuinely overbuilt, simply deferring closure decisions does not remove fixed costs.

## Reasonable inference

Given those facts, my reasonable inference is:

### 1) Capacity reduction will likely need to be material
A credible 2030 turnaround for a mature German OEM in a shrinking European market usually requires one or more of:
- closing or materially downsizing at least one site,
- concentrating production into fewer plants,
- eliminating one or more shifts,
- or removing substantial installed capacity that is no longer needed.

If the goal is competitiveness by 2030, I would not regard a restructuring as credible unless it achieves roughly **10–20% effective installed-capacity reduction in the German footprint**, or an economically equivalent reduction in output capability and fixed cost.  
That is not a precise forecast — it is a turnaround benchmark. The exact number depends on product mix, EV ramp, and utilisation.

### 2) Labour-cost relief likely needs to be significant but not necessarily dramatic in nominal wages
The company does not necessarily need a broad, permanent across-the-board wage cut if other mechanisms are large enough. However, I would expect some combination of:
- wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- reduction/removal of legacy protections,
- longer working time or higher annual hours,
- shift flexibility,
- temporary sacrifices in allowances or premium pay,
- and slower progression of labour cost per vehicle,

to amount to a **meaningful recurring labour-cost relief**, not a symbolic concession.

As a rough restructuring view, I would want to see **mid-single-digit to low-double-digit percentage labour-cost reduction per unit** at the German sites, relative to current run-rate, once all measures are fully phased in. If the underlying labour cost base is very distorted, the required change could be larger.

### 3) Headcount reduction alone is not enough unless it comes with utilisation gains
If headcount falls but the same number of sites and lines remain largely fixed, the company may not capture enough savings. For a manufacturing turnaround, the important metric is **cost per vehicle at normalised utilisation**, not just absolute employee count.

## What combinations could credibly achieve the objective

### Option A: Deep consolidation without formal closures
This is credible only if it includes all of the following:
- one or more sites lose major volume or shifts;
- production is transferred to fewer plants;
- line rationalisation is real and permanent;
- significant voluntary exits / retirement bridges;
- working-time changes and premium-pay restraint;
- product concentration to raise utilisation;
- management overhead and support-function reductions.

This can work **if the fixed-cost base actually falls**.  
But if “no closure” means only soft reductions and promises, it will probably be insufficient.

### Option B: One or more plant closures plus broader network simplification
This is the cleanest way to remove excess installed capacity and fixed cost, and it typically provides the strongest economics. It also carries the highest labour, political, and governance friction.

From a pure turnaround standpoint, if the network is materially overbuilt, a closure can be the most efficient way to achieve:
- durable capacity reduction,
- lower overhead absorption,
- better utilisation at the retained sites,
- and clearer 2030 economics.

### Option C: Time-bound restructuring compact with escalation
This is viable as a bridge, but only if it is disciplined:
- immediate labour-cost and flexibility measures now;
- measurable milestones for utilisation, margin, and savings;
- a pre-agreed trigger for deeper site action if targets are missed.

This is often the best **negotiation architecture**, but it is not a substitute for actual capacity reduction if the business case later proves insufficient.

## What I would not regard as sufficient

I would be skeptical of any package that relies mainly on:
- wage restraint alone,
- short-time work or temporary labour concessions,
- voluntary exits without footprint reduction,
- redeployment without line shutdowns,
- or general “productivity improvements” without quantified fixed-cost removal.

Those measures may help, but they rarely solve a structural overcapacity problem by themselves.

## Practical conclusion

If I were assessing what would likely be required to restore competitiveness by 2030, I would expect the company to need:

- **meaningful German capacity reduction** — likely equivalent to at least one site’s worth of production or a comparable multi-site consolidation effect;
- **durable labour-cost relief** that affects recurring cost, not just one-off cash outflow;
- **higher utilisation at retained sites**;
- **a smaller fixed-cost base**;
- **some headcount reduction, preferably mostly voluntary but not necessarily entirely so**;
- **investment/product commitments** to make the retained footprint viable.

### My neutral judgment
A **no-closure, no-forced-redundancy** settlement could only be credible if it delivers **closure-like savings and capacity removal** through consolidation, line shutdowns, and shift eliminations. If it does not, then it is unlikely to restore competitiveness by 2030.
