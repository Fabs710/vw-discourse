On the facts given, the minimum credible route to restore competitiveness by 2030 is **not** “no action” and probably **not** wage relief alone. It is a package that combines:

- **material capacity reduction**
- **durable fixed-cost reduction**
- **labour-cost relief**
- **higher utilisation and productivity**
- **workforce flexibility and redeployment**
- and, if necessary, **one or more site rationalisations**

## My assessment

### What is demonstrated by the scenario
- Management says the German cost base is structurally too high.
- The company faces shrinking demand, strong low-cost competition, and margin pressure.
- The brand is missing its return-on-sales target.
- The governance structure makes forced change difficult, so a durable settlement has to be both **economically sufficient** and **politically executable**.

That is enough to conclude that a **meaningful restructuring is required**.

### What is a reasonable inference
Given the scale of the problem, a turnaround by 2030 likely requires:
- **a lower German production footprint**
- **fewer shifts / fewer lines / fewer fixed-cost units**
- **headcount reduction**
- **some form of labour-cost moderation**
- and **productivity gains that are real, not aspirational**

A pure “flexibility only” or “temporary contribution only” solution is unlikely to close a structural gap of this kind.

### What is uncertain
We do **not** have the critical plant-level data:
- current utilisation by plant,
- fixed cost per vehicle,
- model allocation,
- break-even volumes,
- labour cost per unit,
- logistics and tooling constraints,
- and the savings from each alternative scenario.

Without that, any precise closure count would be speculative. So the right answer is a **range** and a **package logic**, not a fake level of precision.

# What capacity reduction is likely required

For a large mature carmaker with German overcapacity and above-target labour costs, a credible 2030 turnaround typically requires:

- **roughly a 10% to 20% reduction in domestic production capacity**
- or an equivalent reduction in fixed-cost footprint through consolidation, shift removal, and line stoppage

If the current German footprint is materially underutilised, the nearer end of that range may still be insufficient unless paired with stronger labour-cost relief and product concentration.

## In operational terms, that usually means:
- removing at least **one substantial production unit’s worth of capacity**, or
- consolidating output from multiple sites into fewer core lines, with real volume transfer and fixed-cost elimination

If the company tries to preserve all sites in full operating form, the cost savings are likely too small.

---

# What labour-cost relief is likely required

A durable turnaround by 2030 would usually need a combination equivalent to:

- **mid-single-digit to low-double-digit percentage reduction in labour cost per vehicle** in the German base
- plus working-time and flexibility measures that lower paid idle time and improve utilisation
- plus headcount adjustment that reduces the wage bill structurally

That could come from a mix of:
- moderated wage growth or temporary wage restraint
- bonus and allowance reductions
- longer working time for the same fixed pay in selected units
- elimination or dilution of historical guarantees
- voluntary exits / early retirement
- redeployment rather than external replacement
- productivity gains from process simplification and automation

### Important distinction
- **Temporary concessions** help liquidity and near-term cash flow.
- **Permanent cost-base reduction** is what restores competitiveness.

If the goal is 2030 competitiveness, the package must produce recurring annual savings, not just a one-year bridge.

# Which combinations could credibly work

## Option 1: Closure-led restructuring
This is the strongest economic lever.

### Credible if:
- one or more plants are structurally uncompetitive
- consolidation can remove enough fixed cost quickly
- the company can absorb the industrial-relations and political cost

### Likely outcome:
- strongest savings
- clearest capacity reduction
- highest execution risk
- highest labour and governance resistance

This is the most straightforward route economically, but not necessarily the most executable in this governance setting.

---

## Option 2: Deep consolidation without formal closure
This is the most plausible compromise if closures are politically blocked.

### It must include:
- shutdown of lines or shifts
- concentration of product allocation into fewer plants
- substantial voluntary exits / early retirement
- genuine reduction in installed capacity
- binding productivity commitments

### What it can achieve:
If done hard enough, it can approximate closure-level economics **only if** it removes real fixed cost and real capacity.  
A cosmetic “no closure” plan that merely spreads the pain will not be enough.

### Key limitation:
This works only if management can actually take out enough cost. If the sites remain broadly intact, savings will be too small.

---

## Option 3: Time-bound restructuring compact with deferred escalation
This is a credible sequencing tool, not a solution by itself.

### Structure:
- immediate labour measures now
- plant-by-plant economics review
- milestones for savings and utilisation
- escalation to deeper cuts or shutdowns if targets are missed

### Why it can work:
- buys time
- reduces immediate conflict
- keeps pressure on both sides
- lets the company test whether alternatives are sufficient

### Risk:
If the triggers are weak, it becomes a delay tactic.

---

# My view on the minimum credible package

If the objective is to restore competitiveness by 2030 without relying on closures as the primary lever, the company would likely need all of the following:

1. **Capacity reduction equivalent to at least 10%–20% of domestic output**
2. **Headcount reduction large enough to eliminate surplus fixed labour cost**
3. **Material labour-cost moderation**, likely in the form of:
   - wage restraint,
   - bonus cuts,
   - working-time changes,
   - and removal or softening of legacy guarantees
4. **High voluntary separation / redeployment share**
5. **Product and investment commitments** to retained plants
6. **Enforceable milestones** so the savings are real by 2030

If the company cannot achieve something in that range, then competitiveness is unlikely to be restored.

---

# Practical conclusion

## Most likely economically sufficient paths
- **Best economically:** plant closure(s) or equivalent shutdown(s), plus labour-cost relief
- **Best politically executable if closures are blocked:** deep consolidation without formal closure, but only if it removes substantial capacity and fixed cost
- **Weakest:** no-closure, no-forced-redundancy package built mainly on temporary concessions and productivity promises

## Bottom line
A credible 2030 turnaround probably requires:
- **real capacity reduction**
- **durable labour-cost relief**
- and **site-level restructuring**, whether formally called closures or not.
