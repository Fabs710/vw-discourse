Management, investors, and even the experts keep trying to reframe this as if the only “real” restructuring is one that begins with plant closures. I reject that framing.

Let me state my position clearly in response to the round 2 arguments:

## To Management
You say the German cost base is structurally too high. I do not deny there is pressure. But you still have not demonstrated that **closures or compulsory redundancies are necessary** rather than merely the most convenient option for management.

The expert opinions you invoked did **not** say closures are the only route. They said closures are the most straightforward route, or that a no-closure solution is usually the weakest economically. That is not the same as proving necessity.

You are asking us to accept irreversible damage to sites and livelihoods on the basis of a preferred management shortcut. We will not do that.

What you still owe us is a **plant-by-plant business case** and a serious comparison of alternatives. Until then, “we need closures” is a demand, not evidence.

## To the Union
I agree with you on the essentials:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of collective bargaining,
- no one-sided wage dumping.

Where I need to be careful is this: I do **not** accept that we should bargain away the employment-security framework just to create room for management’s restructuring ambitions. Temporary contributions, yes — if they are truly temporary, fair, and matched by binding guarantees. Permanent weakening of worker protections, no.

So I support your resistance, but I will not let “shared sacrifice” become a code word for workers surrendering their security while management keeps all strategic freedom.

## To the Regional State Government
You say you want a socially responsible path and reject closures as the automatic answer. Good. That is the correct starting point.

But I need you to do more than ask for options. If you really mean it, then use your blocking position to insist on a package that:
- keeps every German plant open,
- avoids forced layoffs,
- and preserves the co-determination and job-security framework.

The state should not become the enabler of closures through procedural pressure or by treating labour as the only side that must prove its case. Management brought this crisis to the table. Management must prove why its preferred solution is unavoidable.

## To the Controlling Family
You say you do not want a closure-first strategy because of the political and industrial damage. On that point, I agree. You know as well as anyone that this company’s strength has always depended on stable labour relations and a domestic industrial base.

But I will be blunt: if you really believe in long-term stewardship, then you should not back any plan that destroys the very workforce and plant network on which that value depends.

If you want value preservation, then back:
- site guarantees to 2030,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- investment commitments,
- product allocation to German plants,
- and a restructuring built on productivity, redeployment, and voluntary measures.

Do not hide behind “economic realism” if the real effect is to shift all risk onto labour.

## To Investors
You want durable savings and deal certainty. I understand that. But there is no certainty in a deal that detonates industrial peace and provokes strikes, political resistance, and a breakdown of trust.

You keep saying “no cosmetic settlement.” Fine — neither do we. But a settlement that preserves every plant, protects jobs, and still delivers productivity gains, temporary contributions, and operational flexibility is not cosmetic. It is the only kind of settlement that can actually last.

The real question is not whether you can write off some capacity in a spreadsheet. The question is whether you can do so without permanently damaging the company’s industrial backbone and governance stability.

## On the experts
I respect the expertise, but I reject the leap from “closure is economically easiest” to “closure is therefore necessary.”

That is a management logic, not a law of nature. A no-closure package may be harder, may require more discipline, and may demand a stronger commitment from all sides. But that does not make it invalid. In fact, in a co-determined company with a blocking state stake and a workforce that will not accept forced redundancies, that is precisely the kind of package that must be seriously tested before anyone talks about closure.

## What we are prepared to discuss
We remain prepared to discuss:
- voluntary exit programs,
- retirement bridges,
- redeployment,
- internal mobility,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary and limited employee contributions,
- productivity and process improvements,
- and binding investment commitments.

But only under the conditions we have stated from the start:
- **no German plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **binding site and job guarantees through 2030**
- **preservation of co-determination and the job-security framework**

## What we are not prepared to accept
We will not accept:
- closure as the default “solution”
- forced layoffs dressed up as structural necessity
- temporary concessions used as a bridge to later closures
- or a restructuring that weakens the workforce to improve accounting ratios

## My direct challenge back to management
If you insist that no-closure restructuring cannot work, then show the proof:
1. Plant-by-plant economics
2. Required savings by 2030
3. What exact measures would be needed under a no-closure scenario
4. Why those measures are allegedly insufficient
5. Why closures would not simply trigger larger costs through conflict, loss of trust, and execution risk

Until you do that, your argument is incomplete.

## My direct challenge back to the facilitator’s “compromise” paths
A “closure deferred” mechanism is not acceptable if it is simply a slow-motion path to the same outcome. That would just postpone the confrontation while keeping plants and workers under permanent threat.

A “consolidation without formal closure” package is only acceptable if it truly means:
- every German plant stays open,
- no site is emptied out,
- no compulsory redundancies are used,
- and the job-security framework remains intact.

If “consolidation” is just closure by another name, then we reject it.

## Bottom line
Our position has not changed:

- **No plant closures in Germany**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Binding job and site security through 2030**
- **Real co-determination in shaping the restructuring**
- **Negotiated, limited, and fair employee contributions only if those protections are in place**

If management wants a serious settlement, it must stop treating closures as the benchmark and start proving that there is a viable alternative. We are ready to defend every German plant and every job. That is not obstruction — that is our mandate.
