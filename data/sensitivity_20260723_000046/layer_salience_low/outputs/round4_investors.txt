I’ll be direct.

## Our position
We support a restructuring that **durably restores margins and competitiveness**. We do **not** support a deal that avoids hard economic adjustment and leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

What matters to us is not whether the solution is called a closure, consolidation, or compact. What matters is whether it delivers:

- **real, recurring savings**
- **durable capacity reduction**
- **lower fixed costs**
- **higher utilisation**
- **credible execution through 2030**
- and a path back to **competitive returns on sales**

A cosmetic peace is not acceptable. A delay tactic is not acceptable. A settlement that keeps the cost base too high is not acceptable.

---

## Where we agree

### With management
We agree that:
- the problem is structural, not temporary;
- temporary concessions alone are insufficient;
- and investment/future product allocation must be part of the package.

That said, management still needs to prove that its preferred path actually maximises value after execution risk, not just on paper.

### With the family holding
We agree that:
- a purely symbolic compromise is value-destructive;
- and industrial peace has value only if it supports competitiveness.

We also agree that the company cannot preserve the current footprint by default if that means preserving an uncompetitive cost structure.

### With the expert input
We agree with the core economic conclusion:
- **no-action is not credible**
- **labour-cost relief alone is not enough**
- **meaningful capacity and fixed-cost reduction is required**

That is the central point the market needs to see.

---

## Where we disagree

### To the Works Council
We do **not** accept the insistence that “no German plant closures” should function as a starting principle or quasi-veto.

That is not a restructuring strategy; it is a constraint. If the company is overbuilt, then some sites may be underutilised or subscale. If that is the case, the market should not be asked to fund preservation for its own sake.

We are not saying every plant must close. We are saying:
- if deep consolidation can remove real fixed cost and installed capacity, that is fine;
- if it cannot, then closures or equivalent shutdowns remain legitimate options.

What we will not accept is a package that:
- preserves all sites,
- avoids forced redundancies,
- preserves guarantees in full,
- and still expects the market to believe margins will recover.

That is not credible.

### To the Union
We agree that workers should not bear the burden alone, and permanent wage dumping would be unacceptable. But the union still hasn’t shown a labour package that clearly closes the gap at scale.

Voluntary exits, working-time changes, redeployment, and temporary contributions are useful tools. They are not sufficient unless they are:
- large enough,
- durable enough,
- and tied to real footprint reduction.

Temporary pain relief is not the same as structural repair.

### To the Regional State Government
We understand the political and social constraints. But the state cannot use its blocking position to freeze the necessary adjustment and still expect a viable company by 2030.

If you want to preserve industrial employment, you need to support the option that actually restores competitiveness. That may mean accepting rationalisation at some sites if the evidence supports it.

---

## What would move us
We would support a package if it credibly demonstrates one of the following:

1. **Deep consolidation without formal closures** that still produces:
   - equivalent recurring annual savings,
   - real capacity reduction,
   - a lower fixed-cost base,
   - and measurable margin improvement by 2030;

or

2. **Closure / shutdown of structurally non-viable capacity**, if the numbers show that consolidation cannot achieve the same result.

But the burden of proof is on those opposing closures to show that their alternative is economically equivalent, not merely politically preferable.

---

## What is not enough
We would not support:
- a no-closure settlement with weak savings;
- temporary concessions sold as long-term competitiveness;
- guarantees that lock in the current cost structure;
- or an outcome that leaves return on sales structurally impaired.

If the company is still carrying too much fixed cost in 2029, then this process will have failed, regardless of how peacefully it was concluded.

- closure,
- deep consolidation without formal closure,
- and a time-bound restructuring compact,

including:
- fixed-cost removal,
- utilisation effects,
- break-even volumes,
- logistics/tooling constraints,
- execution risk,
- and expected recurring savings by 2030.

Without that, the discussion remains too abstract for capital allocation purposes.

---

## Bottom line
Our standard is simple:

- **Yes** to hard restructuring
- **Yes** to real capacity reduction
- **Yes** to durable labour-cost relief if it is material
- **Yes** to investment and product commitments
- **Yes** to evidence-based site rationalisation if needed
- **No** to cosmetic settlement
- **No** to structurally impaired margins
- **No** to a blanket no-closure principle if it blocks competitiveness

We are not here to protect any party’s comfort. We are here to protect long-term shareholder value. If the numbers support deep consolidation, we can live with that. If they do not, then the market should not be asked to finance avoidance.
