## 1. Decision context

The company needs a durable restructuring to restore competitiveness by 2030. The central decision is no longer whether change is necessary, but whether the German footprint can be made viable through deep consolidation without formal plant closures and compulsory redundancies, or whether the economics require some irreversible footprint reduction.

Governance is decisive: co-determination gives labour an effective veto, the controlling family can steer but not simply impose, the regional state has blocking influence, and investors can pressure but not command. This makes a negotiated package essential, with any durable solution needing both economic credibility and political/social survivability.

---

## 2. Stakeholder assembly

Higher-standing stakeholders were weighted more heavily in the synthesis:
- Group & brand management
- Works council
- Industrial union
- Regional state government
- Controlling family holding
- Institutional investors
- Independent expert views

Minority positions were recorded, but positions with formal governance power and direct operational legitimacy were given greater weight.

## 3. Final positions

### Group & brand management
Management holds that the cost base and overcapacity are structurally too high and that durable savings must be large enough to restore competitiveness. It accepts plant-by-plant review and is open to deep consolidation, but insists that closures and compulsory redundancies remain on the table if alternatives fail to remove enough recurring cost and capacity.

### Works council
The works council rejects plant closures and compulsory redundancies as a starting point and insists that management has not proven closures are necessary. It is open to voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time changes, and limited labour contributions, but only within a framework of binding site and job guarantees through 2030 and protection of co-determination.

### Industrial union
The union aligns with the works council on no forced redundancies, no closures, and no dismantling of collective bargaining. It is willing to discuss capped, temporary employee contributions if they are matched by durable guarantees and real burden-sharing by management and owners, but not if workers are financing their own insecurity.

### Regional state government
The state supports a hard restructuring but opposes making closures the default answer. It favors plant-by-plant review, meaningful capacity adjustment, and reciprocal burden-sharing, while insisting that any settlement preserve the region’s industrial anchor and avoid gutting employment or co-determination.

### Controlling family holding
The family supports a hard restructuring and agrees the current cost structure is uncompetitive. It does not endorse a blanket no-closure veto, but also rejects a closure-first script that would create avoidable value destruction through conflict and political escalation; it wants evidence-based site decisions and a survivable path.

### Institutional investors
Investors want real, recurring savings, lower fixed costs, and durable capacity reduction by 2030. They reject cosmetic compromise and are willing to accept deep consolidation without formal closures only if it delivers closure-like economics; otherwise, closures or equivalent shutdowns remain legitimate.

### Expert: financial restructuring / industrial turnaround
The expert concludes that the company needs real capacity reduction, durable fixed-cost removal, and meaningful labour-cost relief. A no-closure settlement is only credible if it achieves roughly closure-like economics through shift eliminations, line shutdowns, volume concentration, and measurable savings.

### Expert: plant-level industrial economist
The plant-level economist is more specific: a credible turnaround likely requires effective installed-capacity reduction in the domestic footprint on the order of 10–20%, or an economically equivalent reduction in output capability and fixed cost. Deep consolidation can work only if it truly removes capacity and recurring cost at scale.

---

## 4. Genuine conflicts

1. **No closures vs. economic sufficiency**  
   Labour-side stakeholders treat no closures as a red line; management, investors, and experts say that is acceptable only if the package removes enough real capacity and fixed cost. These positions are structurally incompatible unless a closure-equivalent consolidation package is found.

2. **Job guarantees vs. restructuring credibility**  
   Labour seeks binding employment and site guarantees through 2030; management argues that such guarantees become unrealistic if they block necessary restructuring. Both cannot hold in full if the company is genuinely overbuilt.

3. **Voluntary adjustment vs. compulsory measures**  
   Labour wants voluntary exits, redeployment, and flexibility; management insists those may be insufficient and reserves compulsory redundancies/closures as fallback tools. The disagreement is about the minimum mechanism capable of delivering durable savings.

4. **Political protection vs. governance realism**  
   The state wants to defend the industrial base and avoid closures, but also cannot support a package that leaves the company uncompetitive. Its blocking power creates leverage, but not immunity from economic constraints.

---

## 5. Areas of convergence

- All parties agree the company has a real competitiveness and cost-base problem.
- All reject purely cosmetic or temporary fixes.
- All accept that investment and future product allocation must be part of any settlement.
- All acknowledge that prolonged conflict and uncertainty would be damaging.
- All are open, in principle, to plant-by-plant fact-finding and scenario comparison.
- There is shared recognition that employee contributions, if any, must be limited, meaningful, and reciprocal.

---

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker

1. **Speed vs. legitimacy**  
   Closures can deliver the cleanest economics, but they are politically and socially costly. A negotiated consolidation path may be slower but more durable.

2. **Footprint preservation vs. fixed-cost reduction**  
   Preserving all sites may satisfy labour and the state, but only if it does not leave the company structurally uncompetitive.

3. **Voluntary measures vs. enforceability**  
   Voluntary exits and flexibility are less disruptive, but may not produce sufficient savings without hard triggers or fallback options.

4. **Labour peace vs. transformation depth**  
   A deal that avoids confrontation may underdeliver economically; a deeper restructuring may trigger industrial conflict and governance blockage.

5. **Symbolic closure avoidance vs. real economic equivalence**  
   “No closure” can be acceptable only if the package genuinely achieves closure-like savings and capacity reduction.

---

## 7. Integrative pathways

### Pathway A: Plant-by-plant proof process
Launch an independent site-by-site economic review using agreed criteria: utilisation, break-even volume, fixed cost per unit, logistics/tooling constraints, and savings potential under multiple scenarios. Closures would only be considered where alternatives fail an agreed threshold.

### Pathway B: No-closure compact with closure-equivalent economics
Keep sites formally open but implement hard capacity reduction: shift removals, line shutdowns, volume transfers, voluntary exits, early retirement, working-time changes, and product concentration. This only works if the savings are measurable and durable.

### Pathway C: Time-bound restructuring compact with escalation
Adopt a phased agreement: immediate labour contributions and flexibility, binding investment commitments, and objective review milestones. If the targets are missed, pre-agreed escalation to deeper site rationalisation is triggered.

### Pathway D: Reciprocal guarantees package
Tie limited employee contributions to binding site, employment, product, and investment guarantees; pair them with management/owner commitments on overhead reduction and capital support.

- Can deep consolidation without closures truly match closure-level savings?
- What temporary employee contribution is defensible, and for how long?
- What binding guarantees can be credibly offered without freezing necessary restructuring?
- What objective triggers would justify escalation from consolidation to closure?

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction

1. **The dispute narrowed from “whether to restructure” to “what counts as enough restructuring.”**  
   Early rounds established shared diagnosis; later rounds exposed the real conflict as the acceptable mechanism of adjustment.

2. **“No closures” is not inherently a full solution or a full obstruction.**  
   Through the exchange, it became clear that no-closure positions can be economically credible only if they deliver closure-like fixed-cost and capacity reductions.

3. **Plant-level economics became the key missing evidence.**  
   Multiple stakeholders converged on the need for site-by-site proof, showing that abstract principles were masking a lack of comparative operational data.

4. **The family holding occupies a pivotal middle position.**  
   It does not want a closure-first script, but it also rejects a blanket preservation mandate. This makes it a potential broker if evidence is credible.

5. **Political legitimacy and industrial legitimacy are both blocking forces.**  
   The state and labour can stop bad deals, but cannot by themselves make the company competitive; management and investors can define economic targets, but cannot impose them unilaterally.

6. **A durable deal likely requires a “closure-equivalent” test.**  
   The interaction made explicit that any non-closure package must be judged against the savings, utilization, and fixed-cost outcomes closures would have delivered.

7. **Conflict costs are now part of the economic equation.**  
   The parties increasingly acknowledged that prolonged strikes, distrust, and governance escalation could destroy as much value as poorly designed closures.
