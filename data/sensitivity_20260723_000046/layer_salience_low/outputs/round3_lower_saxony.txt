I will speak plainly.

I agree with everyone on one point: **the company needs a real restructuring, not a cosmetic pause**. The expert inputs make that clear. If the German footprint is structurally too expensive and underutilized, then doing nothing or relying only on temporary concessions would be irresponsible.

But I do **not** accept the conclusion that therefore plant closures and compulsory redundancies must become the default answer.

## To management
You have made the strongest case for the existence of a problem, but not yet a convincing case that **closures are unavoidable as the first move**.

What I accept:
- the cost base is too high,
- capacity must come down,
- savings must be durable,
- and investment in future products must be part of the deal.

What I do **not** accept:
- treating closures as the benchmark solution,
- using “all options on the table” as leverage to force acceptance of a closure-first path,
- or demanding worker sacrifice before you have shown why a deep consolidation package cannot deliver comparable results.

If you want the regional state to support a tough restructuring, then bring forward **plant-by-plant economics** and compare:
1. no closure / deep restructuring,
2. consolidation and line reductions,
3. closure where truly unavoidable.

Until that comparison is on the table, your case for closures is not proven. It is asserted.

## To the works council
I strongly support your rejection of **compulsory redundancies** and your insistence that German sites must not be sacrificed lightly. That is entirely consistent with our responsibility as an anchor shareholder and regional actor.

Where I am more cautious is this: a blanket “no closures, no matter what” position can become a blockade if it is not paired with a credible alternative that really cuts capacity and fixed costs. The expert assessment says that a pure no-closure package is usually the weakest option unless the savings are unusually large.

So I will support:
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- redeployment,
- shift reductions,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary and fair employee contributions,
- and serious productivity measures.

But I will not support an arrangement that simply preserves the current footprint and postpones the real adjustment. If we want to protect jobs long term, the business must remain viable long term.

## To the union
I agree that workers should not be made to finance their own insecurity, and I agree that any contribution must be:
- limited,
- time-bound,
- fair,
- and matched by commitments from management and owners.

I also agree that the collective bargaining framework cannot be casually dismantled in the name of competitiveness.

But I do not accept the implication that the entire burden can be solved through wage architecture and temporary concessions. The industrial economics experts were clear: **labour cost relief alone is unlikely to be enough** unless it is very large and paired with capacity reduction.

So the union’s contribution menu is useful, but incomplete unless it is tied to a footprint that is genuinely viable.

## To the controlling family
You are right to worry about conflict, trust, and value destruction. I share that concern.

But long-term stewardship also means not allowing the company to drift into a structure that cannot compete. A plan that preserves every site regardless of economics is not stewardship either.

What I need from ownership is clarity:
- if management proves that a site is structurally non-viable, will you support closure?
- or will you insist on preserving every plant regardless of the numbers?

I am not asking for symbolic toughness. I am asking whether you will back a serious, economically grounded restructuring if it becomes necessary.

## To investors
I understand your demand for durable savings and deal certainty. That is legitimate.

But “durable” does not automatically mean “closure-first.” It means the company must have a footprint and cost base that actually works by 2030. If that can be achieved through deep consolidation without formal closures, that is preferable from my perspective because it preserves the company as a regional anchor and reduces social and political damage.

If it cannot be achieved, then we need to see the evidence clearly. But I will not endorse closures as a first assumption.

## My position
Let me be clear on where I stand:

### I am prepared to support
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- voluntary and socially responsible headcount adjustment,
- redeployment and qualification,
- shift and working-time changes,
- temporary, capped employee contributions,
- binding investment commitments,
- and a negotiated industrial roadmap.

### I am **not** prepared to support
- German plant closures as the default solution,
- compulsory redundancies as the starting point,
- a restructuring that guts regional employment,
- or any outcome that sidelines the state’s co-determination and veto role.

That is not a tactical preference. It is the core of my mandate.

## What would move me
I would be open to a tougher package if management can show, in a credible and quantified way, that:
- a deep consolidation package cannot meet the required savings,
- or that a specific site is truly unavoidable to adjust,
- and that any structural reduction is accompanied by real investment, product allocation, and employment protection for the remaining footprint.

Without that proof, I will continue to oppose closure-led restructuring.

## On the compromise paths raised by the moderator
- A **“proof first”** process is reasonable, and I support it.
- A **consolidation without formal closure** package is worth serious examination, but only if it removes real capacity and not just appearances.
- A **deferred closure trigger** is acceptable only if it is not a slow-motion threat designed to break resistance; it must be based on clear, independent criteria.

## Bottom line
I support a **serious, durable restructuring**.

I do **not** support:
- making closures the starting point,
- forcing layoffs onto the workforce,
- or treating regional industrial capacity as disposable.

If the economics truly require difficult decisions, then management must prove that honestly and transparently. Until then, the burden of proof remains on those who want to close plants, not on those defending jobs and sites.
