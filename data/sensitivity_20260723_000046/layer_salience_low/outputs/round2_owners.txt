I appreciate the expert’s assessment, because it confirms what the governance side has been saying all along: **the company needs a durable reduction in fixed cost and capacity**, not a cosmetic pause.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, investors, and the expert that:

- the current German cost base is structurally too high;
- the business cannot remain competitive by preserving the status quo;
- temporary concessions alone will not solve the problem;
- and any settlement must be durable enough to protect value through 2030 and beyond.

I also agree with the regional state that a solution must survive the real governance environment. A plan that collapses into prolonged industrial conflict or political deadlock is not a solution; it is value destruction.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** agree that plant closures should be treated as the default or first answer.

The expert said something important: closures are the **most straightforward** route, not the only conceivable one. That distinction matters. In a company with co-determination, a blocking state stake, and a controlling family that must think in decades, the “cleanest” spreadsheet answer can be the worst ownership answer if it triggers:

- strike escalation,
- reputational damage,
- political intervention,
- loss of trust,
- supplier disruption,
- and years of weakened industrial relations.

So I am not prepared to endorse a closure-first strategy simply because it is easiest to model.

## To management
You are right that we need **material and durable savings**. But I want to push back on the idea that this requires an immediate acceptance of closures as the baseline.

What I need from management is not rhetoric about “all options,” but a **plant-by-plant economic case**. If a site is truly unsustainable, show it clearly. If alternatives can achieve comparable savings, show that too.

At this stage, I do **not** see evidence that closures are the only way to reach the needed cost reset. I do see a need for:

- shift reductions,
- line consolidation,
- product rationalisation,
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- and a serious productivity programme.

If those measures can deliver the required savings, they are preferable to closures because they preserve value and reduce instability.

But I am also not willing to support a package that preserves the full current footprint and merely buys time with temporary labour concessions. That would be a delay, not a restructuring.

## To the Works Council
I understand and respect the red lines on forced redundancies and the desire to keep German sites open. I also understand why you insist on binding guarantees.

But I have to be equally direct: **a blanket refusal of any meaningful capacity reduction is not sustainable**.

The expert’s view makes that clear. If the company really is overbuilt for the market ahead, then some reduction in installed capacity is unavoidable. The real question is whether that adjustment happens:

- through managed restructuring,
- or through a much worse future crisis.

So I cannot support a position that says “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no hard structural change” and leaves the company with the same economics. That would protect the appearance of stability while eroding the business underneath it.

What I can support is a serious effort to preserve sites where possible through:
- voluntary measures,
- redeployment,
- internal flexibility,
- and negotiated labour contributions.

But those must be part of a package that genuinely lowers the structural cost base.

## To the Union
I agree that worker contributions must be **limited, negotiated, and matched**. I also agree that permanent wage dumping would be unacceptable and would undermine labour peace.

Where I disagree is with any implication that the company can solve this mostly through bargaining generosity from labour while leaving the operating structure largely intact.

The expert was clear: labour-cost relief alone is unlikely to be enough unless it is unusually large and paired with capacity change. That means the union should not treat this as a fight over wage architecture alone. The issue is also footprint, utilisation, and product allocation.

If the workforce is asked to contribute, I will expect management to commit to:
- concrete investment,
- product commitments,
- and a credible industrial roadmap.

But those commitments must sit alongside real operational change, not instead of it.

## To the regional state government
I hear your insistence that closures must not be treated as the automatic answer. I share that caution. Your request for plant-by-plant scenarios is reasonable, and I support transparency.

But I also need to be candid: the burden of proof cannot fall only on management. If the alternative packages are serious, they must be quantified. If they are not, then we should not pretend they are equivalent to structural restructuring.

So yes, I support your call for options. But I will not support a process that uses the demand for options as a way to block difficult decisions indefinitely.

## To investors
You are right to insist on durable savings and deal certainty. I support that. But certainty is not the same as maximum closure. A settlement that preserves enterprise value, reduces fixed costs, and avoids a destructive conflict can be more valuable than a “clean” restructuring that creates years of instability.

The investor test should be:
- does the package restore margins,
- does it lower the fixed cost base,
- and does it do so without permanently impairing the enterprise?

That is the standard I am using.

## My position after this round
I am prepared to back a **serious negotiated restructuring**, but not a closure-first script.

That means I can support:
- material capacity reduction,
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary and meaningful employee contributions,
- productivity improvements,
- and binding investment commitments.

What I cannot support is:
- a permanent impairment of enterprise value,
- a breakdown of labour peace,
- or a strategy that depends on forcing closures through confrontation rather than proving necessity.

## My request for the next step
Before any hard commitment on closures, I want management to produce:

1. **A plant-by-plant economic analysis**
2. **A comparison of savings under three packages**
   - no closures with deep restructuring
   - partial consolidation / line reduction
   - closure where unavoidable
3. **A clear statement of what savings are needed by 2030**
4. **A quantified employee contribution**
5. **A binding investment and product roadmap**

If the numbers show that closures are truly unavoidable, then we can discuss that honestly. But I will not start from that conclusion, and I will not endorse unnecessary destruction of industrial value.

So my position is clear:

- **Yes** to durable restructuring.
- **Yes** to real cost and capacity reduction.
- **No** to assuming closures are the first or only route.
- **No** to any outcome that permanently weakens the company’s value or governance stability.
