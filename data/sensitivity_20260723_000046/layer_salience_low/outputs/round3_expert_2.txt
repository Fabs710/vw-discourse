## Bottom line
On the facts given, a credible 2030 turnaround likely requires:

- **material capacity reduction**
- **durable fixed-cost reduction**
- **some labour-cost relief**
- **higher utilisation / better product concentration**
- **workforce flexibility and redeployment**
- and possibly **one or more site rationalisations** if plant-level economics are structurally weak

A **pure no-action** solution is not credible.  
A **pure labour-cost-only** solution is also not credible.

## What I would regard as economically sufficient
Without plant-level data, I would not put a hard number on “the” required closure count. But for a large mature German OEM with visible overcapacity and margin pressure, the minimum credible package would usually need to achieve, in combination:

- **roughly low double-digit percent capacity reduction** in the domestic footprint, or an equivalent fixed-cost removal through consolidation
- **mid-single-digit to low-double-digit percent labour-cost reduction per vehicle** on a recurring basis
- **structural headcount reduction**, not just natural fluctuation
- **higher utilisation at the remaining plants**
- **real product-line concentration**, not just reassignment on paper

If the company cannot get close to that scale, it is unlikely to restore competitiveness by 2030.

## Important correction to the Round 3 framing
The statement that a no-closure package is “the weakest economically unless the savings are modest” is broadly right, but it needs a qualifier:

- A **no-formal-closure** package can be economically strong **if and only if** it removes real capacity and fixed cost through shift eliminations, line shutdowns, product concentration, and binding headcount reduction.
- In that case, it may be **economically close to a closure**, even if not labelled one.

So the real test is not the label. The real test is:
- **does the package eliminate fixed cost and installed capacity, or does it merely defer the problem?**

## What would not be enough
These measures alone would generally be insufficient if the structural gap is large:

- temporary wage restraint only
- bonus cuts only
- voluntary exits only
- redeployment without volume transfer
- working-time flexibility without footprint reduction
- promises of productivity improvement without hard capacity actions

Those can help, but they rarely close a structural overcapacity gap by themselves.

## Execution risk matters
In this governance setting, the economically strongest package is not always the most executable. The practical trade-off is:

- **Closures**: strongest cost effect, highest conflict and implementation risk
- **Deep consolidation without formal closure**: possibly nearly as effective, lower symbolic conflict, but only if it really cuts capacity
- **No-closure/no-forced-layoff package with limited concessions**: lowest conflict, but most likely insufficient economically

## My assessment
If management needs durable savings by 2030, the package should probably include at least one of the following at meaningful scale:

- a site shutdown, or
- a decisive line/shift elimination with real volume transfer, or
- a combination of both plus labour-cost moderation

If none of those occurs materially, I would expect the savings to be too small relative to the structural problem.
