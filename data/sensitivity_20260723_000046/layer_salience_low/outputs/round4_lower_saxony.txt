I will be direct.

I agree with management, investors, and the experts on one essential point: **this company needs a real restructuring, not a cosmetic pause**. The cost base is too high, the market is under pressure, and temporary measures alone will not solve a structural problem.

But I do **not** accept the attempt to turn that diagnosis into a closure-first strategy that treats German plants and jobs as the default adjustment variable.

## To Management
You have made the case that the problem is serious. I do not dispute that.

What I still do **not** see is a convincing proof that **plant closures are necessary as the first or only serious lever**. You keep saying closures are the cleanest or strongest tool. That may be true. It is **not** the same as proving they are unavoidable.

If you want support for hard restructuring, then bring the facts to the table:
- plant-by-plant economics,
- break-even volumes,
- utilisation,
- fixed-cost per unit,
- and a genuine comparison of scenarios.

Show me why deep consolidation, line shutdowns, shift reductions, voluntary exits, redeployment, and working-time changes cannot achieve the required durable savings. Until then, closures are still a preference, not a necessity.

## To the Works Council
I agree with you on the red lines:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no hollowing out of co-determination,
- and no one-sided burden shifting onto workers.

That said, I also need to be clear: a blanket “no closures under any circumstances” position is only defensible if a serious alternative can truly deliver the same economics. I am prepared to defend sites, but I will not defend a solution that preserves the footprint while the business remains uncompetitive.

So I support your demand for proof. But I also expect openness to **real** capacity adjustment if the numbers show it is unavoidable.

## To the Union
I agree that workers must not finance their own insecurity.

Temporary and capped contributions can be discussed only if they are matched by:
- binding investment commitments,
- product allocation to German sites,
- credible job protections,
- and a fair distribution of burden between labour, management, and owners.

What I will not accept is permanent weakening of the collective framework or wage dumping disguised as competitiveness. That would damage the company’s long-term stability.

## To the Controlling Family
You are right that a bad settlement can destroy value. I agree.

But stewardship also means not allowing the company to drift into a structure that cannot compete. So I need a clear answer from ownership: if management proves that a site is structurally non-viable, will you back a serious restructuring decision, or will you insist on preserving every site regardless of the economics?

That question matters. The regional state will not endorse an arrangement that simply transfers the crisis onto workers and communities.

## To Investors
You are right that we need durable savings, not theatre.

But durable does **not** automatically mean “closures first.” Durable means the company ends up with a footprint, cost base, and product allocation that actually work by 2030. If that can be achieved through deep consolidation without formal closures, that is preferable. If it cannot, then the evidence must be clear.

I will support a tough package if it is:
- economically real,
- socially responsible,
- and executable without destroying the company’s regional anchor role.

## My position
Let me restate it plainly:

### I am prepared to support
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- voluntary exits and early retirement,
- redeployment and qualification,
- shift and working-time changes,
- temporary and limited employee contributions,
- binding investment commitments,
- and a plant-by-plant economic review.

### I am **not** prepared to support
- German plant closures as the default answer,
- compulsory redundancies as a starting point,
- or any restructuring that guts regional employment and sidelines co-determination.

That is not rhetoric. It is the core of my mandate.

## What would move me
I would move only if management can show, in hard numbers, that:
1. a deep consolidation package cannot meet the required savings, or
2. a specific site is truly non-viable and cannot be preserved without undermining the company’s future.

If that is proven, then I will engage seriously on the least destructive path that still protects the company’s long-term health and the region’s industrial base.

## What I will not do
I will not sign up to a restructuring that starts from the assumption that the state must accept closures and job destruction because management prefers a faster route.

The state is an anchor shareholder, not a rubber stamp.

We need an independent plant-level industrial economist to compare, site by site, the savings and execution risks of:
- closure,
- deep consolidation without formal closure,
- and a time-bound restructuring compact.

Without that, this debate risks staying at the level of slogans rather than evidence.

## Bottom line
I support a hard, durable restructuring.

I do **not** support making German plant closures the starting point.
I do **not** support compulsory redundancies as a first resort.
I do **not** support weakening co-determination or gutting regional employment.

If the numbers truly require difficult decisions, then prove it openly and plant by plant. If a serious no-closure alternative can deliver the same durable result, I will support it. But I will not abandon the region’s industrial anchor on the basis of assumptions presented as inevitabilities.
