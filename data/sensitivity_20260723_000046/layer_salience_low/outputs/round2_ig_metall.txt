I’ll be equally direct.

## To the Works Council
I agree with you on the essentials:

- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No German plant closures**
- **No one-sided restructuring where workers pay everything**

That remains my line as well.

Where I want to sharpen the point is this: a durable settlement must not just preserve jobs in the abstract; it must also **protect the collective bargaining framework**. If management uses restructuring to permanently weaken wage standards, transfer risk onto employees, or bypass the collective agreement, then that is not a fair compromise — it is a structural defeat for the workforce.

So yes, we are aligned on resisting closures and forced layoffs. But I also insist that any deal must preserve the **collective wage architecture** and not create a precedent for permanent wage dumping disguised as competitiveness.

## To Management
You keep saying closures and structural cuts are necessary. I hear the economic pressure. I do not dismiss it. But you have not shown that **closures are the only route** — only that they are the route that gives management the most unilateral room.

That is not enough.

If you want the workforce to contribute, then the company has to show real willingness to contribute too. That means:

- **no forced layoffs**
- **no plant closures as a starting point**
- **no permanent dismantling of collective standards**
- **binding investment commitments**
- **product and volume guarantees for German sites**
- **management and owners taking their share of sacrifice**

You asked for a concrete package. Here is the union’s position:

1. **Temporary employee contribution** may be discussed  
   - time-limited  
   - clearly capped  
   - linked to site and employment guarantees

2. **Working-time flexibility** can be part of the package  
   - but only with compensation and safeguards  
   - not as a hidden wage cut

3. **Capacity reduction** can be discussed  
   - through redeployment, retirement paths, qualification, and voluntary measures  
   - not through compulsory redundancies

4. **A solidarity-based contribution fund or bonus waiver** can be considered  
   - if management also contributes  
   - and if it protects jobs and investment

What I will not accept is a package where:
- workers give up pay and guarantees,
- management gets structural freedom,
- and the company still reserves the right to close plants later.

That would be asking the workforce to finance its own insecurity.

## To the Regional State Government
I agree with your emphasis on regional responsibility and political reality. But that has to mean more than moral concern. If you have influence in governance, then use it to insist on a **real negotiated solution** rather than letting management define “competitiveness” as job destruction.

I support your rejection of one-sided closures. But I also need you to understand: if the company tries to break collective bargaining norms, the conflict will not stay technical. It will become industrial and political very quickly.

A workable state-backed settlement should include:

- job protection
- site protection
- investment commitments
- and a fair distribution of burden

Not a package that quietly shifts the cost onto the workforce while calling it modernization.

## To the Controlling Family
You say you want long-term value, not conflict. Then the practical implication is simple: **no restructuring strategy should be pursued that depends on forcing the workforce into surrender**.

You are right that cosmetic solutions are not enough. But the answer is not automatically closures. The answer is a negotiated restructuring with real operational changes, real cost contributions, and real investment commitments — without breaking the collective framework.

If the owners want stability, they should back a settlement that workers can live with and that can hold over time. Otherwise you may get short-term accounting gains and long-term industrial damage.

## To Investors
You want durable savings and deal certainty. So do we — but not at any price.

A “credible” plan is not one that simply cuts labour costs the hardest. A credible plan is one that can actually be implemented without detonating the workforce, the co-determination structure, and the company’s reputation in Germany.

If the company forces a confrontation, you may get strikes, delays, and political intervention. That is not certainty; that is value destruction.

So the investor test should be this:

- Is there a **shared-sacrifice package**?
- Is there **binding employment and site protection**?
- Are the savings **durable**?
- Are the concessions **balanced** between labour, management, and owners?
- Does the package preserve the **collective wage framework**?

If yes, the union can support hard but fair adjustments. If no, then there is no deal.

## Our concrete bargaining line
We are prepared to discuss a package built on:

- **time-limited employee contribution**
- **working-time flexibility**
- **voluntary rather than compulsory workforce reduction**
- **redeployment and qualification**
- **temporary bonus waivers or solidarity contributions**
- **capacity adjustment without closures**
- **binding investment and product commitments**
- **employment and site security through 2030**

But we will not agree to:

- plant closures
- forced redundancies
- permanent wage dumping
- dismantling of the collective agreement
- open-ended insecurity

## Bottom line
We are not saying “nothing changes.”  
We are saying **change must be shared, fair, and reversible where possible**.

If management wants serious cooperation, it must stop treating closures and forced cuts as the benchmark solution. Bring forward a package where the company, owners, and management also contribute — and where the workforce is not made to bear the full cost of restructuring.

If that is on the table, we can negotiate. If not, we will oppose it with everything available to us.
