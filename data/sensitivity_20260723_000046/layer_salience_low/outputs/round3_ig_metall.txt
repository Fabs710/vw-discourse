I’ll be direct.

## To the Works Council
I agree with you on the core red lines:

- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no German plant closures**
- **no dismantling of collective bargaining**

That remains my position as well.

Where I want to sharpen the point is this: we must not let “restructuring” become a code word for **workers absorbing all the pain while management preserves maximum strategic freedom**. If the burden-sharing package is fair, then employees can contribute. If it is one-sided, it is not a settlement — it is a transfer of risk onto labour.

I also agree with you that temporary concessions must be truly **temporary, capped, and matched by binding guarantees**. But I will not accept any arrangement that weakens the collective wage framework permanently in the name of flexibility.

## To Management
You keep saying the cost base is structurally too high. I do not deny the pressure. But you still have not proven that **closures are necessary**, rather than simply the most convenient way to force the issue.

The experts said closures are the most straightforward route. That is not the same as saying they are unavoidable.

So here is the challenge back to you:

- Show the **plant-by-plant economics**
- Show the **required recurring savings by 2030**
- Show why alternatives cannot deliver them
- Show what a **no-closure or consolidation package** would look like in numbers
- Show why that package would fail

Until then, “we need closures” is a preference, not a proof.

And let me be equally clear: I am **not** open to a deal that uses restructuring to permanently weaken wages, hollow out collective agreements, or create a precedent for labour dumping disguised as competitiveness.

## To the Regional State Government
I appreciate that you reject a purely cosmetic compromise. So do I.

But if you have a blocking stake and real governance influence, then use it to insist on a deal that **protects industrial employment in Germany** instead of acting as a pressure point for forced cuts.

You asked for a socially responsible path. Good — then back a settlement that includes:

- no forced redundancies
- site protection
- investment commitments
- and a fair distribution of burden

Do not ask the workforce to prove its loyalty while management’s preferred solution remains the default.

## To the Controlling Family
You are right that a bad deal can destroy value. But a deal that destroys trust, wages, and job security also destroys value — just more slowly.

If you want stewardship, then support a restructuring that workers can live with. That means:

- no closure-first script
- no one-sided burden shifting
- binding commitments on investment and future products
- and a settlement that preserves the collective framework

If ownership wants long-term value, it should not back an approach that makes industrial peace impossible.

## To Investors
You want durable savings, not symbolism. I understand that.

But durable does not automatically mean closures. Durable means the company remains viable in 2030 and beyond **without breaking the workforce and governance structure** that make it workable in the first place.

A settlement based on:
- voluntary exits,
- working-time flexibility,
- limited employee contributions,
- productivity measures,
- product allocation,
- and meaningful management/owner contribution

is not cosmetic if it is properly designed and enforced.

What I will not accept is a package where the workforce makes concessions first, and management keeps the option to close plants later anyway.

## My position on the expert view
The experts have helped frame the economics, but I reject the leap from:

> “closures are the easiest solution”

to

> “closures are therefore the necessary solution.”

That is not a neutral conclusion. It is a management preference unless proven otherwise.

If management claims no-closure restructuring cannot work, then it must show the numbers and the implementation case. If it cannot, then closure is not justified — it is simply the path with the least resistance for management.

## What we are prepared to discuss
We remain prepared to discuss a package built on:

- **temporary and limited employee contributions**
- **working-time flexibility**
- **voluntary exits**
- **retirement bridges**
- **redeployment and qualification**
- **productivity improvements**
- **binding investment commitments**
- **site and employment guarantees through 2030**

Those are serious tools.

But they are only acceptable if the deal also includes:
- **no German plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **preservation of the collective bargaining framework**
- **real co-determination in implementation**

## What we are not prepared to accept
We will not accept:

- closures as the default answer
- forced layoffs in any form
- temporary concessions used as a stepping stone to later closures
- permanent wage dumping
- or “restructuring” that simply shifts the crisis onto workers

## On the compromise paths
A “closure deferred” path is unacceptable if it is just a slow-motion closure plan.

A “consolidation without formal closure” path is only acceptable if it genuinely means:

- all German plants stay open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and the savings are real, durable, and fairly shared

If “consolidation” is closure by another name, we reject it.

## Bottom line
My position remains:

- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No German plant closures**
- **Protect the collective wage agreement**
- **Fair employee contribution only in a genuinely shared-sacrifice package**
- **Binding investment and job/site guarantees through 2030**

If management wants a serious agreement, bring forward a quantified alternative that proves those goals can be met without closures and without forced layoffs. Until then, the union will continue to resist any attempt to make the workforce bear the bulk of the restructuring pain.
