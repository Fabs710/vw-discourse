We have now moved into the only part of this discussion that matters: **whether management can prove, with numbers, that it needs closures and compulsory redundancies**. So let me respond directly.

## To management
You keep repeating that closures and forced layoffs must remain “on the table.” That is not a plan; it is a threat posture.

The expert has already said something important: **non-closure measures can materially reduce the gap, but whether they are sufficient depends on the size of the gap and the data.** Exactly. And those data have still not been put on the table in a way that justifies breaking the company’s social contract.

So I ask again, very concretely:

- What is the exact savings gap in euros and in return-on-sales points?
- What is the share from labor costs versus overhead, procurement, product mix, and capital allocation?
- What savings have already been secured on the management and owner side?
- What does each German plant contribute, and what exactly would be lost by closing one?

Until you answer that transparently, “closures may be necessary” remains an assertion, not a proof.

And I will be equally clear: even if there is a gap, that does **not** automatically entitle management to close plants or impose compulsory redundancies. It means you must first exhaust the alternatives that preserve industrial capacity and jobs.

## To the trade union
I agree with you on the fundamentals:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no plant closures,
- no dismantling of the collective bargaining framework.

That is the correct line. Where we need to stay sharp is on the meaning of “employee contribution.” It must remain:
- limited,
- time-bound,
- reversible,
- and matched by binding guarantees.

We should not allow a temporary contribution to become a permanent extraction. The workforce cannot be expected to pay twice: first through concessions, then through broken guarantees.

## To the regional state government
You are right to say the company is a regional anchor and that closures would be socially and economically damaging. But your support only matters if it is used to **block closures and forced redundancies in practice**, not just in principle.

If you are serious, then stand behind:
- binding site guarantees through 2030,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and a restructuring based on capacity shifts, redeployment, and investment.

Do not hide behind “conditional support” while leaving the door open to the very outcome you say you oppose.

## To the family holding
Long-term value is not protected by threatening to blow up the company’s social settlement. Once trust is broken, it is not restored by saying “we mean well.” The workforce, suppliers, and regions will remember who forced the first breach.

If you truly want long-term value, then back a solution that:
- keeps every German plant open,
- avoids compulsory redundancies,
- and gives co-determination a real role in shaping the transformation.

Anything else risks creating permanent conflict that will cost more than the supposed savings.

## To investors
You keep saying the numbers must work. Fine — but then prove that closures are necessary, not just convenient.

The expert did **not** say closures are automatically required. The expert said they are usually more effective if the gap is large. That is very different. You are using uncertainty as a reason to demand the harshest instrument first. I reject that logic.

If you want a financially credible solution, look at the full package:
- procurement and overhead reductions,
- management cost restraint,
- working-time measures,
- internal redeployment,
- retraining,
- voluntary exits,
- volume shifts,
- and negotiated workforce contributions.

Those are not slogans. They are real instruments. And they can be made durable if management and owners are prepared to do their part.

## What is still unacceptable
Let me state the red line plainly, because it keeps getting blurred:

- **No closure of any German plant.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No removal of the historic job-security framework.**

That remains our position. It is not softened by repeated pressure, nor by the fact that others keep calling it “economically necessary” without proving it.

## What could still be discussed
We are prepared to discuss a restructuring package that includes:
- transparent cost data,
- binding investment commitments,
- capacity realignment **without closures**,
- temporary working-time and contribution measures,
- redeployment and qualification,
- and a genuine co-determination role in implementation.

But that discussion starts from the premise that German plants stay open and jobs are secured.

## My challenge to management
If you claim there is no alternative, then I invite you to do the responsible thing and **put the numbers under independent scrutiny**.

[SUMMON: Independent plant-level cost and capacity auditor to compare the savings from a site-preserving package versus a closure-based package, using plant-level cost, utilization, and headcount data.]

Because until that comparison exists, there is no justification for asking workers to accept closures or forced layoffs.

## Bottom line
The workforce side is not here to rubber-stamp a restructuring that socializes the pain and privatizes the decision.

We are open to a serious, durable, quantified package — but only one that:
- keeps every German plant open,
- avoids compulsory redundancies,
- preserves co-determination and job security,
- and distributes sacrifice fairly.

If management cannot deliver that, then it is not asking for restructuring. It is asking the workforce to surrender the company’s future.
