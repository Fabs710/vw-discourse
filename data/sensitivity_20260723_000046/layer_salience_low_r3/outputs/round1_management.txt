I appreciate that both the Works Council and the union are at least acknowledging the need for restructuring. But let me be equally clear: **a package that preserves every site, avoids hard capacity reduction, and leaves the cost base largely intact is not a solution**. It is a postponement of the crisis.

We are not discussing a normal cyclical downturn. We are facing a **structural competitiveness problem**:
- German factory costs are materially above target,
- the European market is shrinking,
- Chinese and other competitors are undercutting us,
- and the brand is missing its return-on-sales target.

If we do not make a **durable** reduction in cost and capacity, we will not have the resources to invest in the products, software, battery technologies, and platforms needed for 2030. That is the real issue.

So where I agree with you:
- **Yes**, restructuring must be negotiated.
- **Yes**, workforce contributions can be part of the solution.
- **Yes**, qualification, redeployment, and working-time tools should be used where they produce real savings.
- **Yes**, investment commitments matter — but only if they are matched by a credible economic base.

Where I disagree:
- **No**, I cannot accept that “no plant closures” is a fixed red line if the numbers do not work otherwise.
- **No**, I cannot accept a deal that rules out compulsory redundancies regardless of whether we achieve the necessary cost reduction.
- **No**, I cannot accept a cosmetic compromise that protects all sites on paper while leaving us uncompetitive in practice.

You both speak of shared sacrifice. Fine — but it must be **real sacrifice**, not symbolic concessions by management and reversible adjustments by labour. We need:
1. a **substantial and durable labour-cost contribution**,  
2. a **clear reduction in overcapacity**, and  
3. a package that materially improves the brand’s medium-term return on sales.

That means every option must be on the table, including **site consolidation and closures**, if that is what it takes to restore competitiveness. I am not announcing a closure for its own sake. I am saying that if alternative measures cannot deliver the same structural effect, then closures cannot be excluded.

I also want to address the implied framing that management is simply “shifting the burden.” The burden already exists — it is being carried by a cost base that is too high and a market position that is too weak. If we refuse to act now, the burden will be worse later: more losses, weaker investment, and greater risk to far more jobs.

So my position is this:
- I am prepared to discuss a negotiated settlement.
- I am prepared to accept alternative measures **only if** they deliver equivalent structural savings.
- I am **not** prepared to sign up to a plan that rules out the very tools needed to fix the problem.

If you want a secure future for German production, then you need to engage with the reality that some combination of **capacity reduction, labour-cost adjustment, and possibly closures** will be required. Otherwise, we are not restructuring — we are just delaying the reckoning.
