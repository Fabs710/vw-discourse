We are now at the point where rhetoric must give way to **verifiable commitments**.

I will speak plainly: the employee side is **not** moving from its core position.  
**No plant closures in Germany. No compulsory redundancies. No dismantling of the job-security framework.** Those are not bargaining chips. They are the minimum conditions for any serious settlement.

### Where I agree with others
I agree with management, the state, the family holding, and investors on one thing: the company needs a **durable restructuring**, not a cosmetic one. I also agree that the current cost base must come down and that future investment in products, software, battery capability, and platforms is essential.

But agreement on the diagnosis does **not** mean acceptance of management’s preferred methods.

### Where I strongly disagree
Management keeps framing closures and forced redundancies as unavoidable. That is exactly the kind of false choice we reject.

You have not shown that destroying German plants and breaking job-security guarantees is the only way to achieve the required savings. You say “every option must be on the table,” but in practice that means loading the risk onto workers first and asking them to pay for strategic mistakes they did not make.

That is not a credible turnaround plan. It is a transfer of risk and pain onto the workforce.

### What a credible package would have to contain
If management and shareholders want real movement from the works council, then the package must include all of the following:

1. **Binding site guarantees for every German plant through 2030**  
   Not vague intentions. Not conditional language that can be reopened at the first market shock.

2. **Binding employment guarantees through 2030**  
   No compulsory redundancies. If headcount reduction is needed, it must come through retirement, voluntary departures, redeployment, and qualification.

3. **A real co-determination role in the restructuring design**  
   The works council must be involved in shaping volumes, allocations, investment sequencing, and workforce planning — not presented with a fait accompli after management has already decided who loses.

4. **Measured, reversible workforce contributions only**  
   We are prepared to discuss limited temporary sacrifices if they are genuinely limited, time-bound, and matched by guarantees. That can mean working-time adjustments, internal flexibility, and negotiated cost measures. It does **not** mean open-ended wage cuts or the destruction of protections.

5. **Concrete investment commitments for German sites**  
   If the company wants the workforce to help carry the transformation, then the company must commit to the future of those sites — production, platforms, and training.

### On the “savings target”
Management and investors keep speaking about “equivalent structural savings,” but they still avoid giving a transparent breakdown of what exactly is required and why closures are supposedly necessary.

So I ask directly:
- What is the precise savings gap?
- How much comes from labor cost?
- How much comes from product mix and capacity utilization?
- How much comes from management overhead, procurement, and capital allocation?
- What have you already done on the management and capital side?

Until those numbers are laid open, I do not accept the argument that German plants must be sacrificed.

### On alternatives
If the company wants structural savings without closures and compulsory redundancies, then the path is clear enough in principle:
- capacity reductions through **production shifts and volume realignment**,
- working-time arrangements,
- redeployment across plants,
- qualification and retraining for electric and software-related jobs,
- efficiency improvements in logistics and procurement,
- lower margins on management privilege before asking workers for sacrifices.

But this has to be done in a way that preserves the industrial base. We are not agreeing to “alternative measures” as a code word for slower layoffs by another name.

### To the state government
Your concern for regional ecosystems is correct. But it is not enough to say you oppose closures in principle while still treating them as negotiable later. If you mean what you say, then use your influence to secure:
- binding plant guarantees,
- investment commitments,
- and a no-forced-layoffs agreement.

That is where your political and shareholder power should be used.

### To the family holding
You say you care about long-term enterprise value. Then show it. Long-term value is not built by breaking trust, igniting permanent labour conflict, and turning German plants into bargaining chips. If you want durability, support a settlement that protects the workforce and stabilizes the company.

### To investors
You are right to insist on profitability. But profitability does not require taking a chainsaw to the company’s social fabric. A settlement that avoids closures and forced redundancies can still be financially credible if management is forced to make a real contribution and if the company stops treating labour as the default adjustment variable.

### Our limit
Let me be absolutely clear:  
If the answer remains plant closures or compulsory redundancies, then there is **no basis for agreement** from the works council. We will use every means available under co-determination and industrial action to resist it.

### What we are prepared to discuss now
We are prepared to discuss a **no-closure, no-forced-redundancy restructuring package** with:
- quantified savings contributions from all sides,
- binding investment and product commitments,
- temporary and reversible labour measures,
- and a genuine role for co-determination in implementation.

That is the only path that can be both economically serious and socially acceptable.

So my message in Round 2 is unchanged, but sharper:  
**Do not come back to us with closures dressed up as necessity. Bring a real alternative that preserves every German plant and every job guarantee.** If you can’t, then you are not proposing restructuring — you are proposing to break the company’s social contract.
