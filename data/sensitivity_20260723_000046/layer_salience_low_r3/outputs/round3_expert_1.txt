From a plant-economics and capacity-utilization standpoint, the discussion is now framed correctly: the question is **not** whether closures are desirable, but whether a **site-preserving package** can remove enough **structural fixed cost** and **excess capacity** to close the competitiveness gap.

## What can be said with confidence

### 1) Non-closure measures can produce real savings
Measures such as:
- working-time reductions,
- shift reductions,
- voluntary departures,
- redeployment,
- procurement and overhead cuts,
- and negotiated labor concessions

can materially lower cost. In many manufacturing settings, they can be significant.

### 2) But they are usually weaker than closure on fixed-cost removal
If plants remain open, a large share of the following usually remains:
- site overhead,
- maintenance,
- utilities,
- supervision,
- internal logistics,
- engineering/support functions,
- depreciation and other site-fixed commitments.

So a no-closure package typically saves **less** than a closure package unless it also removes a lot of capacity and headcount permanently.

### 3) The decisive issue is permanence
Temporary wage restraint or time-limited concessions do **not** substitute for structural savings.  
To be equivalent to a closure-based package, a site-preserving plan would need to permanently reduce:
- output capacity,
- staffing,
- indirect labor,
- and fixed overhead.

## What would make a no-closure package credible
A site-preserving solution could be economically credible if it includes all or most of the following:
- elimination of one or more shifts,
- permanent reduction in headcount proportional to lost volume,
- consolidation of production into fewer lines,
- closure of internal cost centers or support functions,
- sustained working-time reduction,
- and measurable overhead cuts at plant level.

If those elements are present, the package may come close to closure economics.

## What would make it unlikely to work
A no-closure package is unlikely to close a structural gap if it relies mainly on:
- temporary wage moderation,
- voluntary, slow attrition,
- one-time sacrifice payments,
- or general efficiency promises without capacity removal.

Those measures improve cash flow, but often leave too much fixed cost in place.

## What is still missing
I cannot say whether the proposed site-preserving package is sufficient for this company without:
- plant-by-plant utilization rates,
- fixed overhead by site,
- direct and indirect headcount,
- labor cost per vehicle,
- transfer/logistics costs if volume shifts,
- closure/wind-down costs,
- and the exact savings target in euros and margin points.

Without that, no one can honestly prove that:
- closures are necessary, or
- closures are avoidable.

## Independent conclusion
Based on industrial cost logic alone:

- **Closure-based packages usually have the larger structural savings potential.**
- **Site-preserving packages can work, but only if they permanently remove enough capacity and fixed cost.**

So the real test is:

> Can the no-closure package deliver the same structural effect, not just temporary relief?

If the answer is no, it will probably not close the competitiveness gap. If yes, then it may be economically sufficient.
