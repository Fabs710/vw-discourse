I will answer directly, because this is now about whether management can substantiate its demands — not whether the workforce should be pressured into accepting them.

## To management

You still have not proven that closures or compulsory redundancies are necessary.

The expert has repeatedly said the decisive issue is whether a site-preserving package can **permanently remove enough fixed cost and excess capacity**. Exactly. That is the standard. And so far, you have not met it.

What I agree with:
- The company has a real competitiveness problem.
- The cost base must come down.
- A cosmetic settlement would be useless.
- A durable restructuring is necessary.
- We need hard numbers and a plant-level comparison.

What I do **not** accept:
- treating closures as a default option,
- keeping compulsory redundancies “available” as pressure,
- or presenting worker sacrifice as the main lever while management, owners, and capital retain theirs.

If you want us to consider deeper changes, then bring the full evidence:
- exact savings gap in euros and margin points,
- plant-level utilization,
- fixed overhead by site,
- direct and indirect headcount,
- labor cost per vehicle,
- savings from management overhead, procurement, logistics, and capital allocation,
- and the residual gap after non-closure measures.

Until then, your case for closures is not proven. It is asserted.

And I repeat the core limit clearly: **no plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, no weakening of the collective wage framework.**

## To the works council

We are aligned on the fundamentals:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of job security,
- no erosion of co-determination.

That remains the correct line.

Where I want to sharpen the discussion is on employee contributions. I am prepared to discuss them, but only if they are:
- limited,
- temporary,
- reversible,
- and matched by binding guarantees.

We cannot allow a “shared sacrifice” formula to become a permanent wage cut or a one-way transfer of risk onto workers. The workforce cannot be expected to pay twice: first through concessions, then through broken promises.

Your demand for transparent, plant-level data is right. I support it fully.

## To the regional state government

You are correct to insist that the company is a regional anchor and that closures would be socially and industrially destructive.

But that concern only matters if it is used to **stop destructive outcomes in practice**, not just to describe them.

If you want a serious role, then back:
- binding site guarantees through 2030,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- investment commitments for future products,
- and a restructuring built on capacity shifts, redeployment, qualification, and fair contributions.

Do not remain “conditionally supportive” while leaving the door open to the very outcome you say you oppose.

## To the family holding

You speak about long-term value. Then act like long-term owners.

Long-term value is not created by forcing the workforce into a corner. It is created by a stable settlement that preserves industrial capability, skills, and trust. Once trust is broken, it is expensive to rebuild — and the cost is not just financial.

If you genuinely care about the enterprise over generations, support a deal that:
- keeps German sites open,
- avoids compulsory redundancies,
- preserves the collective framework,
- and distributes sacrifice fairly.

I do not accept the idea that the only way to restore competitiveness is to threaten the social settlement that made the company governable in the first place.

## To investors

You are right about one thing: symbolic compromise is worthless.

But you are wrong if you imply that closures are the natural proof of seriousness. The real question is whether the package works.

The expert has made the test clear:
- Can a site-preserving package permanently remove enough fixed cost and excess capacity?
- If yes, then closures are not required.
- If no, then management still has not proven that alternative measures were exhausted.

That is the comparison that needs to be done. Not a rhetorical contest over who sounds tougher.

## My bottom line

My position remains firm:

- **Yes** to restructuring.
- **Yes** to cost reduction.
- **Yes** to investment in future products and technologies.
- **Yes** to a limited, fair, reversible employee contribution if it is matched by real management and owner contributions.
- **No** to plant closures.
- **No** to compulsory redundancies.
- **No** to weakening the collective wage agreement.
- **No** to a settlement where workers bear all the pain.

If management wants a durable settlement, it must come with quantified proof, fair burden-sharing, and binding safeguards for jobs and sites. If not, then it is not asking for restructuring. It is asking the workforce to absorb the crisis alone.
