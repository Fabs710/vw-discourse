From the perspective of dispersed institutional investors, the central issue is not who “wins” the confrontation, but whether the final package restores **credible, durable competitiveness** and therefore protects enterprise value.

Let me be direct: we are **not** interested in a cosmetic compromise that postpones the cost problem. If the restructuring leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive, margins impaired, or the company locked into recurring conflict, that is value-destructive and unacceptable to us.

### Where I agree
I agree with management, and partly with the family holding and state, on the diagnosis:
- the German cost base is too high,
- capacity is too large for the current market,
- the European demand environment is weaker,
- and the brand is missing its return-on-sales target.

That means **real structural action is required**. We do not support “business as usual” dressed up as transformation.

I also agree that a prolonged strike or endless confrontation is harmful. It destroys earnings, delays implementation, and raises uncertainty. So a negotiated settlement is preferable — but only if it is economically meaningful.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the Works Council and union position as currently stated if “no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” are treated as absolute red lines regardless of the economics. If alternative measures can truly deliver equivalent savings, fine. But if they cannot, then those red lines become a guarantee of underperformance, and we would oppose that outcome.

At the same time, I do **not** endorse management using closures and layoffs as a default first move. A deep cut that triggers prolonged industrial conflict, legal paralysis, and reputational damage can destroy value just as surely as inaction. So “we can close plants” is not, by itself, a credible strategy. It has to be justified by a hard business case and by a clear comparison with other measures.

### What would move us
We would support an agreement if it contains all of the following:
1. **Measurable, durable cost reduction** that closes the competitiveness gap.
2. **Clear capacity adjustment** aligned with realistic demand.
3. **A credible labor contribution** that is meaningful, not symbolic.
4. **A path to stable margins and return-on-sales improvement** within a reasonable horizon.
5. **Deal certainty** — i.e., a settlement that reduces the risk of recurring conflict.

We are open on the form:
- If targeted plant consolidation is genuinely necessary, it can be acceptable.
- If the same savings can be achieved through working-time flexibility, redeployment, wage restraint, and capacity rationalization without closures, that may be preferable.
- But whatever is chosen must be **structural**, not temporary.

### Our limit
What we will **not** support is an outcome that:
- preserves all sites regardless of economics,
- leaves labor costs untouched in substance,
- or relies on vague future promises rather than verified savings.

That would be a weak settlement, and from a shareholder perspective it would simply defer value erosion.

### Bottom line
So our position is:
- **Yes** to restructuring.
- **Yes** to a negotiated package.
- **Yes** to workforce and management accountability.
- **No** to symbolic compromises.
- **No** to a settlement that does not deliver competitive margins.

If the other stakeholders want our backing, they need to show a concrete, financially credible path back to profitability — not just a politically palatable one.
