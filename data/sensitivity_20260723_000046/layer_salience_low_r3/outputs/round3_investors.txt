The capital-markets view is now clear: **we support restructuring only if it is structurally credible, durable, and implementation-safe.** We are not invested in any particular instrument. We are invested in the result: restored margins, lower unit cost, and a believable path back to return-on-sales targets.

## Where we agree

### With management
We agree that the company has a **real competitiveness problem**:
- cost base too high,
- capacity misaligned,
- margins too weak,
- and future investment must be funded somehow.

We also agree that **symbolic compromise is worthless**. A deal that merely postpones the problem will not protect shareholder value.

### With the expert
The expert has been useful in framing the issue correctly: **non-closure measures can work, but only if they permanently remove enough fixed cost and excess capacity.** That is the right test.

### With labour, in part
We agree that:
- a prolonged strike would be value-destructive,
- the company needs durable restructuring, not a cosmetic one,
- and any employee contribution should be limited and not purely punitive.

But that agreement stops where the economics stop.

## Where we disagree

### With the works council and union
We do **not** accept a blanket position of:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no weakening of the job-security framework,
**before** the plant-level economics have been proven.

That stance is not a solution; it is a veto. If a site-preserving package truly closes the gap, fine — we would support it. But that has **not** been demonstrated yet.

You keep asserting that working-time measures, redeployment, voluntary exits, procurement savings, and temporary contributions are enough. They **might** be. But “might be” is not enough for capital allocation. We need proof.

### With management
At the same time, we do **not** support management using closures and compulsory redundancies as a default discipline device.

“Everything on the table” is acceptable only if it is accompanied by a transparent, quantified comparison. Otherwise it sounds like leverage, not strategy. If management cannot show why alternatives fall short, then it has not earned support for the harsher tools.

### With the state and family holding
We understand the concern about regional stability and long-term trust. But long-term value is not protected by preserving an uncompetitive footprint indefinitely.

If the no-closure path is to be defended, it must be defended on **numbers**, not sentiment. The burden is on the parties proposing that route to show it closes the structural gap.

## What would move us

We would support a **site-preserving solution** if it can be shown, with plant-level data, that it achieves:

1. **durable fixed-cost reduction**
2. **real capacity reduction**
3. **meaningful labor-cost improvement**
4. **credible margin recovery**
5. **implementation certainty**

If those conditions are met, then we have no ideological preference for closures.

If those conditions are **not** met, then a no-closure settlement is simply a delay mechanism, and we would not support it.

## What we need to see next
At this point, the missing piece is not more rhetoric. It is a **side-by-side financial and operational comparison** of:
- a site-preserving package, and
- a partial consolidation package.

That comparison must include:
- plant-level utilization,
- fixed overhead,
- direct and indirect headcount,
- labor cost per vehicle,
- transfer/logistics costs,
- closure or consolidation costs,
- and the durability of savings.

## Bottom line

Our position is straightforward:

- **Yes** to restructuring.
- **Yes** to negotiated alternatives if they are sufficient.
- **Yes** to disciplined, durable cost reduction.
- **Yes** to investment in future products and technologies.
- **No** to cosmetic compromise.
- **No** to a settlement that leaves the cost base structurally impaired.
- **No** to pre-committing against any structural option without proof.

So we are not choosing between “jobs” and “shareholder value.” We are choosing between:
- a package that **really restores competitiveness**, and
- one that merely postpones the reckoning.

We will back the first, and only the first.
