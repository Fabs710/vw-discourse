From an industrial cost-and-capacity standpoint, the key question is not whether closures are politically difficult, but whether a **no-closure / no-forced-redundancy package can close the structural cost gap with sufficient durability**.

## What can be said with confidence
A site-preserving restructuring can sometimes deliver meaningful savings through:
- reduced working time,
- wage moderation or temporary concessions,
- redeployment and headcount attrition,
- elimination of overtime and agency labor,
- overhead cuts,
- procurement and logistics savings,
- improved utilization,
- and product/factory mix changes.

But in large automotive plants, the largest and most durable savings usually come from one or more of:
- **capacity removal**
- **fixed-cost removal**
- **headcount reduction in both direct and indirect functions**
- **simplification of the footprint**
- **asset rationalization**

That is because automotive manufacturing has high fixed costs, and when utilization is below efficient levels, the cost per vehicle rises sharply.

## The core economic test
A no-closure package can only be judged sufficient if it can do all of the following:

1. **Remove enough fixed cost permanently**
   - not just defer it,
   - not just shift it elsewhere,
   - not just spread it over time.

2. **Reduce effective capacity enough to match demand**
   - if installed capacity remains materially above demand, savings leak away.

3. **Cut indirect cost as well as direct labor**
   - plant-level restructuring often fails when indirect and support functions remain too large.

4. **Be durable through 2030**
   - temporary wage sacrifices or working-time cuts are not equivalent to structural savings.

5. **Preserve execution certainty**
   - if the measures depend on repeated renegotiation, the saving is weaker and riskier.

## My assessment of feasibility
### A no-closure solution is realistic only if:
- overcapacity is moderate, not severe;
- the company can achieve high labor flexibility and permanent productivity gains;
- a large share of savings can come from overhead, procurement, logistics, and management simplification;
- and plant utilization can be lifted materially without needing to remove sites.

### It is less realistic if:
- German plants are significantly underutilized;
- fixed cost per vehicle is structurally too high;
- the product mix does not support enough volume;
- or multiple sites are duplicating functions and capacity.

In that case, a no-closure solution may reduce the gap, but **not fully close it**.

## Important distinction
There is a difference between:
- **closing the competitiveness gap**, and
- **narrowing the gap enough to buy time**.

Non-closure measures are often good at the second, less often sufficient for the first.

## What evidence would be needed
To validate whether the no-closure path is enough, I would need plant-level data on:

- annual output by site
- design capacity and practical capacity
- utilization rates
- direct labor headcount
- indirect/support headcount
- labor cost per vehicle
- energy and logistics cost per vehicle
- overhead allocation by site
- capital employed by plant
- cost to keep each site open
- modeled savings from each alternative measure
- one-time restructuring costs
- expected durability of each saving through 2030

Without that, no one can honestly claim that closures are unnecessary or unavoidable.

## Bottom line
**Can non-closure measures realistically close the competitiveness gap?**  
- **Sometimes yes, but only if the gap is moderate and the savings package is deep, permanent, and plant-specific.**
- **If the gap is large and driven by excess capacity and high fixed cost, non-closure measures are unlikely to fully close it on their own.**

So the economically correct answer is: **possible in principle, but not presumptively sufficient**. The burden is on the data to show that the residual gap after alternatives is small enough to accept.
