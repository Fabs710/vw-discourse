We are hearing the right diagnosis in parts, but not yet a fully credible path to the required outcome.

I agree with management on one essential point: **the company cannot defend its future with a purely symbolic restructuring**. If the German cost base remains structurally uncompetitive, then margins, investment capacity, and ultimately the brand’s position will continue to erode. From a long-term ownership perspective, that is unacceptable. We need a solution that restores competitiveness, not a temporary calm that postpones deterioration.

At the same time, I do **not** accept that the only serious answer is an automatic move to plant closures and compulsory redundancies. That is too blunt, and it risks destroying long-term enterprise value through prolonged conflict, reputational damage, and the loss of industrial capability that cannot easily be rebuilt. A deep cut that triggers years of confrontation can be just as value-destructive as inaction.

To the Works Council and the union: I understand your insistence on protecting jobs and sites, and I recognize that a one-sided burden on labour would not be sustainable. But I have to be equally clear: **“no closures, no layoffs” cannot be a complete answer unless you can show an alternative that produces the same structural savings**. The family holding is not interested in preserving the status quo for its own sake. We are interested in a company that can compete and generate durable value. If alternative measures genuinely deliver that, they deserve serious consideration. If they do not, then red lines become a way of avoiding the real problem.

To the regional state government: your point about regional ecosystems and the company’s anchor role is well taken. Stability matters, and the company should not be managed as if social consequences were irrelevant. But we should also be honest that preserving every structure at any cost can weaken the whole enterprise. If the business model is not fixed, then the regional base you want to protect will eventually be put at greater risk. So the state’s support is important — but it should be tied to a credible competitiveness plan, not used to freeze necessary adjustment.

My position, therefore, is this:

- **Yes** to a negotiated restructuring.
- **Yes** to workforce contributions if they are part of a durable turnaround.
- **Yes** to capacity reductions, working-time measures, redeployment, qualification, and targeted cost measures.
- **No** to cosmetic solutions that leave the underlying cost problem unresolved.
- **No** to any outcome that permanently impairs the company’s value or leaves the ownership structure unable to govern for long-term performance.

I would be open to avoiding closures **if and only if** the alternative package is robust enough to produce equivalent structural savings and a credible path back to competitiveness. That means measurable cost reduction, not just promises; real capacity alignment, not paper savings; and a settlement stable enough to avoid recurring labour conflict.

If management, labour, and the state can produce that, the family holding will support it through governance. If not, then we should not pretend that preserving every site is a sustainable strategy.
