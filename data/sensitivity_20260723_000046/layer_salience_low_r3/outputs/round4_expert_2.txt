From a plant-level cost and capacity perspective, the comparison turns on one question:

**Can the site-preserving package remove enough fixed cost and excess capacity, permanently, to match the structural savings of a closure-based package?**

## Short answer
- **A closure-based package usually delivers larger and more durable savings** if there is meaningful overcapacity and duplicated fixed cost.
- **A site-preserving package can still be sufficient** if the savings gap is modest and most of the cost base can be removed through labor flexibility, overhead cuts, procurement, and utilization gains.
- Based on the information provided, **we do not have the plant-level data needed to prove that the no-closure path is economically equivalent**.

So the economically cautious conclusion is:

> **The closure-based package is more likely to produce greater savings, but the comparison is not settled without plant-level numbers.**

## Why closures often save more
In automotive manufacturing, the largest durable savings usually come from:
- removing a plant’s fixed overhead,
- eliminating underutilized capacity,
- reducing indirect labor and support functions,
- avoiding duplicated logistics and utility costs,
- and simplifying the footprint.

That matters because when utilization is low, the cost per vehicle rises sharply. If a site remains open, many fixed costs remain in the system even after headcount cuts.

## Why a site-preserving package can still work
A no-closure package can be economically credible if it includes enough of the following:
- permanent working-time reduction,
- voluntary exits and attrition,
- redeployment out of excess functions,
- removal of overtime and agency labor,
- indirect headcount cuts,
- management/overhead reductions,
- procurement and logistics savings,
- and a real increase in utilization.

But there is a limit:
- **temporary wage concessions are not the same as structural savings**
- **reversible measures are weaker than permanent capacity removal**
- **headcount cuts that leave excess capacity intact often only partially close the gap**

## The key sensitivity
The answer depends mainly on four data points:

1. **How large is the overcapacity?**  
   If installed capacity materially exceeds demand, closure becomes more valuable.

2. **How fixed-cost-heavy are the plants?**  
   The more fixed cost per vehicle, the harder it is for non-closure measures to fully close the gap.

3. **How much indirect labor can be removed without closures?**  
   Non-closure packages often save less than expected if support functions remain large.

4. **How durable are the labor concessions?**  
   If concessions expire or must be renegotiated, savings are weaker than a permanent footprint reduction.

## What I would need to compare the packages properly
For a credible side-by-side assessment, I would need plant-level data on:

Without that, no one can honestly claim:
- that closures are unnecessary, or
- that closures are definitely required.

## Bottom-line judgment
If management’s claim is that the company faces a **large structural gap**, then **closure-based consolidation is more likely to generate the bigger savings**.

If the gap is **moderate** and the company can achieve substantial permanent reductions in overhead, capacity use, and labor intensity without closures, then a **site-preserving package could be enough**.

At present, the evidence shown here is **insufficient to prove equivalence**, so the more conservative economic reading is that **the closure-based package likely has the greater savings potential**.
