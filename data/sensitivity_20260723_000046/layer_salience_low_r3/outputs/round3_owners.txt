We are now at the point where the discussion has to be judged by one standard only: **does the package restore structural competitiveness without creating avoidable, value-destructive instability?**

That is the family holding’s test. Not sentiment. Not theatrical toughness. Not a settlement that looks politically neat but leaves the enterprise weak.

## Where I agree

I agree with management, investors, the state, and the expert on the core diagnosis:
- the cost base is too high,
- capacity is misaligned,
- margins are too weak,
- and the company needs durable restructuring to fund future investment.

I also agree with labour and the state on one important point:
- a prolonged strike, legal deadlock, or social breakdown would destroy value.

So yes, the problem is real. Yes, stability matters. And yes, a purely unilateral approach would be the wrong answer.

## Where I disagree with labour

I do **not** accept the works council’s and union’s blanket insistence on:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no meaningful weakening of job-security frameworks,
before the economics have been proven.

That position may be understandable, but it is not yet sufficient as an ownership answer.

The expert has made the key point clearly: a no-closure package can work only if it **permanently removes enough fixed cost and excess capacity**. That is the test. Until that is demonstrated, I cannot support an unconditional ban on structural measures.

What I do agree with is this:
- temporary employee contributions,
- working-time measures,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- and capacity shifts
are valid instruments.

But they are only valid if they are **durable enough** to close the gap. If they merely postpone the problem, they are not a solution — they are deferred decline.

## Where I disagree with management

I also do **not** give management a blank cheque.

Management is correct that the package must be structurally effective. But saying “closures and redundancies must remain on the table” is not the same as proving they are necessary.

So I reject any posture that treats closures as the default discipline mechanism. Severity is not strategy. If closures are truly needed, they must be justified by hard data, not used as a pressure tactic.

What management still has to prove is:
- the exact savings gap,
- the share attributable to labour cost,
- the share attributable to overhead, procurement, and capital allocation,
- and why a site-preserving package cannot close that gap.

Until then, I do not accept the argument that the German footprint must be cut as a first resort.

## Where I disagree with the state

I respect the state’s concern for regional stability, and I share it. But the state cannot simply block structural options in principle and then expect competitiveness to appear by goodwill.

If the company is to remain an anchor employer, it must survive as a competitive business. Preserving every structure regardless of economics would ultimately weaken the very regional base the state wants to protect.

So I support the state’s demand for:
- transparent data,
- no rush to closures,
- and a serious comparison of alternatives.

But I do **not** support turning that into an automatic veto against necessary structural change if the numbers ultimately point there.

## Where I agree with investors

I agree with investors that symbolic compromise is worthless.

A deal that merely buys calm for a few quarters while leaving the cost base structurally impaired is not value creation. It is a pause before the next crisis.

But I also reject the idea that the only credible sign of seriousness is to keep closures fully open-ended from day one. The right test is not whether the measure is harsh; it is whether it works.

## What would move me

The family holding would support a no-closure solution **if and only if** it can be shown, with plant-level numbers, that it delivers:

1. **durable fixed-cost reduction**
2. **real capacity reduction**
3. **meaningful labor-cost improvement**
4. **credible investment room for future products**
5. **implementation certainty without recurring conflict**

If that can be demonstrated, we have no ideological interest in closures for their own sake.

If it cannot, then closures and consolidation cannot be ruled out.

## What I will not support

I will not support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a package that preserves every site while leaving the company uncompetitive,
- a permanent impairment of enterprise value,
- or an outcome that weakens governance control by letting industrial stalemate decide the business.

That last point matters. The family holding is a long-term steward, not a spectator. Our role is to protect the core asset’s value over generations. A settlement that creates lasting conflict or structural weakness would fail that duty.

## [SUMMON: independent plant-level restructuring auditor]
We now need an **independent plant-level restructuring auditor** to compare, site by site:
- utilization,
- fixed overhead,
- direct and indirect headcount,
- labor cost per vehicle,
- transfer and logistics costs,
- consolidation or closure costs,
- and the durability of savings from each package.

That comparison should test two scenarios:
1. a **site-preserving package**, and  
2. a **partial consolidation package**.

Without that, this discussion remains too abstract to justify a final governance position.

## My current position, plainly stated

- **Yes** to restructuring.
- **Yes** to durable cost reduction.
- **Yes** to negotiation and shared sacrifice.
- **Yes** to alternative measures if they are structurally sufficient.
- **No** to a blanket no-closure rule.
- **No** to a blanket no-redundancy rule.
- **No** to an agreement that protects form while eroding value.

So my support goes to the package that proves it can restore competitiveness and margins **without** creating unnecessary instability. If that means a serious site-preserving solution, I am open to it. If it does not, then the harder structural measures remain available.
