I appreciate that everyone has moved beyond slogans and is now talking about structure, savings, and implementability. That is the right level of discussion. But I need to be equally clear: **I will not support a package that uses plant closures or compulsory redundancies as the primary adjustment tool in Germany** unless it is demonstrably the only remaining option after genuinely serious alternatives have been tested and found insufficient. We are not there.

### To management
I agree with you on the diagnosis: the company has a real competitiveness problem, and it is not enough to preserve the current footprint for sentiment’s sake. I also agree that a durable solution must produce real savings and room for investment.

Where I disagree is your insistence that closures and forced redundancies remain fully open-ended from the start of the process. That posture creates unnecessary mistrust and undermines the social partnership needed to make any restructuring work in Germany. More importantly, it assumes away the value of the instruments that have not yet been fully exhausted:
- targeted capacity reductions,
- production shifts,
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- procurement and overhead reductions,
- and a negotiated employee contribution.

You have not shown that these cannot deliver the required structural effect. Until you do, I do not accept that plant closures are justified.

### To the works council and trade union
I agree with your central principle: **German sites and jobs must be protected**. The company is not a purely financial object; it is a regional economic anchor, and destabilizing it would have serious consequences for employment, suppliers, and public finances.

I also agree that workers should not be treated as the default adjustment variable while other actors remain sheltered. A fair settlement must include contributions from all sides.

That said, I want to be frank with you as well: simply repeating “no closures, no redundancies” is not sufficient unless it is paired with a package that shows how the savings gap is closed. If we are to defend site protection politically and in governance, we need a credible industrial logic behind it. Otherwise we risk defending a status quo that eventually collapses under its own weight.

So I am open to a **site-preserving restructuring**, but only if it is backed by measurable savings, investment commitments, and a real plan for competitiveness. That is the standard I will use.

### To the controlling family holding
Your point about value destruction through prolonged conflict is well taken. I share the concern that a deep, socially explosive cut can do long-term damage. But the answer cannot be to keep closures on the table as a blunt discipline device and then expect the workforce and the state to absorb the pressure.

If you genuinely want long-term value, then you should support a settlement that preserves industrial capability in Germany, rather than treating local employment as a variable to be optimized away. A company this important does not build durable value by undermining the trust that holds its production system together.

### To investors
You are right to insist that any solution must be economically credible. I do not dispute that. But credibility is not the same as maximal short-term cost cutting.

A package that protects jobs and plants, avoids prolonged conflict, and still produces verifiable savings can be more credible than a “hard” package that triggers strikes, legal deadlock, and reputational damage. The question is not whether there is sacrifice; the question is **where** it falls and whether the resulting settlement is stable.

### On the concrete trade-off
Here is where I stand:

- I support **durable cost reduction**.
- I support **capacity alignment** where needed.
- I support a **fair, limited, reversible employee contribution**.
- I support **investment commitments** for German sites.
- I support a **strong co-determination role** in shaping the restructuring.

What I do **not** support:
- a blank cheque for closures,
- compulsory redundancies as a default instrument,
- or a restructuring that weakens the company’s regional base to the point that it ceases to be an anchor employer.

### What I would need to see
If management wants support from the state shareholder, then bring forward a package with:
1. a transparent savings calculation,
2. a quantified comparison of alternatives,
3. site-by-site investment commitments,
4. a no-forced-redundancy approach,
5. and a clear role for co-determination in implementation.

If those elements are present, I can defend a serious restructuring politically and in the boardroom. If not, I will not help sell a plan that risks gutting regional employment under the banner of competitiveness.

### My bottom line
I am prepared to support a tough restructuring. I am **not** prepared to support one that sacrifices plants and jobs in Germany without proving that less damaging options have failed.

So my position remains:

- **Yes** to restructuring.
- **Yes** to shared sacrifice.
- **Yes** to measurable, durable savings.
- **Yes** to investment and future products.
- **No** to closures as the default solution.
- **No** to compulsory redundancies.
- **No** to sidelining the state’s co-determination and veto role.

If you want my support, bring a **site-preserving, socially responsible restructuring** with numbers, guarantees, and a credible industrial plan. Otherwise, you will not have it.
