I will respond directly, because the discussion is now about facts, not framing.

## To management
You are still asking us to accept that closures and compulsory redundancies must remain available, but you have not proven that they are necessary. The expert’s last intervention confirms exactly what I have been saying: **non-closure measures can be sufficient in some cases, but the decisive question is whether they remove enough structural fixed cost and capacity**.

That means the burden is on you to show, with plant-level numbers:
- the exact savings gap,
- what portion comes from labor,
- what portion comes from overhead, procurement, and capital allocation,
- and what permanent savings are achieved by a site-preserving package versus closure.

Until you do that, “closures remain on the table” is not a business case. It is pressure.

And let me be equally clear: even if you show a gap, that does **not** automatically justify closures or forced redundancies. It means you must first exhaust alternatives that protect employment and the collective bargaining framework.

## To the works council
I agree with you on the red lines:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no plant closures,
- no dismantling of the collective agreement.

That remains the correct line. The expert has not changed that. What has changed is only that the economic comparison must be made honestly and at plant level. That is fine. We welcome that scrutiny.

But I also want to sharpen the issue: if we are discussing employee contributions, they must be **limited, temporary, reversible, and fair**. They cannot become a permanent wage cut in disguise. Workers cannot be asked to carry the transformation twice — once through concessions and again through the loss of guarantees.

## To the regional state government
You are right to insist on regional stability and on using your influence to block destructive outcomes. But your support only matters if it is exercised **before** closures become the path of least resistance.

So I ask you to be concrete:
- support binding site guarantees through 2030,
- support no compulsory redundancies,
- support a restructuring based on capacity shifts, redeployment, qualification, and investment.

If you are serious, then use your governance power to make those conditions real, not merely aspirational.

## To the family holding
You speak about long-term value. Then act like long-term owners.

Long-term value is not protected by threatening industrial conflict and forcing the workforce into a corner. It is protected by a stable settlement that preserves industrial capability, keeps skills in the company, and avoids a destructive cycle of strikes, distrust, and legal deadlock.

If you back closures as the default discipline tool, you may get short-term paper savings and long-term damage. That is not responsible stewardship.

## To investors
You are right that symbolic compromise is not enough. I agree. But I reject the leap from “the package must be real” to “therefore closures and forced layoffs are acceptable.” That conclusion does not follow.

The expert laid out the real test:  
Can a site-preserving package permanently remove enough fixed cost and excess capacity?

That is the question. Not “which pain instrument looks toughest,” but “which package actually closes the gap.”

If the answer is yes, then a no-closure solution is not just socially better; it is economically defensible. If the answer is no, then management still has not proven its case — because the comparison has not been done transparently.

## Where I stand after the expert input
The expert did **not** tell us that closures are inevitable. The expert said:
- closures usually save more if overcapacity is large,
- site-preserving solutions can work if they remove enough fixed cost and capacity,
- and we need plant-level data to know which is true here.

That is precisely why I will not move from my core position.

## What can move the union
A fair package can still move us if it includes all of the following:
- binding no-closure commitments for German plants,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- a durable collective-bargaining framework,
- temporary and reversible employee contributions,
- real management and owner contributions,
- and verifiable investment commitments for the future.

That is a shared-sacrifice package. That is what we are prepared to defend.

## What will not move us
We will not accept:
- closures dressed up as inevitability,
- compulsory redundancies,
- permanent erosion of wage standards,
- or a restructuring where workers absorb the pain while management and owners retain all freedom.

I support the call for an independent plant-level comparison, but it must be done properly and not as a pretext for closure.

We need an **independent plant-level cost auditor** to compare:
1. a site-preserving package, and  
2. a closure / consolidation package,

using:
- utilization,
- fixed overhead,
- direct and indirect headcount,
- labor cost per vehicle,
- transfer costs,
- closure costs,
- and savings durability.

That comparison should be published to the supervisory-board side and the bargaining parties, because only then can we test whether management’s claims are real or merely convenient.

## Bottom line
My position remains unchanged, and the expert’s intervention does not alter it:

- **Yes** to restructuring.
- **Yes** to fair, limited, reversible employee contributions.
- **Yes** to capacity adjustment and internal redeployment.
- **Yes** to investment in future products and sites.
- **No** to plant closures in Germany.
- **No** to compulsory redundancies.
- **No** to weakening the collective wage framework.

If management wants a serious settlement, bring quantified evidence and a package that preserves jobs and sites. If not, then do not pretend that “competitiveness” justifies making workers carry all the pain.
