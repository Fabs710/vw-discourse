I’ll respond directly.

## To the Works Council and Industrial Union

I hear your repeated red lines: **no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no weakening of the collective framework**. I understand why you are drawing them. But I do **not** accept them as a starting point for a credible restructuring unless you can demonstrate that a site-preserving package closes the structural gap.

The expert has now made the key point very clearly: **a no-closure package can work only if it permanently removes enough fixed cost and excess capacity.** That is exactly the test. And so far, you have not shown that your preferred package does that at the scale we need.

You keep saying:
- working-time measures,
- redeployment,
- voluntary exits,
- qualification,
- procurement savings,
- temporary contributions.

Those are useful instruments. I do not dismiss them. But useful is not the same as sufficient. They may reduce pain. They may even help bridge a period. But they do not automatically solve a structurally uncompetitive cost base.

So my disagreement is simple:
- I agree that restructuring must be durable.
- I disagree that preserving every German plant and every job guarantee is compatible with that durability unless the numbers prove it.

And that proof is still missing.

## To the Regional State Government

You say you will not support closures or compulsory redundancies unless they are the last resort. I respect the political concern, but I do **not** accept the implied sequencing as a veto on structural adjustment from the outset.

If the company’s cost base remains too high, then protecting the footprint first and hoping competitiveness returns later is not responsible governance. It is deferred decline.

You are asking for:
- site guarantees,
- no forced redundancies,
- co-determination,
- investment commitments.

Fine. But that only works if it comes with **credible, quantified savings**. Otherwise we are simply preserving capacity that the market is already punishing.

## To the Family Holding

You speak about long-term value, and I agree that value destruction through endless conflict would be bad. But long-term value is also destroyed by allowing the business to remain structurally uncompetitive.

A settlement that avoids hard decisions while leaving the cost base too high is not stewardship. It is avoidance.

If you want long-term value, then support a package that actually restores:
- margins,
- capacity discipline,
- labor-cost competitiveness,
- and investment room to 2030.

That may involve closures, or it may not. But it must involve **real structural savings**. That is the only standard I will accept.

## To the Institutional Investors

You are right about one thing: symbolic compromise is worthless. I agree.

But you are also being too quick if you treat closures as the default proof of seriousness. The right question is not whether a measure looks tough. The right question is whether it closes the gap. That is also my position.

Where I differ from the labor side is that I will not pre-commit to “no closures” if the data show that the non-closure package leaves us short. The expert has explicitly said that closure-based packages usually produce larger and more durable savings when overcapacity is large. That matters.

So I am asking for the same thing you are asking for:
- hard numbers,
- plant-level cost data,
- utilization data,
- permanent savings by measure,
- and a comparison of the residual gap.

But if that comparison shows that the site-preserving package is insufficient, then we cannot hide behind political comfort.

## What I agree on

There is broad agreement on several points:
- the situation is serious,
- we need a durable restructuring,
- future investment is essential,
- and a prolonged strike would damage everyone.

I also agree that any package must be financially credible and implementation-safe.

## What I do not accept

I do **not** accept:
- a blanket ban on closures,
- a blanket ban on compulsory redundancies,
- or a package of temporary concessions that leaves the company structurally weak.

I also do **not** accept the argument that because a measure is painful, it must therefore be wrong. In a market like this, pain is not the issue. The issue is whether we restore competitiveness.

## The actual trade-off

Let’s be precise:

- If alternative measures can produce equivalent structural savings, I am open to them.
- If they cannot, then capacity consolidation remains necessary.
- If labor-cost contributions are limited and reversible only, then they will not be enough on their own.
- If the company keeps every site open but cannot remove enough fixed cost, then we are only postponing the problem.

That is not an acceptable outcome for management, owners, or investors.

## What would move me

I will move only if the package contains:
1. **quantified, durable savings** at the necessary scale,
2. **real capacity reduction**,
3. **a meaningful labor-cost contribution**,
4. **clear investment commitments** to future products and technology,
5. **implementation certainty** sufficient to restore confidence.

And let me be blunt: if the employee side wants us to rule out closures, then you need to show how the package removes the same structural cost. So far, that has not been demonstrated.

- a site-preserving package,

with:

## Bottom line

My position remains firm:

- **Yes** to negotiation.
- **Yes** to a durable restructuring.
- **Yes** to alternative measures if they are structurally sufficient.
- **No** to a blanket no-closure rule.
- **No** to a blanket no-redundancy rule.
- **No** to a package that preserves footprint at the expense of competitiveness.

If the numbers show that a site-preserving solution truly closes the gap, I will support it. If not, then we must be willing to use the structural measures that the business reality requires.
