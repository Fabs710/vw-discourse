I’ll respond directly.

### To the Works Council
I agree on one point: this must be a **durable restructuring**, not a cosmetic exercise. But your central demand — **“no plant closures in Germany, no compulsory redundancies, no dismantling of the job-security framework”** — is not compatible with the economic reality we face **unless** you can show an alternative that delivers **equivalent structural savings**.

So far, you have not done that. You have listed instruments:
- working-time measures,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- internal efficiency,
- voluntary departures,

but you have **not demonstrated** that these are sufficient to close the competitiveness gap. That is the key issue.

I am not interested in breaking the social contract for its own sake. I am saying the social contract cannot be defended if the company becomes structurally uncompetitive and loses its ability to invest, earn, and employ people in the future. Protecting every site at any cost is not protection; it is slow decline.

### To the Industrial Union
You are right that a prolonged conflict is harmful. But I reject the idea that management is trying to impose “one-sided pain” as a matter of principle. The reason labour cost is central is because it is one of the biggest levers we can still move quickly enough to restore competitiveness. That is not ideology; it is arithmetic.

You ask for openness on the savings gap. Fair enough. Here is the substance:

- We need a **material reduction in structural cost**.
- We need **capacity aligned to demand**.
- We need **labor cost down in a durable way**.
- And we need the freedom to invest in future products and technologies.

If your package can genuinely deliver that without closures or compulsory redundancies, then show it in quantified terms. Not slogans. Not principles. Numbers.

A “shared-sacrifice package” is only credible if the sacrifice is real and durable. Temporary or reversible measures may help bridge a year, but they do not solve a long-term cost base problem. We are not trying to buy a few quiet quarters. We are trying to restore the brand’s return on sales through 2030.

### To the Regional State Government
I hear your concern about regional ecosystems and the company’s role as an anchor employer. I do not dismiss that. But I also cannot accept a position that effectively says: preserve every location first, then worry about competitiveness later.

That sequencing is backwards.

If the business is not fixed, then the regional base is eventually weakened anyway — only more slowly and at greater cost. A state shareholder should want a company that survives and grows, not one that preserves today’s footprint while bleeding future capacity.

So yes, I welcome your support for a balanced solution. But “balanced” cannot mean that management is denied the tools needed to reduce overcapacity. You cannot ask for competitiveness and simultaneously rule out the structural measures that create it.

### To the Controlling Family Holding
You are right to warn against a purely symbolic restructuring. I agree. But your caution about conflict cannot become a reason to avoid hard decisions. The longer we defer structural adjustment, the more value erodes.

I am prepared to support an agreement that avoids closures **only if** it achieves the same structural effect. That means:
- real capacity reduction,
- real labour-cost improvement,
- and a credible path back to margins.

Without that, a no-closure solution is just a delay mechanism.

### To the Institutional Investors
You are correct that symbolic compromises are worthless. I appreciate that. But I want to be equally clear: a settlement that is politically easier to digest but materially insufficient is not acceptable to management either.

We need a package that is:
1. **structurally effective**,  
2. **implementation-safe**, and  
3. **financially credible**.

That means the burden cannot sit solely on management overhead or vague future efficiency gains. Labour contribution has to be real, and capacity has to come down. If the workforce’s proposal cannot produce those effects, then it is not a solution.

### On the actual trade-off
Here is the reality that everyone needs to confront:

- If alternative measures can produce the necessary savings, I am open to them.
- If they cannot, then **site consolidation and closures remain on the table**.
- If there is no willingness to adjust labour costs in a meaningful way, then there is no basis for a durable settlement.
- If there is no durable settlement, then the company continues to drift toward weaker margins and less investment — which is a worse outcome for everyone.

I am not confusing “hard” with “fair.” I am saying hard decisions are unavoidable because the market environment has changed and our cost position is too weak.

### What would move me
A package could still move me if it contains:
- **measurable, durable cost reduction** at the scale required,
- **credible capacity alignment**,
- **a substantial labour-cost contribution**,
- and enough implementation certainty to restore investor and owner confidence.

What will **not** move me:
- absolute bans on closures and compulsory redundancies,
- reversible concessions dressed up as transformation,
- or a settlement that preserves the current footprint but leaves the company uncompetitive.

### Bottom line
So my position remains firm:

- **Yes** to negotiation.
- **Yes** to shared sacrifice.
- **Yes** to alternative measures **if** they deliver equivalent structural savings.
- **No** to a blanket no-closure rule.
- **No** to a blanket no-redundancy rule.
- **No** to any deal that fails to materially reduce the cost base.

If the employee side wants to prove that plant closures are unnecessary, then bring forward a package that actually closes the gap. Until then, I will continue to insist that all structural options remain available.
