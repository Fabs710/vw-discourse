I will be equally direct in Round 2.

### Where I agree
I agree with the Works Council, the state, and even management on one fundamental point: **this company needs a real restructuring, not a cosmetic one**. The cost base must come down, production volumes have to be aligned with demand, and the company needs room to invest in future products, software, and battery-related capabilities.

I also agree that a prolonged conflict is harmful. A strike is not a goal in itself; it is leverage. If we can secure a durable and fair settlement, that is better for everyone.

### Where I disagree — and strongly
What I do **not** accept is the attempt to define “durable restructuring” as a package that only becomes real if workers absorb the full pain through **closures, compulsory redundancies, and wage framework destruction**.

That is not shared sacrifice. That is burden shifting.

Management keeps asking for “real sacrifice,” but so far the sacrifice demanded from labour is concrete, while the contribution from owners, top management, and capital is still too vague. If the company wants durable savings, then the burden must be shared on all sides, including:
- management overhead,
- capital allocation discipline,
- owner-backed support for the transition,
- and a meaningful, limited employee contribution.

### To management
You say every option must be on the table, including closures and redundancies. I say: **not as a first principle, and not as an ultimatum**.

You have not demonstrated that German closures and forced layoffs are the only way to achieve the required savings. You have asserted it. That is not enough.

If you want labour to move, then show the numbers openly:
- What is the exact savings gap?
- What portion comes from labour costs?
- What portion comes from management, procurement, overhead, and product allocation?
- What savings would a combination of working-time adjustments, capacity realignment, internal redeployment, and negotiated cost measures produce?

Until that is transparent, “closures may be necessary” is just leverage language, not a proven necessity.

### To the Works Council
I stand with you on the core red lines:
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No plant closures in Germany.**
- **No dismantling of the collective bargaining framework.**

That is still my position.

But I also want to sharpen one point: if we are asking workers to contribute, then that contribution has to be **limited, reversible, and fair**. I am not interested in open-ended wage cuts, permanent concessions, or a one-way ratchet where employees pay today and lose again tomorrow.

So yes, I support:
- temporary working-time measures,
- a negotiated employee contribution,
- voluntary and socially managed headcount reduction,
- redeployment and retraining,
- and capacity reduction where it does not mean closures or forced layoffs.

What I will not support is a deal that gives away core protections and calls that “future security.”

### To the state government
You are right to insist on regional stability. If you mean that seriously, then your influence should be used to **block closures and forced redundancies**, not merely to soften them after the fact.

I also agree that the company is an anchor for the region. That is exactly why this cannot be treated as a short-term balance-sheet exercise. Destroying employment relationships and industrial capability would damage the regional ecosystem for years.

### To the family holding and investors
You both speak the language of long-term value, and I take that seriously. Then act like long-term owners.

Long-term value is not protected by creating a deeper conflict, weakening trust, and making the company dependent on years of labour unrest. A settlement that preserves jobs and sites, secures investment, and delivers credible savings is not “soft.” It is economically rational.

What is not rational is assuming that the workforce should pay for every structural problem while ownership and management remain structurally insulated.

### On the actual trade-off
Let me be clear about what can move us:
- a **fair employee contribution**,
- a **durable reduction in labour cost through negotiated means**,
- **capacity adjustments** that do not mean closures,
- **working-time flexibility**,
- **qualification and redeployment**,
- and **binding commitments** on investment and site security.

That is the package I can defend to members.

What cannot move us:
- closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- and any attempt to break the collective wage framework under the banner of restructuring.

If management insists those are unavoidable, then we are not in a bargaining situation anymore — we are in an industrial confrontation.

### Bottom line
So my position remains unchanged, but it is concrete:

**Yes** to restructuring.  
**Yes** to shared sacrifice.  
**Yes** to negotiated employee contributions.  
**No** to plant closures.  
**No** to compulsory redundancies.  
**No** to a one-sided adjustment where workers absorb the pain and everyone else calls it transformation.

If management wants a serious deal, bring a quantified package that preserves jobs and sites, distributes the burden fairly, and keeps the collective wage framework intact. If not, we will defend those interests with the means available to us.
