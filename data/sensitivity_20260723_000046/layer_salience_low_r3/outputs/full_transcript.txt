[Works Council (Betriebsrat) (Plant-level employee representation under co-determination) — Round 1]
As the Works Council, my position is clear:

We will **not accept plant closures in Germany** and we will **not accept compulsory redundancies**. Those are red lines. The workforce cannot be made to pay for management’s failures by losing sites, jobs, and livelihoods.

What we are prepared to discuss is a **serious restructuring that protects all German plants and secures jobs through 2030**, with:
- binding site guarantees,
- binding employment guarantees,
- investment commitments for all German locations,
- a genuine co-determination role for the works council in shaping any transformation,

If the company wants a sustainable solution, it must respect the historic job-security framework and stop treating employees as a disposable cost factor.

So my message is simple:  
**No closures. No forced layoffs. Yes to a negotiated future that secures German sites and jobs.**

## My assessment, method-based

### 1) What plant closures actually do economically
A closure gives three types of structural savings:

- **Fixed-cost elimination**: site overhead, maintenance, utilities baseline, local logistics, security, management layers.
- **Capacity removal**: reduces chronic underutilization and the burden of spreading fixed costs over too few units.
- **Workforce reset**: removes jobs tied to redundant capacity, often with lasting labor-cost effects.

Those savings are durable because they are tied to **permanent capacity exit**.

### 2) What non-closure measures can do
Alternative measures can reduce cost materially, but their effect is often partly limited or conditional:

- **Working-time reductions / flexibility**: useful, but usually only a fraction of total site cost.
- **Redeployment / voluntary departures / retirements**: helpful, but slow and volume-dependent.
- **Wage moderation or temporary contributions**: can cut labor cost, but labor cost is only one part of total unit cost.
- **Production shifts / volume reallocation**: can improve utilization, but only if there is a recipient plant or market demand.
- **Procurement, logistics, overhead reduction**: real savings, but typically not enough alone to offset a major overcapacity problem.
- **Product mix improvement**: can help margins, but that is not the same as eliminating structural excess capacity.

The scenario says management judges German factory costs to be **well above target and above competitors**, and that the core brand misses its return-on-sales target. That suggests the issue is not a small efficiency gap but a **structural competitiveness problem**.

For a structural problem, the relevant test is:

> Can non-closure measures generate a lasting reduction in unit cost equivalent to the savings from removing a site or a major chunk of capacity?

In industrial practice, that is often difficult unless:
- the overcapacity is modest,
- the workforce is already underutilized,
- labor cost is a large share of total conversion cost,
- and the company can reallocate volume without destroying utilization elsewhere.

### 4) What would need to be true for a no-closure solution to work
A no-closure solution could realistically close the gap if most of the following are true:

- **There is enough idle or low-utilization capacity** to absorb volume reductions without severe disruption.

- The required savings are **incremental**, not transformative.

[Expert: Independent plant-level cost and capacit — Round 3]
From a plant-economics perspective, the key question is:

**Can a site-preserving package generate savings comparable to a closure-based package, and under what conditions?**

## Bottom line
- **Closure-based packages usually produce larger and more durable savings** if the closed plant is materially underutilized, has high fixed-cost absorption, and the lost volume can be reallocated without major network disruption.
- **Site-preserving packages can still generate substantial savings** if they achieve:
  - meaningful capacity reduction,
  - sustained volume consolidation,
  - headcount reduction or labor-hour reduction,
  - and permanent reductions in fixed overhead and support cost.

So the answer is **not automatically closure vs. no closure**. The decisive issue is whether the site-preserving package removes enough **structural fixed cost** and **excess capacity**. If it only trims variable labor or offers temporary concessions, it will usually fall short.

---

## How to compare the two packages properly

At plant level, savings come from four main buckets:

1. **Direct labor and overtime**
2. **Fixed plant overhead**
3. **Capacity-related costs**  
   - utilities, maintenance, logistics, internal handling, supervision
4. **Network effects**
   - whether volume is consolidated into fewer lines/plants, improving utilization elsewhere

A closure package captures most of items 1–3 for the closed site, minus:
- wind-down costs,
- severance and closure costs,
- possible transfer/logistics costs,
- and any stranded commitments.

A site-preserving package captures savings only to the extent that it permanently reduces:
- headcount,
- shifts,
- indirect labor,
- working time,
- and fixed overhead.

If the plant remains open with similar infrastructure, a lot of fixed cost remains.

---

## What typically makes closure financially stronger
A closure tends to outperform when the plant has:
- **low utilization**,
- **high fixed cost per unit**,
- **duplicated capacity** in the network,
- **high indirect labor and support overhead**,
- and limited ability to redeploy volume efficiently.

In that case, keeping the plant open often means paying for:
- buildings,
- maintenance,
- supervision,
- quality/engineering support,
- utilities,
- and local logistics
even if the plant is producing less.

That is why closure savings are usually **step-change savings**, not incremental savings.

---

## When a site-preserving package can come close
A no-closure package can be credible if it includes all of the following:

- **Permanent volume reduction** at the site
- **Headcount reduction proportional to output loss**
- **Elimination of one or more shifts or lines**
- **Overhead cuts**, not just shop-floor cuts
- **Working-time flexibility** that lowers paid hours without preserving full staffing
- **Management and support-function reductions**
- **A binding commitment that savings are durable**, not temporary

If those elements are present, a site-preserving package can get close to closure economics, especially when the plant is only moderately overstaffed or the network has spare volume elsewhere.

---

## The main limitation of site-preserving deals
The most common weakness is this:

> labor concessions reduce unit cost, but they do not fully remove fixed cost.

For example:
- a wage moderation deal saves money per hour,
- but if the plant still runs at low utilization,
- the company still carries the site’s fixed burden.

That means a site-preserving deal may improve competitiveness **without fully solving overcapacity**.

---

## Practical ranking by savings durability
In general, from highest to lowest structural savings potential:

1. **Plant closure / capacity removal**
2. **Major line or shift elimination with headcount reduction**
3. **Deep site-preserving restructuring with permanent labor and overhead cuts**
4. **Temporary concessions, wage restraint, or one-time sacrifices**

Temporary measures are useful for liquidity and bridge periods, but they are not equivalent to structural savings.

- direct labor headcount

## Sensitivity of the conclusion

3. **Cut indirect cost as well as direct labor**
   - plant-level restructuring often fails when indirect and support functions remain too large.

4. **Be durable through 2030**
   - temporary wage sacrifices or working-time cuts are not equivalent to structural savings.

5. **Preserve execution certainty**
   - if the measures depend on repeated renegotiation, the saving is weaker and riskier.

- fixed cost per vehicle is structurally too high;
- the product mix does not support enough volume;

In that case, a no-closure solution may reduce the gap, but **not fully close it**.

There is a difference between:
- **closing the competitiveness gap**, and
