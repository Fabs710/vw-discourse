We agree on the diagnosis, but not yet on the remedy.

## To management
We agree that this is a **real structural competitiveness problem** and that a cosmetic settlement would destroy value. We also agree that the solution must be **durable**, not temporary.

Where we disagree is that you still have not shown why **closures or compulsory redundancies** must remain the default structural option rather than a last resort. Repeating that they are “on the table” is not a financial case.

The expert’s point is the right one: the real test is whether a **site-preserving package permanently removes enough fixed cost and excess capacity**. That is the benchmark we care about. But you have not yet provided the plant-level evidence that alternatives fall short.

If you want capital markets to back a harder restructuring, then bring:
- the exact savings gap in euros and margin points,
- plant-level utilization and fixed overhead,
- direct vs. indirect headcount,
- labor cost per vehicle,
- procurement, logistics, and management overhead savings,
- and the residual gap after non-closure measures.

Without that, we cannot conclude that closures are necessary rather than merely convenient.

## To the works council and union
We agree with you on one point: **temporary concessions are not enough** if the company remains structurally impaired. We also agree that a strike or prolonged conflict would be value-destructive.

But we do **not** accept a blanket no-closure / no-redundancy position unless it is backed by numbers showing that the alternative package closes the competitiveness gap. A veto is not a strategy.

We are open to a site-preserving solution, but only if it delivers:
- real and permanent cost reduction,
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- durable labor-cost improvement,
- and implementation certainty.

What we will not support is a package that protects every site in form while leaving the cost base structurally uncompetitive in substance.

## To the regional state government
We understand your political responsibility to the region, and we agree that closures would be socially damaging. But protecting employment only works if the business stays competitive enough to fund it.

So we support:
- transparent plant-level data,
- no rush to closures,
- serious testing of alternatives,
- and a site-preserving solution if it is economically sufficient.

We do **not** support turning the state’s role into an effective veto against structural adjustment if the economics prove that stronger action is required.

## To the family holding
We agree that value destruction through conflict would be bad. We also agree that the company cannot remain permanently uncompetitive.

Where we differ is that we do not accept closures as proof of seriousness. The proof is whether the package restores margins and return-on-sales targets.

If a no-closure package can truly remove enough structural cost, then that is the better outcome. If it cannot, then the company needs to be honest about that sooner rather than later.

## Our position
We would back a restructuring package if it meets these conditions:
1. **durable fixed-cost reduction**
2. **real capacity reduction**
3. **credible labor-cost savings**
4. **clear margin recovery path**
5. **implementation certainty**

We will not back:
- symbolic compromise,
- temporary bridges presented as solutions,
- or a settlement that leaves the cost base structurally impaired.

using:
- utilization,

## Bottom line
We are not here to defend any particular instrument. We are here to protect risk-adjusted shareholder value.

So:
- **Yes** to restructuring.
- **Yes** to durable savings.
- **Yes** to negotiation if it is economically real.
- **No** to cosmetic peace.
- **No** to a package that leaves margins structurally impaired.
- **No** to pre-committing against structural measures before the numbers are known.
