The discussion is still trying to turn a question of **proof** into a question of **pressure**. I will not accept that.

We have heard, again and again, that closures and compulsory redundancies must “remain available.” That is not evidence. It is a threat wrapped in management language.

## To management
You still have not shown that a site-preserving solution fails.

The expert’s point was very clear: a no-closure package can work if it **permanently removes enough fixed cost and excess capacity**. That means the burden remains on you to prove, with plant-level numbers, that the alternative is insufficient.

So far, I have heard:
- general claims about competitiveness,
- general claims about overcapacity,
- general claims about labor cost,
- but no verified, plant-by-plant case that breaking historic job guarantees is unavoidable.

That is not good enough.

If your case is serious, then bring:
- the exact savings gap in euros and margin points,
- the fixed-cost structure by site,
- the headcount split between direct and indirect labor,
- the utilization rates by plant,
- the savings from procurement, overhead, management cost, and capital allocation,
- and the residual gap after all non-closure measures.

Until then, you are not proving necessity. You are simply insisting on the harshest option because it gives you the most leverage.

And let me be explicit: **I do not accept closures or compulsory redundancies as a fallback if the company has not exhausted all site-preserving alternatives.**

## To the trade union
We are aligned on the red lines:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no plant closures,
- no dismantling of the collective agreement.

That remains the correct line.

Where we must stay disciplined is on employee contributions. I will not allow “shared sacrifice” to become a permanent wage cut, a disguised deterioration of working conditions, or an erosion of job security that never gets reversed.

If there is any contribution at all, then it must be:
- limited,
- temporary,
- reversible,
- and matched by binding guarantees on sites and jobs.

Anything else is not a contribution. It is a concession without protection.

## To the regional state government
You keep saying you want to protect the region. Then use your power to do it, not just your vocabulary.

A state that is serious about its anchor employer should not stand by while management tries to make closures and forced layoffs normal instruments of restructuring. If you mean what you say, then support:
- binding site guarantees through 2030,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no plant closures,
- and a restructuring based on capacity shifts, redeployment, qualification, and investment.

Your role is not to politely observe while the company chips away at the social settlement. Your role is to prevent that outcome.

## To the family holding
You say you are thinking in generations. Then act like a long-term owner, not a short-term disciplinarian.

A company that breaks its job-security framework once can break it again. A company that normalizes closure threats will also normalize distrust. That does not create long-term value; it creates a permanent conflict premium.

If you want durable value, then support a settlement that:
- keeps every German plant open,
- avoids compulsory redundancies,
- preserves co-determination,
- and gives the workforce a fair role in the transformation.

I do not accept the idea that “structural competitiveness” requires sacrificing the very trust that makes the company governable.

## To investors
You are right about one thing: symbolic compromise is useless.

But you are wrong if you think the only credible sign of seriousness is to keep closures on the table. Credibility comes from a transparent comparison, not from the threat of destruction.

The expert has not said that closures are inevitable. The expert has said:
- closures usually save more if overcapacity is large,
- site-preserving packages can work if they permanently remove enough fixed cost,
- and the data are decisive.

Exactly. So let us use the data. But until that comparison shows a real gap that cannot otherwise be closed, I reject any move toward plant closures or forced redundancies.

## What I agree on
I do agree on some points:
- the company has a structural competitiveness problem,
- a cosmetic settlement would be worthless,
- future investment is necessary,
- and conflict would be damaging.

I also agree that the company must become more efficient.

What I do **not** agree with is the leap from “more efficient” to “therefore closures and forced layoffs are acceptable.” That leap has not been justified.

## What is still unacceptable
My position remains unchanged:
- **No closure of any German plant.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No weakening of the historic job-security framework.**
- **No settlement that turns workers into the sole adjustment variable.**

That is not rhetoric. That is the limit of what the workforce can accept.

## What could still be discussed
If management wants a serious solution, then the discussion has to stay within these conditions:
- transparent plant-level data,
- investment commitments,
- capacity adjustments without closures,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and retraining,
- temporary and reversible contributions,
- and a real co-determination role in implementation.

That is the only path I will engage constructively on.

## [SUMMON: Independent plant-level restructuring auditor]
We now need the independent auditor to do more than compare abstract models. I want a **plant-by-plant, employment-by-employment comparison** of:
1. a site-preserving restructuring with no closures and no forced layoffs, and  
2. a consolidation scenario with closures and job cuts,

including:
- fixed overhead,
- utilization,
- indirect labor,
- direct labor,
- logistics,
- transfer costs,
- closure costs,
- and savings durability through 2030.

That is the only way to test whether management’s claims are fact or pressure.

## Bottom line
I will not soften the core position because others repeat “competitiveness” often.

The workforce side remains clear:
- restructuring, yes;
- fairness, yes;
- limited employee contribution, if properly protected, yes;
- closures, no;
- compulsory redundancies, no;
- erosion of co-determination and job security, no.

If the numbers truly prove that a site-preserving solution cannot work, then present those numbers openly. Until then, there is no justification for demanding that workers sacrifice plants, jobs, and the company’s social contract.
