As the regional state government and shareholder, I will be equally direct.

I **agree** with management on one point: the company does have a serious competitiveness problem, and pretending otherwise would be irresponsible. A sustainable solution must improve the cost base and create room for investment in future products, technologies, and platforms.

But I **strongly disagree** with the implication that this automatically legitimizes plant closures or compulsory redundancies as the preferred path. That is not a neutral technical choice; it would have major consequences for regional employment, supplier networks, municipal finances, and the company’s role as an economic anchor. We cannot treat German sites as disposable assets.

So my position is:

- **Yes** to restructuring.
- **Yes** to meaningful, durable savings.
- **Yes** to a negotiated employee contribution if it is fair and part of a balanced package.
- **No** to a one-sided solution that outsources the adjustment to workers and communities.
- **No** to closures and mass layoffs as the first resort.

To the Works Council and the union: I hear your red lines, and I understand why they are there. Protecting jobs and sites is not sentimental; it is economically and socially rational for a company embedded in a regional ecosystem. I also agree that a negotiated solution should include:
- binding investment commitments,
- qualification and redeployment,
- working-time instruments,
- and a genuine social-partnership framework.

But I will also be frank with you: simply saying “no closures, no layoffs” is not enough unless you can help deliver a credible alternative that closes the competitiveness gap. If the alternative measures do not produce real, measurable savings, then the company’s future becomes endangered in a different way. So I need more than resistance; I need a workable restructuring logic that preserves sites while actually improving performance.

Where I draw the line is this:  
I will not support a package that **guts regional employment** or sidelines the state’s role in co-determination and veto rights. Those are not bargaining chips to be removed because management wants speed. The special statute and the board structure exist for a reason, and any serious proposal must respect them.

My preferred path is therefore a **socially responsible restructuring** with:
1. targeted capacity adjustments where they are genuinely necessary,  
2. negotiated labour contributions that are limited and reversible,  
3. strong investment and product commitments for German sites,  
4. no forced redundancies, and  
5. no plant closures unless every less damaging option has been exhausted and demonstrated insufficient.

That is not me denying reality. It is me insisting that the company’s long-term health must not be purchased by destroying the very regional base that helped build it. If management wants support from the state as shareholder and political actor, it will need to bring forward a plan that preserves the company’s role as an anchor of employment and industrial capability.

So my message is:  
**Restructure, yes. Sacrifice, yes. But not by sacrificing whole communities and permanently weakening the company’s regional foundation.**
