## 1. Decision context
A major German carmaker is facing a structural competitiveness crisis: excess capacity, high fixed costs, weak European demand, and pressure from lower-cost competitors. Management seeks durable restructuring, potentially including German plant closures and job cuts; labour, the state, and the controlling family resist closures and forced redundancies. Because of co-determination, ownership concentration, and a blocking regional-state stake, the decision requires coalition-building rather than unilateral action.

## 2. Stakeholder assembly
Higher standing / more decisive actors:
- **Group & brand management**: initiates restructuring and controls operational diagnosis.
- **Works council**: half the supervisory board; effective veto on closures/relocations.
- **Controlling family holding**: majority voting rights; strong governance influence.
- **Regional state government**: blocking-minority stake and political legitimacy.

Important but more limited:
- **Trade union**: bargaining counterpart; can strike and shape labour-cost concessions.
- **Institutional investors**: capital-market legitimacy, but limited direct control.

## 3. Final positions
### Group & brand management
Management maintains that the business needs a **durable structural reduction in cost and capacity**. It will not pre-commit to keeping all German sites open or excluding compulsory redundancies, arguing that flexibility must remain until the numbers prove a no-closure package is sufficient.

### Works council
The works council rejects closures and compulsory redundancies as a matter of principle and insists management has not proven necessity. It demands plant-level evidence, binding site protection through 2030, and any worker contribution to be limited, reversible, and matched by guarantees.

### Industrial union
The union agrees the company needs restructuring and durable savings, but refuses closures, forced layoffs, or weakening of the collective framework. It supports limited temporary contributions only if paired with binding guarantees and a fair burden split.

### Regional state government
The state insists on a **site-preserving, no-forced-layoff** path as the starting point, consistent with its political role and blocking power. It supports restructuring and investment, but will not endorse closures absent strong plant-level proof that alternatives fail.

### Controlling family holding
The family agrees that cosmetic compromise is unacceptable and that the company needs durable improvement. It will support a site-preserving solution if it can credibly restore competitiveness, but will not accept a blanket no-closure rule or loss of managerial flexibility in advance.

### Institutional investors
Investors support deep restructuring and reject symbolic compromise. They are open to a no-closure package only if it delivers permanent fixed-cost reduction, real capacity reduction, and implementation certainty; otherwise they want structural options kept open.

### Independent expert
The expert does not take a political side; it provides the decisive technical framing. A no-closure package can work only if it permanently removes enough fixed cost and excess capacity; closures usually generate larger, more durable savings when overcapacity is substantial.

## 4. Genuine conflicts
- **Plant closures vs. no closures**: Labour/state oppose closures; management/family/investors keep them available.
- **Forced redundancies vs. employment guarantees**: Employee/state side rejects them; management/family/investors refuse to exclude them categorically.
- **Temporary/reversible contributions vs. durable structural savings**: Labour wants limited and reversible concessions; management/investors want savings that materially change the cost base.
- **Who bears the burden of proof**: Labour/state demand proof that site-preserving options fail; management/family/investors argue proof must come from comparing packages, not from pre-committing to one outcome.
- **Governance logic**: The state and co-determination stakeholders want influence approaching a veto on social outcomes; management/family want decision space to restructure the footprint.

## 5. Areas of convergence
- All parties accept there is a **real structural competitiveness problem**.
- All agree that **cosmetic or purely symbolic compromise is unacceptable**.
- All support **future investment** in products, software, batteries, and platforms.
- All accept that **prolonged conflict or strike action would be destructive**.
- All now support an **independent plant-level comparison** of site-preserving versus consolidation scenarios.
- All agree the analysis must be **quantified**, including savings gap, utilisation, headcount, overhead, and savings durability.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker
- **Short-term social stability vs. long-term industrial viability**
- **Plant/site preservation vs. structural cost removal**
- **Labour peace vs. managerial flexibility**
- **Durable cost reduction vs. political acceptability**
- **Speed of action vs. evidentiary certainty**
- **Shared sacrifice vs. one-sided burden shifting**
- **Investment commitments vs. conditionality on restructuring outcomes**

## 7. Integrative pathways
### Pathway A: Two-stage settlement with an evidence gate
Agree a temporary framework: no closures and no compulsory redundancies during an audit phase, full data access, and no final instrument commitments. Then negotiate the permanent package once plant-level evidence shows the residual gap.

### Pathway B: Site-preserving first, quantified fallback
Make the no-closure solution the preferred option, but define a numeric trigger for fallback consolidation if audited residual savings remain insufficient after all non-closure measures.

### Pathway C: No forced redundancies, conditional capacity adjustment
Commit to no compulsory redundancies, while using voluntary exits, redeployment, retraining, working-time changes, overhead cuts, and procurement/logistics savings first. Closures remain a last-resort issue only if durable savings remain inadequate.

## 8. Open questions
- What is the **exact savings gap** in euros and margin points?
- How much can be removed from **fixed cost, indirect labour, and overhead** without closures?
- What is the **plant-by-plant utilisation** and capacity picture?
- Which proposed savings are **temporary**, which are **reversible**, and which are **permanent**?
- What burden-sharing split across **labour, management, owners, and capital allocation** would be viewed as fair and credible?
- What specific evidence threshold would each side accept as sufficient to justify or reject closures?

## 9. Recommended next step
Adopt a **time-limited evidence-gate process**:
1. Freeze final decisions on closures and forced redundancies during a defined audit period.
2. Mandate the independent expert to produce a plant-level side-by-side comparison of:
   - site-preserving restructuring, and
   - partial consolidation.
3. Require quantified outputs on savings gap, fixed-cost removal, utilisation, headcount, implementation certainty, and durability through 2030.
4. Resume bargaining on the permanent package only after those numbers are verified.

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction
- The real dispute is no longer about whether restructuring is needed; it is about **which instrument may be used before necessity is proven**.
- The parties’ positions hardened around **evidence standards**, not just substantive preferences: labour/state demand proof that no-closure options fail; management/family/investors demand proof that they succeed.
- A subtle but important convergence emerged: **everyone accepts plant-level data are decisive**, which creates a procedural opening even though substantive red lines remain intact.
- The expert clarified a non-obvious distinction: a site-preserving package may **narrow** the gap or buy time without fully closing it; that is why the debate is about **equivalence**, not simply “some savings.”
- The interaction revealed a realistic compromise structure: **no-closure/no-forced-redundancy during the evidence phase**, with a **quantified fallback trigger** if the residual gap remains too large.
- It also became clear that “shared sacrifice” is politically acceptable only if burden-sharing is visibly distributed across **management, owners, and capital structure**, not treated as a workforce-only adjustment.
