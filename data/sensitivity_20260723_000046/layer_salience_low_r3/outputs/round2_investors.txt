From the perspective of dispersed institutional investors, the discussion has now moved to the right question: **can the package produce durable, verifiable competitiveness gains — or not?** That is the only standard that matters for us.

## Where we agree

I agree with management, the family holding, the state, and the expert on the core diagnosis:

- the German cost base is too high,
- capacity is not aligned to current demand,
- the brand is missing its return-on-sales target,
- and the company needs a restructuring that is **structural**, not symbolic.

I also agree with all sides that a prolonged strike or endless conflict would be value-destructive. Deal certainty matters. We do not want a settlement that simply buys a few quiet months and then returns us to margin erosion and renewed confrontation.

## Where I disagree

I do **not** accept the works council and union position if “no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” are treated as absolute conclusions before the economics have been proven.

The expert’s intervention is important here: **non-closure measures can help, but they are not automatically a full substitute for permanent capacity removal** when the gap is structural and large. That is exactly the issue. We are not going to endorse a solution that preserves every site and every guarantee on paper while leaving the company uncompetitive in practice.

At the same time, I also do **not** support management’s implication that closures and forced redundancies should be the default answer. They are tools, not a strategy. If used badly, they can destroy value through conflict, implementation delays, and reputational damage.

## What would move us

We would support a site-preserving solution **only if it can be shown quantitatively** that it closes the competitiveness gap. That means the package must demonstrate:

1. **Measured structural savings**
   - not temporary savings,
   - not accounting shifts,
   - not vague future efficiencies.

2. **Clear capacity reduction**
   - actual alignment of output and footprint to demand.

3. **Meaningful labor-cost improvement**
   - real contribution, not symbolic gestures.

4. **A credible path to margin recovery**
   - with evidence that return-on-sales can improve sustainably.

5. **Implementation certainty**
   - so the settlement does not trigger recurring conflict.

## On the works council and union proposal

I respect the insistence on protecting jobs and sites, but I do not think the current position is financeable as stated.

You are asking investors to believe that:
- working-time adjustments,
- redeployment,
- voluntary exits,
- procurement savings,
- and temporary labor contributions

will be enough to solve a structural overcapacity and cost problem in a large German carmaker.

That may be true. But it is **not yet proven**.

So my position is not “closures now.” My position is:

- if the no-closure plan is **quantitatively sufficient**, then it deserves support;
- if it is **not sufficient**, then it is not a solution and management must retain the ability to use stronger structural measures.

## On management

Management is right to insist on durable savings, but it still needs to be more explicit about the numbers.

If you want capital-market support, bring us:
- the savings gap in hard terms,
- the expected contribution from labor measures,
- the expected contribution from overhead and procurement,
- and the residual gap after alternatives are exhausted.

Without that, “all options must remain open” sounds more like leverage than a transparent restructuring case.

## On the state and family holding

I agree with both of you that a long conflict would damage long-term value and regional stability. That said, long-term value is not protected by freezing an uncompetitive structure either.

If the state and the family want investor support for a site-preserving deal, they need to help produce a package that is **economically equivalent** to closures, not just politically preferable.

## Our line

We will **not** support:

- a cosmetic compromise,
- a settlement that leaves the cost base structurally impaired,
- or a package that relies on vague promises instead of verifiable savings.

We **can** support:

- negotiated restructuring,
- alternative measures,
- limited and fair workforce contributions,
- and investment commitments,

**but only if the numbers show that the restructuring actually restores competitiveness.**

## Conclusion

So our position is straightforward:

- **Yes** to restructuring.
- **Yes** to negotiated alternatives if they are sufficient.
- **Yes** to durable cost reduction and capacity alignment.
- **No** to a settlement that preserves every site while failing the economics.
- **No** to symbolic compromise.

If the employee side wants to prove that closures are unnecessary, then the burden is on them to present a quantified package that closes the gap. If they cannot, then we should stop pretending that a no-closure outcome is automatically credible.
