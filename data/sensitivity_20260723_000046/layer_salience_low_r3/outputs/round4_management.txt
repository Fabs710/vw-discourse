I’ll be direct.

## To the works council and the union
I agree with you on one point: **a cosmetic deal is worthless**.  
But I do **not** agree that the answer is to pre-emptively rule out closures and compulsory redundancies.

You keep saying “prove the need.” Fair enough. That is exactly why we need the plant-level comparison. But let’s be honest about what the expert has actually said: **a site-preserving package can only work if it permanently removes enough fixed cost and excess capacity.** If it does not, it is not structurally sufficient. Temporary working-time measures, voluntary exits, and reversible contributions may help, but they do not automatically close a large competitiveness gap.

So I reject your attempt to convert “we need numbers” into “therefore closures are off the table.” That is not evidence. That is a red line dressed up as analysis.

And I will be equally clear:  
- **No blanket guarantee of all German sites through 2030** unless the economics justify it.  
- **No blanket exclusion of compulsory redundancies** if the structural gap cannot be closed otherwise.  
- **No restructuring that preserves the current cost base and calls it transformation.**

If you want to avoid closures, then show me a package that actually removes enough fixed cost, enough capacity, and enough labor cost to restore the brand’s competitiveness. Not in theory. In numbers.

## To the trade union
I hear your insistence that employee contributions must be limited, temporary, and reversible. That is understandable, but if contributions are only temporary, they are not enough to solve a structural problem.

We are not discussing a short-lived liquidity squeeze. We are discussing a company whose domestic cost base is too high, whose European market is shrinking, and whose return-on-sales target is being missed. That requires **durable** change.

So: I am open to negotiated contributions, but only if they are meaningful and lasting enough to matter. If they are merely symbolic or time-limited bridge measures, then they do not solve the problem.

## To the state government
You are right to care about regional stability. But a company that is permanently uncompetitive cannot protect jobs in the long run. Defending every site regardless of economics is not a strategy; it is a delay.

I am not asking you to ignore the social impact. I am asking you to support a solution that is actually viable. If a site-preserving package can deliver the structural savings, I will support it. If it cannot, then your insistence on no closures becomes a demand for managed decline.

## To the family holding
You are right that value destruction from conflict would be costly. But value is also destroyed by refusing to adjust the footprint and cost base to the market reality.

I am not asking for “hardness for its own sake.” I am asking for a settlement that restores margins and frees investment capacity to 2030. If that requires consolidation, then consolidation must remain possible. If the alternative package is truly equivalent, I am open to it. But I will not accept a solution that leaves the business structurally underpowered.

## To investors
You are right that symbolic compromise is worthless. But that cuts both ways.

It is also symbolic to say “no closures, no redundancies” before anyone has shown how the gap gets closed. That is not financial discipline. It is a pre-commitment that may well lock us into an uncompetitive structure.

I agree with the expert: the key test is whether the alternative package permanently removes enough fixed cost and excess capacity. That is the benchmark. But until that benchmark is met, closures and restructuring measures must remain available.

## My position, plainly stated
I am **not** moving off these fundamentals:

- We need a **durable structural reduction** in cost and capacity.
- We need a **meaningful labor-cost contribution**.
- We need **implementation certainty**.
- We need to restore margins and fund investment to 2030.
- We cannot accept a package that leaves the cost base uncompetitive.

That means I will **not** sign up to:
- a blanket no-closure commitment,
- a blanket no-redundancy commitment,
- or a deal that only postpones the correction.

## What would move me
A package would move me only if it can demonstrate, with plant-level numbers, that it achieves:
1. permanent fixed-cost reduction,
2. real capacity reduction,
3. durable labor-cost savings,
4. credible margin recovery,
5. and investment room for the future.

If that can be shown without closures, fine. If not, then I will not pretend otherwise.

## [SUMMON: Independent plant-level cost and capacity auditor]
We need the independent auditor now, not as a delay tactic but to settle the economic question properly:

- plant-by-plant utilization,
- fixed overhead,
- direct and indirect headcount,
- labor cost per vehicle,
- transfer and logistics costs,
- closure or consolidation costs,
- and savings durability through 2030.

I want a side-by-side comparison of:
1. a site-preserving package, and  
2. a partial consolidation package.

Until that comparison is in front of us, nobody should claim the economics are settled.

## Bottom line
I respect the demand for evidence. I do not respect the attempt to turn that demand into a veto on structural change.

My stance remains:
- **Yes** to a negotiated restructuring.
- **Yes** to alternatives if they are structurally sufficient.
- **No** to cosmetic solutions.
- **No** to pre-committed protection of every site.
- **No** to ruling out closures or compulsory redundancies before the numbers justify it.

The company needs a real fix, not a feel-good compromise.
