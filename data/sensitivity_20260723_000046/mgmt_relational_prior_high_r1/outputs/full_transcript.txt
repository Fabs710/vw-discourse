[SUMMON: Independent industrial restructuring analyst — to quantify closure vs. non-closure savings, employment impacts, and feasibility of capacity reduction without plant closures]

[Expert: Independent industrial restructuring ana — Round 2]
From an industrial restructuring perspective, the key question is **whether a large, durable cost reduction can be achieved without closing German plants**. The answer is: **sometimes yes, but only if the required savings are moderate and the company can materially reduce labor and fixed costs through internal measures; if the gap is large, non-closure options usually fall short unless they are combined with deep productivity gains and a major change in the production footprint**.

## 1) What plant closures typically save versus non-closure measures

### Plant closure: what it tends to deliver
A closure can remove:
- fixed overhead at the site,
- maintenance and utilities,
- local logistics and support costs,
- underutilized labor tied to that site,
- and, if production is transferred, some manufacturing duplication.

In automotive manufacturing, the **largest savings usually come not just from headcount reduction, but from eliminating an entire fixed-cost base**. That includes depreciation on underused assets, site services, indirect labor, and the cost of carrying low-volume complexity.

### Non-closure restructuring: what it can deliver
Without closing plants, savings usually come from:
- **shift reductions / line consolidation**
- **lower staffing levels through attrition and redeployment**
- **working-time reductions or variable schedules**
- **wage concessions / suspension of bonuses / removal of allowances**
- **productivity improvements and better labor utilization**
- **simplified product mix and higher utilization**
- **purchasing, overhead, and process efficiency**

These measures can be meaningful, but they usually do **not** fully replicate the savings from removing an entire site unless the plant is already only mildly underutilized.

## 2) The central economic constraint: utilization

The feasibility of avoiding closure depends heavily on **capacity utilization**.

- If a plant is running near full utilization, there is less room to save by consolidation without cutting output.
- If there is substantial overcapacity, you can often reduce cost by:
  - eliminating a shift,
  - shortening operating hours,
  - consolidating lines,
  - or reallocating models across plants.

But if total demand is shrinking and the company has several plants competing for a smaller volume pool, then **non-closure savings often become arithmetic-limited**: you can reduce labor cost, but you cannot easily remove the fixed cost of multiple sites unless you actually close or mothball some capacity.

## 3) Employment impact: closures versus non-closure

### Closure-based scenario
A plant closure usually means:
- direct job losses at the site,
- secondary losses among contractors and local suppliers,
- some redeployment to other sites, but usually limited by geography and skill match,
- and potential losses from early retirement / severance / attrition programs.

Net employment reduction is typically **large and concentrated**.

### Non-closure scenario
A non-closure restructuring can reduce employment through:
- attrition,
- retirement,
- voluntary separation,
- short-time work,
- redeployment,
- reduced use of temporary labor,
- and internal transfer.

This can substantially reduce headcount **without immediate forced redundancies**, but it usually takes longer and may require:
- a hiring freeze,
- limits on backfilling,
- lower overtime,
- and lower plant-specific staffing ratios.

So the employment effect can be significant, but it tends to be **more gradual and less certain** than closure.

## 4) Can capacity be reduced without closing plants?

Yes, but only under specific conditions:

### More feasible when:
- demand decline is modest or temporary,
- plants are flexible and can absorb model shifts,
- there is overlap in product lines across sites,
- the company can lower shifts or output without breaking logistics,
- and labor-cost concessions are material.

### Less feasible when:
- the company has multiple structurally underused plants,
- product portfolios are fragmented,
- plants are specialized and not easily repurposed,
- fixed costs are high relative to output,
- and competitors operate with lower labor and overhead costs.

In a capital-intensive industry like auto manufacturing, **capacity reduction without closure usually works best as a partial answer, not a complete substitute**, unless management’s savings target is relatively modest.

## 5) A practical rule of thumb

Without company-specific audited data, I would frame it this way:

- **If the restructuring gap is mainly labor-cost driven and the company can cut 5–10% of German labor and overhead expense through working-time, wage, and efficiency measures**, a non-closure package may be viable.
- **If the company needs a much larger structural cost reset**, especially one requiring removal of persistent fixed costs, then **at least some site rationalization or production transfer is usually economically necessary**.

That does not automatically mean a full plant closure. It can mean:
- one plant’s output is reduced sharply,
- a site loses a shift,
- a model is moved elsewhere,
- or part of a facility is mothballed.

But if management claims it needs a “durable” step-change, then the burden of proof is high for a pure non-closure solution.

## 6) What a credible non-closure package would need

To be economically credible, a non-closure package should include all of the following:

1. **Quantified savings target**
   - annual and run-rate savings,
   - one-time restructuring costs,
   - payback period.

2. **Capacity plan**
   - which shifts are removed,
   - which plants lose volume,
   - expected utilization after restructuring.

3. **Labor-cost measures**
   - wage moderation or temporary concessions,
   - bonus suspension,
   - working-time adjustments,
   - headcount reduction path.

4. **Fixed-cost reduction**
   - management overhead cuts,
   - support-function consolidation,
   - procurement and logistics savings,
   - asset rationalization.

5. **Timeline and durability**
   - what is temporary versus permanent,
   - what reverses if the market improves,
   - what is locked in for several years.

6. **Employment handling**
   - voluntary exit programs,
   - redeployment capacity,
   - retraining,
   - handling of surplus labor in each plant.

Without these numbers, “non-closure” is more a political position than a restructuring plan.

## 7) Bottom line

My evidence-based assessment is:

- **A non-closure restructuring is feasible if the savings requirement is moderate and there is enough flexibility to cut shifts, reduce labor cost, and improve utilization.**
- **If the savings requirement is large and the company has genuine structural overcapacity, then avoiding all plant closures becomes difficult and may be insufficient for competitiveness.**
- **The critical variable is not the principle of closure versus no closure, but the ratio of required savings to reducible fixed cost and excess capacity.**

If you want, I can next set out a **side-by-side scenario table**:
- closure-based,
- non-closure,
- and hybrid,
with likely savings, employment effects, implementation risk, and feasibility.

[Expert: Company restructuring finance lead / ope — Round 2]
Below is the **finance/operations assessment** the parties are asking for. I will stay within quantified restructuring economics and avoid labor/political/legal positions.

## Executive answer

A **non-closure restructuring can work only if the required margin gap is modest** and the company can extract enough savings from:
- labor-cost reset,
- shift reduction,
- utilization improvement,
- overhead cuts,
- and productivity gains.

If the brand’s **return-on-sales gap is structurally large**, then **non-closure measures usually do not close the full gap** because they cannot remove enough fixed plant overhead and underutilized capacity. In that case, the minimum economically credible package is typically a **hybrid**:
- **major internal measures**, plus
- **some capacity rationalization** through plant-line consolidation, shift removal, or partial site shutdown.

## 1) What must be quantified first

To answer “closure vs. non-closure,” you need three numbers:

1. **Target profit uplift needed**
   - Current ROS
   - Target ROS
   - Revenue base

   Formula:
   \[
   \text{Required EBIT uplift} = (\text{Target ROS} - \text{Current ROS}) \times \text{Revenue}
   \]

2. **Available non-closure savings**
   - labor concessions
   - indirect headcount reduction
   - shift rationalization
   - procurement and overhead cuts
   - productivity gains

3. **Residual gap**
   \[
   \text{Gap} = \text{Required EBIT uplift} - \text{Non-closure savings}
   \]

If the residual gap remains material, a no-closure solution is not financially sufficient.

---

## 2) Operating logic: why closures matter so much in autos

In automotive manufacturing, the cost base is split between:

- **variable costs**: direct materials, logistics, some energy, piece-rate labor elements
- **fixed / semi-fixed costs**: plant overhead, maintenance, indirect labor, depreciation, support functions, utilities, and underabsorbed factory burden

A closure or true site shutdown removes a **whole fixed-cost stack**. That is why closures are often disproportionately powerful versus wage moderation alone.

### Practical implication
- **Wage concessions** improve cost per vehicle.
- **Shift cuts** improve utilization.
- **Headcount reduction** lowers labor cost.
- But **only site rationalization** removes a large share of the fixed-cost burden associated with excess capacity.

## 3) Scenario framework

Because I do not have audited company data in the prompt, I will set out a **scenario model** using generic but realistic automotive restructuring mechanics. These are **scenario estimates**, not verified company facts.

### Scenario A — Non-closure restructuring
Measures:
- wage/bonus/allowance reset
- working-time flexibility
- attrition and voluntary exits
- indirect overhead reduction
- line balancing and shift cuts
- productivity improvement

Typical savings range:
- **labor-side savings:** ~5–10% of German labor cost base
- **overhead savings:** ~2–4% of brand revenue or equivalent
- **total durable EBIT uplift:** often **mid-hundreds of millions to low-single-digit billions** depending on size

Constraint:
- savings are usually **limited by the inability to eliminate enough fixed factory overhead**

### Scenario B — Hybrid restructuring
Measures:
- all Scenario A measures, plus
- consolidation of selected lines/models
- loss of one shift at one or more plants
- transfer of volume from structurally underutilized sites
- partial site or module shutdown

Typical savings range:
- **materially higher**, often enough to remove **a full site-level fixed-cost stack**
- better chance of reaching a **step-change** in ROS

### Scenario C — Full closure-led restructuring
Measures:
- closure of one or more German plants
- redeployment where possible
- deeper workforce reduction
- elimination of site fixed costs

Typical savings range:
- highest annual run-rate savings
- highest one-time restructuring cost
- greatest execution risk and volume disruption

## 4) Illustrative savings bridge

To make the logic concrete, here is a **generic example**.

Assume:
- brand revenue = **€40 billion**
- current ROS = **2.0%**
- target ROS = **5.0%**

Then:
\[
\text{Required EBIT uplift} = (5\% - 2\%) \times 40bn = 3\% \times 40bn = €1.2bn
\]

### Example savings bridge
| Item | Annual EBIT impact |
|---|---:|
| Wage/bonus moderation | €150m–€300m |
| Attrition / workforce reduction | €100m–€250m |
| Shift rationalization / utilization gains | €100m–€250m |
| Indirect overhead cuts | €100m–€200m |
| Procurement / logistics / productivity | €150m–€300m |
| **Total non-closure savings** | **€600m–€1.3bn** |

### Interpretation
- If the required uplift is around **€600m–€900m**, a non-closure package may be feasible.
- If the required uplift is around **€1.2bn+**, non-closure becomes much harder unless productivity and labor concessions are unusually strong.
- If the gap is **well above €1bn**, a hybrid or closure-led solution is usually the only credible route to restore target ROS.

---

## 5) Plant-by-plant logic: what to look for

The key metric is **cost per unit of eliminated capacity**, not just headcount.

For each plant, estimate:
1. annual output
2. utilization rate
3. direct labor per unit
4. site fixed overhead
5. logistics and supply-chain duplication
6. depreciation and maintenance burden
7. relocation feasibility of the product

### Decision rule
A plant becomes a closure or major consolidation candidate when:
\[
\text{avoidable annual fixed cost} + \text{avoidable variable cost from lost volume} \gt \text{cost of transferring volume}
\]

If a plant is below, say, **70–75% utilization** and has limited product flexibility, it usually becomes a strong rationalization candidate in a shrinking market. If a site is above that range and deeply integrated, non-closure options are more plausible.

---

## 6) Minimum production/capacity reduction needed

Without company-specific data, I can only provide the formula and a planning range.

### Capacity reduction formula
Let:
- \(F\) = annual fixed factory and overhead cost tied to excess capacity
- \(V\) = variable cost savings from reduced output or efficiency
- \(T\) = target EBIT uplift needed

Then:
\[
\text{Needed capacity reduction} \approx \frac{T - \text{non-capacity savings}}{\text{EBIT saved per unit of capacity removed}}
\]

In auto plants, **EBIT saved per 1 percentage point of capacity removed** can be very material because it removes labor underabsorption and site overhead.

### Rule of thumb
- **Small ROS gap:** about **1 plant shift equivalent** may be enough
- **Moderate gap:** **one full line or plant segment**
- **Large gap:** **one whole plant or multiple sites** may be needed

That is why management often pushes beyond labor concessions toward footprint reduction.

## 7) What a credible non-closure package must include

A non-closure plan is only credible if it is quantified across four buckets:

### A. Labor-cost reset
- temporary wage moderation
- bonus suspension/reduction
- allowances removed or restructured
- working-time adjustments
- lower overtime and flex premiums

### B. Workforce adjustment
- attrition
- voluntary exits
- redeployment
- hiring freeze
- temporary labor reduction

### C. Industrial footprint optimization
- shift removals
- line consolidation
- model allocation changes
- lower utilization targets at selected sites

### D. Corporate overhead reduction
- engineering, admin, and SG&A cuts
- procurement savings
- logistics simplification
- reduced complexity in product mix

If any one of these four is weak, the package tends to fall short.

### Non-closure is feasible if:
- the ROS gap is **moderate**
- excess capacity is **limited or transient**
- labor cost reset is **material and durable**
- utilization can be lifted without major capex
- management overhead can be reduced sharply

### Non-closure is not enough if:
- the brand has **persistent structural overcapacity**
- several plants operate at low utilization
- the market is shrinking
- fixed-cost burden is too high
- competitors are structurally cheaper

In that case, management may still be able to avoid full closures, but only by accepting a **partial footprint reduction**.

[Expert: Independent industrial restructuring ana — Round 3]
I can address the restructuring economics and operations only.

## Core assessment

A **non-closure restructuring can work only if the required savings gap is modest enough** to be covered by combinations of:
- labor-cost reset,
- shift reduction / line underutilization,
- attrition and voluntary exits,
- internal redeployment,
- productivity gains,
- overhead reduction,
- and selective capex restraint.

If the gap is **large and structural**, then non-closure measures usually run into hard limits because they do **not remove enough fixed cost**. In that case, you typically need some mix of:
- plant closure,
- line consolidation,
- major footprint rationalization,
- or a permanent reduction in installed capacity.

## What can realistically be saved without closure

Typical order-of-magnitude effects in a large automotive restructuring:

- **Working-time / shift changes:** meaningful but limited; often reduces variable and semi-fixed costs, but does not eliminate the full site fixed-cost burden.
- **Voluntary attrition / internal redeployment:** useful for headcount reduction, but slow and bounded by age profile, skill mix, and demand for internal transfers.
- **Temporary labor reduction:** fast savings, but usually not enough alone.
- **Wage moderation / allowance cuts:** can lower unit labor cost, but durability depends on contract duration and compliance.
- **Overhead cuts:** can be significant at group level, but often smaller than market assumptions once essential engineering, compliance, and program support are preserved.
- **Productivity gains / line balancing:** real, but implementation risk is high and savings often take time to materialize.

## Feasibility without closures

A **non-closure solution is most plausible when**:
1. the savings gap is not too large,
2. there is clear underutilization at one or more sites,
3. the company can consolidate shifts or lines without major logistics disruption,
4. the workforce can be reduced mostly through attrition and voluntary exits,
5. and management accepts a smaller, phased target rather than an immediate step-change.

A **non-closure solution is unlikely to be sufficient when**:
1. several plants are structurally underloaded,
2. fixed site costs are high,
3. product volumes are declining,
4. and the required margin uplift is large enough that labor-only measures cannot bridge it.

## Capacity reduction without shutting plants

This is possible in principle, but there are constraints:

- **Shift reduction** can remove effective capacity if demand is weak, but the site remains open and retains much of its fixed cost.
- **Line consolidation** can reduce capacity materially if products can be migrated with limited retooling.
- **Sinking one model/one line** may create savings if the same plant can still operate at higher utilization on remaining products.
- **Partial footprint shrinkage** works only if the company can truly mothball part of a plant and not simply spread fixed overhead across fewer units.

In practice, **partial capacity reduction without closure usually delivers less savings than a full closure** because:
- building costs remain,
- maintenance and utilities remain,
- site-level management remains,
- and some logistics, tooling, and support costs remain.

## Employment impact

If management wants a **durable** savings package without closure, the employment reduction typically has to come from:
- attrition,
- voluntary severance,
- early retirement,
- temporary labor removal,

[Expert: Independent industrial restructuring ana — Round 4]

## Bottom line
A **credible non-closure restructuring is sometimes feasible**, but only if the company can close a **moderate** savings gap through measures that reduce cost and capacity **without removing entire plants**. If the required gap is **large**, non-closure measures usually become insufficient because too much **fixed cost** remains embedded in the footprint.

## What typically works without plant closures
The main non-closure levers are:

- **Working-time reductions / shift rationalization**
- **Temporary pay or allowance adjustments**
- **Voluntary exits and attrition**
- **Internal redeployment**
- **Reduction of temporary labor**
- **Line consolidation within plants**
- **Lower complexity / SKU rationalization**
- **Overhead cuts**
- **Supplier and logistics savings**
- **Productivity improvements**
- **Deferred or rephased investment**

These can produce meaningful savings, but they differ in durability:
- **Most durable:** overhead cuts, line consolidation, permanent headcount reduction, footprint simplification
- **Less durable:** temporary pay concessions, short-term working-time cuts, one-off measures

## What non-closure measures usually cannot do well
Without plant closures, the company often struggles to eliminate:

- **plant-level fixed overhead**
- **underutilized building, utility, and maintenance costs**
- **duplicative production capability**
- **mismatch between installed capacity and demand**
- **inefficient site-specific logistics**

That is why non-closure packages often work only if the overcapacity problem is not too deep.

## Key economic test
The decision hinges on whether the company can reach a target such as:

- **EBIT margin recovery**
- **return-on-sales improvement**
- **cash break-even improvement**
- **lower breakeven volume per vehicle**

If the gap is mostly **labor-cost** and **indirect-cost** driven, a non-closure package may work.  
If the gap is mostly **structural capacity overhang**, then a non-closure package may leave too much cost untouched.

## Rough scenario logic
A useful way to think about it:

### Scenario A: Non-closure only
Best when:
- volume decline is moderate
- one or more plants are underutilized but can be reconfigured
- there is enough scope for productivity and labor cost savings
- management and owners also contribute materially

Risk:
- savings may plateau before reaching the required margin target
- execution can be complex and slow
- savings may be partly temporary

### Scenario B: Hybrid
Usually the most realistic compromise:
- no full closure initially
- but capacity is cut through **line consolidation**, **shift elimination**, **site specialization**, and **redeployment**
- some workforce reduction occurs through voluntary exits and attrition
- partial footprint shrinkage happens inside plants rather than via closure

This often captures much of the savings of closure while limiting social damage.

### Scenario C: Closure-based
Usually necessary when:
- demand has structurally shifted downward
- multiple sites are duplicative
- fixed cost per unit is too high even after internal measures
- the company needs a step-change in cost base, not incremental savings

This produces the strongest savings, but also the highest one-time cost and highest conflict risk.

## Employment impact
Without closures, employment reductions still usually occur through:
- attrition
- voluntary exit programs
- early retirement
- reduced temp labor
- redeployment to EV, battery, software, or adjacent functions
- reduced hours / short-time work if demand weakens

So “non-closure” does **not** mean “no job loss.” It means job reductions are managed **without shutting plants**.

## One important constraint
A company can preserve plants while still reducing capacity, but only up to a point. If each site remains open, there is usually a floor beneath which cost cannot be pushed because each plant retains:

- management layer
- maintenance
- utilities
- compliance
- logistics
- canteen/security/indirect labor
- depreciation and site overhead

That floor is why complete closure sometimes becomes economically decisive.

## Practical conclusion
From an industrial restructuring perspective:

- **If the savings gap is modest:** a non-closure or hybrid solution can be credible.
- **If the savings gap is moderate but not tiny:** a hybrid solution is usually more realistic than pure non-closure.
- **If the savings gap is large:** plant closures or equivalent footprint exits become increasingly likely to be required for a durable solution.

## 1. Decision context
The company faces a genuine restructuring crisis driven by weak demand, high German cost levels, EV-transition investment pressure, and persistent overcapacity. The decision is not whether to restructure, but whether the restructuring can be made credible without German plant closures and compulsory redundancies, or whether footprint reduction must remain available if quantified savings fall short.

Governance makes this unusually difficult: co-determination gives labor an effective veto, the controlling family can shape strategy through governance, and the regional state has blocking rights and political legitimacy concerns. The transcript shows the discussion moving from principles to a numbers test, but the necessary plant-by-plant bridge has not yet been produced.

## 2. Stakeholder assembly
- **Group & brand management**: high executive authority; strongest operational claim.
- **Controlling family holding**: very high governance power and legitimacy.
- **Regional state government**: high institutional standing and blocking power.
- **Works council**: high legitimacy and formal veto power under co-determination.
- **Industrial union**: high bargaining power via collective agreement and strike capacity.
- **Institutional investors**: lower formal governance power, but important capital-markets legitimacy.

## 3. Final positions
### Group & brand management
Management insists the cost base is structurally uncompetitive and that closures and compulsory redundancies must remain available if internal measures do not close the gap. It supports burden-sharing and internal measures, but rejects any framework that precludes irreversible tools before the economics are tested.

### Works council
The works council rejects German plant closures, compulsory redundancies, and weakening of job-security/co-determination guarantees. It accepts real restructuring, internal flexibility, and quantified burden-sharing, but only if an all-sites-open path is tested first and closures are not treated as a preloaded fallback.

### Industrial union
The union shares the works council’s red lines on closures and forced layoffs, while being somewhat more pragmatic on temporary employee contributions. It is prepared to discuss a time-limited, measurable contribution if paired with binding site and job guarantees, but not as a back door to permanent erosion.

### Regional state government
The state supports restructuring in principle but insists that its veto role, regional stability mandate, and governance rights be respected. It strongly resists closures as a first resort and wants an all-sites-open test before any footprint reduction is contemplated.

### Controlling family holding
The family agrees that the company cannot accept a cosmetic settlement and supports material restructuring. However, it rejects any permanent prohibition on closures or compulsory reductions, arguing that management must retain the tools needed to restore competitiveness if the numbers require it.

### Institutional investors
Investors want a durable margin reset and lower fixed costs, but also want to avoid conflict and execution risk. They will accept a non-closure solution if it truly closes the gap, but will not support a deal that preserves excess capacity and leaves the cost base structurally impaired.

## 4. Genuine conflicts
1. **Sequence conflict**: labor/state want an all-sites-open test first; management/family/investors want closures to remain live now.
2. **Irreversibility conflict**: labor treats closures and compulsory redundancies as unacceptable defaults; management treats them as necessary contingencies.
3. **Guarantee conflict**: labor seeks binding site/job security through 2030 and beyond; management wants flexibility to alter guarantees if needed.
4. **Governance conflict**: the state and labor view co-determination and veto rights as protective architecture; management/family/investors see them as possible obstacles to competitiveness.
5. **Economic sufficiency conflict**: all sides agree numbers matter, but disagree on whether non-closure measures are likely to be enough.

## 5. Areas of convergence
- The current situation is unsustainable and requires real restructuring.
- Cosmetic measures are unacceptable.
- Burden-sharing is necessary.
- Internal measures should be fully exploited first where possible.
- A negotiated settlement is preferable to industrial conflict.
- A quantified, plant-by-plant savings bridge is required.
- Management, ownership, and labor should all contribute in some form.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker
- **Competitiveness vs. social legitimacy**: deeper footprint reduction likely improves economics but increases conflict and political damage.
- **Speed vs. verification**: moving fast may satisfy investors and management, but the transcript shows trust is too low to skip the verification phase.
- **Durability vs. flexibility**: labor wants durable guarantees; management wants retained strategic flexibility.
- **Conflict avoidance vs. restructuring credibility**: excluding closures may reduce immediate conflict, but only works if numbers prove the gap can still be closed.
- **Regional stability vs. enterprise value**: the state prioritizes local industrial stability, while investors and management prioritize long-term competitiveness.

## 7. Integrative pathways
### A. Two-stage process
First test a verified all-sites-open scenario with no closure announcement during the verification stage. Only if the residual gap remains above an agreed threshold do the parties reopen discussion on footprint reduction.

### B. Time-limited non-closure pact
A temporary agreement could freeze closures and compulsory redundancies for a defined period in exchange for quantified labor contributions, management/owner contributions, and independent verification of savings.

### C. Hybrid restructuring
Use non-closure measures aggressively: shift cuts, line consolidation, internal redeployment, voluntary exits, reduced temporary labor, overhead cuts, and productivity gains. This can materially reduce capacity without immediate site closure, while preserving an escalation path if the residual gap remains too large.

### D. Conditional safeguards
If deeper adjustments later become necessary, require advance agreement on triggers, consultation procedures, transition supports, and site-specific protections rather than open-ended management discretion.

## 8. Open questions
- What is the actual savings gap after all non-closure measures?
- How much fixed cost remains tied to each plant?
- What volume and utilization assumptions are being used?
- What level of employee contribution is temporary, measurable, and reversible?
- What concrete management and owner contributions are on the table?
- What threshold distinguishes “moderate” from “large” residual gaps?
- What specific protections would the state require before any footprint reduction?
- Can a hybrid plan restore ROS without closures, or only delay the need for them?

## 9. Recommended next step
Commission a verified, plant-by-plant savings bridge and hold a structured negotiation on three scenarios:
1. all-sites-open,
2. hybrid capacity reduction without closures,
3. closure-based restructuring as fallback.

The bridge should show fixed costs, non-closure savings, management/owner contributions, residual gap, one-time restructuring costs, headcount impact, and margin/ROS effects. No side should announce irreversible action before that comparison is completed.

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction
- The real dispute is not “restructuring or not,” but **whether irreversible measures are legitimate before numbers are verified**.
- Labor’s position hardened around a **sequence demand**: first prove the all-sites-open path fails, then discuss footprint reduction. That sequencing was much clearer after several rounds.
- Management’s position also became more explicit: it is not demanding closures immediately, but it is refusing to surrender them as an option in advance.
- The state’s role is not merely political; it is acting as a **governance-based stabilizer** that wants to prevent rushed closure logic.
- The expert clarified a crucial distinction: **non-closure is feasible only if the savings gap is moderate**, which reframes the debate from ideology to threshold-testing.
- A possible compromise emerged only after repeated iteration: **all-sites-open first, with pre-agreed triggers and a structured fallback**, rather than an immediate closure/no-closure binary.
