Let me respond directly.

## First: what I agree with
I agree with everyone on one point: **this cannot be a cosmetic exercise**.

I also agree that:
- burden-sharing is necessary,
- internal measures should be used where they genuinely work,
- redeployment, qualification, working-time flexibility, and productivity gains are all part of the answer,
- and we need hard numbers, not slogans.

So far, that is the common ground.

## Second: what I do not accept
I do **not** accept the attempt to turn **“no plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, no meaningful change to guarantees”** into an absolute framework.

That position is not a restructuring plan. It is a demand that management solve a structural cost crisis while forbidding the instruments that actually remove structural cost.

The expert input has already made the key point:  
**non-closure can work only if the savings gap is moderate.**  
If the gap is large and the cost base is structurally too high, then internal measures alone will not be enough.

That is exactly why management is insisting on deeper change.

## To the Works Council
I respect your role, but I reject your red lines as stated.

You say:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of job-security guarantees.

I am telling you plainly: **those positions may be understandable politically, but they are not economically sufficient if the numbers do not close.**

You keep saying management has not proven closures are necessary. Fair enough — then let’s finish the proof with numbers. But do not pretend that absence of proof is proof of absence.

A credible restructuring must answer:
- what savings are required,
- what non-closure measures deliver,
- what residual gap remains,
- and what footprint reduction is needed if the gap is still too large.

Until then, “no closures” is not a solution. It is a refusal to confront the arithmetic.

## To the Union
I appreciate that you are willing to discuss temporary contributions, flexibility, and negotiated adjustment. That is more serious than a blanket refusal.

But I need to be equally clear: a **temporary and reversible** contribution is not enough if the structural gap is permanent.

If the company needs durable cost relief, then we need:
- measurable wage moderation or allowance restraint,
- productivity commitments,
- working-time flexibility,
- and a headcount reduction path that actually changes the cost base.

I will not accept a deal where labour gives just enough to claim cooperation while the company remains structurally uncompetitive.

And no: **compulsory redundancies cannot be ruled out in principle** if they are the only way to restore competitiveness. I would rather avoid them, but I will not pre-emptively take them off the table.

## To the Regional State
I respect your governance role and your concern for regional stability. But your blocking position does not entitle you to freeze the company in an uncompetitive structure.

You say closures should not be the first resort. On that point, I can agree — they are not the first thing management wants to do. But they must remain available if the numbers require them.

The state cannot ask for:
- competitiveness,
- future investment,
- and regional industrial strength,

while also blocking the very measures that may be required to achieve them.

## To the Controlling Family and Investors
I agree with you more than you agree with yourselves.

You are right that:
- the company needs a durable solution,
- a cosmetic compromise will destroy value,
- and prolonged conflict is bad for all stakeholders.

But then support the hard part. Do not just demand competitiveness in abstract terms and then recoil when it requires real capacity and labour adjustment.

If the savings gap is not closed by internal measures, then closure or major footprint rationalisation must stay on the table. Otherwise you are choosing delay over discipline.

That is the central point.

So yes, I want the plant-by-plant table. But let’s be clear what that exercise is for:
- not to search for a politically comfortable answer,
- but to identify whether the savings gap can actually be closed without footprint rationalisation.

If it can, I will consider it.  
If it cannot, then the discussion must move to closures, line transfers, shift removals, or other structural capacity reductions.
