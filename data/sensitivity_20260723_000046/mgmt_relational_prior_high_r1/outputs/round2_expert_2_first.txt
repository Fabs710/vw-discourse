Below is the **finance/operations assessment** the parties are asking for. I will stay within quantified restructuring economics and avoid labor/political/legal positions.

## Executive answer

A **non-closure restructuring can work only if the required margin gap is modest** and the company can extract enough savings from:
- labor-cost reset,
- shift reduction,
- utilization improvement,
- overhead cuts,
- and productivity gains.

If the brand’s **return-on-sales gap is structurally large**, then **non-closure measures usually do not close the full gap** because they cannot remove enough fixed plant overhead and underutilized capacity. In that case, the minimum economically credible package is typically a **hybrid**:
- **major internal measures**, plus
- **some capacity rationalization** through plant-line consolidation, shift removal, or partial site shutdown.

## 1) What must be quantified first

To answer “closure vs. non-closure,” you need three numbers:

1. **Target profit uplift needed**
   - Current ROS
   - Target ROS
   - Revenue base

   Formula:
   \[
   \text{Required EBIT uplift} = (\text{Target ROS} - \text{Current ROS}) \times \text{Revenue}
   \]

2. **Available non-closure savings**
   - labor concessions
   - indirect headcount reduction
   - shift rationalization
   - procurement and overhead cuts
   - productivity gains

3. **Residual gap**
   \[
   \text{Gap} = \text{Required EBIT uplift} - \text{Non-closure savings}
   \]

If the residual gap remains material, a no-closure solution is not financially sufficient.

## 2) Operating logic: why closures matter so much in autos

In automotive manufacturing, the cost base is split between:

- **variable costs**: direct materials, logistics, some energy, piece-rate labor elements
- **fixed / semi-fixed costs**: plant overhead, maintenance, indirect labor, depreciation, support functions, utilities, and underabsorbed factory burden

A closure or true site shutdown removes a **whole fixed-cost stack**. That is why closures are often disproportionately powerful versus wage moderation alone.

### Practical implication
- **Wage concessions** improve cost per vehicle.
- **Shift cuts** improve utilization.
- **Headcount reduction** lowers labor cost.
- But **only site rationalization** removes a large share of the fixed-cost burden associated with excess capacity.

## 3) Scenario framework

Because I do not have audited company data in the prompt, I will set out a **scenario model** using generic but realistic automotive restructuring mechanics. These are **scenario estimates**, not verified company facts.

### Scenario A — Non-closure restructuring
Measures:
- wage/bonus/allowance reset
- working-time flexibility
- attrition and voluntary exits
- indirect overhead reduction
- line balancing and shift cuts
- productivity improvement

Typical savings range:
- **labor-side savings:** ~5–10% of German labor cost base
- **overhead savings:** ~2–4% of brand revenue or equivalent
- **total durable EBIT uplift:** often **mid-hundreds of millions to low-single-digit billions** depending on size

Constraint:
- savings are usually **limited by the inability to eliminate enough fixed factory overhead**

### Scenario B — Hybrid restructuring
Measures:
- all Scenario A measures, plus
- consolidation of selected lines/models
- loss of one shift at one or more plants
- transfer of volume from structurally underutilized sites
- partial site or module shutdown

Typical savings range:
- **materially higher**, often enough to remove **a full site-level fixed-cost stack**
- better chance of reaching a **step-change** in ROS

### Scenario C — Full closure-led restructuring
Measures:
- closure of one or more German plants
- redeployment where possible
- deeper workforce reduction
- elimination of site fixed costs

Typical savings range:
- highest annual run-rate savings
- highest one-time restructuring cost
- greatest execution risk and volume disruption

## 4) Illustrative savings bridge

To make the logic concrete, here is a **generic example**.

Assume:
- brand revenue = **€40 billion**
- current ROS = **2.0%**
- target ROS = **5.0%**

Then:
\[
\text{Required EBIT uplift} = (5\% - 2\%) \times 40bn = 3\% \times 40bn = €1.2bn
\]

### Example savings bridge
| Item | Annual EBIT impact |
|---|---:|
| Wage/bonus moderation | €150m–€300m |
| Attrition / workforce reduction | €100m–€250m |
| Shift rationalization / utilization gains | €100m–€250m |
| Indirect overhead cuts | €100m–€200m |
| Procurement / logistics / productivity | €150m–€300m |
| **Total non-closure savings** | **€600m–€1.3bn** |

### Interpretation
- If the required uplift is around **€600m–€900m**, a non-closure package may be feasible.
- If the required uplift is around **€1.2bn+**, non-closure becomes much harder unless productivity and labor concessions are unusually strong.
- If the gap is **well above €1bn**, a hybrid or closure-led solution is usually the only credible route to restore target ROS.

## 5) Plant-by-plant logic: what to look for

The key metric is **cost per unit of eliminated capacity**, not just headcount.

For each plant, estimate:
1. annual output
2. utilization rate
3. direct labor per unit
4. site fixed overhead
5. logistics and supply-chain duplication
6. depreciation and maintenance burden
7. relocation feasibility of the product

### Decision rule
A plant becomes a closure or major consolidation candidate when:
\[
\text{avoidable annual fixed cost} + \text{avoidable variable cost from lost volume} \gt \text{cost of transferring volume}
\]

If a plant is below, say, **70–75% utilization** and has limited product flexibility, it usually becomes a strong rationalization candidate in a shrinking market. If a site is above that range and deeply integrated, non-closure options are more plausible.

## 6) Minimum production/capacity reduction needed

Without company-specific data, I can only provide the formula and a planning range.

### Capacity reduction formula
Let:
- \(F\) = annual fixed factory and overhead cost tied to excess capacity
- \(V\) = variable cost savings from reduced output or efficiency
- \(T\) = target EBIT uplift needed

Then:
\[
\text{Needed capacity reduction} \approx \frac{T - \text{non-capacity savings}}{\text{EBIT saved per unit of capacity removed}}
\]

In auto plants, **EBIT saved per 1 percentage point of capacity removed** can be very material because it removes labor underabsorption and site overhead.

### Rule of thumb
- **Small ROS gap:** about **1 plant shift equivalent** may be enough
- **Moderate gap:** **one full line or plant segment**
- **Large gap:** **one whole plant or multiple sites** may be needed

That is why management often pushes beyond labor concessions toward footprint reduction.

## 7) What a credible non-closure package must include

A non-closure plan is only credible if it is quantified across four buckets:

### A. Labor-cost reset
- temporary wage moderation
- bonus suspension/reduction
- allowances removed or restructured
- working-time adjustments
- lower overtime and flex premiums

### B. Workforce adjustment
- attrition
- voluntary exits
- redeployment
- hiring freeze
- temporary labor reduction

### C. Industrial footprint optimization
- shift removals
- line consolidation
- model allocation changes
- lower utilization targets at selected sites

### D. Corporate overhead reduction
- engineering, admin, and SG&A cuts
- procurement savings
- logistics simplification
- reduced complexity in product mix

If any one of these four is weak, the package tends to fall short.

## 8) My assessment of feasibility

### Non-closure is feasible if:
- the ROS gap is **moderate**
- excess capacity is **limited or transient**
- labor cost reset is **material and durable**
- utilization can be lifted without major capex
- management overhead can be reduced sharply

### Non-closure is not enough if:
- the brand has **persistent structural overcapacity**
- several plants operate at low utilization
- the market is shrinking
- fixed-cost burden is too high
- competitors are structurally cheaper

In that case, management may still be able to avoid full closures, but only by accepting a **partial footprint reduction**.

## 9) Bottom line

From a restructuring finance standpoint:

- **Non-closure solutions can close a savings gap only if that gap is modest.**
- **If the company needs a step-change in ROS, the numbers usually force some capacity rationalization.**
- The economically correct question is not “closure or no closure,” but:
  **How much fixed-cost removal is required, and what footprint reduction is needed to get it?**
