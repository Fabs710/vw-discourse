Thank you. The expert input is useful because it confirms exactly what management has been saying: **a non-closure solution may work only if it delivers real, durable savings at scale**. That is the test. Not slogans, not moral appeals, not a promise that everything will remain intact.

## Where I agree
I agree with the works council, union, state, family shareholder, and investors on one point: **this must be a real restructuring, not a cosmetic exercise**. I also agree that internal measures should be used wherever they are genuinely effective:
- working-time adjustments,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- shift rationalisation,
- productivity improvements,
- and a negotiated employee contribution.

I also agree that a prolonged conflict would be damaging. Strikes, uncertainty, and reputational harm all destroy value.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the core red line that every German plant must remain open no matter what the economics are.

The expert has just confirmed the problem: **if the savings gap is large and overcapacity is structural, non-closure measures often fall short**. That is exactly the situation management is confronting. We are not discussing a mild margin tweak; we are discussing a company that is missing its return-on-sales target, facing a shrinking market, and carrying a cost base above competitors. In that context, preserving every site as a principle is not a strategy.

So let me be very clear:

- **No, I will not accept a settlement that leaves the cost base uncompetitive.**
- **No, I will not accept guarantees that lock in overcapacity through 2030 regardless of market reality.**
- **No, I will not accept a deal that shifts all structural adjustment onto future investment budgets.**

## On closures
The expert also makes the key point that closures are not the only possible route, but they are often the clearest way to remove persistent fixed cost. That matters.

I am not married to closures as an ideological end. But I am absolutely committed to the outcome: **durable capacity and cost reduction**. If the non-closure package truly closes the gap, fine — but it must be quantified, credible, and sufficient. If it does not, then closures remain on the table.

That means the current stance from labour — “no closures, no matter what” — is not acceptable.

## On compulsory redundancies
I hear the opposition to forced layoffs. I am not eager for them either. But I will not rule out workforce reductions if they are needed to restore competitiveness. The expert has shown that non-closure reductions can be gradual, but they are slower and less certain. We are under time pressure.

So my position is:
- preference for voluntary and negotiated exits,
- use of attrition and redeployment first,
- but no artificial ban on compulsory measures if the economics require them.

## On labour cost contribution
The expert is also clear that wage moderation, bonuses, allowances, and working-time measures can contribute meaningfully. Good. That is exactly the direction we need.

But I want to be equally direct: a **temporary and limited contribution** is not enough if the structural gap is permanent. We need a **genuine labour-cost contribution** that is durable enough to matter. If labour wants to avoid closures, then labour must help close the gap, not just symbolically.

That means management will require:
- measurable wage and allowance restraint,
- productivity commitments,
- working-time flexibility,
- and a credible headcount reduction path.

## On management and owner contributions
I welcome the repeated calls for management and owner contributions. Fine. We are prepared to do our part. But those contributions cannot substitute for structural plant and labour adjustments.

I will not accept an arrangement where:
- management cuts a bit of overhead,
- owners make statements,
- and labour is expected to carry the entire operational adjustment while the industrial footprint remains unchanged.

That is not a restructuring. That is postponement.

## On the state
I respect the state’s role and the governance reality. But the state cannot use its veto to freeze the company in place. The public interest is not served by protecting every current site if that leaves the company weaker, less investable, and ultimately more exposed.

The state should be helping to secure a viable future, not protecting an unsustainable structure. If that means accepting difficult site decisions, then the honest answer is: yes, that may be necessary.

## On the controlling family and investors
You both say you want durable competitiveness. Good. Then back that up by supporting real structural change.

I agree with the family shareholder and investors that a cosmetic compromise would destroy value. But I disagree with the implication that we can get to competitiveness while treating closures as politically impossible. If the savings are not achievable otherwise, then refusing closures is simply choosing decline more slowly.

## What I need to see next
The expert has given a framework, but not the company-specific numbers. So I want the next round to move from principle to arithmetic.

[SUMMON: Company restructuring finance lead / operations planning expert to present the quantified savings gap by plant, the fixed-cost base, and the minimum capacity reduction required to restore the brand’s return-on-sales target.]

Specifically, we need:
1. the savings target required to restore margins,
2. the savings achievable through non-closure measures,
3. the gap that remains,
4. and which plants or lines are structurally underutilized.

Until we have that, the blanket rejection of closures is not credible.

## Bottom line
My position remains firm:

- I support a negotiated restructuring.
- I support serious non-closure measures if they are sufficient.
- I do **not** accept “no closures” as an absolute principle.
- I do **not** accept preserving every guarantee if that prevents competitiveness.
- I do **not** accept a deal that leaves the company unable to fund future products and technology through 2030.

If the non-closure package truly delivers the required savings, I will consider it. If it does not, then the company must have the freedom to act accordingly — including plant rationalisation.
