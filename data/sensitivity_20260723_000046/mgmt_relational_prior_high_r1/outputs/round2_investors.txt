I’ll be blunt: capital markets care about **credible margin restoration**, not about whether the restructuring is politically elegant.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family shareholder, and the finance expert on the core point: **the current cost base is not competitive, and a cosmetic compromise would destroy value**. If the brand is missing its return-on-sales target and German operations remain structurally too expensive, then continued drift is not acceptable.

I also agree with labour and the state on one important issue: **a prolonged strike war is value-destructive**. Uncertainty, lost production, and reputational damage will hurt enterprise value quickly. So yes, a negotiated settlement is preferable to open conflict.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** support the workforce’s attempt to make **“no plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, no dismantling of guarantees”** into an absolute veto unless a fully quantified alternative closes the savings gap.

That position may be understandable, but from a shareholder perspective it is only acceptable if it is backed by numbers that actually restore competitiveness. So far, I have not seen that.

I also do **not** support management treating closures as the default answer before exhausting credible non-closure alternatives. The expert input was useful here: closures may be economically powerful, but they are not automatically the best option if a sufficiently deep internal restructuring can deliver the same run-rate savings with less execution risk.

## What matters to us
We need a package that does four things:

1. **Closes the cost gap durably**
2. **Restores margins and returns on sales**
3. **Avoids prolonged industrial conflict**
4. **Is credible to the market and implementable under the governance constraints**

That means we need to see a real bridge, not slogans.

## On the non-closure alternative
A non-closure solution is acceptable to us **only if** it is big enough and durable enough. That means:
- meaningful labor-cost reset,
- working-time flexibility,
- headcount reduction through attrition/redeployment,
- overhead cuts,
- shift rationalization,
- and quantified productivity gains.

If that package genuinely closes the EBIT gap, then fine — it is likely better risk-adjusted value than closures because it reduces political and execution risk.

But if it does **not** close the gap, then insisting on “no closures” becomes a demand to preserve excess capacity and structural inefficiency. That is not stewardship; it is value destruction.

## On plant closures
We are **not ideologically attached** to closures. We are attached to **outcomes**.

But let me be equally clear: if the company needs a step-change in competitiveness and the numbers show that internal measures fall short, then some form of capacity rationalization must remain on the table. That may mean closure, but it may also mean line transfers, shift elimination, or partial site consolidation. I am not wedded to any one instrument.

What I cannot support is:
- preserving every site regardless of economics,
- locking in overcapacity through 2030,
- and expecting shareholders to fund that indefinitely.

## On job security and labour contributions
I respect the social dimension, but long-standing guarantees cannot be treated as untouchable if they prevent the business from becoming viable.

What I would support:
- temporary and limited employee contributions,
- negotiated wage restraint or allowance reductions,
- voluntary exits and attrition,
- redeployment and qualification,
- and working-time flexibility.

What I would **not** support:
- open-ended guarantees that block structural change,
- or a settlement where labour contributes marginally while the cost base remains uncompetitive.

## On management
Management has made the right diagnosis, but it still needs to prove the execution case.

I want management to come back with:
- the **savings target** needed to restore ROS,
- the **non-closure savings bridge**,
- the **residual gap**,
- and the **minimum capacity reduction** required if closures are avoided.

Without that, you are asking investors to endorse a strategy on faith.

## On the state and governance
I respect the state’s role and the co-determination structure, but governance rights are not a license to freeze the company in an uncompetitive shape. The public interest is not served by defending every current plant if the result is a weaker company with less future investment capacity.

## My line
Capital markets will support a tough restructuring if it is:
- quantified,
- durable,
- and sufficient to restore competitiveness.

We will **not** support:
- a cosmetic settlement,
- a deal that leaves the cost base structurally impaired,
- or an outcome that substitutes political comfort for economic reality.
