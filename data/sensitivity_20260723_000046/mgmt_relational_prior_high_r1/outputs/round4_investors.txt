I’ll be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family shareholder, and the finance expert on the core issue: **this is a structural margin problem, not a cosmetic one**. If German costs remain above peers and the brand stays below its return-on-sales target, equity value keeps leaking.

I also agree with labour and the state on one important point: **a prolonged strike conflict would be value-destructive**. Lost output, damaged customer confidence, and governance noise are all bad for shareholders. A negotiated outcome is clearly preferable.

And I agree that the restructuring must include:
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time adjustments,
- productivity improvements,
- overhead cuts,
- and some form of employee contribution if it is real and durable.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** support the labour side trying to make **“no plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, no meaningful change to guarantees”** the default endpoint.

That may be politically understandable, but from a capital-markets perspective it is only acceptable if the numbers show it still restores competitiveness. So far, that proof has not been delivered.

At the same time, I also do **not** support management treating closures as the presumed answer before the plant-level economics are fully laid out. “May be necessary” is not the same as “proven necessary.” If the company wants investors to back a hard restructuring, it needs hard evidence.

## My position on closures
We are **not ideologically attached to closures**. We are attached to outcomes:
- restored margins,
- lower fixed costs,
- credible competitiveness,
- and durable savings.

If a non-closure package genuinely closes the EBIT gap, that is likely the better risk-adjusted route because it lowers conflict risk and execution risk.

But if it does **not** close the gap, then insisting on no closures becomes a demand to preserve excess capacity and an uncompetitive cost base. That would be value destruction, not stewardship.

## My position on compulsory redundancies
I would prefer to avoid forced layoffs because they raise execution risk and can worsen the conflict. But I will **not** rule them out in principle if they are needed to restore competitiveness.

My preference order is:
1. attrition
2. voluntary exits
3. redeployment/retraining
4. working-time changes
5. targeted labour-cost concessions
6. compulsory measures only if the economics leave no credible alternative

If labour wants to avoid the last step, it should help close the gap in a way that is **durable**, not symbolic.

## On the state and governance
I respect the state’s role and the co-determination framework. But governance rights are not a license to freeze the company in an uncompetitive structure. The public interest is not served by defending every current site if the result is a weaker company with less future investment capacity.

At the same time, I do **not** support using governance pressure to force through a cosmetic settlement that looks socially palatable but leaves margins impaired.

## Bottom line
Capital markets will support a tough restructuring if it is:
- quantified,
- durable,
- and sufficient to restore competitiveness.

We will **not** support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a deal that leaves the cost base structurally impaired,
- or an outcome that preserves every current structure while expecting shareholders to absorb the consequences.

If the non-closure path closes the gap, good — take it.  
If it does not, then the company must have the freedom to rationalize capacity further.

That is the only position that protects long-term shareholder value.
