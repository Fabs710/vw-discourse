I’ll be equally direct in round 2.

## Where I agree
I agree with the works council, the state, and even management on one central point: **this cannot be a cosmetic restructuring**. The company needs a **real, durable solution** that restores competitiveness and gives the business a future.

I also agree that:
- **burden-sharing is necessary**,
- **internal redeployment, qualification, and working-time instruments** should be used,
- and **a negotiated package is preferable to open conflict**.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the idea that the only serious path is plant closures and mass job cuts. That is still being presented too quickly as if it were unavoidable.

What I need to see first is a **quantified non-closure alternative** that shows:
- the size of the savings gap,
- how much can be closed through capacity adjustments,
- what working-time and flexibility measures can deliver,
- what productivity improvements are realistic,
- and what management and owners are contributing.

Until that is on the table, closures remain a management preference, not a proven necessity.

## To management
You keep saying structural change is needed. Fine. But **structural change is not the same thing as closure**.

If you want the workforce to contribute, then you need to stop treating labour as the only adjustable cost factor. We are prepared to discuss:
- a **temporary, negotiated employee contribution**,
- **forgoing bonuses or other elements of compensation**,
- **working-time adjustments**,
- and **capacity reduction through internal measures**,

but only if this is linked to **binding guarantees on jobs and sites** and not used as a pretext for layoffs later.

What I will not accept is:
- open-ended insecurity,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or a restructuring where workers give, and owners simply watch.

## To the works council
I support your red lines on **no plant closures** and **no compulsory redundancies**. Those are not just symbolic issues; they are about preserving industrial capacity, bargaining power, and trust.

That said, I want to be clear about the collective-bargaining side: if we are going to ask members to accept sacrifice, it must be **fair, limited, and reversible where possible**. We should not hand management a blank cheque for permanent concessions.

So yes:
- protect all German sites,
- protect against forced layoffs,
- preserve the collective framework,

but the settlement must also include a **credible labour contribution** so management cannot claim we refused every adjustment.

## To the state
I welcome your support against closures and forced redundancies. But I agree with you only if that support is used to force a **serious alternative**, not just to delay the inevitable until the next crisis.

If the state wants to use its governance role responsibly, then it should press for:
- a negotiated restructuring package,
- binding investment commitments,
- site and employment guarantees,
- and fair contributions from management and ownership.

## To the controlling family and investors
You are right about one thing: the company needs competitiveness. But you are wrong if you think competitiveness can be restored by pushing the whole adjustment onto labour.

A “durable” solution cannot mean:
- labour gives up pay and security,
- sites are hollowed out,
- and ownership gets to present that as responsible governance.

That is not a durable settlement. It is conflict deferred.

## What I can support
I can support a package if it contains all of the following:
1. **No plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **A quantified employee contribution that is time-bound**
4. **Management and owner contributions**
5. **Capacity reduction through internal measures**
6. **Binding investment and site guarantees**
7. **Preservation of the collective wage framework**

## What I reject
I reject any package that:
- uses “competitiveness” as a blanket justification for closures,
- makes workers bear the full burden,
- breaks the collective framework,
- or treats strikes and mobilisation as if they were the problem rather than the last resort.

## Bottom line
We are ready to negotiate hard. We are not ready to surrender on the core protections of jobs, sites, and collective bargaining.

If management wants a settlement, it must bring forward a **real non-closure package with numbers**. If it does not, then it should expect continued industrial action and escalating resistance.
