I’ll be equally clear in round 4.

## Where I agree
I agree with the works council, the state, and the expert on the most important point: **this has to be a real restructuring, not a cosmetic one**.

I also agree that:
- burden-sharing is necessary,
- internal measures must be fully counted,
- redeployment, qualification, working-time flexibility, and productivity gains belong on the table,
- management and owners must contribute,
- and we need **plant-by-plant numbers**, not slogans.

That is the right basis for discussion.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the repeated effort to keep **closures and compulsory redundancies** permanently alive as if that were the neutral, responsible starting point.

It is not neutral. It is a threat that shifts the burden onto workers before the company has proven that less destructive options are insufficient.

### On closures
The works council is right to say that closures are not proven. The expert’s assessment was not “closures are necessary.” It was: **non-closure can work if the savings gap is moderate**. That is a very different statement.

So my position remains:
- **no plant closures in Germany**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **preserve the collective wage framework**
- **preserve job security and co-determination**

If management wants to argue otherwise, it must show that every serious internal option has been exhausted and still fails. We are not there.

### On compulsory redundancies
I reject the idea that forced layoffs must remain on the table “in principle.” In practice, that means management keeps the gun loaded while asking workers to trust the process.

That is not a fair bargaining basis.

If the company needs workforce adjustment, the sequence is clear:
1. attrition
2. voluntary exits
3. internal transfers
4. retraining and redeployment
5. reduced temporary labour
6. working-time solutions
7. fair employee contribution

Only after that would anyone even be talking about whether the restructuring design is wrong. I am not conceding forced layoffs as an acceptable instrument.

## On the works council’s position
I support the works council’s insistence that no German plant should be treated as disposable and that job security is part of the company’s social constitution.

Where I would add one thing: **the employee contribution we have already signaled cannot become an open-ended sacrifice**. It must be:
- temporary,
- measurable,
- reversible,
- and tied to binding guarantees.

If employees contribute, then management and owners must contribute materially too. Not symbolically. Not rhetorically.

## On management’s argument
Management keeps saying “we need structural change” and “the numbers may require it.” Fine — then bring the numbers.

But what I reject is the leap from:
- “there is a cost problem”
to
- “therefore closures and forced redundancies must stay on the table.”

That leap has not been justified.

If internal measures plus a fair labour contribution close most or all of the gap, then management has no credible basis to insist on plant closure logic. If there is a residual gap, then we discuss **what management and owners are prepared to do**, not whether workers should absorb the rest through site sacrifice.

## To the regional state
I agree that your governance role must be respected. I also agree that regional stability matters.

But respect for your role cannot become a way to dilute worker protections later. If the state is serious about its anchor-shareholder responsibility, it should be using that role to **prevent a rushed closure path**, not to normalize it.

## To the controlling family and investors
You are right about one thing: a cosmetic compromise would destroy value.

But you are wrong if you think the only serious alternative is closure pressure. A non-closure package can still be economically serious if it includes:
- capacity reduction through shifts and utilization,
- internal redeployment,
- overhead reductions,
- productivity gains,
- and real management/owner contribution.

That is exactly what should be tested first.

## What I need to see now
We still do **not** have the verified bridge that would justify changing my position.

So I repeat the request:

[SUMMON: Company restructuring finance lead and operations planning expert to provide a verified plant-by-plant savings bridge showing current fixed costs, savings from non-closure measures, management/owner contribution, residual gap, and the minimum additional capacity reduction required if all German plants remain open.]

I want the table to answer:
- what each site contributes to the savings gap,
- how much can be removed without plant closures,
- what management cuts are included,
- what owner contribution is included,
- and what remains after all of that.

## My line remains firm
I will support:
1. **No plant closures in Germany**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Preservation of the collective wage agreement**
4. **Binding site and job guarantees**
5. **A fair, time-limited employee contribution only in exchange for real guarantees**
6. **Management and owner contributions**
7. **Capacity reduction through internal measures only**

I will not support:
- closures as a fallback threat,
- forced layoffs by any name,
- or a deal where labour gives permanent pain while others preserve optionality.

## Bottom line
We are prepared to solve the crisis.  
We are not prepared to let workers carry it alone.

If management wants a durable settlement, it needs to stop treating the workforce as the adjustable variable of last resort and come back with the numbers showing a serious non-closure path.

Until then, my answer stays the same: **no closures, no compulsory redundancies, and a fair shared-sacrifice package only.**
