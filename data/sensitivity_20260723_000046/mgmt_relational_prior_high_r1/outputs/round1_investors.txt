From the perspective of dispersed capital-market shareholders, the central question is not who “wins” the confrontation, but whether the company returns to a credible, competitive cost base fast enough to protect enterprise value.

So let me be direct.

I agree with management, and with the family shareholder, on the core diagnosis: **the current cost structure is not sustainable**. If German operations remain above target on cost and the brand continues to miss its return-on-sales goals, then shareholder value will keep eroding. In that scenario, a refusal to act decisively is not prudence — it is value destruction.

I also agree with the union, works council, and state on one point: **a purely cosmetic settlement would be unacceptable**. A deal that preserves all structures, all guarantees, and all overcapacity while pretending competitiveness has been restored would not be credible to the market. It would delay the problem, not solve it.

Where I differ is on the instrument.

### On plant closures
I am **not attached to closures as a principle**. If comparable, durable savings can be achieved through alternative means — capacity reduction, working-time adjustments, productivity gains, targeted labour-cost contributions, and binding redeployment — then that is preferable from a risk-adjusted value perspective. Closures are disruptive, politically costly, and often trigger prolonged conflict. That conflict itself destroys value.

But I will be equally clear: **“No closures under any circumstances” is not a serious position unless it comes with a quantified alternative that fully closes the gap**. If the no-closure option does not deliver sufficient structural savings, then it is simply a demand to keep excess capacity and preserve an uncompetitive cost base. I cannot support that.

### On job security and labour contributions
I understand the workforce’s insistence on avoiding forced redundancies and preserving social stability. However, from a shareholder standpoint, long-standing guarantees cannot be sacrosanct if they prevent the business from becoming viable.

What I would support is:
- a **time-bound, negotiated employee contribution**,
- **internal redeployment and qualification** where it genuinely reduces costs,
- **working-time flexibility**,
- and **targeted adjustments to pay or allowances** if they are durable and substantial.

What I would not support is a settlement that asks for sacrifices from capital, state, and customers in the abstract while leaving labour-cost rigidity untouched.

### On management
Management is right to insist on real structural change. But management also needs to be judged on execution, not rhetoric. If the market is to believe in a turnaround, we need:
- **specific numbers**,
- **timelines**,
- **site-by-site impact**,
- and a **clear bridge back to margin targets**.

A statement that “something must be done” is not enough. A credible restructuring must show exactly how the savings will be achieved, how durable they are, and how they will improve returns on sales over the near and medium term.

### On the works council and union
Your position is understandable, but your red lines are too absolute if they prevent economic adjustment. A company with structurally excessive costs cannot be forced to carry every existing site and every existing guarantee indefinitely. That would protect employment in the short term while weakening the company until it can no longer invest, which ultimately harms everyone, including shareholders and workers.

If you want opposition from capital markets to remain limited, then the alternative package must be:
- **quantified**,
- **durable**,
- **implementable under the governance constraints**,
- and **large enough to restore competitiveness**.

Anything less will be seen as delay.

### On the regional state
I respect the governance reality and the public interest role, but the state should not use its position to freeze the company in an uncompetitive structure. The public interest is not served by preserving every job at any cost if that leaves the company weaker, less investable, and more vulnerable over time. A viable industrial base matters more than symbolic resistance to every closure.

### Bottom line
Capital markets will support **tough restructuring** if it is:
- credible,
- durable,
- sufficiently deep,
- and not endlessly diluted by conflict.

We will **not** support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a settlement that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive,
- or a prolonged standoff that destroys value through strikes, delays, and uncertainty.

So my message to all sides is simple: bring forward a package that can be defended with numbers. If the non-closure alternative truly delivers the required savings, we can support it. If it does not, then insisting on it is not responsible stakeholder governance — it is value destruction.
