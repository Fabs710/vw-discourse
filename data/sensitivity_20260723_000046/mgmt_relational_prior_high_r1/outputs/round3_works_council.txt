Let me be equally clear in round 3: **the burden of proof is still on management, and it has not been met**.

What I hear from management and the shareholders is a familiar pattern: “competitiveness,” “durable savings,” “structural change,” and then, almost immediately, the assumption that this must mean **closures and pressure on jobs**. I reject that as a starting point.

## Where I agree
I agree with the expert and with everyone who says this must be a **real restructuring**. I also agree that the company needs:
- a quantified savings bridge,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time flexibility,
- shift and utilization changes,
- overhead reductions,
- and contributions from management and ownership.

I do **not** deny the cost problem. I deny the claim that the only serious answer is to sacrifice German plants and workforce guarantees.

## Where I disagree
### 1) Management has still not proven that closures are necessary
The finance assessment was careful: **non-closure can work if the savings gap is moderate**. That is exactly why I will not accept the leap from “the company is under pressure” to “therefore a plant must close.”

What is missing is not rhetoric. What is missing is:
- the actual savings target,
- the plant-by-plant cost position,
- the non-closure savings available,
- the residual gap,
- and the minimum footprint change needed if — and only if — internal measures truly fail.

Until that is shown transparently, closures are not a necessity. They are a management preference.

### 2) “Durable” does not mean “break the social contract”
Management and the family shareholder keep saying that guarantees and job security cannot be allowed to block competitiveness. But that is exactly the point of co-determination and the job-security framework: to prevent short-term cost cutting from destroying the industrial base and trust that make this company viable in the first place.

A settlement that:
- tears up job security,
- legitimizes forced redundancies,
- and opens the door to German plant closures,
is not durable. It is a recipe for conflict, strikes, damaged productivity, and long-term instability.

### 3) Shareholders are underestimating the cost of conflict
The investors and the family keep speaking as if plant rationalization is the clean solution and labour resistance is an obstacle. It is not that simple.

You do not preserve value by provoking:
- warning strikes,
- public confrontation,
- legal disputes,
- loss of trust,
- and operational disruption across the German sites.

The works council’s position is not emotional theatre. It is a rational defense of industrial stability. If you force the company into a conflict spiral, you will destroy more value than you save.

## On compulsory redundancies
I remain firm: **no compulsory redundancies**.

The repeated suggestion that they must stay “on the table” is not a neutral statement. It is a threat hanging over every discussion. It shifts the burden onto workers while management keeps the option to impose its will later.

That is unacceptable. If the company cannot restructure without forced layoffs, then the restructuring model is wrong. The right path is:
- attrition,
- voluntary exits,
- internal transfers,
- reduced use of temporary labour,
- working-time solutions,
- qualification,
- and capacity planning that protects every German site.

## On closures
Let me say it plainly again: **no plant closures in Germany**.

Not as a first resort. Not as a fallback. Not as a bargaining chip. If we accept the logic that a plant can be closed whenever management says the numbers are difficult, then the whole job-security architecture becomes meaningless.

There is no serious settlement for the workforce if German plants are treated as disposable.

## On the “non-closure is only feasible if the gap is moderate” argument
Fine — then show whether the gap is actually moderate once all internal options are fully counted.

That is exactly why I support the next step being a **plant-by-plant scenario table**. But let us be honest about what that means:
- It must include **management and owner contributions**,
- it must fully count productivity and overhead options,
- and it must not assume closures as the baseline and then call that “inevitable.”

If a non-closure package cannot close the gap, then I want to see **why not**, in hard numbers, not assumptions.

## What I will support
I will support:
1. **Binding site guarantees for all German plants through 2030**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **No closures**
4. **A quantified, time-limited employee contribution only if it is tied to job and site guarantees**
5. **Management and owner contributions**
6. **Capacity reduction through internal measures only**
7. **Preservation of co-determination and the existing job-security framework**

## What I will not support
I will not support:
- closures as a “necessary” option without proof,
- forced layoffs under any name,
- concessions that are permanent while guarantees are temporary,
- or a restructuring that uses labour as the only adjustable variable.

## My challenge to management, shareholders, and the expert
If you want to convince me, do not keep speaking in abstractions. Put the numbers on the table:

- What is the exact savings gap?
- How much of it can be closed through non-closure measures?
- What are the one-time costs of a non-closure package?
- What capacity reductions can happen without closing a plant?
- What contributions are management, owners, and the state making?

If that table shows that every German plant can stay open and the company can still recover, then good — we have the right path. If it does not, then the numbers will expose not the weakness of the workforce’s position, but the weakness of the restructuring strategy.

## Bottom line
My position has not changed because the arguments against it are still not strong enough.

- **No plant closures in Germany**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Preserve co-determination and job security**
- **Negotiate only on a quantified package that protects sites and jobs**

If management wants my cooperation, it must stop treating the workforce as the shock absorber for a strategic failure.
