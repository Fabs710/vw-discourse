I’ll be direct.

## My position has not changed
The workforce will **not** accept:
- **German plant closures**
- **compulsory redundancies**
- or the dismantling of the **job-security and co-determination framework**

Those are not bargaining chips. They are the line between restructuring and breaking the social contract of this company.

## Where I agree
I agree with the expert, the union, and even management on one point: **this must be a real restructuring**. Cosmetic measures, delay tactics, or denial are not acceptable.

I also agree that:
- burden-sharing is necessary,
- internal measures must be fully counted,
- productivity improvements matter,
- working-time flexibility can be used,
- redeployment and qualification should be expanded,
- management and owners must contribute,
- and we need a plant-by-plant, quantified bridge.

That is all sensible. What is not sensible is using the lack of a final number as a pretext to keep closures and forced layoffs on the table from the start.

## Where I strongly disagree
### 1) Management is still trying to make closures look inevitable
You keep saying closures must remain available “if the numbers require them.”  
Fine — then show the numbers.

But until you do, closures are **not proven**. They are a management preference dressed up as economic necessity.

The expert’s own logic is clear: **non-closure can work if the gap is moderate**. That means the correct next step is to test the non-closure path honestly, not to threaten sites before that test is complete.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies are not an acceptable default
Management and shareholders keep presenting forced layoffs as an instrument of “discipline.” I call that what it is: shifting the burden onto workers after years of labour contribution and investment.

If the company needs workforce adjustment, then the first tools are:
- attrition,
- voluntary exits,
- internal transfers,
- reduced temporary labour,
- training,
- and workload redistribution.

If those are not enough, that is a sign the restructuring design is wrong, not that workers should lose protection.

### 3) “No plant closures” is not an emotional slogan
It is an industrial policy position.

Closing a German plant is not just a line item. It destroys:
- jobs,
- skills,
- local supplier networks,
- trust,
- and the legitimacy of the whole group’s industrial model.

And once you break the promise that a plant can be closed whenever profitability is under pressure, then every site becomes permanently vulnerable. That is exactly what we will not allow.

## To management
You say the cost base is structurally too high. Maybe.  
You say internal measures may not be enough. Possibly.  
But you still have not shown:
- the exact savings gap,
- the site-level fixed cost structure,
- what internal measures deliver by plant,
- and what residual gap remains after management and owner contributions.

Without that, your closure argument is premature.

So here is my challenge again: **put the verified plant-by-plant bridge on the table**. Not a general story. Not a threat. Real numbers.

## To the union
I support your red lines on compulsory redundancies and closures.  
I also support temporary, limited employee contributions **only if** they are tied to:
- binding site guarantees,
- preservation of jobs,
- and a clear end date.

But I want to be explicit: a “temporary contribution” cannot become a back door to permanent erosion of labour standards. If employees give, the company must give too — and management and owners must give materially, not symbolically.

## To the regional state
I agree with you that the state’s role and veto rights must be respected. That is not a problem; it is part of the governance architecture.

But I also need clarity: will you use that role to defend **all German plants**, or will you allow pressure to be used to chip away at them one by one? Because if the state is serious about regional industrial stability, it should say plainly that closures are not an acceptable first move.

## To the controlling family
You say you want long-term value. Then stop treating workers as if they are the obstacle to value.

The real risk to value is not the workforce defending jobs. The real risk is:
- provoking strikes,
- destroying trust,
- and forcing an industrial conflict that costs far more than a negotiated non-closure package would.

If you truly want a durable solution, support a package that protects sites and jobs first, then discuss contributions.

## To the investors
You are right that a cosmetic compromise would destroy value. I agree.  
But you are wrong if you think the only alternative to that is closure.

A non-closure solution can still be economically serious if it includes:
- capacity reduction through shifts and lines,
- internal redeployment,
- overhead cuts,
- productivity gains,
- and real management/owner sacrifice.

Do not confuse “no closure” with “no restructuring.”

## On the moderator’s suggested “conditional no-closure pact”
I do **not** accept a framework that quietly pre-positions closures as a fallback trigger.

Why? Because once closures are embedded as the second stage, the whole process becomes a countdown to plant sacrifice. That is not a protection mechanism; it is a delayed threat.

What I can accept is this:
- a **full non-closure scenario** to be evaluated first,
- with binding guarantees for jobs and sites through 2030,
- and with no compulsory redundancies.

Only if management can prove that the non-closure path truly cannot work would the numbers need to be revisited — and even then, the burden of proof remains exceptionally high.

But I am not agreeing to make closure an automatic option in any staged process.

## On what I will support
I will support:
1. **All German plants kept open**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Binding site guarantees through 2030**
4. **A quantified restructuring package**
5. **Internal capacity reduction only**
6. **Management and owner contributions**
7. **Preservation of co-determination and job security**

## On what I will not support
I will not support:
- plant closures as a “last resort” without exhausting and proving all alternatives,
- any forced layoff mechanism,
- or any deal where labour gives certainty away and management keeps optionality.

That is unacceptable.

If you want a durable deal:
- show the numbers,
