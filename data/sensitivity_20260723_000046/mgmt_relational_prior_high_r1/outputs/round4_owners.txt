## Where I agree
I agree with management, the expert, and the investors on the central point: **this cannot be a cosmetic restructuring**. The current cost base and overcapacity problem are real, and if we do not restore competitiveness and margins, long-term value will keep eroding.

I also agree with labour and the state on one important point: **a prolonged conflict would destroy value**. Strikes, reputational damage, and operational disruption are not acceptable. A negotiated solution is clearly preferable if it is credible.

And I agree that the first set of measures should be fully exploited:
- redeployment
- qualification
- working-time flexibility
- shift rationalisation
- productivity improvements
- overhead and management contribution

Those are sensible and necessary.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the attempt to turn **“no plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, no material change to guarantees”** into a hard starting framework.

That is not a restructuring strategy. It is a constraint set that risks leaving the company with structurally too high a cost base. If the numbers do not close, then we need the freedom to adjust capacity and footprint. Pretending otherwise would be value-destructive.

## On the Works Council
I respect your role, but I reject the claim that closures are already ruled out simply because management has not yet presented the final site-level bridge.

You are right that the burden of proof matters. But absence of final proof is not proof that closures are unnecessary.

The expert’s point was clear: **non-closure can work if the savings gap is moderate**. That is conditional, not conclusive. So yes, show the numbers — but do not pre-empt the result by declaring all irreversible options off-limits.

If the non-closure package truly closes the gap, I would prefer it because it likely lowers conflict risk and preserves more industrial value. If it does **not**, then we cannot pretend the company can be fixed without deeper capacity adjustment.

## On the Union
I appreciate that you are more constructive in tone than a blanket refusal. But I do **not** accept compulsory redundancies being ruled out in principle if the economics ultimately require workforce reduction.

My preference order is clear:
1. attrition
2. voluntary exits
3. internal transfers
4. retraining and redeployment
5. working-time solutions
6. targeted employee contribution
7. compulsory measures only if nothing else closes the gap

I would rather avoid forced layoffs. But I will not rule them out if they are necessary to restore competitiveness and preserve the company’s future investment capacity.

## On the Regional State
I respect the state’s governance role and the special statute. That does **not** mean I accept a de facto veto over economic adjustment.

You say closures should not be the first resort. I can accept that. They are not the first thing anyone wants to do.

But I do **not** accept the implication that the state can prevent footprint rationalisation even if the verified economics show it is required. Regional stability matters, but a weak company with no margin headroom is not a stable outcome either.

## On the controlling family and investors
I agree with you more than you seem to realize:
- a cosmetic compromise would destroy value,
- prolonged conflict would destroy value,
- and we need a durable solution.

Where I disagree is if anyone thinks we can secure durability while excluding the tools that remove structural cost. If the savings gap is large, then capacity reduction must remain available — including closures if necessary. That is not eagerness for confrontation; it is realism.

## What the expert has clarified
The expert’s economics are useful, and they point in one direction:
- **non-closure is feasible only if the savings gap is moderate**
- **partial capacity reduction without closure often leaves too much fixed cost in place**
- **if the required EBIT uplift is large, footprint rationalisation becomes increasingly likely**

That is the key issue. We are not debating whether there is a problem. We are debating how much of the problem can be removed without footprint change.

## My position, plainly
I am not here to force closures for their own sake. I am here to protect the long-term value of the company.

That means:
- I support a serious restructuring.
- I support burden-sharing.
- I support internal measures fully and first.
- I support a non-closure solution if it is sufficient.
- I do **not** accept no closures as an absolute principle.
- I do **not** accept no compulsory redundancies as an absolute principle if the economics leave no credible alternative.
- I do **not** accept a deal that leaves overcapacity and an uncompetitive cost base intact.

If the numbers show that an all-sites-open package truly closes the gap, I will prefer it because it likely reduces execution risk and conflict. But if they do not, then the company must have the freedom to rationalize capacity.

## Bottom line
We should stop debating slogans and force the arithmetic.

Bring the verified plant-by-plant numbers. Then we can determine whether a non-closure solution is genuinely sufficient, or whether deeper footprint adjustment is unavoidable.

Until then, I will not endorse any framework that rules out the necessary instruments in advance.
