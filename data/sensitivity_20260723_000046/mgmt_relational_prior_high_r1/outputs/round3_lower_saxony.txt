I will be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family shareholder, the union, the works council, and the expert on one central point: **this company needs a real restructuring, not a cosmetic one**. The cost gap cannot be ignored, and burden-sharing is necessary. I also agree that internal measures must be part of the package:
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time adjustments,
- shift rationalisation,
- productivity improvements,
- overhead cuts,
- and contributions from management and ownership.

I also agree that we need **numbers, not rhetoric**. A plant-by-plant, quantified scenario is the right next step.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept management’s repeated leap from “there is a cost problem” to “therefore plant closures and compulsory redundancies must remain on the table.”

That is not yet proven.

The expert input was careful: **non-closure can work if the savings gap is moderate**; if it is large, it becomes harder. That is a qualified statement, not a mandate for closures. What management still owes us is a transparent, plant-level bridge showing:
- the exact EBIT uplift required,
- what non-closure measures can deliver,
- what the residual gap is,
- and what footprint reduction would actually be needed.

Until then, closures are not “necessary”; they are one possible management preference.

## To management
I respect the need for competitiveness. But I reject the framing that preserves of every current safeguard must automatically be sacrificed to achieve it.

You have not yet demonstrated that all non-destructive options are exhausted. So I will not give a blank cheque for:
- German plant closures,
- forced redundancies,
- or the dismantling of job-security and co-determination protections.

A durable restructuring that destroys trust in the regions, triggers strikes, and weakens the company’s social legitimacy is not durable in any meaningful sense.

## To the Works Council and Union
I agree with your core concern: **the company must not solve a structural issue by gutting regional employment**. I also agree that forced redundancies should be avoided if at all possible, and that no settlement should treat workers as the only adjustable cost factor.

But I need to be equally frank: if the company is under severe structural pressure, then a purely defensive posture is not enough either. A credible non-closure package must be quantified and must include a real labour contribution that is:
- temporary or limited,
- fair,
- and clearly tied to binding job and site guarantees.

So I support your demand for site protection, but I want it embedded in a serious cost bridge, not just declared as a principle.

## To the controlling family and investors
You are right that a cosmetic compromise would destroy value. I do not dispute that.

But you are wrong if you think the only alternatives are either full closure logic or an untouched status quo. There is a middle path that protects the company as a regional anchor **if** the numbers support it:
- selective capacity reduction through shifts and lines,
- internal redeployment,
- temporary labour-cost contributions,
- management overhead reductions,
- and investment commitments tied to productivity.

That path must be tested before any discussion of closures in Germany.

## To the state’s own role
I will say this clearly: **the state’s veto and co-determination role must be respected**. That is not obstruction; it is legitimate governance under the special statute and part of the company’s social constitution.

And I will not support any outcome that:
- sidelines the state,
- removes its blocking role,
- or uses “restructuring” to push through a decision that would permanently damage regional employment.

That line is firm.

## What would move me
I am open to a tough compromise only if it protects the following:
1. **No German plant closures as a first resort**
2. **No compulsory redundancies unless every credible alternative is exhausted and proven insufficient**
3. **Preservation of the state’s co-determination and veto role**
4. **A quantified restructuring package with plant-level savings and capacity data**
5. **Binding investment and future-product commitments**
6. **A fair, time-limited employee contribution if — and only if — jobs and sites are secured**
7. **Management and owner contributions that are real, not symbolic**

That is the only basis on which I can support restructuring.

## What I reject
I reject:
- closures presented as inevitable before the numbers are fully shown,
- forced layoffs as a default instrument,
- any deal that hollows out the company’s regional footprint,
- and any arrangement that weakens the company’s social legitimacy while claiming to save it.

## Bottom line
I am prepared to back a serious restructuring that preserves the company as a strong regional anchor and protects the state’s role in governance.

I am **not** prepared to accept a restructuring that sacrifices German plants and jobs as the easy solution.

If the numbers show that a non-closure route truly closes the gap, then that is the better path. If they do not, then I will still insist that every alternative protecting sites and employment must be exhausted before any irreversible step is taken.
