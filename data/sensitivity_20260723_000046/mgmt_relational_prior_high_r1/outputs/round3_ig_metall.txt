I’ll be just as direct in round 3.

## Where I agree
I agree with the works council, the state, and even parts of management on one crucial point: **this cannot be solved cosmetically**. The company needs a **real restructuring**, and the expert analysis makes clear that simple, temporary fixes are unlikely to be enough if the savings gap is large.

I also agree that:
- burden-sharing is necessary,
- working-time flexibility, redeployment, qualification, and productivity measures should be part of the package,
- management and ownership must contribute,
- and we need hard numbers, not slogans.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept management’s leap from “there is a cost gap” to “therefore closures and forced layoffs must stay on the table.”

That is not a conclusion the numbers have forced yet. The expert did **not** say that non-closure is impossible. The expert said it depends on:
- the size of the savings gap,
- the amount of fixed cost that can be removed internally,
- and whether utilization can be improved enough.

That is exactly why I am not moving on the core red lines.

## On compulsory redundancies
I remain firm: **no compulsory redundancies**.

That is not a rhetorical flourish. It is a basic line between restructuring and management imposing the crisis on the workforce. If the company cannot restore competitiveness without forced layoffs, then management has not built a socially sustainable plan.

We are prepared to discuss:
- attrition,
- voluntary exits,
- internal transfers,
- reduced temporary labour,
- qualification and redeployment,
- and working-time instruments.

But we will not sign up to a model that gives management the right to force people out after workers have already made sacrifices.

## On plant closures
I also remain firm: **no plant closures in Germany**.

The works council is right that the company’s social legitimacy and industrial base cannot be treated as expendable. And the state is right that the company’s regional anchor role matters. A settlement that normalizes closures as the default answer would tear through trust and provoke exactly the conflict that destroys value.

To management, shareholders, and investors: if you want a durable settlement, stop treating plant closures as the first serious option. They are not a bargaining tactic we will reward.

## Where I do see room
I do see room for a serious package if it respects three conditions:

1. **Job and site security**
   - not empty language, but binding guarantees

2. **Fair contribution from labour**
   - temporary, limited, and linked to security

3. **Meaningful contributions from management and owners**
   - overhead cuts, investment commitments, and governance responsibility

That is the only basis on which we can discuss a durable contribution from employees.

## On the “the gap may be too large” argument
This is the key point where I want to push back.

Management and the family keep implying that because the company is under pressure, the burden automatically shifts to labour. That is not how this works.

If the gap is large, then **all sides** have to contribute more:
- management through overhead and restructuring of its own structures,
- owners through capital patience and responsible governance,
- labour through a fair but limited contribution,
- and the company through industrial planning that avoids throwing away productive capacity.

A large gap does **not** automatically justify closures or forced layoffs. It means the compromise has to be deeper and more balanced.

## What I need to see
The next step has to be a **plant-by-plant scenario table** with real numbers:
- required EBIT uplift,
- savings possible without closures,
- one-time restructuring costs,
- capacity reduction options without plant shutdowns,
- employment impact,
- and what each side contributes.

I want that analysis to include:
- shift reduction,
- working-time changes,
- use of attrition and voluntary exits,
- internal redeployment,
- productivity gains,
- overhead cuts at management level,
- and the labour contribution we have already signaled as possible.

That is the only honest way to test whether a non-closure path is viable.

## What I will support
I can support a package only if it includes:
1. **No plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Binding site guarantees**
4. **A time-limited and reversible employee contribution**
5. **Management and owner contributions**
6. **Capacity reduction through internal measures**
7. **Preservation of the collective wage framework**

## What I will not support
I will not support:
- a deal where workers give up pay and security while management preserves its own flexibility,
- a restructuring that uses “durability” as a cover for dismantling labour standards,
- or a package that leaves the company’s future dependent on conflict and coercion.

## Bottom line
We are not the obstacle to restructuring. We are the condition for a restructuring that can actually last.

If management wants a settlement, bring the numbers and bring a fair package.  
If it wants confrontation, then it should be clear: the union has the means to respond, and we will not accept workers bearing all the pain while others keep their options open.
