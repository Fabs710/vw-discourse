## Bottom line
A **credible non-closure restructuring is sometimes feasible**, but only if the company can close a **moderate** savings gap through measures that reduce cost and capacity **without removing entire plants**. If the required gap is **large**, non-closure measures usually become insufficient because too much **fixed cost** remains embedded in the footprint.

## What typically works without plant closures
The main non-closure levers are:

- **Working-time reductions / shift rationalization**
- **Temporary pay or allowance adjustments**
- **Voluntary exits and attrition**
- **Internal redeployment**
- **Reduction of temporary labor**
- **Line consolidation within plants**
- **Lower complexity / SKU rationalization**
- **Overhead cuts**
- **Supplier and logistics savings**
- **Productivity improvements**
- **Deferred or rephased investment**

These can produce meaningful savings, but they differ in durability:
- **Most durable:** overhead cuts, line consolidation, permanent headcount reduction, footprint simplification
- **Less durable:** temporary pay concessions, short-term working-time cuts, one-off measures

## What non-closure measures usually cannot do well
Without plant closures, the company often struggles to eliminate:

- **plant-level fixed overhead**
- **underutilized building, utility, and maintenance costs**
- **duplicative production capability**
- **mismatch between installed capacity and demand**
- **inefficient site-specific logistics**

That is why non-closure packages often work only if the overcapacity problem is not too deep.

## Key economic test
The decision hinges on whether the company can reach a target such as:

- **EBIT margin recovery**
- **return-on-sales improvement**
- **cash break-even improvement**
- **lower breakeven volume per vehicle**

If the gap is mostly **labor-cost** and **indirect-cost** driven, a non-closure package may work.  
If the gap is mostly **structural capacity overhang**, then a non-closure package may leave too much cost untouched.

## Rough scenario logic
A useful way to think about it:

### Scenario A: Non-closure only
Best when:
- volume decline is moderate
- one or more plants are underutilized but can be reconfigured
- there is enough scope for productivity and labor cost savings
- management and owners also contribute materially

Risk:
- savings may plateau before reaching the required margin target
- execution can be complex and slow
- savings may be partly temporary

### Scenario B: Hybrid
Usually the most realistic compromise:
- no full closure initially
- but capacity is cut through **line consolidation**, **shift elimination**, **site specialization**, and **redeployment**
- some workforce reduction occurs through voluntary exits and attrition
- partial footprint shrinkage happens inside plants rather than via closure

This often captures much of the savings of closure while limiting social damage.

### Scenario C: Closure-based
Usually necessary when:
- demand has structurally shifted downward
- multiple sites are duplicative
- fixed cost per unit is too high even after internal measures
- the company needs a step-change in cost base, not incremental savings

This produces the strongest savings, but also the highest one-time cost and highest conflict risk.

## Employment impact
Without closures, employment reductions still usually occur through:
- attrition
- voluntary exit programs
- early retirement
- reduced temp labor
- redeployment to EV, battery, software, or adjacent functions
- reduced hours / short-time work if demand weakens

So “non-closure” does **not** mean “no job loss.” It means job reductions are managed **without shutting plants**.

## One important constraint
A company can preserve plants while still reducing capacity, but only up to a point. If each site remains open, there is usually a floor beneath which cost cannot be pushed because each plant retains:

- management layer
- maintenance
- utilities
- compliance
- logistics
- canteen/security/indirect labor
- depreciation and site overhead

That floor is why complete closure sometimes becomes economically decisive.

## Practical conclusion
From an industrial restructuring perspective:

- **If the savings gap is modest:** a non-closure or hybrid solution can be credible.
- **If the savings gap is moderate but not tiny:** a hybrid solution is usually more realistic than pure non-closure.
- **If the savings gap is large:** plant closures or equivalent footprint exits become increasingly likely to be required for a durable solution.

## What I would want to see in a proper bridge
To assess feasibility, the company needs a plant-by-plant table showing:

1. current fixed costs by site  
2. utilization rate by site  
3. savings from shift cuts / line consolidation  
4. savings from headcount reduction and labor concessions  
5. savings from overhead reductions  
6. management and shareholder contributions  
7. one-time restructuring costs  
8. residual gap after all non-closure measures  
9. margin/ROS impact under each scenario

Without that, no one can honestly say whether non-closure is sufficient.
