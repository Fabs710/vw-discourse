## Core assessment

A **non-closure restructuring can work only if the required savings gap is modest enough** to be covered by combinations of:
- labor-cost reset,
- shift reduction / line underutilization,
- attrition and voluntary exits,
- internal redeployment,
- productivity gains,
- overhead reduction,
- and selective capex restraint.

If the gap is **large and structural**, then non-closure measures usually run into hard limits because they do **not remove enough fixed cost**. In that case, you typically need some mix of:
- plant closure,
- line consolidation,
- major footprint rationalization,
- or a permanent reduction in installed capacity.

## What can realistically be saved without closure

Typical order-of-magnitude effects in a large automotive restructuring:

- **Working-time / shift changes:** meaningful but limited; often reduces variable and semi-fixed costs, but does not eliminate the full site fixed-cost burden.
- **Voluntary attrition / internal redeployment:** useful for headcount reduction, but slow and bounded by age profile, skill mix, and demand for internal transfers.
- **Temporary labor reduction:** fast savings, but usually not enough alone.
- **Wage moderation / allowance cuts:** can lower unit labor cost, but durability depends on contract duration and compliance.
- **Overhead cuts:** can be significant at group level, but often smaller than market assumptions once essential engineering, compliance, and program support are preserved.
- **Productivity gains / line balancing:** real, but implementation risk is high and savings often take time to materialize.

## Feasibility without closures

A **non-closure solution is most plausible when**:
1. the savings gap is not too large,
2. there is clear underutilization at one or more sites,
3. the company can consolidate shifts or lines without major logistics disruption,
4. the workforce can be reduced mostly through attrition and voluntary exits,
5. and management accepts a smaller, phased target rather than an immediate step-change.

A **non-closure solution is unlikely to be sufficient when**:
1. several plants are structurally underloaded,
2. fixed site costs are high,
3. product volumes are declining,
4. and the required margin uplift is large enough that labor-only measures cannot bridge it.

## Capacity reduction without shutting plants

This is possible in principle, but there are constraints:

- **Shift reduction** can remove effective capacity if demand is weak, but the site remains open and retains much of its fixed cost.
- **Line consolidation** can reduce capacity materially if products can be migrated with limited retooling.
- **Sinking one model/one line** may create savings if the same plant can still operate at higher utilization on remaining products.
- **Partial footprint shrinkage** works only if the company can truly mothball part of a plant and not simply spread fixed overhead across fewer units.

In practice, **partial capacity reduction without closure usually delivers less savings than a full closure** because:
- building costs remain,
- maintenance and utilities remain,
- site-level management remains,
- and some logistics, tooling, and support costs remain.

## Employment impact

If management wants a **durable** savings package without closure, the employment reduction typically has to come from:
- attrition,
- voluntary severance,
- early retirement,
- temporary labor removal,
- and internal transfer.

If those channels are insufficient, then the remaining options become:
- redeployment to growth areas,
- lower staffing per vehicle,
- or forced reductions if contractually and legally available.

So from an operational standpoint, **“no closure” does not mean “no job loss”**; it usually means **job loss through softer channels** if the package is to be material.

## Bottom line

From a restructuring perspective:

- **Non-closure is feasible only if the savings gap is moderate.**
- **It becomes less credible as the gap widens.**
- **Capacity can be reduced without closure, but not always enough to restore competitiveness.**
- **If the company needs a step-change in fixed-cost removal, closures or equivalent footprint rationalization are usually the most effective tools.**
