Yes. Within my remit, the key issue is not the rhetoric around closures; it is whether the **all-sites-open restructuring** can close the company’s **operating cost gap** enough to restore the brand’s return-on-sales target.

## What I can say now, factually

### 1) Core finance logic
If the brand is below its ROS target, the bridge is:

**ROS improvement = volume / mix effects + variable cost reductions + fixed-cost reductions + labor concessions + overhead cuts - one-time restructuring charges**

A non-closure solution only works if the **fixed-cost base per vehicle** can be reduced enough even while all German sites stay open.

### 2) Why “keep every plant open” is hard
Each open plant retains a fixed-cost floor:
- site management
- maintenance
- utilities and energy baseload
- indirect labor
- compliance / security / logistics
- depreciation and local overhead

So even if you cut shifts, headcount, and temp labor, you often leave too much fixed cost in place if installed capacity is materially above demand.

### 3) What a non-closure package can realistically deliver
A credible non-closure package usually gets savings from:
- attrition / voluntary exits
- redeployment
- shift elimination
- shorter working time
- reduced temporary labor
- management overhead cuts
- procurement/logistics savings
- line consolidation inside plants

But the biggest limitation is that these do **not fully remove plant-level fixed overhead**. That is why the savings are often meaningful but not unlimited.

## Minimum restructuring logic
To determine the minimum production / overhead reduction needed, the company needs three numbers:

1. **Current annual fixed cost base**  
2. **Target annual cost reduction needed to restore ROS**  
3. **Savings achievable without closures**

Then:

**Residual gap = Target reduction - non-closure savings**

If residual gap > 0, then the minimum additional capacity reduction must come from:
- deeper shift cuts,
- further line consolidation,
- more permanent headcount removal,
- or footprint reduction.

## Scenario framework

### Scenario A — Non-closure only
Best case if:
- demand decline is moderate
- one or more plants can be run at much lower utilization
- labor-cost concessions are material
- management/owner contributions are real

Typical risk:
- savings stop short because plant fixed overhead remains

### Scenario B — Hybrid
Usually the most realistic compromise:
- no immediate closures
- but one or more plants are specialized, shifted down, or partially consolidated
- headcount reduces via voluntary exits and attrition
- some product families are moved or merged

This often captures much of the savings of closure while avoiding full shutdowns.

### Scenario C — Closure-based
Needed when:
- overcapacity is deep
- multiple sites overlap structurally
- utilization is too low to carry the fixed-cost burden
- ROS target requires a step-change, not incremental savings

This gives the strongest fixed-cost reset, but with the highest one-time cost and execution risk.

## Finance conclusion
Based on the economics alone:

- If the savings gap is **modest**, an all-sites-open package can work.
- If the savings gap is **moderate**, a hybrid solution is usually the most credible.
- If the savings gap is **large**, keeping all plants open usually leaves too much fixed cost and is unlikely to restore ROS on a durable basis.
