I’ll respond directly, because we are now beyond generalities.

## First: what I agree with
I agree with all sides on one thing: **this cannot be cosmetic**.

I also agree that:
- burden-sharing is necessary,
- internal measures must be fully exhausted,
- redeployment, qualification, working-time flexibility, and productivity gains all belong in the package,
- management and ownership must contribute,
- and we need a plant-by-plant, quantified bridge rather than more slogans.

So yes: the discussion has moved to the right terrain.

## Where I disagree — fundamentally
I do **not** accept the attempt by labour, the state, and parts of the shareholder bloc to convert **“no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no material change to guarantees”** into the default framework.

That is not a restructuring mandate. It is a constraint set that would leave management with no credible tools to remove structural cost. If we accept that framework first and ask the numbers afterward, we are not restructuring — we are preserving overcapacity and hoping for a better market.

That is not acceptable.

## On the Works Council
You keep saying closures are “not proven.” Fair enough — but you are treating the absence of a final plant-level proof as if it were a reason to rule out closures in advance. It is not.

The expert assessment did **not** say non-closure is sufficient. It said **non-closure can work if the savings gap is moderate**. That is a conditional statement, not a conclusion.

So the burden of proof cuts both ways:
- yes, management must quantify the gap,
- but labour cannot pre-empt the conclusion by declaring all irreversible options off-limits before the evidence is complete.

Your position risks becoming this:
- preserve every site,
- preserve every guarantee,
- reject compulsory reductions,
- and still demand a competitive cost base.

Those four things do not automatically fit together.

## On the Union
You are more pragmatic than the Works Council in tone, and I appreciate that. But your red line on compulsory redundancies is still too absolute if we are serious about restoring competitiveness.

I prefer attrition, voluntary exits, redeployment, and working-time solutions. I would much rather avoid forced layoffs.

But I will not rule them out in principle if the arithmetic shows they are needed to restore the brand’s return-on-sales target and free up investment capacity for 2030. If we do that now, we hand the company a permanent cost handicap and call it social responsibility.

That would be irresponsible.

## On the Regional State
I respect the state’s governance role and the special statute. That does not mean I accept a political veto over economic reality.

You say closures should not be the first resort. I can accept that. They are **not** the first thing management wants to do.

But you are going too far if you imply that site guarantees and co-determination protections must remain untouched even if they obstruct a sustainable cost reset. A company cannot compete in a shrinking European market with a cost base that stays structurally above target because the governance system forbids the required adjustment.

That is not industrial policy. That is managed decline.

## On the Controlling Family
I agree with you more than you seem to realize:
- a cosmetic compromise would destroy value,
- prolonged conflict would destroy value,
- and competitiveness matters.

Where I disagree is that you seem to want the discipline of structural change without the implications of structural change. If the savings gap is large and the cost base is too high, then capacity reduction must remain available — including closures if necessary.

Support for competitiveness has to be real, not conditional on avoiding hard decisions.

## On Institutional Investors
You are right to demand a durable margin reset. You are right that value destruction will follow if the company clings to excess cost and overcapacity.

But I also hear a dangerous tendency in your position: “if the non-closure package is sufficient, take it; if not, closures are fine.” That sounds reasonable, but only if the company is actually allowed to reach the point where closures remain on the table.

So I agree with your outcome focus. I do **not** agree with any framework that makes the path to that outcome unavailable from the outset.

## On the expert’s economics
The expert has been helpful, but let’s not overstate the comfort of the non-closure case. The expert was clear:
- non-closure works only if the gap is **modest**,
- partial capacity reduction without closure often leaves too much fixed cost in place,
- and if the company needs a step-change in ROS, internal measures can fall short.

That is the key point everyone keeps skirting.

A lot of the labour-side argument assumes that:
- shift cuts,
- attrition,
- redeployment,
- and overhead trimming

will somehow be enough.

They may not be. If they are not, then the company needs the freedom to reduce installed capacity more decisively. That may mean line consolidation. It may mean footprint rationalization. It may mean closures.

I am not deciding the form in advance. But I am not accepting a process where the form is prohibited before the economics are tested.

## My position, plainly
I remain firm on the core issue:
- **No cosmetic deal**
- **No permanent cost base that remains uncompetitive**
- **No framework that forbids closures or compulsory reductions regardless of the numbers**
- **No settlement that leaves the brand short of its 2030 competitiveness target**

At the same time, I am open on the route:
- if the verified numbers show a non-closure package genuinely closes the gap, I will consider it,
- but if they do not, then capacity rationalization must stay available.

That is not extremism. That is fiduciary responsibility.

## Bottom line
We are not here to preserve the current structure. We are here to restore competitiveness.

If the labour side wants to avoid closures and compulsory redundancies, then it must help close the gap in a way that is:
- durable,
- quantified,
- and sufficient.

If it cannot do that, then it cannot demand that management relinquish the tools needed to fix the company.

Bring the numbers. Then we can talk seriously.
