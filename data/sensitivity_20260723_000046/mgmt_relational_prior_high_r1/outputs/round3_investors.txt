## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family shareholder, and the finance expert on the central point: **this is a structural margin problem, not a cosmetic one**. If the brand is missing its return-on-sales target and the German cost base stays above peers, then value erosion continues. From a capital markets perspective, that is unacceptable.

I also agree with labour and the state on one important issue: **a prolonged strike war would destroy value fast**. Lost production, customer disruption, and governance noise all damage equity value. So a negotiated settlement is clearly preferable to open conflict.

And I agree that internal measures should be fully used:
- redeployment
- qualification
- working-time flexibility
- shift rationalisation
- productivity gains
- management/overhead cuts
- and a fair employee contribution if it is real and durable

## Where I disagree
I do **not** support the workforce trying to make **“no plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, no meaningful change to guarantees”** an absolute veto unless a quantified alternative truly closes the savings gap.

That position is only acceptable if it can be shown, in hard numbers, to restore competitiveness. So far, that proof has not been provided.

I also do **not** support management treating closures as the default answer before fully exhausting credible non-closure options. The expert input was useful precisely because it showed the nuance: non-closure can work if the savings gap is moderate, but if the gap is large and fixed costs are structurally too high, then internal measures alone may fall short.

So the real question is not ideology. It is: **what package closes the gap durably, with the least execution risk and the clearest path back to margin target?**

## My position on closures
We are **not ideologically attached to closures**. We are attached to outcomes:
- restored margins
- lower fixed costs
- credible competitiveness
- and implementable savings

If a non-closure package genuinely closes the EBIT gap, that is likely the better risk-adjusted outcome because it reduces political and execution risk.

But if it does **not** close the gap, then insisting on “no closures” becomes a demand to preserve excess capacity and structural inefficiency. That is not stewardship. That is value destruction.

## My position on compulsory redundancies
I would prefer to avoid forced layoffs, because they increase conflict and can impair execution. But I will **not** rule them out in principle if they are necessary to restore competitiveness.

Preference order:
1. attrition
2. voluntary exits
3. redeployment/retraining
4. working-time changes
5. targeted labour-cost concessions
6. compulsory measures only if the economics leave no credible alternative

If labour wants to avoid that last step, then it needs to help close the gap in a way that is durable, not symbolic.

## On the state and governance
I respect the state’s role and the co-determination structure. But governance rights are not a license to freeze the company in an uncompetitive shape. The public interest is not served by defending every current site if the result is a weaker company with less future investment capacity.

At the same time, I do **not** support any attempt to use governance pressure to force through a cosmetic deal that looks “socially acceptable” but leaves margins impaired. That would be a market-facing failure.

## Bottom line
Capital markets will support a tough restructuring if it is:
- quantified
- durable
- and sufficient to restore competitiveness

We will **not** support:
- a cosmetic settlement
- a deal that leaves the cost base structurally impaired
- or an outcome that preserves every current structure while expecting shareholders to absorb the consequences

If the non-closure path closes the gap, good — take it.  
If it does not, then the company must have the freedom to rationalize capacity further.

That is the only position that protects long-term shareholder value.
