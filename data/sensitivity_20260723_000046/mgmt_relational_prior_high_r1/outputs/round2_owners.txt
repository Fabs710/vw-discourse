I will be direct.

I agree with the broad diagnosis from management, the investors, and, to a degree, the state: **the current cost structure is not sustainable** and this cannot be solved by optics or delay. The company needs a restructuring that is **durable**, not symbolic. If we fail to restore competitiveness and margins, we destroy long-term enterprise value.

I also agree with labour on one important point: **a one-sided solution would be self-defeating**. A prolonged conflict, strikes, and reputational damage would erode value further. So yes, burden-sharing is necessary, and yes, internal measures should be used first where they are genuinely effective.

Where I disagree is with any attempt to turn **“no closures” into an absolute principle**.

That is not a governance position I can support unless a quantified alternative truly closes the gap. The expert testimony made that clear: if the savings requirement is large and structural, then **non-closure measures may not be enough**. In that case, refusing all footprint rationalisation is not stewardship — it is postponing decline.

## My position on the competing arguments

### To the Works Council and Union
You are right to insist that the burden must be shared and that internal redeployment, qualification, working-time flexibility, and negotiated labour contributions belong in the package.

But I do **not** accept:
- no plant closures under any circumstances,
- no compulsory redundancies under any circumstances,
- and no material change to job-security guarantees if they block competitiveness.

Those are too rigid. They may be politically understandable, but they are not economically sufficient by themselves. If the non-closure package cannot deliver the required durable savings, then you are asking the company to carry overcapacity indefinitely. That weakens the company and ultimately puts more jobs at risk later.

### To Management
I agree with the diagnosis, but not with any tendency to use closures as the first and easiest answer.

You still need to prove, plant by plant, that:
- the required savings are real,
- the non-closure options have been fully quantified,
- and any remaining gap cannot be bridged by internal adjustment.

You have not yet earned a blank cheque for closures. The burden of proof is still on management. A serious restructuring must show the numbers, not just the threat.

### To the Regional State
I respect the state’s governance role and the importance of regional stability. But the state cannot use its position to freeze the company in a structurally uncompetitive shape.

Your veto is legitimate, but it is not a veto on necessary change. I will not support any position that treats every site as untouchable regardless of economics. The public interest is served by a viable industrial base, not by preserving every current structure until the next crisis.

## What would move me
I am open to a **non-closure settlement** only if it is genuinely credible and quantified. That means:
- measurable capacity reduction,
- meaningful labour-cost contribution,
- durable productivity gains,
- overhead cuts,
- and a clear path back to margin targets.

If that package can close the gap, I would prefer it to closures because it would likely reduce conflict and preserve more of the industrial base.

But if it cannot, then we should not pretend it is sufficient.

## What I need next
I want the discussion to move from principles to a quantified package. Specifically:

1. **Required savings gap**
   - How much EBIT uplift is needed to restore the ROS target?

2. **Non-closure savings**
   - What can be achieved through wage moderation, working-time changes, attrition, redeployment, shift rationalisation, and overhead cuts?

3. **Residual gap**
   - What remains after internal measures?

4. **Capacity reduction options**
   - Which plants or lines are structurally underutilized?
   - What volume can be removed without closures?
   - What is the limit before the numbers stop working?

5. **Contributions from each side**
   - Labour contribution
   - Management contribution
   - Ownership contribution
   - State contribution, if any, consistent with its role

[SUMMON: Company restructuring finance lead and operations planning expert to present a plant-by-plant savings bridge showing required EBIT uplift, non-closure savings, residual gap, and the minimum capacity rationalisation needed if closures are avoided.]

## Bottom line
My position is simple:

- I support a real restructuring.
- I support burden-sharing.
- I support a serious non-closure alternative if it is sufficient.
- I do **not** support preserving every plant and guarantee as a matter of principle.
- I do **not** support a settlement that permanently weakens competitiveness or governance control.

If the numbers show that a non-closure route closes the gap, I can support that. If they do not, then the company must have the freedom to rationalise capacity further.
