I will be direct.

## To management
I agree with you on the diagnosis: the company needs a **durable** reset, not a cosmetic patch. The cost base is too high, and overcapacity cannot simply be wished away.

Where I disagree is on the implication that closures and compulsory redundancies should remain the default lever. They are not a neutral technical tool in this governance setting; they are a high-risk move with real value destruction potential. If you push them too early, you may win a spreadsheet argument and lose the enterprise.

What I need from you is not more insistence, but a **decision-grade comparison**:
- plant-by-plant utilization,
- structural cost per unit,
- fixed-cost base,
- future product allocation,
- savings from each restructuring lever,
- one-time costs,
- execution risk,
- and the effect on margin through 2030.

Until that is in front of us, I will not treat closure-first logic as proven.

## To the works council
I understand and largely support your refusal of a blanket closure-led strategy. I also agree that workers should not be made the sole shock absorber for management’s mistakes.

But I will be equally frank: an absolute “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” line is only credible if there is a serious alternative that actually removes capacity and fixed cost. If the alternative package preserves too much of the current footprint, then it is not a solution either.

So I agree with your insistence on:
- real numbers,
- binding commitments,
- and negotiated adjustment.

I do **not** agree with turning every structural change into a prohibition. If the alternative cannot close the gap, then we have to say so honestly.

## To the union
I agree that industrial peace matters and that a negotiated settlement is usually better than confrontation. I also agree that the workforce should contribute only in a fair and balanced package.

Where I disagree is with any framing that says “no closures” automatically equals “better for long-term value.” Not necessarily. A weak compromise that leaves the business uncompetitive will damage jobs and value later.

If labour is prepared to discuss:
- voluntary exits,
- retirement pathways,
- working-time flexibility,
- qualification,
- internal redeployment,
- and a time-limited contribution,

then that can be part of a serious settlement. But it must come with real structural change, not just reassurance.

## To the regional state government
I appreciate your concern for the regional industrial base and for social stability. That concern is legitimate.

I do not, however, accept the notion that protecting the base means preserving every site and every structure as-is. That would be drift, not stewardship.

Your position should be:
- protect competitiveness,
- preserve viable industrial capability,
- and insist on a package that can actually hold to 2030.

That means you should support negotiated restructuring if it is real, not symbolic. But I would caution against equating “no closures” with “value preservation” automatically. It may be true in some cases; it is not true by definition.

## To institutional investors
You are right to insist on durable savings and execution certainty. I agree with that.

But I will not accept the idea that closures are automatically the superior benchmark of seriousness. In this governance structure, a theoretically clean cut that triggers prolonged industrial conflict, legal blockage, political escalation, and reputational damage may end up worse than a tougher negotiated settlement.

So yes, I support rigorous comparison. No, I do not support pre-judging the answer in favor of closures.

## My position
I am prepared to back a **serious negotiated restructuring** if it contains:
- real and permanent capacity reduction,
- structural fixed-cost reduction,
- credible labour-cost relief,
- lower complexity,
- investment in future products,
- and a stable industrial-relations framework.

I am **not** prepared to back:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a delay strategy,
- or a deal that leaves the German base fundamentally uncompetitive.

I am also **not** prepared to endorse irreversible action before the alternatives are properly tested. That would be poor governance and poor stewardship.

## Bottom line
My line is simple:
- **Durable structural savings are non-negotiable.**
- **Capacity must come down.**
- **The business must regain competitiveness.**
- **But value-destructive instability is not an acceptable route to get there.**

If the numbers show that a non-closure package can truly deliver the same structural outcome, I will support it. If not, then we should not pretend otherwise.
