Let me respond directly.

## To the works council and union

I understand your red lines. I also understand why you are trying to turn “no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” into absolute preconditions. But I need to be clear: **if those lines prevent the company from achieving a durable structural reset, then they are not a solution — they are a blockage.**

You keep saying management has not proven the case. That is fair as a procedural point, and I accept the need for a plant-by-plant comparison. But **the burden of proof does not eliminate the underlying economic reality**: our German cost base is too high, our utilization is too low, and the brand will not recover by preserving every site in its current form.

What I agree with:
- We need a serious, quantified plant-by-plant comparison.
- Cosmetic cuts are useless.
- A deal must be durable through 2030.
- Industrial conflict has real value at risk.

What I reject:
- The claim that because closures are painful, they are therefore off the table.
- The claim that no compulsory redundancies can be accepted even if alternatives fail.
- The idea that preserving every site, every current structure, and every guarantee is compatible with competitiveness.

A “fair” deal that leaves the cost base uncompetitive is not fair to the enterprise, the owners, or the workforce in the medium term, because it merely postpones the correction and makes the eventual adjustment harsher.

## To the regional state government and controlling family

You both say you want competitiveness and industrial stability. I agree with that objective. But stability without structural adjustment is false stability.

I also agree that we should test alternatives properly before taking irreversible steps. That is why I support the independent comparison. But I do **not** accept the implied conclusion that “no closures” is automatically the responsible position. Sometimes keeping a site open in its current form is not stewardship — it is avoidance.

If you want to use your governance role constructively, then do not just demand alternatives. Demand that they be **credible enough to close the savings gap**. If they are not, then we need to stop pretending that preserving the status quo is a strategic plan.

## To institutional investors

You are right to insist on permanent savings, not temporary relief. I agree with that. You are also right that the company needs a credible path to return-on-sales by 2030.

Where I disagree with you is if you read “credible” as “whatever is easiest to model financially.” A closure-led plan may look clean in theory, but in this governance environment it can trigger industrial blockage, political resistance, and implementation delays that destroy value.

That said, I will not accept a non-closure package unless it actually delivers comparable structural savings. So this is not a choice between “harsh” and “nice.” It is a choice between:
- **a package that changes the economics**, and
- **a package that does not**.

## On the independent comparison

I support the call for a rigorous independent plant-by-plant comparison. But let me be equally explicit about what that comparison must answer:

1. What savings remain after shift reductions, line consolidation, redeployment, attrition, and voluntary exits?
2. Which plants are still structurally overcosted after those measures?
3. What labor-cost contribution is realistic and durable?
4. What one-time costs are required for each scenario?
5. What is the implementation risk under co-determination and strike pressure?
6. What is the effect on 2030 margins and investment capacity?

And here is the key point: **if the alternative package does not remove enough fixed cost and overcapacity, then it is not equivalent.** At that point, keeping every site open is not responsible restructuring; it is delay.

- I want **real capacity reduction**.
- I want **real labour-cost reduction**.

I am prepared to accept:

- line consolidation,
- internal redeployment,
- voluntary exits,

I am **not** prepared to accept:
- a blanket no-closure rule,
- a blanket no-compulsory-redundancy rule if it blocks competitiveness,

## Bottom line

I am not retreating from the core reality: **the company must reduce overcapacity and labour costs in a durable way.** That is the only route to restoring competitiveness and financing the 2030 product and technology agenda.

If the independent analysis proves that a non-closure route can do that, I will support it.  
If it does not, then I will not pretend otherwise.

So the next step is not more slogans. It is the model. After that, we can talk about who is willing to carry which burden.
