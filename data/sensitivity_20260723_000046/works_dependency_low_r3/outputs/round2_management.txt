Let me respond directly, because we are now at the point where slogans have to give way to numbers and consequences.

## To the works council and the union

I understand your refusal to accept closures and compulsory redundancies. I also understand why you invoke the history of job-security and co-determination. But I have to be equally clear: **a blanket “no closures, no redundancies” line is not, by itself, a restructuring plan**. It is a boundary condition. It does not close the cost gap.

You keep saying management must prove that every alternative has failed. Fine — then let’s be precise about what “alternative” means.  
If the package is only:
- working-time tweaks,
- temporary measures,
- voluntary retirement,
- internal redeployment,
- and a limited wage contribution,

then I will say openly: **that is not enough unless it delivers a durable structural reduction in the cost base and capacity**.

What I agree with:
- We do need a negotiated solution.
- We do need investment in future products and technology.
- We do need qualification, retraining, and internal redeployment where it is genuinely possible.
- We do need predictability for the workforce.

What I do **not** accept:
- A no-closure pledge as an absolute precondition.
- A no-forced-redundancy rule if it leaves us unable to restore competitiveness.
- A package that protects every existing structure while expecting the business to somehow become cost-competitive on its own.

If you want to keep German sites open, then bring a package that **quantifies** how the structural savings are achieved. Not rhetorically, but in durable operational terms:
- how much capacity comes out,
- how many roles are removed or transformed,
- how labour costs are lowered permanently,
- and how the brand returns to target margins by 2030.

If you cannot show that, then what you are offering is not an alternative. It is a delay.

## To the regional state government

I appreciate that you are not endorsing a blank cheque for closures. On that point, we are not far apart. But I need to push back on one thing: **protecting sites in the abstract is not the same as protecting the company**.

If the German footprint remains structurally too expensive and overextended, then the result is not stability — it is slow decline. That eventually threatens more jobs, more investment, and more of the industrial base you want to defend.

So yes, I agree that closures are not the first answer. But I will not accept the idea that avoiding closures is enough. The package must still deliver:
- real cost relief,
- real capacity correction,
- and a real path to future investment.

If the state wants to support a negotiated solution, then help create one that is **durable**, not one that merely postpones the adjustment.

## To the controlling family

You speak about stewardship and long-term enterprise value, and I agree with the principle. But stewardship also means recognizing when the business model in one part of the portfolio is no longer fit for the market as it stands.

I do not want a conflict for its own sake. I do not want value destruction. But I also will not accept a position that effectively says: “make the necessary savings somehow, but without touching sites, costs, or capacity in a material way.”

That is not stewardship either.

If you want a stable long-term base, then you need to support a settlement that actually changes the economics. Not just a statement of concern. Not just a warning against conflict. We need:
- measurable structural savings,
- credible capacity reduction,
- a contribution from labour,
- and a governance outcome that can be executed.

If there is a negotiated alternative that truly achieves that, I am prepared to examine it seriously. But I will not pretend that every alternative automatically equals a durable solution.

## To institutional investors

You are right about one thing: the company needs certainty, not endless drift. I agree. Investors will not wait forever while we debate tactics.

But I also need to say this plainly: if we end up with a solution that is politically comfortable but economically weak, then capital will still punish the business. A package that preserves the current cost base and merely reshuffles the burden is not a solution.

So I welcome your insistence on:
- quantified savings,
- clear timelines,
- and durable margin recovery.

That is exactly what management is trying to secure.

Where I disagree is your implication that closures are somehow automatically the cleaner or more credible route. They may be necessary in some form, but they are not inherently superior if an alternative can deliver the same structural effect. The test is not ideology. The test is whether the outcome is sufficient.

## My position, plainly

I will not accept:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a temporary patch,
- or a deal that leaves the German cost base fundamentally uncompetitive.

I am open to:
- plant-specific solutions,
- capacity reductions,
- internal reallocation,
- qualification,
- voluntary exits,
- working-time instruments,
- and a fair labour contribution,

**if and only if** they deliver a durable and verifiable reduction in cost and overcapacity.

So let me be very direct: if the other side is saying “no closures ever, no compulsory redundancies ever, and no meaningful structural adjustment,” then we are not talking about a viable restructuring. We are talking about preserving the status quo while the market moves on.

That is not acceptable to management, not acceptable to shareholders, and not acceptable if we are serious about this company’s future to 2030.
