From a plant-level restructuring perspective, the key issue is **not** whether the company needs change — it clearly does — but whether a **non-closure package can remove enough permanent cost and capacity** to be economically equivalent to a closure-led plan.

## My assessment

### 1) When a non-closure path can be credible
A no-closure or no-forced-redundancy solution can work if it delivers, in aggregate:

- **material fixed-cost reduction**
- **real capacity reduction** through shift cuts/line consolidation
- **higher utilization at the remaining sites**
- **durable labor-cost relief**
- **product reassignment or specialization**
- **low execution risk under co-determination**

In practice, that means the package must do more than “buy time.” It must **permanently lower the cost base**.

### 2) When it is not credible
It is not credible if it mostly relies on:

- temporary labor concessions,
- one-off savings,
- delayed headcount reduction,
- or internal reallocations that do not actually reduce overcapacity.

If the company remains structurally overbuilt after the package, then the problem is deferred, not solved.

## What “equivalence” should mean
A non-closure scenario should be judged against closures on at least five dimensions:

1. **Permanent savings**  
   Not just annual savings in year 1, but sustained savings through 2030.

2. **Capacity reduction**  
   Enough volume and line capacity must be removed so that the remaining footprint is efficient.

3. **One-time cost**  
   Severance, relocation, conversion, retraining, and ramp-down costs matter.

4. **Implementation certainty**  
   A weaker plan that can actually be executed may outperform a stronger plan that cannot pass governance or labor relations barriers.

5. **Conflict cost**  
   Industrial action, loss of trust, and implementation delay are real economic costs and should be priced in.

## What I would want to see before calling a non-closure package equivalent
A decision-grade plant-by-plant model showing:

- current utilization
- unit cost and fixed-cost base
- savings from shift reductions
- savings from line consolidation
- savings from attrition / voluntary exits / retirement pathways
- effect of working-time and labor-cost concessions
- one-time restructuring costs
- employment impact
- implementation risk
- industrial conflict risk
- 2030 margin impact

Without that, no one can honestly claim equivalence.

## My bottom line
- A **closure-led plan** may be economically rational if overcapacity is deep and permanent.
- A **non-closure plan** can also be rational, but only if it produces **comparable structural savings** and is **actually executable**.
- If the non-closure package cannot close the savings gap, then it is not a turnaround; it is delay.
