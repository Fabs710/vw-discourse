We agree on one thing: the company needs a credible, durable return to competitiveness. If the cost base stays structurally too high, the equity story deteriorates further and capital will keep rerating the business downward.

But I need to be equally direct about where we disagree.

### What we support
- A **real** restructuring, not a cosmetic one.
- **Durable cost reduction** and capacity correction.
- A solution that restores **return-on-sales** and protects future investment.
- A negotiated outcome if it is **quantified, enforceable, and sufficient**.

### What we do not support
- A settlement that preserves the current cost structure while calling it “transformation.”
- A prolonged conflict that destroys margins, cash flow, and investor confidence.
- Vague promises of “shared sacrifice” without measurable outcomes.
- Any package that leaves the company with an **uncompetitive German footprint**.

### To the works council and union
We hear your rejection of plant closures and compulsory redundancies. We understand why you are taking that line. But from a capital markets perspective, that position is only viable if you can show a **credible alternative that delivers equivalent structural savings**.

A temporary working-time measure, short-term wage restraint, or internal reallocation alone is not enough if it does not materially and durably lower the cost base. We will not support an arrangement that merely delays the problem and keeps margins impaired. If the result is continued overcapacity and persistent cost inflation, then the share price will continue to reflect that reality.

So: if you want to rule out closures, bring a package that **actually closes the cost gap**. Not rhetorically, but financially.

### To management
We agree that the problem is structural, not temporary. Where we would press you harder is on **proof and discipline**. If plant closures are necessary, then show why alternatives are insufficient and how the savings will be durable. If alternatives exist, then quantify them honestly.

What we will not back is a plan that asks investors to accept years of uncertainty while management and stakeholders debate tactics. Deal certainty matters. We need:
- clear numbers,
- a clear timeline,
- and a settlement that can be executed.

### To the regional state government
Your resistance to irreversible damage is understandable, but investor support depends on whether the proposed arrangement restores competitiveness. Protecting sites is not the same as protecting value. If the final package merely freezes structural inefficiency, capital will still leave.

### To the controlling family
We appreciate the focus on long-term enterprise value. But “no permanent impairment” also means not allowing the business to drift into chronic underperformance. The risk to value is not only a harsh restructuring; it is also a weak one. If the company needs a hard reset on cost and capacity, investors need to see that it is real and durable.

### Our bottom line
We are open to either route:
1. **deep structural cuts including closures**, if that is the only credible way to restore competitiveness; or
2. **an alternative negotiated package**, but only if it produces **comparable, durable savings** and a clear margin recovery path.

What we will not support is a middle ground that is politically easier but financially inadequate.

If the next proposal includes:
- quantified structural savings,
- a credible reduction in German cost base,
- a believable path to target margins,
- and sufficient certainty that this is not just deferred pain,

then we can support it. If not, we will treat it as insufficient and value-destructive.
