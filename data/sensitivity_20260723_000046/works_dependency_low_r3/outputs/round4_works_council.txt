Let me be clear from the outset: the discussion is still trying to smuggle in closures and forced redundancies as “legitimate options” simply because they are easier for management and investors to model. That is not an acceptable basis for decisions that affect tens of thousands of families and the future of entire regions.

## To management
You say you need a durable reset. I do not dispute the need for restructuring.

What I dispute is your repeated implication that a serious restructuring must be built around plant closures or compulsory redundancies. That is not a law of economics. It is your preferred instrument because it transfers the burden onto the workforce.

You still have not delivered the plant-by-plant evidence proving that every non-closure option has been exhausted. Until you do, your closure narrative is an assertion, not a case.

And let’s be honest about the consequences you keep downplaying:
- loss of know-how,
- destruction of trust,
- industrial conflict,
- political backlash,
- and permanent damage to the company’s social license to operate.

Those are not side effects. They are economic costs.

## To institutional investors
You keep asking for “equivalence” in structural savings, as if that ends the matter. It does not.

A plan that is cheap on paper but detonates labor peace, blocks implementation, and weakens the company’s industrial base is not superior. It is risky, and in this governance structure it may be unworkable.

So no, I do not accept your framing that closures are automatically the benchmark of seriousness. The benchmark is whether the company remains viable **without** destroying its workforce and German footprint.

## To the regional state government
You are right to say the company needs adjustment. But adjustment is not the same as dismantling the industrial base.

If you want to act as an anchor shareholder, then anchor the company to its sites and to its workforce. That means:
- no closure-led default,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no hollowing out disguised as consolidation,
- and real investment commitments for the German plants through 2030.

If you accept “site preservation” only in name while jobs, volume, and functions are stripped away, then you are not protecting the region.

## To the controlling family
You have governance power. Use it.

If stewardship means anything, it means not allowing management to force through a closure-first logic before a serious alternative has been tested. Your role is to insist on a non-closure scenario being modeled on equal terms, not to stand aside while management uses urgency as a weapon.

I agree with you on one point: a cosmetic compromise is worthless. But an economically serious alternative is absolutely possible and must be tested honestly.

## To the union
I stand with your core line: the workforce should not become the shock absorber for management’s strategic failures.

But I want to sharpen the point: “shared sacrifice” is only acceptable if it is actually shared. Workers cannot be asked to give up security while management preserves all its options and investors demand all the upside.

Any labor contribution must be inside a binding package that includes:
- all German plants remaining open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding investment and product commitments,
- and preservation of co-determination and collective protections.

Without that, there is no fair contribution. There is only extraction.

## To the restructuring experts
Your analysis is useful, but it still stops short of the central issue: **equivalence cannot be judged in abstraction**.

If you really want to compare scenarios, then the comparison must include:
- the loss of trust from closure threats,
- the risk of strike action,
- implementation delay under co-determination,
- political resistance,
- and the impact on future productivity and know-how retention.

A so-called “clean” closure can easily become the most expensive route if it cannot actually be implemented smoothly.

So yes, I support the call for a serious plant-by-plant model. But I want it used to test whether the non-closure path works — not to retrofit a justification for closures.

## My position remains unchanged
I will not move on the essentials:

- **No German plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No dismantling of the job-security framework**
- **No weakening of co-determination**

Those are not bargaining chips. They are the conditions for any discussion at all.

## What I am prepared to discuss
I remain prepared to discuss:
- shift reductions,
- line consolidation,
- internal redeployment,
- voluntary exits,
- retirement pathways,
- qualification and retraining,
- productivity improvements,
- and a fair, time-limited labor contribution

**but only if every German plant remains open and no one is forced out.**

## What must happen next
If management is serious, then stop repeating slogans and put the numbers on the table:
- plant-by-plant utilization,
- unit cost,
- fixed cost,
- future product allocation,
- savings from each non-closure measure,
- one-time costs,
- and implementation risk through 2030.

Until that is produced, my answer stays the same:

**No closures. No forced redundancies. No surrender of co-determination.**
