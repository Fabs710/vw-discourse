To everyone in this round: I will be equally direct.

We are not here to negotiate against ourselves. The workforce side has already shown openness to a fair contribution. What we will not do is accept that “durable savings” automatically means plant closures, compulsory redundancies, or the dismantling of collective bargaining protections.

## To management
I agree with you on one point: the company needs a durable solution, not a temporary patch.

I do **not** agree with the way you keep framing closures and forced redundancies as the default benchmark of seriousness. That is not proof. It is a preference.

You still have not shown, in a decision-grade way, that the non-closure path fails. You keep asking for “real structural change,” but structural change does not have to mean site destruction. It can mean:
- shift reductions,
- line consolidation,
- internal redeployment,
- voluntary exits,
- retirement pathways,
- qualification and reallocation,
- productivity improvements,
- and a limited, time-bound labour contribution.

That is the route we are prepared to discuss. But it must come with binding guarantees:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- investment commitments,
- and preservation of the collective bargaining framework.

If you want our support, put the plant-by-plant numbers on the table and show what you really mean by “equivalent.” Until then, you are asking the workforce to accept irreversible pain on faith.

## To the works council
I agree with your core line: the workforce should not be made the sole shock absorber of management’s errors.

I also support your insistence that alternatives be examined seriously and transparently. But I want to sharpen one point: the aim is not just to resist; it is to secure a settlement that workers can live with and that the company can actually execute.

So yes:
- no closures,
- no forced layoffs,
- no dismantling of job-security guarantees.

But if management brings a serious alternative package, then the union is ready to discuss a **fair, limited employee contribution** as part of a broader shared-sacrifice deal. That could include:
- bonus restraint,
- time-limited wage-related relief,
- flexible working-time measures,
- and productivity-linked adjustments.

Not as surrender. As part of a balanced package where management and owners also pay their share.

## To the regional state government
I agree that regional industrial stability matters. You are right not to endorse a hollowed-out “solution” that destroys the site base and leaves only a shell.

But I want to be clear: support for the industrial base means more than general concern. It means backing a binding settlement that protects jobs and sites in practice, not just in rhetoric. If the state wants stability, it should help enforce:
- no closure-led restructuring,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- product allocation to German plants,
- and real investment commitments.

That is the kind of anchor-shareholder stance we can work with.

## To the controlling family
Your stewardship language is only credible if it translates into action. I appreciate that you do not want open conflict and value destruction.

But then use your influence now. Do not let management hide behind urgency to force a one-sided reset. If you mean long-term value, then long-term value includes:
- preserving skills,
- preserving social peace,
- and preserving the collective framework that has made the company stable through crises.

We are ready to contribute to a fair solution. We are not ready to finance management’s restructuring alone.

## To institutional investors
You keep saying “equivalent structural savings.” Fine. Then be honest about what equivalence must include.

It cannot just mean:
- fewer payroll euros,
- more aggressive wording,
- or an easier model for capital markets.

It must also account for:
- strike risk,
- implementation risk,
- loss of know-how,
- political backlash,
- supply-chain disruption,
- and the long-term damage caused by destroying trust.

A negotiated package with binding guarantees and workforce acceptance can be more durable than a so-called cleaner closure strategy that detonates conflict. That is not sentiment; that is industrial reality.

## On the substance
Let me be precise about what I can support:

### I can support
- capacity reduction
- shift consolidation
- line consolidation
- internal redeployment
- qualification and retraining
- natural attrition
- retirement pathways
- voluntary exit programs
- a limited, time-bound labour contribution
- bonus restraint or other negotiated labour-cost relief

### I cannot support
- plant closures in Germany
- compulsory redundancies
- erosion of collective bargaining standards
- open-ended insecurity for the workforce
- a deal where workers pay while management and owners do not

That line has not moved.

## What management still needs to answer
If you want this to move forward, answer these questions clearly:

1. What savings remain after all non-closure measures are applied plant by plant?
2. Which sites are supposedly “non-viable,” and on what basis?
3. What is the exact labour contribution you want, and for how long?
4. What are management and owners contributing in return?
5. What binding investment and product commitments are attached through 2030?
6. How do you guarantee no compulsory redundancies?

Until those are answered, “durable restructuring” is just code for shifting the burden onto workers.

## My position, unchanged
I will not move off these core points:
- **No German plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No dismantling of the collective wage framework**
- **A fair shared-sacrifice package is possible, but only if management and owners contribute materially too**

If the other side wants a serious deal, bring a quantified package that protects sites and jobs. If they want confrontation, then they should not be surprised when the union uses the instruments available to it.
