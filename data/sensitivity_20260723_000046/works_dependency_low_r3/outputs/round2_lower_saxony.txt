The expert assessment is useful, but it does not change the basic political and governance reality: **a solution that destroys German sites or forces compulsory redundancies is not acceptable to the state as anchor shareholder**. We will not endorse a “durable reset” that is durable only because it permanently weakens the regional industrial base and strips workers of security.

### Where I agree
I agree with management and the expert on one central point: **the company needs real structural improvement**. Cosmetic measures, temporary wage restraint, or vague promises will not be enough. The cost base must come down, capacity must be adjusted, and the plan must be executable through 2030.

I also agree that equivalence has to be tested honestly, not emotionally. A non-closure alternative cannot just be a softer label for the status quo.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept management’s framing that closures and compulsory redundancies are simply the normal or inevitable route. The expert is right that closure can be “cleaner on paper,” but in this governance structure, paper-clean is not the same as workable or legitimate.

A plan that provokes prolonged conflict, political backlash, and the loss of trust is not a superior plan just because it looks neat in a model. The long-term damage to value, skills, and regional stability has to be included in the equation. If it is, then closure-first looks much less automatic.

And I want to be explicit: **German plant closures are not a default instrument here**. If you want to invoke them, the burden of proof is very high.

### To management
You now have the right task in front of you: show, plant by plant, what savings come from each lever, and prove that a negotiated alternative cannot close the gap.

But let me be equally clear: if your only “realistic” answer is closure and forced layoffs, then that tells me management has not sufficiently explored the alternatives that preserve the company’s regional anchor role. The state will not rubber-stamp that.

### To the works council and union
Your insistence on no closures and no compulsory redundancies is politically and socially understandable, and I share the core objective of protecting sites and jobs. But I will also say this frankly: if that line becomes an absolute refusal of any meaningful structural adjustment, then it will not be a viable settlement either.

A negotiated package must include **real** contributions:
- capacity reduction,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary exits,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity measures,
- and, if necessary, a time-limited labour contribution.

What I will not support is a package that protects every existing guarantee without accepting any serious change in operations. That would simply postpone the conflict.

### To the controlling family
You are right to warn against permanent value destruction and open conflict. Use that position constructively. If you want stewardship to mean anything, then it should mean blocking a crude closure-first strategy and pushing management toward a balanced industrial settlement.

But stewardship also means not hiding behind “long-term value” while allowing the workforce and the region to absorb the full adjustment. We expect the owners to contribute visibly and not merely comment from the sidelines.

### To institutional investors
You are right that drift is value-destructive. But your interpretation of “credible” needs to include execution reality under German co-determination and political constraints. A theoretically optimal cut that cannot be implemented cleanly is not better than a negotiated package that is slightly less aggressive but actually holds.

If the final package preserves the company as a strong industrial player, secures investment, and delivers durable savings without ripping out the regional base, then that is value-preserving. If it is built on confrontation and social rupture, then it is not.

### My position going forward
The state will support **a negotiated restructuring only if it meets these conditions**:
1. German sites remain open.
2. No compulsory redundancies.
3. Real, quantified structural savings.
4. Binding investment and product commitments.
5. A credible contribution from management and owners.
6. Co-determination is respected, not bypassed.

If those conditions cannot be met, then I will oppose the plan. I am not prepared to trade the company’s role as a regional anchor for a short-term correction of margins.

### [SUMMON: independent plant-level restructuring and site-viability analysis]
I want an independent assessment **plant by plant** that shows:
- current utilization,
- cost per unit,
- future product allocation,
- the savings from shift reduction, line consolidation, internal redeployment, and voluntary exit measures,
- and whether these measures can be sufficient **without closures**.

That analysis must also compare the **long-term value at risk** from industrial conflict and site closure against the value case for a negotiated settlement.

We need facts, not just pressure.

So my bottom line remains unchanged:
- **No closure-led restructuring as the default**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Yes to serious structural adjustment**
- **Yes to a negotiated package if it truly protects jobs, sites, and the company’s future**

If the numbers show that an alternative can genuinely close the gap, then we should build it. If not, management still does not get a blank cheque to sacrifice the regional base.
