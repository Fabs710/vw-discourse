From a plant-level restructuring and site-viability perspective, the situation is clear on the economics and less clear on the exact remedy because the key data are still missing.

## What the evidence supports
A German auto group with:
- weak European demand,
- high domestic labor and overhead costs,
- overcapacity,
- and sub-target return on sales

usually does need a **structural** reset, not a temporary one.

That means the restructuring must deliver:
1. **permanent fixed-cost reduction**
2. **meaningful capacity reduction**
3. **lower complexity**
4. **credible future product allocation**
5. **an implementable workforce adjustment path**

Without those, the problem is only postponed.

## What is plausible
A **non-closure scenario** can be viable in principle if it is not just cosmetic. It would need to combine, plant by plant:
- shift cuts
- line consolidation
- product reassignment
- internal redeployment
- voluntary exits
- retirement pathways
- working-time measures
- indirect-cost reduction
- and some form of labor-cost contribution

If that package removes enough fixed cost and underutilization, it can be economically comparable to closure-led restructuring.

## What is not plausible
A plan that:
- keeps every site at current scale,
- avoids compulsory redundancies,
- avoids closures,
- and offers only short-term concessions

is usually **not** equivalent to a serious restructuring. It would not change the cost base enough.

## What I would need to judge equivalence properly
To assess whether a non-closure package is truly equivalent, I would need, for each German plant:
- current and projected utilization
- cost per unit / conversion cost
- fixed overhead
- direct labor cost
- one-time restructuring cost
- future model allocation
- logistics impacts
- break-even volume
- planned headcount by function
- savings by measure and by year through 2030

Without that, nobody can responsibly say whether closures are economically necessary.

## My judgment on the decision logic
The right test is not:
- “closures versus no closures” in the abstract.

The right test is:
- **does the alternative package produce the same durable economic outcome with lower execution risk?**

In this governance setting, execution risk matters a lot:
- co-determination gives labor real blocking power,
- industrial action can destroy value,
- political resistance can delay implementation,
- and trust loss can reduce future productivity.

So even if closure looks cleaner on paper, it may be inferior if it cannot be executed.

## Operationally, the most realistic path is usually hybrid
In large German automotive restructurings, the most workable solution is often:
- preserve some sites in reduced/specialized form,
- consolidate shifts and lines,
- remove excess capacity gradually,
- use voluntary workforce reduction first,
- keep compulsory redundancies as a fallback rather than a design principle,
- and tie any labor contribution to binding investment and product commitments.

That is often more implementable than a pure closure strategy.

## Bottom line
A **non-closure, no-forced-redundancy** solution is only credible if it delivers:
- **real capacity reduction**
- **real permanent cost reduction**
- **real labor-cost relief**
- **future volume for the retained plants**

If it does not, then it is not a restructuring; it is delay.
