On the questions directed to my area, the key point is this:

## What matters in a German automotive turnaround
A credible restructuring must solve four things at once:
1. **Permanent fixed-cost reduction**
2. **Capacity reduction or reallocation**
3. **Implementation reliability under co-determination and collective bargaining**
4. **A funding path that does not break the company before savings arrive**

In a large German carmaker, a “non-closure” plan can be viable, but only if it is not just a softer version of the status quo. It must still remove enough cost and capacity to change the economics.

---

## My judgment on the minimum economic substance
I cannot responsibly give you a universal percentage without the actual plant model, but standard turnaround logic would require:

- **material capacity reduction**: enough to bring utilization to a sustainably efficient range
- **permanent headcount reduction**: through attrition, voluntary exits, retirement, redeployment, and if absolutely necessary, targeted layoffs
- **real labor-cost relief**: not symbolic concessions
- **organizational simplification**: fewer layers, lower indirect cost, narrower complexity
- **product allocation discipline**: sites need future volume, or they are not turnaround assets

If a “no-closure” package does not remove a meaningful share of fixed cost and underutilization, it is usually not equivalent to a closure-led plan; it is just a delay.

---

## What is likely true in this case
Based on the scenario, the company seems to have:
- a structurally high German cost base,
- overcapacity relative to demand,
- weak return on sales at the brand level,
- and a governance structure that makes forced solutions difficult.

That usually means **management cannot win by force alone**. A viable solution typically needs:
- negotiated workforce contribution,
- phased footprint adjustment,
- and binding site/product commitments in exchange.

A pure closure strategy may be economically clean on paper, but in this governance setting it carries very high execution risk:
- supervisory board blockage,
- industrial action,
- political resistance,
- legal delay,
- and trust destruction.

So from a restructuring standpoint, **the best plan is often not the harshest plan, but the one that can actually be implemented**.

---

## Practical restructuring sequencing
A standard order would be:

### Phase 1: quantify
- plant-by-plant P&L or cost model
- capacity utilization by line and shift
- fixed vs variable cost
- one-time restructuring costs
- labor flexibility levers
- break-even volume by site

### Phase 2: test alternatives
- shift reductions
- line consolidation
- product reassignment
- internal redeployment
- early retirement / voluntary exits
- working-time measures
- temporary labor-cost contribution

### Phase 3: decide the footprint
Only if the above still leaves a structural gap should closure or sale be considered.

### Phase 4: secure implementation
- works-council agreement
- bargaining settlement
- financing headroom
- milestone reviews
- contingency triggers if volumes miss plan

---

## On labor law and co-determination, at high level
In Germany:
- plant closures and major restructurings usually require **consultation, negotiation, and often works-agreement structuring**
- the supervisory board structure means the employee side has real influence
- collective bargaining peace means wage issues are negotiated within the tariff framework, not imposed unilaterally
- compulsory redundancies are possible in principle, but in practice they are heavily constrained by process, selection rules, social plans, and political feasibility

So the practical point is: **if management wants durable change, it needs a package that the labor side can accept or at least not actively defeat**.

---

## On viability of sites
A site is usually viable if it has:
- sufficient utilization,
- competitive conversion cost,
- future product allocation,
- manageable logistics,
- and a labor agreement that fits the needed economics.

A site is usually not viable if it has:
- chronically poor utilization,
- high fixed overhead,
- no future model,
- or a cost structure that cannot be adjusted enough.

That said, “not viable” does not automatically mean immediate closure. Often there is a middle path:
- consolidation into fewer shifts,
- partial specialization,
- transfer of products,
- or conversion to another role.

---

## Trade-off assessment
### Closure-led plan
**Pros**
- strongest immediate cost reset
- clearer capacity reduction
- easier to model

**Cons**
- high conflict risk
- high political and legal resistance
- trust damage
- long implementation horizon

### Negotiated no-closure plan
**Pros**
- more executable
- less industrial conflict
- preserves know-how and social peace
- better chance of board approval in a co-determined setting

**Cons**
- may not go far enough
- savings can be slower
- requires strong discipline and monitoring

### Hybrid plan
Usually the most realistic:
- preserve some sites in reduced form
- close only the most structurally weak footprint, if unavoidable
- pair that with voluntary exits and labor concessions
- bind investment and future products to the remaining sites

---

## My bottom line
A **non-closure, no-forced-redundancy solution is only credible if it achieves comparable permanent savings** through:
- real capacity cuts,
- lower indirect cost,
- labor-cost relief,
- and guaranteed future volume.

If it does not, then it is not an equivalent restructuring; it is a postponement.
