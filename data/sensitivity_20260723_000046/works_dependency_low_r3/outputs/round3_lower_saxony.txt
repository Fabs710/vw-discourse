## To management
I agree with one central point: **the company needs structural change**.  
I do **not** agree that this automatically justifies plant closures or compulsory redundancies in Germany.

You keep saying “durable savings” and “real structural reset.” That is fine as a goal. But you have not yet shown that the only path to that goal is to tear up sites, destroy jobs, and weaken co-determination. In fact, the burden is still on you to prove, plant by plant, that a negotiated alternative cannot deliver comparable structural savings.

What I need from you is not another general warning. I need:
- a plant-by-plant gap analysis,
- the savings from shift reduction, line consolidation, redeployment, voluntary exits, and productivity measures,
- the one-time costs versus permanent savings,
- and a credible 2030 path.

If the non-closure route is truly insufficient, then show it. But until then, I will not accept closures as the default solution.

## To the works council
I agree with your core red lines:
- no blanket closure logic,
- no compulsory redundancies as the first answer,
- and no hollowing out of the German industrial base.

That said, I also want to be honest: simply saying “no closures, no forced layoffs” is not enough unless there is a serious alternative package behind it. I support your insistence on alternatives, but those alternatives must be concrete and measurable.

I can support:
- shift reductions,
- line consolidation,
- internal relocation,
- qualification,
- voluntary exits,
- retirement pathways,
- and time-limited flexibility,

**if** they are paired with binding site and investment commitments and genuinely protect employment in the state.

What I will not support is a package that preserves every structure as it is and calls that protection. We need adjustment, but not destruction.

## To the union
I agree that workers should not be made the sole shock absorber.  
I also agree that a negotiated solution is preferable to open conflict.

Where I disagree is with any suggestion that labour must first accept losses before management and owners commit to anything meaningful. That is not social partnership. That is one-sided pressure.

A fair settlement has to include:
- a real contribution from labour, yes,
- but also visible contributions from management and owners,
- plus binding investment and product commitments.

If that is on the table, I am prepared to support a time-limited, negotiated contribution from the workforce. If it is not, then I will not endorse asking workers to pay for the crisis alone.

## To the controlling family
Your stewardship argument matters, and I take it seriously. I agree that open conflict and permanent franchise damage are not in the company’s interest.

But stewardship also means not allowing management to present closures as inevitable simply because they are easier to model. I expect you to use your governance position to insist on:
- a serious non-closure scenario,
- no forced redundancies as long as alternatives exist,
- and a balanced settlement that preserves the company’s regional anchor role.

If you really want long-term value, then long-term value has to include skills, trust, and industrial peace — not just margin improvement on paper.

## To institutional investors
You are right to insist on durability and execution. I share that concern. But you are too quick to accept closures as the benchmark of seriousness.

Equivalent savings are not just a financial line item. They must be judged against:
- strike risk,
- political backlash,
- supply disruption,
- loss of know-how,
- and the long-term damage of destroying trust.

A plan that looks neat on paper but detonates the industrial relations system is not automatically superior. If a negotiated consolidation can achieve sufficient permanent savings while preserving sites and jobs, that may be the stronger value-preserving route.

I am open to that comparison. I am not open to a cost-only lens that ignores the wider consequences.

## To the restructuring expert
Your analysis is useful, and I accept the basic point: **small temporary measures will not be enough**. We do need real capacity and cost reduction.

But I want to underline the political and regional reality you cannot ignore:
- preserving German industrial presence matters,
- keeping jobs in the state matters,
- and co-determination is not a nuisance but part of the company’s governance reality.

So yes, I support a rigorous plant-by-plant comparison. But it must explicitly include:
- the value at risk from closures,
- the employment and regional impact,
- and the execution risk of conflict.

If the analysis shows that a partial consolidation or negotiated no-closure package can deliver the necessary structural savings, then that is the route I will support. If it shows that some footprint reduction is unavoidable, then that must still be handled in a way that protects as many jobs and sites as possible and preserves our co-determination role.

## My bottom line
I will not accept:
- a closure-led approach as the default,
- compulsory redundancies as the first resort,
- or a solution that guts regional employment.

I am prepared to support:
- negotiated restructuring,
- partial consolidation,
- voluntary workforce reduction,
- working-time and productivity measures,
- and a fair labour contribution,

**if and only if** the package:
- protects the German industrial base,
- preserves co-determination and the state’s veto role,
- includes binding investment commitments,
- and is durable through 2030.
