From a restructuring and turnaround perspective, the discussion is now at the right level: the issue is no longer whether adjustment is needed, but **which combination of measures delivers durable savings with the lowest total value destruction**.

## My assessment, in general terms

### 1) What is factually true in this type of case
- If a German automotive plant network has **structural overcapacity**, high fixed labor cost, and weak utilization, then **some footprint adjustment is usually necessary**.
- If the company is protected by **strong co-determination, collective bargaining, and multiple blocking shareholders**, then a purely management-driven closure program is often **hard to execute**.
- In that setting, a closure-led plan may be economically plausible but **governance-fragile**.
- Conversely, a no-closure plan can be politically and socially attractive, but it is only credible if it produces **comparable permanent savings**, not just delay.

### 2) What is likely true
A viable non-closure restructuring at a large German carmaker typically needs some combination of:
- shift reductions and line consolidation,
- site specialization,
- internal redeployment,
- voluntary exits / retirement pathways,
- working-time and cost concessions,
- overhead reduction,
- and selective product allocation changes.

In practice, if management is asking for a “non-closure equivalent,” the burden is to show that these measures together deliver:
- enough **fixed-cost removal**,
- enough **capacity reduction**,
- and enough **implementation certainty**
to restore margin by the target year.

### 3) My judgment
A **blanket no-closure, no-forced-redundancy line** is not automatically unreasonable in Germany, but it is only sustainable if:
- volumes remain sufficient,
- the company can reassign products,
- labor contributions are meaningful,
- and the savings are durable.

If the plants are genuinely and deeply uncompetitive, then preserving every current structure can become a **delay mechanism** rather than a turnaround.

## What I would require in a decision-grade comparison
To judge equivalence properly, you need a plant-by-plant model showing:
1. current utilization,
2. unit labor and conversion cost,
3. fixed-cost burden,
4. savings from shift cuts,
5. savings from line consolidation,
6. savings from attrition / retirement / redeployment,
7. one-time restructuring cost,
8. legal and implementation risk,
9. industrial conflict risk,
10. and 2030 margin effect.

Without that, “closures are necessary” and “closures are unacceptable” are both **positions**, not yet **proven cases**.

## Practical restructuring logic
A non-closure compromise is economically credible only if it does one of two things:
- either **remove enough capacity** that the remaining footprint is efficient, or
- **change the product and cost structure** enough that current capacity becomes adequately loaded.

If it does neither, then it is not a turnaround solution.

## German context
In Germany, compulsory redundancies and plant closures are not just economic decisions; they are also:
- co-determination issues,
- collective bargaining issues,
- and political legitimacy issues.

That means management must think not only in terms of theoretical savings, but in terms of **execution probability**. A theoretically optimal plan that cannot pass supervisory board, works council, or political thresholds may have lower real value than a somewhat weaker but executable compromise.

## Bottom line
From my standpoint:
- **Cost reset is necessary.**
- **Capacity reduction is likely necessary.**
- **A negotiated non-closure package is possible only if it is truly equivalent in permanent savings.**
- **If it is not equivalent, delaying footprint decisions will only make the eventual adjustment more painful.**
