I agree with one essential point from management: the current cost base cannot remain unchanged if the company is to stay competitive. If the business keeps losing margin, everyone’s position becomes weaker over time, including the workforce, the state, and the owners. So I do not dismiss the need for restructuring.

But I also need to be explicit about what I cannot support.

I do **not** support a value-destroying conflict, and I do **not** support a symbolic “hard reset” that permanently damages the company’s industrial base in Germany. Plant closures may be legally and economically available in principle, but they are not a free instrument. Once you cross that line, you create major risks: industrial unrest, reputational damage, political backlash, and a weakened long-term platform for the brand. That is not stewardship; that is short-termism.

So my position is:

1. **Competitiveness must improve materially and durably.**
2. **A negotiated solution is strongly preferable.**
3. **Plant closures and forced redundancies are not my preferred path and would need the strongest possible justification.**
4. **Any labour contribution must be matched by real structural measures and credible commitments from management.**

To the works council and the union: I understand your refusal of closures and compulsory redundancies. That position is understandable from the workforce perspective. But if “no closures, no redundancies” becomes an absolute veto with no willingness to accept any meaningful adjustment, then we will simply end up in a prolonged standoff that erodes the company further. That outcome would not protect jobs; it would endanger more of them over time. If you want to rule out closures, then the alternative must be more than temporary instruments or vague promises. It must produce real, measurable, and durable savings.

To management: you have made the case for urgency, and I accept that the company needs a credible path back to margin and investment capacity. But a plan that starts with closure as the default answer is not, by itself, a sufficient governance case. Before any irreversible step, I would expect you to show that alternatives have been seriously tested and that the package is not merely shifting pain to employees while preserving every other structure. If you want owner support, bring forward a settlement that is enforceable, balanced, and clearly linked to future product strength.

To the state government: your insistence on industrial presence and stability is aligned with long-term value in important respects. But we should also be honest that preserving every site at any cost is not automatically value-preserving. The question is whether the company can reduce costs and capacity in a way that protects the core asset. That requires realism, not just protectionism.

From the family holding’s perspective, the non-negotiables are simple:
- no permanent impairment of enterprise value,
- no erosion of governance control,
- no drawn-out conflict that destroys trust and capital,
- and no settlement that delays necessary change until it becomes more destructive later.

So I will not endorse a blanket rejection of restructuring. But I will also not give a blank cheque to closure-led cuts. The burden is on management to prove that the proposed path is necessary, proportionate, and durable. And if there is a negotiated alternative that achieves comparable structural improvement while preserving labour peace and the company’s long-term industrial strength, that is the route I would favor.

What I need to see next is not rhetoric, but a concrete package:
- quantified savings,
- credible capacity adjustment,
- durable labour-cost improvement,
- protection of future investment,
- and a stable governance arrangement that can actually hold.

Absent that, I would remain skeptical of any deep-closure strategy.
