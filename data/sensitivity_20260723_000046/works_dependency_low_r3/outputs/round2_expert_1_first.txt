Here is the independent restructuring assessment, staying within turnaround and industrial logic.

## 1) First principle: what has to be true for any solution to work

A German automotive restructuring of this scale has to do **four things at once**:

1. **Remove structural excess capacity**
2. **Lower the permanent fixed-cost base**
3. **Protect enough industrial capability to support future products**
4. **Be executable under German co-determination and collective-bargaining constraints**

If any one of those fails, the plan is usually unstable.

So the real question is not “closures or no closures?”  
It is: **what combination of site action, workforce reduction, labour-cost relief, and productivity measures gets the company back to target margins by 2030 with acceptable execution risk?**

---

## 2) What is typically true in German automotive turnarounds

### Fact
In a mature European car operation, the largest cost blocks are usually:
- direct labour,
- indirect labour and overhead,
- plant fixed costs,
- logistics and complexity,
- and underutilized capacity.

### Likely implication
If the brand is structurally below target on return on sales, then **small, temporary measures are almost never enough**. You usually need some combination of:
- headcount reduction,
- reduction of shifts or lines,
- product rationalization,
- supplier and logistics simplification,
- wage restraint or one-time labour contribution,
- and tighter site utilization.

### Judgment
If management says it needs a “durable” reset, that is credible in principle. But if the alternative package relies mainly on voluntary measures, temporary working-time changes, and morale-based productivity gains, it is often **not sufficient** unless the overcapacity is modest.

---

## 3) Closure-led savings vs negotiated alternatives

### A. Closure-led route
This usually delivers:
- fast removal of fixed costs,
- cleaner capacity reduction,
- stronger certainty on the savings line,
- and clearer margin improvement.

But it also creates:
- high industrial conflict risk,
- legal and co-determination obstacles,
- potential long lead times,
- reputational damage,
- relocation costs,
- supply-chain disruption,
- and the risk of losing know-how or local flexibility.

### B. Negotiated alternative route
This can include:
- capacity reduction without outright closure,
- fewer shifts,
- line consolidation,
- internal redeployment,
- natural attrition,
- retirement schemes,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary wage elements or bonus restraint,
- and productivity agreements.

This route often delivers:
- lower conflict,
- better social legitimacy,
- better preservation of skills,
- and less political damage.

But it often has weaknesses:
- savings may be slower,
- the labour-cost reduction may be less durable,
- headcount reduction may be insufficient,
- and the plan may not remove enough fixed cost if the footprint remains too broad.

### Practical conclusion
A negotiated alternative can be equivalent **only if** it produces:
- real site consolidation,
- measurable fixed-cost removal,
- and a credible step-down in capacity.

If it just avoids the hard decisions, it is not equivalent.

## 4) What “equivalent” should mean in this case

Management is right to demand quantification.  
Labour is right that equivalence cannot be judged only on a simple wage spreadsheet.

A proper comparison should include:

1. **Direct savings**
   - payroll
   - overhead
   - utilities
   - maintenance
   - logistics

2. **Capacity effect**
   - units removed
   - shifts removed
   - line utilization after restructuring

3. **One-time restructuring costs**
   - severance
   - site mothballing
   - transfer costs
   - pension or social-plan costs

4. **Implementation risk**
   - strike risk
   - legal delay
   - governance blockage
   - supplier disruption

5. **Value at risk**
   - brand damage
   - delivery reliability
   - talent retention
   - political escalation

A plan can be economically better overall even if it looks more expensive in the short term.  
But it still has to close the structural gap.

## 5) Plant-by-plant logic: how to judge viability

I cannot validate individual plants without actual numbers, but the standard plant-viability test is straightforward.

A plant is usually at risk when several of these are true:
- low utilization,
- high labor content per unit,
- weak product pipeline,
- limited export competitiveness,
- high logistics cost,
- aging equipment,
- excessive indirect headcount,
- and poor flexibility for EV or next-generation production.

A plant is more likely to survive when it has:
- a strong future product allocation,
- high utilization,
- modular production potential,
- strong quality performance,
- lower conversion cost,
- and strategic importance in the network.

### Judgment
If the company has “structural overcapacity,” then at least some German footprint reduction is usually required.  
The remaining question is whether that reduction comes through:
- one or more full closures,
- partial closures,
- shift elimination,
- or product reassignment plus headcount cuts.

In German automotive cases, **partial closure or consolidation** is often the compromise shape if full closures are politically blocked.

---

## 6) Labour-cost adjustment: what is realistic

### Fact
Wage cuts alone rarely solve an automotive turnaround.  
The bigger levers are usually:
- headcount,
- utilization,
- complexity,
- and permanent overhead reduction.

### Likely implication
A negotiated labour contribution can help, but it must be:
- time-limited or structurally linked to lower output,
- legally and collectively enforceable,
- and large enough to matter.

### Judgment
If management needs a meaningful reset, a labor-side contribution may be appropriate as part of a broader package, but **it cannot substitute for capacity correction**.  
Conversely, if management insists on broad wage cuts while refusing to adjust the footprint, the package is usually unstable and politically explosive.

---

## 7) Employment impact: what tends to be sustainable

The sustainable employment reduction mix in a German industrial turnaround typically comes from:
- natural attrition,
- retirement pathways,
- voluntary exit programs,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and transfer,
- and elimination of roles through process simplification.

Compulsory redundancies are:
- fast on paper,
- but often slow in execution,
- legally and industrially costly,
- and more likely to provoke escalation.

### Judgment
If the works council and union hold blocking positions, the most executable route is usually:
- fewer compulsory measures,
- more voluntary exits,
- more time,
- but stronger binding commitments from labor on flexibility and productivity.

If management needs a very large and very fast reduction, the risk of deadlock rises sharply.

---

## 8) Durability to 2030

For savings to be durable to 2030, they must not depend on:
- short-term wage restraint alone,
- one-off accounting actions,
- or temporary production shifts.

Durable savings usually come from:
- permanent capacity removal,
- structural overhead reduction,
- product simplification,
- lower complexity,
- and a new operating model with lower fixed cost.

### Judgment
This is the key test.  
If a negotiated package can permanently remove capacity and overhead, it can be durable.  
If it only defers the pain, it will fail by 2030 or earlier.

---

## 9) Implementation risk under German governance

### Fact
In a co-determined system with blocking positions, the risk of implementation failure is high if any core stakeholder is forced into defeat.

### Likely implication
A technically good plan can still fail if:
- the supervisory board cannot reach the required majority,
- labour believes commitments are not credible,
- or political stakeholders fear regional industrial loss.

### Judgment
The highest-probability successful outcome is usually **a package deal**, not unilateral management action.  
In this kind of governance structure, “best economic plan” is less important than “best executable plan.”

## 10) My expert assessment of the current standoff

### What management is right about
- The company likely does need structural capacity and cost reduction.
- A purely symbolic compromise would be dangerous.
- Savings must be durable, not temporary.

### What labour is right about
- Closure and forced redundancy are not automatically the right answer.
- A spreadsheet-only approach can destroy value if it triggers prolonged conflict.
- Site security and investment commitments matter for credibility.

### My judgment
A closure-led plan may maximize mathematical savings, but it also maximizes execution risk.  
A negotiated plan may preserve more social stability, but it only works if it is **hard enough** on capacity and fixed cost.

So the real answer is:

> **A viable solution probably requires some combination of footprint reduction, shift/line consolidation, voluntary workforce reduction, and limited labour-cost contribution — not just one of these in isolation.**

If the non-closure alternative cannot achieve a comparable permanent cost reset, then it is not an equivalent restructuring.  
If the closure-led plan cannot be implemented without destructive conflict, then it may also be economically inferior in practice.
