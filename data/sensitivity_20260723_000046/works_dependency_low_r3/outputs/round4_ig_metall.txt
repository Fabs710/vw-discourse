I will be equally direct in round 4.

We are now at the point where everyone agrees a structural adjustment is needed, but management and investors are still trying to make **closures and compulsory redundancies** the default language of seriousness. I reject that framing.

## To management
You say a durable reset is necessary. On that, we agree.

Where we do **not** agree is your repeated claim that if a site is high-cost or underutilized, then closure or forced layoffs become the natural answer. They do not. They become your preferred answer when you want the workforce to absorb the largest share of the pain.

You still have not produced the plant-by-plant case showing that:
- shift reductions,
- line consolidation,
- product reassignment,
- internal redeployment,
- voluntary exits,
- retirement pathways,
- working-time tools,
- productivity improvements,
- and a fair time-limited labour contribution

cannot close the gap.

Until you do, closures are not “necessary”; they are **unproven**.

And let me be clear: a deal that preserves management’s strategic freedom while stripping workers of security is not a restructuring partnership. It is one-sided risk transfer.

## To institutional investors
You keep insisting on “equivalence” in permanent savings. Fine — but equivalence is not just a margin calculation.

If you push a closure-heavy plan into a co-determined company with strong strike capacity, then you must also price:
- industrial conflict,
- execution delay,
- know-how loss,
- political blockage,
- and the damage to trust and productivity.

A paper-clean solution that cannot be implemented cleanly is not superior. It is fragile.

So I agree with you on one point: we need durable savings.  
I disagree with your implication that closures are automatically the benchmark. They are not if they trigger a conflict that costs more and takes longer.

## To the regional state government
You are right to care about competitiveness and the industrial base. But those goals are not served by turning the German footprint into a shrinking remainder under the banner of “adjustment.”

If you want to be an anchor shareholder, then anchor the company to:
- site security,
- employment stability,
- investment,
- and an industrial strategy that preserves skills and volume in Germany.

I can support:
- shift reductions,
- partial consolidation,
- specialization,
- internal transfers,
- qualification,
- voluntary reduction paths,
- and a limited workforce contribution.

I cannot support a package that labels itself “balanced” while workers are asked to carry the downside alone.

## To the controlling family
You have governance leverage. Use it in a way that actually tests alternatives, not one that merely allows management to continue presenting closure pressure as inevitable.

Your stewardship role should mean:
- requiring a decision-grade non-closure scenario,
- insisting that management quantify the savings gap by plant,
- and refusing any rush to irreversible cuts before alternatives are tested.

That is the responsible use of power here.

## To the works council
I agree with your core red lines:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of job security,
- no hollowing out of co-determination.

I also agree that the company must not be allowed to “consult” its way into a pre-decided outcome.

Where I would add one point is this: our side has already shown openness to a **fair labour contribution**. That remains true. But it must be inside a binding package where management and owners also contribute materially.

So I stand with you against closure-led blackmail, but I also want us to stay focused on a viable negotiated alternative that protects jobs **and** makes the company workable.

## On the labour contribution
To be concrete: we can discuss
- bonus restraint,
- temporary labour-cost relief,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- retirement pathways,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- and productivity measures.

But only if the package includes:
- no closures,
- no forced redundancies,
- binding investment commitments,
- and a credible product allocation plan through 2030.

No one should misunderstand this: workers can contribute. Workers will not be the only ones contributing.

## What is still missing
At this stage, the essential missing piece is not another round of slogans. It is the **independent plant-by-plant comparison** that shows, for each site:
- current utilization,
- unit cost,
- fixed cost,
- savings from non-closure measures,
- one-time costs,
- employment impact,
- and implementation risk.

That model must also include:
- the value at risk from industrial conflict,
- and the cost of losing trust if management keeps pressing closures.

Without that, closure-first arguments remain assertions.

I am prepared to negotiate real restructuring.  
I am not prepared to negotiate away the workforce’s security as the price of management’s plan.
