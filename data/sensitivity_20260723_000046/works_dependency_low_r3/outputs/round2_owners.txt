The expert analysis is helpful, and I agree with the central point: this cannot be solved by symbolism or by temporary measures alone. The company needs a **durable** reduction in cost and overcapacity, not a short-lived truce that leaves the underlying economics unchanged.

At the same time, I do **not** accept the implication that plant closures or compulsory redundancies are therefore the default answer. That is too blunt for a company of this importance, especially under German co-determination and with the reputational, industrial, and political costs that follow from crossing that line.

## Where I agree

### With management
- The cost base is too high.
- The current structure is not competitive enough.
- Savings must be real, measurable, and durable.
- A purely temporary labour concession would not be sufficient.

### With the expert
- Capacity reduction is necessary.
- Fixed-cost reduction is necessary.
- A viable solution must be executable through the governance structure.
- A negotiated package only works if it actually removes structural excess.

### With the labour side
- A settlement that simply shifts pain onto the workforce is not acceptable.
- Trust matters.
- Site and investment commitments matter.
- Industrial peace has value, and conflict destroys value.

## Where I disagree

### To management
I do **not** accept the way you are treating closures as the natural benchmark of seriousness.  
Yes, closures can look “clean” in a model. But this is not a spreadsheet exercise. If a closure-led plan triggers prolonged industrial conflict, political escalation, supplier disruption, and reputational damage, then the apparent savings can be partly or fully offset.

So if you want owner support for irreversible action, you need to show more than urgency. You need to show:
- that alternatives were genuinely tested,
- that the savings are not just accounting savings,
- and that the plan is likely to hold through 2030 without destroying the franchise.

### To the works council and union
I understand why you are holding the line on no closures and no compulsory redundancies. But I will also be blunt: if that position becomes an absolute veto against any meaningful structural change, then it is not a solution either.

If the company has real overcapacity, then some combination of:
- line consolidation,
- shift reduction,
- internal reallocation,
- retirement pathways,
- voluntary exits,
- productivity improvements,
- and possibly limited labour-cost relief

will have to happen. The question is whether that can be done **without** plant closures and compulsory redundancies. It may be possible, but only if it produces a real step-down in fixed cost and capacity.

### To the state government
Your concern for regional stability is understandable and, in part, aligned with long-term value. But I would caution against turning that into a blanket defense of every existing structure. Protecting all sites at all costs is not stewardship; it can become drift.

The relevant test is whether the German footprint after restructuring is still competitive, investable, and durable.

## My view on the expert’s conclusion

The expert is right that the most likely viable compromise is some form of **partial consolidation plus voluntary workforce reduction plus some labour-cost contribution plus investment commitments**.

That is the kind of package I would take seriously.

What I will not support is:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a delay strategy,
- or a package that preserves current capacity and current cost structure while pretending the crisis has been solved.

## What I need next

If management wants to move this forward, I want a concrete comparison of three scenarios, plant by plant:

1. **Closure-led restructuring**
   - expected savings
   - implementation risk
   - one-time costs
   - conflict risk
   - effect on margin by 2030

2. **Negotiated consolidation without closures**
   - which shifts/lines are removed
   - how much capacity actually comes out
   - how headcount is reduced without forced layoffs
   - how durable the labour-cost relief is

3. **Hybrid compromise package**
   - partial consolidation
   - voluntary exits
   - limited employee contribution
   - binding investment and product commitments
   - governance safeguards

That comparison has to be honest about:
- not just payroll savings,
- but fixed-cost removal,
- product allocation,
- execution risk,
- and the value at risk from prolonged conflict.

## My position, plainly

I am **open to a negotiated restructuring** if it is serious.

I am **not open** to:
- permanent value destruction,
- governance paralysis,
- a prolonged industrial conflict,
- or a “solution” that only postpones the need for change.

And I am **not prepared** to endorse closure or forced redundancy as the first or default answer unless management proves there is no comparable alternative.
