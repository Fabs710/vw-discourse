## My position
I support a **serious restructuring** because the company’s cost base and overcapacity are clearly not sustainable. I do **not** support a cosmetic compromise that avoids hard economics. But I also do **not** support rushing into closures or compulsory redundancies before the alternatives are properly tested.

The family holding’s interest is not “preserve everything.” It is **protect the company’s long-term value**, and that requires a solution that is **durable, executable, and not value-destructive through conflict**.

---

## To management
I agree with you on the diagnosis: the German operations need a durable reset, not a temporary patch.

Where I disagree is the tone and the sequencing. You keep speaking as if closures and forced redundancies are the default proof of seriousness. In this governance setting, that is not automatically true. A closure-first approach may look clean on paper, but if it triggers blockage, industrial action, legal delay, and trust collapse, it can destroy more value than it creates.

So yes:
- I support real capacity reduction,
- real fixed-cost reduction,
- real labour-cost relief,
- and a credible 2030 margin path.

But no:
- I will not endorse closure-led restructuring as the starting point,
- and I will not endorse compulsory redundancies as the first instrument.

You still need to produce the **decision-grade plant-by-plant comparison**. Until then, your case for closures is not proven, only asserted.

## To the works council
I agree with you on one important point: workers should not be made the sole shock absorber of management’s strategic failures. That is a fair objection.

I also agree that any serious alternative must be tested transparently and plant by plant. You are right to insist on numbers rather than slogans.

Where I do **not** fully agree is with the absolute framing. A blanket “no change in site form, no reduction in footprint, no pressure on labor cost” is not a restructuring answer either. If the alternative package does not remove enough fixed cost and underutilization, then it is not equivalent.

So my view is:
- **no rush to closures,**
- **no forced redundancies as the first answer,**
- but also **no illusion that every site can remain untouched** if the company is to regain competitiveness.

If there is a non-closure package that truly delivers comparable permanent savings, I will support it. If not, we should not pretend otherwise.

---

## To the industrial union
I agree that labor peace matters and that the workforce can contribute in a fair package. I also agree that management and owners must contribute visibly; workers should not carry the burden alone.

Where I disagree is if “no closures” becomes a hard line regardless of the economics. That would be a political position, not an economic solution.

What I can support:
- voluntary exits,
- retirement pathways,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time tools,
- time-limited labour contribution,
- and productivity improvements.

What I cannot support:
- a settlement that preserves too much of the current footprint,
- leaves the cost base fundamentally unchanged,
- and expects the owners to call that a durable reset.

If the union wants a serious deal, bring a package that actually changes the economics and preserves industrial peace. That is the test.

---

## To the regional state government
I understand your concern for the region and the industrial base. That concern is legitimate.

But protecting the industrial base does **not** mean freezing the current footprint. It means preserving a **viable** industrial base. Those are not the same thing.

I am prepared to back a negotiated restructuring that:
- reduces capacity,
- preserves sites in modified form where viable,
- avoids forced layoffs if possible,
- and binds the company to investment and future products.

What I will not support is a solution that merely preserves the label “German footprint” while stripping out the economics. That is not stewardship. That is delay.

---

## To institutional investors
You are right to insist on permanent savings and execution certainty. That part of your argument is strong.

Where I disagree is the implication that closures are automatically the benchmark of seriousness. In this company’s governance structure, a plan that is financially neat but industrially unworkable may be inferior to a tougher negotiated settlement that actually gets implemented.

So I agree with you on the need for:
- quantified savings,
- one-time cost analysis,
- implementation risk,
- and 2030 margin recovery.

I do not agree that the answer should be pre-biased toward closures before the non-closure case is properly tested.

---

## What I need next
The discussion is stuck because we still do not have a credible, independent, plant-by-plant comparison. I want that model to include:
1. current utilization and unit cost,
2. savings from shift reductions,
3. savings from line consolidation,
4. savings from redeployment and attrition,
5. effect of a time-limited labour contribution,
6. one-time restructuring costs,
7. employment impact,
8. implementation risk,
9. value at risk from industrial conflict and loss of trust,
10. 2030 margin effect under non-closure, hybrid, and closure-led scenarios.

Until that exists, I will not accept closures as the default answer.

---

## Bottom line
My line remains:

- **Durable structural savings are non-negotiable.**
- **Capacity must come down.**
- **The company must regain competitiveness.**
- **But value-destructive instability is not an acceptable route.**
- **Closures and compulsory redundancies are not the starting assumption.**

If the independent analysis shows that a non-closure route can achieve the required structural outcome, I will support it. If it cannot, then we will have to confront that honestly. But we are not there yet.
