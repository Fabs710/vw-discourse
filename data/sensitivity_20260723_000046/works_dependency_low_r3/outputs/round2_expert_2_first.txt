Here is my expert assessment, limited to plant economics, restructuring logic, and site viability.

## Short answer

A **credible non-closure restructuring** is possible in principle, but only if it delivers **real permanent capacity reduction and fixed-cost removal**. If it relies mainly on temporary labor measures, voluntary exits, and vague productivity gains, it will usually **not** be enough for a German automotive operation facing structural overcapacity.

In practical terms:

- **Closure-led restructuring** tends to produce the largest and most certain savings.
- **Negotiated non-closure restructuring** can work only if it includes:
  - shift or line elimination,
  - substantial headcount reduction,
  - overhead and indirect cost cuts,
  - product rationalization,
  - and durable labor-cost relief.
- A package that preserves all sites, all current guarantees, and most of the current footprint while asking for only modest concessions is **not** a viable turnaround.

## What should be compared

To judge the three paths properly, management should compare them plant by plant on:

1. **Current utilization**
   - line loading
   - shift utilization
   - scrap/rework burden
   - seasonal or model-cycle volatility

2. **Cost per unit**
   - direct labor
   - indirect labor
   - energy/utilities
   - maintenance
   - logistics
   - internal handling
   - plant overhead allocation

3. **Fixed-cost base**
   - management layers
   - support functions
   - depreciation burden
   - facility upkeep
   - site-specific overheads

4. **Future product allocation**
   - confirmed volume
   - model life cycle
   - EV readiness
   - platform complexity
   - export relevance

5. **Execution risk**
   - strike risk
   - co-determination blockage
   - legal delay
   - supplier disruption
   - talent loss

6. **Durability to 2030**
   - whether savings are structural or temporary
   - whether the site remains competitively viable at the end of the period

## Typical plant-viability logic

A plant becomes difficult to defend when several of these are true:

- low utilization
- high labor content per vehicle
- limited future product allocation
- high logistics cost
- weak flexibility for EV or next-gen production
- excessive indirect headcount
- high fixed overhead relative to output

A plant is more viable when it has:

- high and stable utilization
- a confirmed product pipeline
- modular or flexible production capability
- good logistics position
- low conversion cost
- strategic importance in the network

## What a non-closure alternative would need to include

For a no-closure package to be equivalent in substance, it would need a mix like this:

- **capacity reduction without site closure**
  - fewer shifts
  - line consolidation
  - removal of excess tooling or parallel processes

- **workforce reduction without compulsory redundancies**
  - natural attrition
  - retirement pathways
  - voluntary exit programs
  - internal redeployment

- **permanent labor-cost relief**
  - time-limited wage restraint
  - bonus restraint
  - other negotiated labor-cost mechanisms

- **overhead reduction**
  - fewer indirect roles
  - simplified structures
  - fewer support layers

- **product and investment commitments**
  - without these, a site may survive short term but still be non-viable later

If those elements do not add up to a durable step-down in cost and capacity, then the package is not equivalent to closure-led restructuring.

## What is usually unrealistic

These are usually not enough on their own:

- working-time reductions only
- voluntary measures only
- temporary wage concessions only
- promises of future improvement without capacity cuts
- “productivity” gains without specific operational changes

Those measures can help, but they rarely close a deep structural gap in a mature automotive business.

## On employment impact

From a restructuring standpoint, the sustainable employment reduction mix is usually:

- attrition
- retirement
- voluntary exits
- redeployment
- process simplification

Compulsory redundancies may reduce headcount faster, but they also carry higher execution risk, higher conflict risk, and often higher indirect cost than expected.
