I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, investors, and the state on the core point: **the current situation is not sustainable**. The company needs a real restructuring, not more delay, and not a cosmetic compromise.

I also agree with the works council and union that:
- the workforce has already carried a heavy burden,
- non-binding assurances are worthless,
- and industrial conflict would destroy value.

And I agree with the specialist assessment that the real question is not the slogan “closure or no closure,” but whether the company can close the **fixed-cost, capacity, and unit-cost gap** with a package that is truly durable.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the labour side’s attempt to turn “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” into a complete restructuring strategy.

That is a red line, not a plan.

At the same time, I do **not** accept management’s tendency to speak as if closures and redundancies are automatically necessary without first laying out a quantified case that they are unavoidable. “Structural problem” is not enough. I need to see the arithmetic.

So my position is simple:
- **I support deep restructuring.**
- **I do not support a weak compromise that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.**
- **I do not accept closure as the default answer without proof.**

## To management
You are right that the problem is structural, not cyclical. But you have not yet done enough to prove that a no-closure package cannot work.

If you want the owners to back hard structural measures, then show:
- the exact savings gap,
- the exact capacity reduction required,
- the exact contribution available from working-time, productivity, product mix, procurement, and overhead,
- and the point at which those measures still leave the business uncompetitive.

Until then, closures are still an option, not a conclusion.

And I need to be clear: I will not support an outcome that destroys long-term enterprise value through rushed conflict or reputational damage. If management pushes too hard without proof, it may win a tactical battle and lose the company’s stability.

## To the Works Council
Your insistence on no closures and no compulsory redundancies is understandable, but it is not sufficient by itself.

I hear your concern about trust, know-how, strikes, and legal conflict. Those are real risks. But I also see the risk of preserving a footprint that the business can no longer carry.

So I do **not** accept “keep every site open” as a final answer unless the numbers actually work.

What would move me is not principle alone, but a package that shows:
- binding site guarantees,
- real capacity reduction without closures,
- voluntary exits and natural attrition,
- meaningful productivity gains,
- fair labour-cost contributions,
- and enforceable investment commitments.

If that package closes the gap, I will consider it. If it doesn’t, then the company needs the freedom to act.

## To the Industrial Union
You are right that the workforce should not be the only side contributing. On that point, I agree.

But I do not accept the implication that industrial action substitutes for economic logic. A strike war is expensive, destabilising, and value-destructive. It is not a solution; it is a symptom of failure to reach one.

If you want the owners to support a no-closure route, then bring a package that is **arithmetically credible**:
- exact savings,
- exact capacity reduction,
- exact burden-sharing,
- exact timing,
- and enforceability.

And let me say this plainly: if the price of “no closures” is a deal that leaves margins broken and the business uninvestable, then that is not labor protection. That is delay.

## To the Regional State Government
I respect your role and your political responsibility. But I cannot support a policy of protecting every site regardless of economic reality.

Long-term regional employment depends on a company that can earn its cost of capital and fund future products. If the footprint remains too large, the region may preserve sites in the short run and lose investment, competitiveness, and jobs later.

So I support your insistence on:
- enforceability,
- investment commitments,
- and shared sacrifice.

But I do **not** support turning the state’s veto into a guarantee that the structure can never be corrected.

## To Institutional Investors
You are right to insist on measurable savings, margin recovery, and execution certainty.

But be careful not to confuse “clean on paper” with “safe in reality.” A closure-based plan can be economically strong and operationally disastrous if it triggers prolonged industrial conflict. Likewise, a no-closure plan can be politically comfortable and financially weak.

I am not backing a symbolic deal. I am backing the option that restores competitiveness **without** destroying the company’s social and governance stability.

## To the expert
Your analysis is helpful, but incomplete without real site-level data. I agree with your conclusion that restructuring is necessary and that a no-closure solution is only credible if the arithmetic closes at sufficient scale.

That is exactly why I will not pre-commit either way. I want the model:
- by site,
- by measure,
- by timing,
- and by net present value.

Without that, nobody should pretend certainty.

## My bottom line
I am not here to preserve the old structure for its own sake.  
I am here to protect the long-term value of the family holding’s core asset.

That means:
- restoring competitiveness,
- protecting margins,
- avoiding prolonged value-destroying conflict,
- and insisting on real numbers before accepting closures or redundancies as necessary.

So my position remains:
- **No cosmetic compromise.**
- **No closure or redundancy decision without quantified proof.**
- **No settlement that leaves the company structurally weak.**

If a no-closure package can truly close the gap, I will support it.  
If it cannot, then management must show why — in numbers, not in pressure.
