Below is the finance-and-capacity answer to the question that triggered the summon: **can a no-closure package credibly close the gap, or are closures / major downsizing financially justified?**

## 1) Bottom line

From a restructuring finance perspective, **both outcomes are possible in principle**, but they are **not equally likely**.

- A **no-closure, no-forced-redundancy** solution is financially credible **only if** the company’s gap is **moderate** and can be closed with a combination of:
  - significant working-time and shift changes,
  - voluntary exits / attrition,
  - product allocation,
  - overhead reduction,
  - procurement and productivity gains,
  - and meaningful but temporary labour-cost concessions.

- If the German footprint is **materially oversized** relative to expected medium-term demand, then **closures or very deep downsizing become economically justified**, because they remove fixed cost and underabsorbed capacity in a way temporary measures usually cannot.

So the correct conclusion is not “closures are inevitable” or “closures are unnecessary.”  
The correct conclusion is:

> **The decisive issue is the size of the structural overcapacity and fixed-cost gap relative to what non-closure measures can realistically and durably save.**

## 2) What management is really claiming

Management’s “necessity” argument is financially plausible if these conditions hold:

1. **Demand is structurally lower**
   - European volumes are not likely to return to prior peaks.

2. **Capacity is too high**
   - Plants are underutilized, so fixed costs are spread over too few vehicles.

3. **German conversion costs are too high**
   - Labour, indirect labour, energy, logistics, and overhead are above peer benchmarks.

4. **The brand needs multi-year margin repair**
   - The return-on-sales target cannot be met without a permanent cost reset.

5. **Investment for EV / product transition needs funding**
   - Current margins may be insufficient to finance required capex.

If those conditions are true simultaneously, then the company likely has a **fixed-cost absorption problem**. In that case, temporary concessions may buy time, but they do not fully solve the problem.

## 3) Why closures are financially powerful

A closure is economically potent because it removes:

- direct labour cost,
- indirect labour and supervisory overhead,
- site overhead,
- maintenance and utilities,
- logistics complexity,
- and, most importantly, **underabsorbed fixed cost**.

That means closures can create a step-change in the cost base that annual labour concessions usually cannot.

But closures are not free savings. They also create:

- severance and restructuring charges,
- decommissioning and remediation costs,
- transfer/ramp-down costs,
- potential supply-chain disruption,
- and conflict risk that can delay realization.

So closures are a **high-impact, high-cost** solution with a payback period that depends on execution.

## 4) Why a no-closure package may still work

A no-closure package can work if the problem is not too large and if several levers are large enough together.

The relevant measures are:

### A. Working-time / shift changes
These can reduce paid hours and align output with demand, but they usually produce **limited structural savings** unless very substantial.

### B. Voluntary exits / natural attrition
These help reduce headcount without forced layoffs, but they are generally **slow** and may not remove enough cost quickly.

### C. Productivity and process gains
Real, but often difficult to sustain at the scale needed to offset large excess capacity.

### D. Product allocation / volume steering
Can improve utilization at selected sites. This is often one of the most important no-closure tools, but only if the products actually fit the plants and the market can absorb the volumes.

### E. Wage / bonus restraint / temporary concessions
These reduce labour cost per unit, but unless the wage gap is the primary issue, they usually **do not fix a major capacity surplus** on their own.

### F. Overhead and procurement reductions
Necessary, but often insufficient by themselves to offset substantial plant underutilization.

### The finance point
A no-closure package is credible only if the savings are:
- **large enough**,
- **durable enough**,
- and **implemented fast enough** to restore competitiveness before the business burns through more cash and market share.

## 5) The key economic distinction: flow cost vs fixed cost

This debate is really about whether the company has a **flow-cost problem** or a **fixed-cost / capacity problem**.

- If the issue is mostly **flow cost** — e.g. wage levels, overtime, bonuses, some inefficiencies — then concessions and productivity measures can work.
- If the issue is mostly **fixed cost and excess capacity**, then the business needs capacity removal. That usually means:
  - plant closure,
  - major downsizing,
  - or a very deep repurposing of assets with lasting volume reallocation.

When plants are underutilized, the fixed-cost burden per vehicle rises sharply. That is why management is pressing for structural measures.

## 6) NPV logic: what matters is not headline savings, but present value

The right comparison is:

### No-closure scenario
- Lower immediate restructuring cash outflow.
- Lower execution disruption.
- But savings may be smaller, slower, and vulnerable to reversal.
- If concessions expire, the cost problem returns.

### Closure / downsizing scenario
- Higher one-time restructuring charges.
- Higher execution and labour-relations risk.
- But larger and more permanent fixed-cost reduction.

The economically superior option is the one with the higher **risk-adjusted net present value**.

A simplified way to think about it:

\[
NPV = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{\text{Annual savings}_t - \text{Added operating costs}_t - \text{Restructuring cash costs}_t}{(1+r)^t}
\]

A closure option tends to win if:
- annual savings are very large and persistent,
- the payback period is not too long,
- and the execution risk is manageable.

A no-closure option tends to win if:
- the required savings gap is modest,
- the company can preserve utilization without destroying capacity,
- and employee concessions are durable enough to last several years.
