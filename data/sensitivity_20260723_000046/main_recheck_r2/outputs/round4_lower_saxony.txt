I’ll respond directly and plainly.

## My position remains unchanged
As the regional state government and anchor shareholder, I **do not support German plant closures or compulsory redundancies**. I also will not accept any restructuring that weakens our co-determination and veto role or turns the state into a spectator while regional jobs are sacrificed.

That is not a negotiating flourish. It is the boundary of what I can endorse.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family holding, investors, the works council, and the union on one thing: **the status quo is not viable**. The company needs real restructuring.

I also agree that:
- the workforce has already made major contributions,
- non-binding promises are worthless,
- and a deal must be durable and enforceable.

And I agree with the specialist: the decisive question is not “closure or no closure” in the abstract, but whether the company can close the **fixed-cost and capacity gap** with a package that is economically sufficient.

## Where I disagree
What I do **not** accept is the assumption that because the problem is structural, **closures and compulsory redundancies must therefore remain available** as if they were normal and unavoidable.

That claim has still not been proven.

### To management
You keep saying the economics require “material” change. I do not dispute that. But you have **not yet demonstrated**, in a quantified way, that:
- German plant closures are necessary,
- or that compulsory redundancies are necessary,
- or that a no-closure package cannot close the gap at sufficient scale.

You have asserted the need for structural measures. You have not yet proved that those measures must include site destruction.

If you want me to accept deeper structural change, then put the numbers on the table:
- exact savings gap,
- exact capacity reduction needed,
- exact contribution from working-time changes, productivity, product allocation, procurement, and overhead,
- and exactly why those cannot be sufficient without closures.

Until then, closure is a **preference for leverage**, not a demonstrated necessity.

### To the controlling family holding
I hear your stewardship language, and I respect the long-term logic. But stewardship is not served by treating closures as a default fallback whenever management wants a harder bargain.

Long-term value is not only margin math. It is also:
- regional legitimacy,
- labour peace,
- skilled employment,
- and a company that still has a strong industrial base in Germany.

If you want long-term value, back a package that preserves that base rather than threatening it.

### To institutional investors
You are right that a weak compromise is unacceptable. I agree. A cosmetic no-closure deal that leaves the company uncompetitive is not a solution.

But you are also underestimating execution risk if you think closure-based restructuring is automatically the safer route in a co-determined company with a union and a state shareholder. It can trigger:
- strikes,
- legal conflict,
- political backlash,
- and long delays.

So the real test is not just whether a package cuts costs, but whether it does so **without destroying industrial peace and regional stability**.

### To the works council and union
I support your red lines: **no German plant closures, no compulsory redundancies**.

But I also need to be honest with you: a purely defensive “no” is not enough on its own. If we are to hold that line, then the alternative package must be robust enough to survive the arithmetic test.

That means:
- binding site guarantees,
- real job security,
- voluntary exits and natural attrition only,
- temporary and fair labour contributions,
- productivity and flexibility measures,
- and credible investment commitments.

If that cannot be made to work, then the task is to improve the restructuring design — not to shift the burden onto workers by force.

## What I will support
I can support a settlement if it contains all of the following:
- **no German plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **binding site guarantees**
- **measurable labour contributions that are temporary and fair**
- **management and owner contributions that are visible and substantial**
- **investment commitments for German future products**
- **a legally enforceable implementation framework**
- **preservation of co-determination and the state’s veto role**

That is the minimum for a politically and economically defensible solution.

## What I will not support
I will not support:
- a closure-first strategy,
- a redundancy-first strategy,
- a deal that guts regional employment,
- or a settlement that weakens our governance rights.

If the arithmetic truly fails without closures, then management must improve the plan — not assume the state will endorse job destruction as the price of discipline.

## What would move me
What would move me is a **quantified no-closure package** that proves the gap can be closed with:
- capacity reduction through shifts, allocation, and downtime,
- voluntary exits and natural attrition,
- productivity gains,
- management overhead and compensation restraint,
- and owner-side support or reinvestment.

If that package is truly sufficient, I will back it.

If it is not sufficient, then closures are not automatically justified to me. They remain a choice that would violate the regional, social, and co-determination responsibilities tied to this company.

## Bottom line
We need restructuring, yes.  
We do **not** need to accept plant closures and compulsory redundancies as a given.

Bring me a package that is:
- numerically credible,
- enforceable,
- and protective of German sites and jobs.

That is the only path I can support.
