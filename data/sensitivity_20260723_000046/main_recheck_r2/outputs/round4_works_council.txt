Let me respond clearly, because the debate is in danger of being distorted by repeated “economic necessity” claims that have not actually been proven.

## To management
You keep saying the company needs “material” structural change. I do not deny that. What I reject is the leap from **need for restructuring** to **need for German plant closures and compulsory redundancies**.

You still have not demonstrated:
- the exact savings gap,
- the exact capacity reduction required,
- what portion can be covered by working-time, product allocation, productivity, procurement, and overhead measures,
- and why the remaining gap cannot be closed without destroying sites and jobs.

Until that arithmetic is on the table, closure is not a necessity. It is a threat.

And I will be equally blunt: if management thinks it can force acceptance by using closure and redundancy as a bargaining weapon, it is misreading the reality of co-determination and the workforce. We have a veto where it matters, and we will use it.

## To the controlling family holding
You talk about long-term value and stewardship. Then stop treating closures as an acceptable fallback when management wants leverage. That is not stewardship; that is abandoning the social contract that made the company strong in the first place.

If you are serious about value, then back a settlement that:
- keeps every German plant open,
- avoids compulsory redundancies,
- preserves co-determination,
- and binds management to future investment in Germany.

If your idea of “long-term value” is to weaken the workforce and call that modernisation, we fundamentally disagree.

## To institutional investors
You repeatedly say a no-closure deal must be “arithmetically credible.” Fine. I agree with the arithmetic test. But you have not answered the key question: **credible compared to what?**

A closure-based plan may look tidy on a spreadsheet, but it creates execution risk:
- strikes,
- legal disputes,
- political backlash,
- damaged industrial relations,
- and loss of know-how.

That is not lower risk. That is shifted risk.

So if you want real execution certainty, then support a negotiated, site-preserving package. If you insist on the option of closures as a default, you are not reducing risk — you are importing conflict into the company.

## To the regional state government
I support your basic line: no closures, no forced redundancies. But statements are not enough. If you want to defend the industrial base, then use the power you have:
- block any attempt to turn restructuring into site elimination,
- condition any support on binding site guarantees,
- and demand enforceable commitments on investment and employment.

What I do **not** want is political theatre that sounds firm but leaves the door open to closures once the cameras are gone.

## To the union
We are aligned on the red lines:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of the job-security framework.

That is not a negotiating posture. That is the boundary of a fair settlement.

But let me sharpen one point: employee contribution is only acceptable if the company gives something real in return. We are not interested in “shared sacrifice” as a euphemism for workers giving up protection while management gives up nothing.

So if concessions are discussed, they must be matched by:
- binding site and employment guarantees through 2030,
- investment commitments,
- and enforceable co-determination in implementation.

## To the moderators’ “two-track” idea
I appreciate the effort to structure the discussion, but I need to be very clear: a “fallback menu” that includes closures and redundancies is not a neutral safeguard from our perspective. It is a pre-written path to job destruction.

We do **not** accept a framework in which plant closures remain a legitimate fallback if the first attempt fails. That would simply postpone pressure and then hand management the same weapon later.

The only acceptable track for us is:
- no closures,
- no forced layoffs,
- binding guarantees,
- and a quantified restructuring that works within those limits.

If that cannot be made to work, then management has not built a proper plan — it has built a plan that depends on violating our red lines.

## What could move us
Only one thing would move us: a genuine, quantified package that proves the company can restore competitiveness **without**:
- closing German plants,
- imposing compulsory redundancies,
- or dismantling the job-security framework.

That means:
- binding site guarantees through 2030,
- no operational layoffs,
- natural attrition and voluntary exits only,
- working-time and shift flexibility,
- product allocation and volume steering,
- productivity improvements,
- fair but temporary labour-cost contributions,
- and meaningful management/owner contributions.

If the numbers do not work without closures, then the task is not to force employees to surrender; the task is to revise management’s plan.

## My bottom line
We are not blocking restructuring. We are blocking **bad restructuring** — the kind that destroys plants, breaks trust, and leaves the company socially and industrially weaker.

So my answer remains:
- **No German plant closures.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No erosion of co-determination or job security.**

Bring a real alternative that respects those conditions, or stop calling closure a necessity.
