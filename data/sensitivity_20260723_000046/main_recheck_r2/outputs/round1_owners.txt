I will be direct as well.

I agree with a number of points made so far:
- The company needs a credible restructuring, not cosmetic adjustment.
- The workforce has already made major sacrifices.
- Non-binding promises are not enough.
- The current cost base and capacity situation cannot simply be preserved unchanged.

Where I do **not** agree is on the implication that the company can restore competitiveness while ruling out the hard decisions that management says may be necessary.

### To the Works Council and the Union
I understand your red lines, but I cannot endorse a position that makes **all** plant closures and **all** compulsory redundancies untouchable in advance.

If the company truly has structural overcapacity and a cost base materially above competitors, then a serious solution must be capable of producing **durable** savings, not only temporary relief. If every site is guaranteed regardless of economics, then the burden of adjustment falls too heavily on pay, hours, and flexibility—and even that may not be enough.

That said, I also share your view that:
- trust has been damaged,
- vague promises are worthless,
- and the workforce cannot be asked to carry the whole burden.

So if employee-side contributions are expected, they must be matched by real commitments from management and ownership. But I will be frank: I do **not** support a blanket promise to preserve every current structure at any cost.

### To Management
I support the objective of restoring competitiveness and margins. That is essential.

But I would caution against treating plant closures as the easy or default answer. Closure-driven restructuring risks:
- prolonged conflict,
- reputational damage,
- political backlash,
- and value destruction through industrial instability.

That is not something I want this company to stumble into casually. If management believes closures are unavoidable, it must show:
- why alternatives are insufficient,
- what savings are required,
- and why the proposed package is the least damaging route.

At this stage, I do not hear enough evidence that closures should be the first conclusion rather than the last resort.

### To the Regional State Government
I appreciate the emphasis on the regional industrial base and co-determination. Those are real constraints, and they matter.

But I would not support a stance that effectively freezes the company in its current footprint irrespective of economics. The company must remain investable and competitive. If the result of blocking necessary restructuring is a slow erosion of margins, market share, and investment capacity, then that also damages the region and the workforce.

So I agree with you on:
- no reckless closures,
- no forced redundancies as a preferred route,
- and a preference for negotiated, socially responsible adjustment.

But I do **not** agree that the burden of proof is so high that management can never reach the point where site rationalisation becomes justified.

### My position
From the perspective of the family holding, the priority is the **long-term value of the enterprise**.

That means:
- I support a real competitiveness drive,
- I support a negotiated solution if it restores margins durably,
- I support labour peace if it is economically credible,
- but I do **not** support a settlement that permanently preserves overcapacity and leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

If an alternative package can achieve comparable savings through working-time, productivity, capacity reduction, and employee contributions **without destroying value**, I am open to it.

If it cannot, then we cannot rule out deeper structural measures simply because they are uncomfortable.

So I am not here to endorse either a closure-at-any-cost strategy or a no-closure-at-any-cost strategy. I am here to insist on a solution that actually restores competitiveness and protects the company’s future.
