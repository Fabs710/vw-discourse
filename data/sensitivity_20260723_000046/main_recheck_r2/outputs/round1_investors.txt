We are aligned on the **goal**, but not yet on the **route**.

From the perspective of capital markets, I support a restructuring only if it produces a **credible, durable return to competitive margins**. That is the test. Anything less is value-destructive.

### Where I agree
I agree with management, the family holding, and even the regional state on several points:

- The status quo is not acceptable.
- The cost base appears structurally too high.
- The company needs real, not cosmetic, savings.
- Non-binding assurances are not enough.
- The workforce has already carried a heavy burden.

I also agree that prolonged industrial conflict would destroy value. Strikes, uncertainty, and reputational damage are bad for earnings, cash flow, and the share price.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** support a solution that simply preserves the current cost structure under a different label.

So I am not here to back:
- symbolic “capacity adjustments” that do not materially reduce costs,
- temporary measures that merely defer the problem,
- or a settlement that leaves the company still structurally uncompetitive.

If the package cannot close the margin gap in a durable way, then it is not credible.

At the same time, I do **not** view plant closures as inherently desirable. They are acceptable only if they are genuinely necessary to restore competitiveness and if alternatives have been exhausted. Closure for its own sake is value-destructive if it triggers endless conflict, legal risk, and execution delay.

### What we need to see
Any acceptable outcome must have four things:

1. **Material, measurable cost reduction**
   - not aspirational,
   - not phased so slowly that the market never sees it,
   - and not dependent on vague future productivity gains.

2. **Durability**
   - savings must survive the next downturn,
   - not disappear after a one-off concession expires.

3. **Execution certainty**
   - a deal is better than a protracted strike war,
   - but only if it can actually be implemented.

4. **A believable path to margins**
   - management should be able to point to how return on sales gets back to target.

### On the employee side
I hear the red lines on no closures and no forced redundancies. I understand the political and social logic.

But from an investor standpoint, those red lines are only acceptable if you can still deliver the economics. If they prevent meaningful restructuring, then they simply shift the cost of adjustment onto shareholders through lower returns.

So my position is straightforward:
- I am open to **working-time changes, productivity measures, capacity reduction, and negotiated labour-cost contributions**.
- I am **not** open to a cosmetic deal that leaves the company with an uncompetitive base.
- I am also **not** open to endless escalation that destroys earnings while everyone waits for a political breakthrough.

### On management
Management needs to be careful not to present closure as the only serious option unless it has truly tested alternatives. Otherwise the market will read this as weak execution and poor labour relations, which is damaging in itself.

But management is right on one core point: **competitiveness must be restored structurally**. If that requires hard decisions, then those decisions need to be made.

### Bottom line
From our side, we will back **the option that credibly restores margins and cash generation**.

That could be:
- a negotiated package with substantial employee contributions and real capacity reductions,
- or deeper structural cuts, if that is the only way to achieve the economics.

What we will **not** support is a settlement that is politically comfortable but financially inadequate.

If the parties want investor support, they need to deliver:
- a specific savings number,
- a credible implementation path,
- and evidence that the company will be competitively positioned after the restructuring.

Otherwise, capital will simply move elsewhere.
