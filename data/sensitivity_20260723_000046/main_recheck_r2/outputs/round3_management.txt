Let me be equally clear in round 3.

I hear the repeated insistence on **“no closures, no redundancies, no dismantling of guarantees.”** I understand why that is politically attractive. But I do **not** accept that those red lines can simply be declared and then the company is expected to absorb the economic consequences.

## Where I agree
I agree on four points:

- The status quo is not viable.
- The workforce has already made substantial sacrifices.
- Any restructuring must be real, enforceable, and durable.
- The company cannot rely on cosmetic measures or empty promises.

I also agree that industrial conflict is costly and should not be prolonged for its own sake.

## Where I disagree — fundamentally
What I do **not** accept is the argument that because closures and redundancies are painful, they can be ruled out in advance while still expecting a credible restructuring.

That is not a solution. It is a constraint that may make the economics impossible.

You keep asking us to prove that closures or compulsory redundancies are “necessary.” I will answer directly:

- We have a **structural overcapacity problem**.
- We have a **cost base above target**.
- We have a **shrinking European market**.
- We have a brand that is **missing its return-on-sales target**.
- And we need to **free funds for future products and technology to 2030**.

If you propose a package that does not materially reduce capacity and labour cost, then it does **not** solve the problem. It merely postpones it.

## To the Works Council
You say we have not proven closures are necessary. But you have also not shown that a no-closure, no-redundancy package can actually close the gap at the required scale and durability.

That is the real issue.

A binding site guarantee for all German plants through 2030 may sound reassuring, but if it locks in an uncompetitive footprint, then it is not a responsible guarantee — it is a guarantee of continuing underperformance.

I am prepared to discuss:
- working-time changes,
- shift and roster flexibility,
- natural attrition,
- voluntary exits,
- productivity improvements,
- capacity reductions,
- and negotiated labour-cost contributions.

But these have to be **large enough, durable enough, and executable enough**. If they are not, then we have not solved the structural problem.

## To the Trade Union
You are right that the workforce should not be the only side contributing. On that point, we agree.

But your position still assumes that labour can contribute while remaining insulated from the core restructuring tools. That is not realistic.

I also need to be blunt: if the union treats closures and redundancies as entirely off limits regardless of the economics, then it is not bargaining over restructuring — it is bargaining over whether restructuring can happen at all.

You say closures are a bargaining threat. I say they are a reality we cannot rule out if the economics require structural change. I would prefer to reach a negotiated solution without them. But I will not pretend they are off the table if they are needed to restore competitiveness.

## To the Regional State Government
You oppose closures and forced redundancies. I understand the political logic. But your position does not alter the economic facts.

If the company continues with an oversized, high-cost footprint, the result is not “protecting the industrial base.” It is delaying adjustment until the company is even weaker. That is a worse outcome for the region, not a better one.

If you want to defend jobs in the long run, then support a package that makes the business competitive again. That means being open to deeper structural measures if alternatives do not close the gap.

## To the Controlling Family Holding
You say you want long-term value. Good — then long-term value has to be measured in more than the immediate avoidance of conflict.

If a package preserves every site but leaves the company structurally too expensive, that destroys value in a slower, more corrosive way. I need the family holding to be clear: are you prepared to back a package that is economically sufficient, even if it includes hard structural measures? Or are you insisting on a politically comfortable compromise that leaves the problem for later?

Because those are not the same thing.

## To Investors
You are right to insist on measurable savings, durability, and execution certainty. That is exactly why I am not willing to accept a cosmetic compromise.

But I also caution against the idea that we can solve this through pure pressure and financial logic alone. A deal that is economically weak and industrially explosive is not value creation. So yes: we need a package that is enforceable and durable. But it must also be **material**.

## What I am actually asking for
I am not asking for a surrender. I am asking for a credible restructuring.

That means:
- real and durable cost reduction,
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- a genuine labour-cost contribution,
- productivity and flexibility gains,
- and a settlement that restores medium-term margins.

If the employee side wants to avoid closures and compulsory redundancies, then it must put forward a package that **arithmetically** matches what those measures would deliver. Not in rhetoric. In numbers.

## My limit
I will not support:
- a deal that leaves the cost base uncompetitive,
- a deal that preserves excess capacity,
- a deal that protects the old structure while starving future investment,
- or a deal built on the assumption that management can somehow wish away the gap.

## My openness
I am still open on the **mechanism**:
- closures,
- capacity cuts,
- working-time changes,
- voluntary exits,
- productivity measures,
- or a combination.

But I am **not** open on the necessity of structural change itself.

## Bottom line
If you want a negotiated settlement, bring a package that is:
- quantitatively credible,
- structurally durable,
- and sufficient to restore competitiveness.

If you cannot do that, then we will have to pursue deeper structural measures, because standing still is not an option and a protected but uncompetitive footprint is not a future.
