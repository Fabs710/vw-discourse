I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family holding, and investors on one essential point: **the company needs a real restructuring**. The status quo is not sustainable, and vague promises are not enough.

I also agree with the works council and the union that:
- the workforce has already carried a heavy burden,
- any solution must be **enforceable**,
- and the burden cannot fall on employees alone.

And I agree with everyone that a prolonged industrial conflict would damage value, trust, and the company’s future.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the way management keeps sliding from “structural change” to “therefore closures and forced redundancies must remain available.”

That leap is not yet justified.

Management has not shown, in a quantified and unavoidable way, that:
- German plant closures are necessary,
- or that compulsory redundancies are required,
- or that the same savings cannot be achieved through a negotiated alternative.

Saying the cost base is high is not the same as proving that closures are the only credible answer.

## To management
You are right that the gap is structural. I do not dispute that. But structural does **not** automatically mean destructive.

What I need from you is not another general warning, but a clean and quantified case:
- What is the exact savings gap?
- What capacity reduction is actually needed?
- What can be achieved through working-time changes, productivity gains, shift adjustments, voluntary exits, and labour-cost contributions?
- What cannot?

Until that is on the table, I will not treat closures as necessary.

And I want to be equally clear: a restructuring that preserves competitiveness by gutting regional employment would not be acceptable to us. That would undermine the company’s role as an industrial anchor in our state.

## To the Works Council and Union
I support your core red lines:
- **no German plant closures**,
- **no compulsory redundancies**.

That is not rhetoric. That is a legitimate boundary for us as a state shareholder and political actor.

I also take seriously your point that labour can contribute, but only in exchange for binding guarantees. That is correct. If the workforce makes concessions, then management, owners, and the company must also contribute in a measurable way.

Where I would press you is this: you are right to reject closures, but you also need to help shape a package that is economically credible. A defensive “no” is not enough on its own. The alternative must be strong enough to close the gap and survive implementation.

So I am looking for:
- binding site guarantees,
- no operational layoffs,
- capacity reduction through non-destructive tools,
- voluntary and natural attrition,
- investment commitments for German products and technologies,
- and enforceable co-determination in implementation.

That is the kind of package I can defend.

## To the Family Holding
You say you do not want closure for its own sake. Good. Then do not treat closures as a normal fallback option if management wants leverage.

If the family wants long-term value, it should back a negotiated solution that protects skilled jobs, industrial capacity, and labour peace. A company of this importance cannot be run as if the social settlement were disposable.

I would also remind you: preserving trust in the workforce and in the regional base is part of enterprise value.

## To Investors
You are right that the final package must be credible and durable. I do not object to that. What I do object to is the assumption that the only credible route is one that puts plant closures and redundancies on the table as the default instrument.

A deal that destroys industrial peace, triggers political backlash, and weakens the regional base may look “clean” on paper and still destroy value in practice.

So yes, I want measurable savings and durability. But I will not support a package that solves the spreadsheet and damages the company’s long-term legitimacy.

## My position, plainly
I will support a restructuring if it:
- preserves German plants,
- avoids compulsory redundancies,
- delivers real and durable cost reductions,
- includes shared contributions from management, owners, and labour,
- and protects the company’s role as a regional anchor.

I will not support:
- German plant closures as a first resort,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or a settlement that sidesteps the state’s co-determination and veto role.

That remains my line.

## What would move me
What would move me is a **quantified alternative package** that shows:
1. the exact savings target,
2. the exact capacity reduction target,
3. the exact contribution from labour,
4. the exact contribution from management and owners,
5. and how the package is made legally and operationally binding.

If that package can genuinely close the gap **without** closures and forced redundancies, then that is the solution I will support.

If it cannot, then the problem is not our red lines — it is that the proposed restructuring is not yet good enough.

## Bottom line
I am not here to preserve every legacy structure at any cost. But I am equally not here to sign off on a restructuring that weakens the company by sacrificing its regional base.

A viable solution must restore competitiveness **and** protect the company’s role as a regional economic anchor. Anything else is not acceptable to us.
