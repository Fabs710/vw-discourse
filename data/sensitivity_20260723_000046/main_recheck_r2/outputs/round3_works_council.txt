Management, owners, and investors keep asking us to prove that a no-closure alternative can work. Fine. We are prepared to discuss numbers. But let me be equally clear: **you have still not proven that German plant closures or compulsory redundancies are necessary.** Saying “the gap is structural” does not automatically justify destroying sites and breaking the job-security framework.

## Where I agree
I agree with all sides on one point: the current setup is not sustainable and a real restructuring is necessary. I also agree that vague promises are useless, and that any solution must be durable and enforceable.

I agree as well that the workforce cannot be the only contributor. But contribution is not the same as surrender.

## Where I disagree
I reject the framing that if the company needs deeper savings, then closures and forced redundancies become legitimate by default. That is not a conclusion; it is a management preference dressed up as inevitability.

You have not shown:
- the exact savings gap,
- the exact capacity reduction needed,
- the exact contribution expected from labour,
- and the exact reason why these cannot be achieved **without** closures and compulsory layoffs.

Until that is on the table, closure talk remains pressure, not proof.

## My position remains unchanged
I will not move on the core red lines:

- **No German plant closures.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No dismantling of co-determination or the job-security framework.**

That is the boundary of any settlement from the works council side.

## What a real alternative must include
If management, owners, and capital want a credible deal, then put a fully quantified package on the table that delivers savings without site destruction:

- binding site guarantees for all German plants through 2030,
- no operational layoffs,
- capacity reduction through shift changes, working-time adjustments, and product allocation,
- natural attrition, retirement, and voluntary exits only,
- productivity gains and process improvements,
- fair labour-cost contributions,
- and binding investment commitments for German future products.

That is not a fantasy package. That is a negotiated restructuring.

## To management
You say you need structural change. I do not dispute that. What I dispute is the assumption that structural change must mean plant closures. If you are serious, show us:
- how much savings come from working-time, productivity, supply chain, and product mix changes,
- what employee contribution would be sufficient,
- and what management and owners are prepared to give in return.

Do not ask us to accept job destruction first and promises later.

## To the union
I stand with you on the red lines. Your warning is right: a deal that only softens the blow while leaving the company structurally weak is no deal at all. We need shared sacrifice, not one-sided sacrifice.

But I want to sharpen the point: if management wants labor flexibility, it must come with enforceable guarantees. No guarantees, no contribution. That should remain the rule.

## To the regional state government
Your position is correct: no closures, no forced redundancies. But that must be enforced, not just repeated. Use your influence to block any attempt to turn “socially responsible restructuring” into a euphemism for plant elimination.

If the state wants to defend the industrial base, it should condition its support on:
- binding German site guarantees,
- investment commitments,
- and a negotiated solution that protects every plant.

## To the family holding
You say you want long-term value. Then stop treating closures as an acceptable fallback whenever management wants leverage. Long-term value depends on skilled workers, labour peace, and trust in the company’s social contract. Once you break that, you do not restore it with spreadsheets.

If you want a strong company in 2030, back a settlement that keeps German plants open and preserves the workforce that makes the products.

## To investors
You want durability and execution certainty. So do we. But industrial peace is part of execution certainty. A closure-driven model may look cleaner on paper, but it risks strikes, legal battles, political conflict, and lasting damage to the company’s reputation and internal cohesion.

A negotiated no-closure package can be the more durable value solution if you actually measure execution risk instead of only accounting arithmetic.

## What would move us
Only one thing would move us: a genuinely credible, quantified alternative that shows closures are not needed.

That means:
- exact savings,
- exact capacity reduction,
- exact timing,
- exact burden-sharing,
- and exact guarantees.

If you cannot show that, then the problem is not our red lines. The problem is that your restructuring plan is not yet complete.

## Bottom line
We remain ready to negotiate a serious restructuring, but not at the price of German plant closures, compulsory redundancies, or the erosion of co-determination.

Bring a package that closes the gap **without** destroying sites and jobs. Until then, our answer stays no.
