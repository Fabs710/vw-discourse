I’ll be direct.

I agree with the Works Council on the **core red lines**: **no German plant closures and no compulsory redundancies**. I also agree with management and the owners on one point: the status quo is not sustainable, and a real restructuring is needed. Where I do **not** agree is on the idea that the only credible restructuring is one that destroys jobs and tears up the collective bargaining framework.

## What the union accepts
We are prepared to discuss a package that includes:
- **capacity reductions**,
- **working-time flexibility**,
- **temporary or partial labour-cost contributions**,
- **bonus and variable-pay adjustments**,
- **productivity measures**,
- and **natural attrition / voluntary exits**.

That is a real contribution. It is not cosmetic.

But it must be part of a **shared-sacrifice deal**, not a one-sided wage and job cut imposed from above.

## What we reject
I reject three things outright:

1. **Forced redundancies**  
   That is not a “tool of adjustment”; it is a line-crossing attack on the workforce.

2. **German plant closures as a bargaining lever**  
   If closures are used to pressure employees into surrender, then management is not negotiating in good faith.

3. **A restructuring where employees absorb the pain while management, owners, and capital remain insulated**  
   That would be both unfair and destabilizing, and it would make industrial peace impossible.

## To management
You keep saying “durable savings” and “structural change,” but you still have not answered the central question: **why must the burden fall primarily on jobs and sites instead of on a balanced package?**

If you want serious union engagement, then put forward:
- the **exact savings target**,
- the **exact capacity reduction target**,
- the **exact contribution expected from labour**,
- and the **exact contribution from management and owners**.

Until then, “competitiveness” is too vague to justify breaking the social contract.

## To the Works Council
We are aligned on the red lines, and I support your insistence on binding guarantees. But I want to sharpen one point: **site security must be matched by an economically credible package**. If we present only a defensive no-no list, management will continue to claim there is no alternative.

So yes, we need:
- binding site security,
- no operational layoffs,
- investment commitments,
- and a co-determined implementation framework.

But we also need to show that a negotiated alternative can genuinely close the cost gap.

## To the regional state government
I welcome your clear rejection of closures and forced redundancies. But that position must not remain purely political. Use your influence to push management toward a settlement that includes:
- binding industrial commitments,
- investment in future products,
- and a fair burden-sharing formula.

If the state wants to preserve the regional industrial base, it should not allow the company to shift the crisis entirely onto the workforce.

## To the family holding
You say you do not want closure for its own sake. Fine — then back that up by insisting on a negotiated alternative instead of leaving the workforce under threat. If the family wants long-term enterprise value, then it should support a settlement that preserves skilled jobs, labour peace, and industrial capacity in Germany.

## To investors
I hear your point about execution certainty and durable margins. But durable margins do **not** come from provoking a prolonged conflict that damages production, reputation, and trust. A fair negotiated deal is more valuable than a “clean” financial model built on industrial unrest.

## What would move us
A deal becomes possible if it contains all of the following:
- **no plant closures**,
- **no compulsory redundancies**,
- **binding site and employment security**,
- **measurable employee contributions**,
- **equivalent contributions from management and owners**,
- **investment commitments for German future products**,
- and **enforceable implementation rules**.

Without those elements, there is no basis for a durable settlement.

## Bottom line
We are not refusing restructuring. We are refusing **one-sided restructuring**.

If management wants a settlement, it must come back with a real shared-sacrifice package that protects jobs and sites and preserves the collective bargaining framework. If it does not, the union will use its leverage accordingly.
