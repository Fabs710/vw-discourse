I’ll be equally direct in round 3.

## Where I agree
I agree with the works council, state government, and even management on one essential point: **the status quo is not viable**. The company needs real restructuring, not slogans. I also agree that **non-binding assurances are worthless** and that any settlement has to be durable and enforceable.

I also agree that the workforce cannot be the only side contributing. We have already said that clearly by putting forward a counter-proposal with labour-cost and capacity measures.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the conclusion that because the gap is structural, **German plant closures and compulsory redundancies must stay on the table as if they were normal tools**.

That is not “economic realism.” That is a bargaining threat.

You still have not shown, in a quantified and unavoidable way, why the needed savings cannot be achieved through:
- capacity reduction,
- working-time adjustments,
- productivity gains,
- shift and allocation changes,
- voluntary exits and natural attrition,
- temporary labour-cost contributions,
- and management/owner contributions.

If the argument is only “we want maximum flexibility,” then say that plainly. But do not dress it up as necessity.

## To management
You say the burden is on everyone to produce a package that closes the gap. Fine. Then be specific:

- What is the exact savings target?
- What capacity reduction is actually required?
- What portion comes from labour-cost measures?
- What portion comes from management overhead, portfolio, or investment decisions?
- What portion do owners accept in the form of lower short-term extraction or support?

Until you put the arithmetic on the table, you are asking workers to accept irreversible job risk on the basis of a general claim.

And I will say this clearly: **if management insists on using closures and forced redundancies as pressure points, it will get conflict, not cooperation**. We still have the strike weapon, and members expect us to use it if necessary.

## To the works council
We are aligned on the red lines:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of the wage framework.

That is not a tactical posture. That is the boundary of a fair settlement.

I also agree with you that guarantees must be binding. But I want to sharpen one point: if we ask the workforce for concessions, then those concessions must be matched by real commitments from management and owners, not just words about “future competitiveness.”

So yes:
- binding site security,
- no operational layoffs,
- investment commitments,
- and enforceable implementation rules.

But also:
- wage and bonus restraint must be limited,
- burden-sharing must be temporary and fair,
- and management must contribute visibly, not symbolically.

## To the regional state government
Your position is the correct one: no closures, no forced redundancies. But I need more than political statements. Use your veto and your influence to ensure the package is built around industrial preservation, not site elimination.

If the state is serious, it should insist on:
- binding site guarantees,
- product commitments,
- and no decision that offloads the entire adjustment onto labour.

## To the family holding
You say you want long-term value. Then back a settlement that preserves the workforce, the industrial base, and labour peace. A family owner cannot talk about stewardship while leaving closures on the table as leverage against employees.

If you want durable value, then insist on a shared-sacrifice settlement:
- owners contribute,
- management contributes,
- labour contributes,
- and German sites remain open.

## To investors
You want execution certainty. So do we. But execution certainty is not improved by threatening plant closures and compulsory redundancies in a co-determined system where the workforce has veto power and the union can mobilise industrial action.

A closure-first or redundancy-first approach may look efficient on a spreadsheet. In reality, it risks:
- strikes,
- loss of trust,
- litigation,
- political backlash,
- and delayed implementation.

That is not value creation. It is self-inflicted risk.

## What would actually move us
We can negotiate on **how** the burden is shared. We cannot negotiate away the red lines.

A deal becomes possible if it contains all of the following:
- **no German plant closures**,
- **no compulsory redundancies**,
- **binding site and job security**,
- **measurable employee contributions**,
- **equivalent contributions from management and owners**,
- **investment commitments for German future products**,
- and **a durably enforceable restructuring framework**.

If management and the owners can bring a package that closes the gap without violating those principles, then we are ready to negotiate details immediately.

If not, then the matter is simple: employees will not pay for restructuring by surrendering their jobs and their bargaining framework, and we will resist accordingly.

## Bottom line
We are not defending the old structure for its own sake. We are defending **fair restructuring**.

That means:
- real savings,
- shared sacrifice,
- no forced layoffs,
- no plant closures,
- and a durable settlement that preserves the collective wage framework.

If the other sides want a deal, bring one that meets those conditions. Otherwise, we will continue to oppose the current course with full leverage.
