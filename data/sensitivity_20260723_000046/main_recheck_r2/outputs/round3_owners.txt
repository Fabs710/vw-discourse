I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with all sides on a few basics:

- The status quo is not acceptable.
- The company needs a real restructuring, not messaging.
- The workforce has already contributed materially.
- Non-binding assurances are not enough.
- A prolonged conflict would destroy value.

I also agree that any settlement has to be **credible, durable, and enforceable**. That is the minimum.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the attempt by management to make closures and compulsory redundancies sound like the only serious answer without first proving that.

And I do **not** accept the labour side’s attempt to rule out German plant closures and compulsory redundancies in advance as if that were a complete strategy.

Both positions are incomplete.

The family holding’s view is simple: **we need the economics fixed, not a symbolic victory for any side**. If the final package leaves the company structurally too expensive, then it will not protect jobs, it will only delay a bigger problem and erode enterprise value.

## To management
You are right about one thing: this is structural, not merely cyclical.

But you have not yet given a sufficiently quantified case for why closures or forced redundancies are unavoidable rather than merely preferred. “Structural overcapacity” is a starting point, not a conclusion.

What I expect from management is:
- the exact savings gap,
- the exact capacity reduction required,
- what can be achieved through working-time, productivity, product mix, and voluntary measures,
- and what cannot.

If the answer is that the numbers do not work without deeper structural measures, then say so clearly and show it. Until then, I will not treat closures as justified by default.

## To the Works Council and Union
I hear your red lines:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding guarantees.

I understand why you take that position. But from an ownership and governance standpoint, I cannot support a package that hard-codes every site into place if that means the business remains uncompetitive.

You keep asking for proof that alternatives cannot work. Fair enough. But you have not yet produced an alternative package with numbers that show it can close the gap with the same durability and certainty.

So I am not moving on this point:
- I will not back a cosmetic compromise.
- I will not back a deal that preserves every legacy arrangement and calls it restructuring.
- I will not back a settlement that protects the old footprint at the expense of the company’s future.

If you want to avoid closures, then bring a package that is **arithmetically credible** and **structurally durable**. Not slogans. Not principles. Numbers.

## To the Regional State Government
Your concern about the industrial base is legitimate. But a blanket political no to closures does not solve the economic problem.

If the company cannot restore competitiveness, the region loses investment, jobs, and influence over time. So I do not share the assumption that preserving every site at all costs is automatically pro-regional. Sometimes it is just delayed damage.

I will support a settlement that protects the German industrial base **if** it also restores margins and competitiveness. If it does not, then it is not stewardship — it is postponement.

## On burden-sharing
Here I am prepared to be constructive, but not vague.

If employees are asked to contribute, then management and ownership must also contribute visibly and measurably. That can mean:
- management compensation restraint,
- overhead and complexity reduction,
- portfolio discipline,
- investment prioritisation,
- and no empty promises about future upside.

But employee contributions must be **durable**, not merely temporary theatre. If labour gives up flexibility and cost, then the company must get lasting relief in return.

## What would move me
A package could move me if it includes:
- quantified and durable savings,
- measurable capacity reduction,
- real labour-cost contributions,
- productivity and flexibility improvements,
- voluntary and phased workforce adjustment,
- investment commitments for future products,
- and a legally enforceable implementation structure.

If that package truly closes the gap without closures, that is preferable.

If it does not, then I will not pretend otherwise: deeper structural measures remain on the table, because the company cannot be forced to choose between competitiveness and fiction.

## Bottom line
I am not here to endorse closures for their own sake.

I am here to protect the long-term value of the enterprise. That means:
- restoring competitiveness,
- protecting margins,
- preserving labour peace where possible,
- and avoiding a value-destructive standoff.

So my position is unchanged:
- no cosmetic compromise,
- no blanket no-closure doctrine without arithmetic,
- and no restructuring that leaves the company structurally weak.

Bring a package that is economically sufficient and enforceable. If you can do that without closures, I will consider it. If you cannot, then the company must retain the ability to act decisively.
