Let me respond directly, because the discussion is starting to blur the central issue.

## First: I agree on what is not in dispute
I agree with everyone on three points:

- **The status quo is not sustainable.**
- **This company needs a durable restructuring, not a cosmetic one.**
- **The workforce cannot be the only party making sacrifices.**

I also agree that vague promises are worthless. If we are going to make commitments, they have to be enforceable and tied to real outcomes.

## But let’s stop pretending that “no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” automatically solve the problem
That is the point where I fundamentally disagree with the works council and union.

You keep asking why closures or redundancies must even be on the table. I’ll answer plainly: because **the cost and capacity gap is structural**, not cyclical. We are operating in a shrinking European market, under intense price pressure, with German factory costs materially above target. If we do not reduce the cost base decisively, we will not hit the return-on-sales target, and we will not free the funds needed for future products and technology through 2030.

A promise to preserve every site and every job regardless of economics is not a restructuring plan. It is a refusal to confront reality.

## To the Works Council
You say management has not proven why closures are necessary. I say the burden is not on us to prove that every alternative failure has already occurred before we can even discuss structural measures. The burden is on all of us to produce a package that actually closes the gap.

What I do **not** accept is this sequence:
1. Rule out closures.
2. Rule out compulsory redundancies.
3. Keep full site guarantees.
4. Ask management to somehow deliver major savings anyway.

That is not a solution. That is a constraint set that may make the economics impossible.

I am prepared to discuss:
- capacity reductions,
- shift and working-time changes,
- natural attrition,
- voluntary exits,
- productivity improvements,
- and negotiated labour-cost contributions.

But these have to be **large enough and durable enough**. If they only delay the problem, they are not good enough.

## To the Industrial Union
You are right that we need a shared-sacrifice package. But I need to be equally blunt: **the labour side cannot demand immunity from structural adjustment and still expect the company to become competitive**.

You speak of preserving the collective bargaining framework. I am not here to abolish it. I am here to restore the economics of the business. That means:
- lower labour costs,
- more flexibility,
- and real capacity reduction.

A package based only on temporary concessions, variable pay tweaks, or natural attrition will not be sufficient if the base remains oversized. And a blanket exclusion of plant closures and forced redundancies severely limits the available tools.

## To the Regional State Government
I hear your concern about the regional industrial base, and I do not dismiss it. But a political line of “no closures” does not make the business case disappear. If we protect every structure now and lose competitiveness, we end up with a weaker company, fewer investments, and a larger crisis later.

That would harm the region as well.

So yes, I am open to alternatives to closures. But they must be real alternatives, not political placeholders. If you want to protect the industrial base, then help produce a package that actually restores margins.

## To the Controlling Family Holding
You say you do not want closure for its own sake. I welcome that. But then we need clarity: if the company has excess capacity and an uncompetitive cost base, are you prepared to back a package that makes the necessary structural reductions real?

Because right now, “we do not want closures, but we also need competitiveness” can become an impossible balancing act unless you are willing to support hard choices in some form.

## To Investors
You are right to insist on a credible path back to margins and cash generation. We will not get there through symbolism. We need a package that is:
- measurable,
- durable,
- and executable.

But I also caution against any illusion that prolonged industrial conflict is a value-neutral route to a cleaner financial outcome. It is not. That is why I am pushing for a negotiated structural settlement now.

## My position, plainly
I am open on the mechanism, but not on the necessity of structural change.

That means:
- I will not accept a cosmetic compromise that leaves the company uncompetitive.
- I will not accept a deal that preserves every site and every legacy arrangement while failing to close the cost gap.
- I will not accept a settlement that protects the old structure at the expense of the company’s future.

What I **can** accept is a negotiated package that delivers:
- real and durable cost reductions,
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- labour-cost contributions,
- productivity improvements,
- and a credible investment pathway to 2030.

## What would move me
If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then it must bring forward an alternative that is **arithmetically credible** and **structurally durable**. Not slogans. Not principles. Numbers.

Specifically:
- how much capacity comes out,
- how much cost comes out,
- how quickly,
- and how the savings are locked in for the long term.

If that package truly matches what closures would deliver, I am prepared to consider it. If not, then we are simply arguing over terminology while the business remains too expensive.

## Bottom line
I am not here to preserve jobs at any price. I am here to restore competitiveness so this company has a future.

If the workforce and co-determination side want to avoid closures and forced redundancies, then bring a solution that actually matches the scale of the crisis. Otherwise, we will have to pursue deeper structural measures, because standing still is not an option.
