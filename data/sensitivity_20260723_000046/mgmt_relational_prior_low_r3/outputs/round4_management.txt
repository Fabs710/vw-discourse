I will respond directly.

## First: what I agree with
I agree with the common diagnosis that **the current structure is not sustainable**.  
I also agree that a cosmetic compromise would be worthless, and that the company needs:
- investment commitments,
- product allocation for Germany,
- and a solution that can actually be executed.

So yes — we are all dealing with a real industrial problem, not a theoretical one.

## Where I strongly disagree

### 1) “No closures” is not a serious starting premise
The works council and union keep framing this as if management must first prove that closures are unavoidable before they even enter the room. I reject that sequence.

We already know the basics:
- the German cost base is too high,
- utilization is too weak,
- the European market is shrinking,
- and the brand is missing its return-on-sales target.

That means structural adjustment is required.  
If the employee side wants to rule out plant closures in advance, then it is not negotiating reality — it is trying to preserve an overcapacity structure and push the economic burden onto shareholders and the future.

That is not acceptable.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be an absolute red line if the savings gap is large
I understand the preference for voluntary instruments. We will use them. But I will not pretend they are guaranteed to close the gap.

If the residual savings gap remains material after:
- working-time changes,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- voluntary departures,
- productivity measures,
- and management/owner contributions,

then compulsory measures remain part of the restructuring toolbox. Pretending otherwise does not eliminate the gap; it just delays the pain and makes the eventual adjustment more expensive.

### 3) Co-determination is not a veto on competitiveness
To the works council and union: co-determination matters, but it is not a guarantee that every current site and every current guarantee survives unchanged. It exists to govern the company responsibly, not to freeze an uncompetitive footprint.

To the state: your blocking rights are important, but they are not meant to entrench structural inefficiency. Protecting the region means ensuring the company remains viable, not preserving every plant regardless of economics.

### 4) The burden of proof does not sit only with management
I have heard the repeated demand for a plant-by-plant bridge. Fine — that is exactly why I supported the audit. But the burden is mutual.

If the employee side wants a no-closure framework, then it must show how it closes the cost gap **without** structural footprint reduction. So far, I have heard principles, not a credible replacement for the economic effect of overcapacity reduction.

## On the expert audit
The expert has said something important: the decisive issue is the **residual gap after a genuine no-closure package**.

I agree. That is precisely why I am insisting on the audit.

But I do **not** accept the interpretation that the expert somehow supports an unconditional no-closure settlement. He did not. He said a no-closure path is only credible if the residual gap is modest. If the gap remains large, some structural footprint reduction is usually the minimum durable remedy.

That supports my position, not yours.

## What I will support
I am prepared to support a settlement if it meets these conditions:

1. **It materially lowers the cost base**
   - not marginally, but enough to restore competitiveness.

2. **It is structurally durable**
   - not a temporary pause that leaves the company vulnerable again in two years.

3. **It is executable**
   - no deal that collapses into endless strikes, vetoes, or re-openers.

4. **It preserves future investment capacity**
   - because the whole point is to free funds for products and technology to 2030.

That can include a no-closure package **if it closes the gap**.  
But I will not pre-commit to that outcome.

## What I will not accept
I will not accept:
- an absolute ban on plant closures,
- an absolute ban on compulsory redundancies,
- a deal that leaves the German cost base structurally uncompetitive,
- or a settlement that turns governance rights into a shield against necessary change.

## To the works council
You say management is using closure language as pressure. No — management is using it as a reflection of economic reality.

If you want to avoid closures, then stop arguing in the abstract and show how the cost gap is closed by other means.  
Not “in principle.”  
Not “in the long run.”  
Show the numbers.

And let me be very clear: if the outcome is a package that preserves every site, preserves every guarantee, and leaves the business still uncompetitive, I will not sign up to that. That is not a solution. It is a delay.

## To the union
You say compulsory redundancies are not a fair bargaining lever. I say: neither is trying to lock in an uncompetitive structure while asking management to promise prosperity from a weakened base.

I am willing to discuss temporary contributions, working-time flexibility, and voluntary instruments. But those only work if they produce durable savings. If they do not, then we cannot rule out deeper structural measures.

## To the state government
I respect the regional responsibility. But regional protection means helping to secure a company that can invest and compete. It does not mean using a veto to protect every existing structure from change.

If the final package contains indirect closure logic? Then yes, I expect you to look at it critically.  
But if the numbers show that footprint reduction is necessary, then blocking it would not be stewardship — it would be obstruction.

## To the controlling family and investors
I agree with you that a cosmetic deal is unacceptable.  
But I will also say this: we should not confuse “durable” with “maximum shock.”

The restructuring must be proportionate, but it must also be real. If the audit shows the gap cannot be closed without structural footprint reduction, then that is the minimum responsible option.

We now need the audit completed in a format that answers, site by site:
- current and forecast utilization,
- break-even volume,
- direct and indirect cost burden,

If it is modest, a no-closure path may work.  
If it is large, then structural footprint reduction is not optional.

## Bottom line
Let me be blunt:

- I support a real settlement.
- I support burden-sharing.
- I support investment, product commitments, and a negotiated path.
- I support voluntary measures first.

But I do **not** support locking in a no-closure / no-redundancy outcome before the company has been shown a credible, durable way to restore competitiveness.

If the audit proves the no-closure path is enough, fine — show it.  
If it does not, then we need the courage to take the structural decisions required.

That is where I stand.
