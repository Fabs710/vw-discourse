We appreciate the clarity from all sides. Let me respond just as clearly from the controlling shareholder perspective.

## Where we agree

We agree on the core diagnosis:
- the current cost base is too high,
- the German footprint is overextended relative to demand,
- and the company needs a durable industrial reset, not a temporary patch.

We also agree that:
- employee contributions cannot be one-sided,
- a prolonged conflict would destroy value,
- and any settlement must be credible to markets, workers, and the supervisory board.

So there is no disagreement about the need for change. The disagreement is about **how much structural change is actually required, and how it is implemented without damaging the enterprise beyond repair**.

## Where I disagree

### 1) To the works council and union: “no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” cannot be absolute
I understand why you are setting hard lines. But from an ownership and governance standpoint, I cannot accept red lines that would make a genuine restructuring impossible before the numbers are even tested.

If the full no-closure, no-forced-layoff route can truly close the cost gap, then show it in detail. But if it cannot, then insisting on those absolutes is not protecting the company — it is preserving an uncompetitive structure until the next crisis.

That would be value-destructive, and I will not support it.

### 2) To the state government: governance rights are for stewardship, not immobilism
I respect the regional role and the responsibility to protect employment. But the blocking position cannot be used to freeze the company in a structurally weak state.

If the company remains overburdened, the result will not be stable jobs. It will be declining margins, weaker investment capacity, and a slower erosion of the German base. That is the outcome I am trying to avoid.

So yes, use governance to insist on process, justification, and balance. No, I will not support using it to preclude meaningful restructuring.

### 3) To management: closure rhetoric alone is not enough
I also want to be direct with management. Saying “closures cannot be ruled out” is not yet a plan. It is a negotiating position.

If you want support from the controlling shareholder, you need to bring a **site-by-site, quantified package**:
- the exact savings target,
- the exact gap to be closed,
- the role of working-time, flexibility, voluntary departures, and productivity gains,
- and only then, if still necessary, the minimum structural footprint changes required.

A blanket closure-led approach is too crude. It risks industrial conflict, political backlash, and permanent reputation damage. That would destroy more value than it creates.

## What we will support

We are prepared to support a **deep restructuring** if it meets three conditions:

1. **It materially restores competitiveness and margins.**  
   Not symbolically, but in a way that actually changes the economics.

2. **It is durable and implementable.**  
   A deal that collapses into strikes, repeated renegotiation, or legal deadlock is not a solution.

3. **It preserves the long-term industrial strength and governance stability of the group.**  
   That means no reckless shock therapy, but also no artificial preservation of everything as it is.

## The practical test

Before anyone demands that we endorse closures or reject them, I want the parties to answer one question with numbers:

**What is the full savings package under a no-closure scenario, and does it close the cost gap credibly?**

If the answer is yes, then we should examine it seriously.  
If the answer is no, then we have to be honest that structural footprint changes remain on the table.

But I will not accept either of these extremes:
- a cosmetic compromise that changes little, or
- a shock strategy that destroys labour peace and enterprise value.

## My bottom line

The controlling family holding will back **hard but orderly restructuring**.  
We will not back:
- a fake compromise,
- a permanent impairment of the company’s competitiveness,
- or a governance stalemate that protects every current structure while margins continue to erode.

So my position is this:
- **bring us a quantified plan**, not slogans;
- **prove what can be done without closures**, if you claim it is enough;
- and if it is not enough, then we need to confront that reality honestly and design the smallest effective restructuring possible.
