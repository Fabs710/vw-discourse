The expert’s assessment confirms what capital markets have been saying: **a no-closure approach is only credible if the savings gap is modest; if the overcapacity problem is structural, limited footprint reduction is more likely needed.** That is exactly the right framing.

## Where we agree
- **Management is right** that the current cost base is uncompetitive and that cosmetic fixes will not restore margins.
- **The expert is right** that a purely voluntary, no-closure package may work only if the gap is small and the savings are unusually large and durable.
- **The family holding is right** that a reckless shock strategy could destroy value through conflict and execution risk.
- **The works council, union, and state are right** that a one-sided restructuring is fragile and likely to fail if it simply dumps the burden on labour.

## Where we disagree
The employee side is still treating **“no plant closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” as absolutes**. From an investor standpoint, that is not an acceptable end state if the company’s economics do not support it.

If the cost base remains structurally too high, then preserving every German site and every legacy guarantee is not protecting value — it is **delaying impairment**. Markets will not reward that, and neither will the share price.

I also do **not** support a cosmetic “shared sacrifice” deal that:
- buys short-term industrial peace,
- leaves the cost base mostly intact,
- and punts the competitiveness problem into the future.

That would be the worst of both worlds: no real savings, but still all the disruption and uncertainty of a restructuring process.

## What we would support
We would support a package only if it delivers all three of the following:

1. **Material, quantified cost reduction**
   - not vague promises,
   - not purely temporary concessions,
   - but a real bridge back to target margins.

2. **Durable structural change**
   - if that requires limited footprint reduction, then it cannot be ruled out in advance;
   - if it can be achieved without closures, then show the numbers.

3. **Implementation certainty**
   - the deal must be executable under the governance constraints,
   - and not collapse into renewed conflict or repeated renegotiation.

## On the expert’s conclusions
The expert’s summary is commercially sensible:
- test the no-closure scenario seriously,
- but do not assume it will be enough,
- and if it is not enough, move to a **hybrid package** or limited footprint reduction.

That is the right standard for shareholders. We do not care whether the label is “no closure” or “hybrid”; we care whether the outcome **restores competitiveness and margins on a durable basis**.

## On labour
We are open to employee contributions, but only if they are:
- **material**,
- **time-bound only where appropriate**,
- and linked to **real structural improvements**.

We will not accept concessions that simply subsidize an overextended footprint. If labour is contributing, management and owners must also show discipline — but the key test is still whether the company becomes cheaper and more competitive in a lasting way.

## Bottom line
Our position is unchanged but sharpened by the expert advice:

- **No cosmetic settlement.**
- **No structurally impaired cost base.**
- **No blind commitment to every current site.**
- **Yes to a quantified, executable restructuring that restores margins.**
- **If no-closure cannot close the gap, then limited footprint reduction must remain on the table.**

If the parties can produce numbers showing that a no-closure scenario truly closes the gap, we will consider it. If not, then the discussion needs to move honestly toward the smallest effective structural reset.
