I will respond plainly.

The current discussion has clarified the real fault line: management and investors want to keep **closure and forced-layoff options open**, while the workforce is being asked to accept uncertainty first and protection later. That is not acceptable.

## To management
I agree with one point: the company needs a real industrial future and a credible cost path. I do **not** agree that this requires putting German plants and German jobs on the chopping block.

You still have not answered the decisive question:
**Why should the adjustment be led by closures and layoffs instead of by management, owners, product planning, internal flexibility, and a genuine contribution from capital?**

The expert has already said what we have been saying from the beginning: a no-closure scenario must be **tested seriously first**. Yet management continues to speak as if structural reduction is the default answer. That is exactly the wrong sequence.

If you claim the gap is structural, then show the full bridge:
- cost gap by site,
- utilization by site,
- what can be saved through working time,
- what can be saved through voluntary instruments,
- what management will give up,
- what owners will contribute,
- what investments and product allocations will secure future work.

Until that is on the table, talk of closures is not necessity. It is pressure.

## To investors and the controlling family
I agree that we need a durable solution, not a cosmetic one. I do **not** accept your conclusion that “durable” means keeping closure options alive until labour submits.

A deal that breaks trust, triggers strikes, and destroys the job-security framework is not durable. It is a short-term financial maneuver that risks permanent industrial damage.

You say a no-closure scenario may not be enough. Fine — prove it with numbers, not with threat language. But you are still not entitled to assume that the answer must be footprint reduction. Before any such conclusion, the company must exhaust:
- working-time reductions,
- internal redeployment,
- retraining,
- voluntary departures only,
- productivity gains,
- and serious management/owner concessions.

If those are insufficient, then we will examine the figures. But I will not accept plant closures as a starting premise.

## To the state government
I agree with your general line that the state should protect the region’s industrial base. But that support must be concrete.

You cannot say “no closures” in principle and then stand aside if management tries to force them through under another name. If a package contains hidden closure logic, capacity stripping, or a backdoor forced-redundancy path, then your blocking rights and supervisory-board influence must be used.

That is not obstruction. That is the proper use of co-owned governance.

## To the union
We are aligned on the essentials:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no German plant closures,
- no erosion of co-determination,
- and no one-sided workforce sacrifice.

I also agree with your point that temporary contributions may be discussable **only if** they are tied to binding guarantees. But let me be very clear: “temporary contribution” cannot become a code word for permanent job insecurity. Any labour contribution must be matched by:
- binding site and job guarantees to 2030,
- concrete product commitments,
- and an explicit no-closure commitment.

Without that, there is nothing to bargain over.

## On the expert’s assessment
The expert’s conclusion is exactly why we reject management’s rush to structural cuts:

- A no-closure scenario is **possible**.
- It must be **quantified first**.
- Limited footprint reduction is only “more probable” if management’s own assumptions are correct.
- But those assumptions have not been sufficiently tested against the alternatives.

So the expert does **not** justify closures. The expert just confirms that management has work to do before it can claim inevitability.

## Our position for this round
We will support only a package that starts from these non-negotiables:

1. **All German plants remain open**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Co-determination remains fully intact**
4. **Historic job-security guarantees are preserved, not dismantled**
5. **Any employee contribution is temporary, proportionate, and matched by binding guarantees**

## What we are prepared to discuss
We are prepared to discuss:
- working-time flexibility,
- internal capacity shifts,
- voluntary departures,
- retraining and redeployment,
- productivity improvements,
- and a negotiated employee contribution,

but only inside a framework that **rules out closures and forced layoffs**.

## What we are not prepared to discuss
We are not prepared to discuss:
- “smallest effective restructuring” if that means closing one site first,
- “site-by-site flexibility” if it is really a path to closure,
- “conditional no-closure” if the condition is simply that workers give up their protections,
- or any plan that weakens the workforce before management and owners have shown their own contributions.

## Final point
The burden of proof is on management and capital, not on the workforce.

If you want a serious settlement, bring a **fully quantified no-closure plan** that protects every German site and every job from compulsory redundancy. If you cannot do that, then you are not asking for restructuring — you are asking labour to ratify a dismantling.

We will not do that.
