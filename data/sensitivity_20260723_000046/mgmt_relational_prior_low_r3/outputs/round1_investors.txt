From the perspective of dispersed capital-market shareholders, the debate is moving in the right direction in one important sense: **everyone acknowledges that the current cost base is too high and that the company needs durable structural savings.** That is the essential starting point, and I appreciate that management, the family holding, and even the employee side are no longer pretending the status quo is acceptable.

But I want to be equally clear: **we will judge this entirely by whether it restores margins and competitiveness in a credible, durable way.** Not by rhetoric, not by social symbolism, and not by whether the outcome feels balanced to each side.

### Where we agree
We agree with management on the core diagnosis:
- the German cost base is uncompetitive,
- structural overcapacity is real,
- and the brand must get back to a sustainable return-on-sales trajectory.

We also agree with the family holding that a reckless, closure-led shock strategy could destroy value if it triggers prolonged industrial conflict and operational instability. We are not looking for chaos. We are looking for a credible path back to better margins.

And we agree with the works council, union, and state government on one limited but important point: **a purely one-sided restructuring that dumps all the burden on labour would be fragile and likely non-permanent.** If it cannot be implemented, it will not help shareholders either.

### Where we disagree
Where I part company with the employee side and the state is on the idea that **“no plant closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” can be treated as fixed absolutes regardless of the economics.** If those positions become non-negotiable, then you are basically asking investors to accept a structurally impaired cost base and hope for a turnaround by wishful thinking.

That would be unacceptable.

Likewise, I do not accept a cosmetic compromise that preserves every site in practice while relying on temporary measures, working-time tweaks, or deferred adjustments that do not materially reduce the cost base. If the final package does not produce a **clear, quantified, durable improvement in competitiveness**, then it is not a solution from our point of view.

### What we need to see
For capital markets, the bar is straightforward:

1. **A concrete, quantified savings plan**
   - exact cost reduction,
   - exact capacity reduction,
   - exact timing.

2. **Durability**
   - not just a one-year labor concession,
   - but a structural reset of the footprint and cost base.

3. **Credibility**
   - specific site actions, not vague promises,
   - and no assumption that the market will reward delay.

4. **Deal certainty**
   - one that can actually be implemented under the governance constraints,
   - without endless conflict or repeated renegotiation.

### On closures and layoffs
I am not fetishizing plant closures. They are not a goal in themselves. But I will say this plainly: **if closures or deeper workforce reductions are necessary to reach a competitive cost base, then they cannot be ruled out in advance.**

The same applies to compulsory redundancies. No investor wants social pain for its own sake, but if the alternative is a permanently bloated structure, then the value outcome is worse. A voluntary-only approach can be helpful, but only if it produces real numbers at scale.

### On employee contributions
We would support a negotiated employee contribution **if and only if** it is:
- material,
- time-bound,
- and linked to genuine structural changes.

Temporary concessions without footprint rationalization are not enough. Employees should not be asked to finance a restructuring that leaves the company with the same problem under a thinner accounting mask.

### On management and owner contributions
We also expect management and the controlling owners to show discipline:
- no complacency,
- no capital allocation that protects legacy structures at the expense of returns,
- and no public commitment to industrial preservation if it undermines financial recovery.

If the family holding wants to protect long-term enterprise value, it should back management in making hard decisions rather than blocking them until the numbers deteriorate further.

### Bottom line
Our position is simple:

- **We support a deep restructuring if it credibly restores margins and competitiveness.**
- **We do not support an outcome that preserves uncompetitive structures under the guise of social compromise.**
- **We will not back a cosmetic settlement.**

If the parties can bring forward a package that delivers real, durable cost reduction and a believable return to target profitability, we will support it. If not, we will treat it as value-destructive delay.
