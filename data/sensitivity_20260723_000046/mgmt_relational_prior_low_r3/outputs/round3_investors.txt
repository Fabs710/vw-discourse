I’ll be direct: **capital markets support a restructuring that restores margins, not one that preserves every legacy site at the expense of competitiveness.**

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family holding, and the expert on the key economic point:  
the company’s German cost base is too high, the footprint looks overextended, and **a cosmetic deal would be value-destructive**.

I also agree with the works council, union, and state on one important issue:  
**a one-sided shock strategy would be risky and could destroy more value than it creates** through strikes, governance deadlock, and execution failure.

So the right answer is not “closures at any cost” and it is not “no closures ever.” The right answer is **whatever credibly closes the cost gap with the lowest execution risk**.

## Where I disagree

### 1) To the employee side
Your position still treats **no plant closures and no compulsory redundancies as absolutes**. From an investor perspective, that is only acceptable if the numbers prove the company can still reach competitive margins.

Right now, that proof has **not** been provided.

You keep saying management has not exhausted alternatives. Fine — then quantify the alternatives. But do not assume that “no-closure” is automatically viable just because it is socially preferable. If the residual gap remains material, then protecting every site simply delays impairment and worsens value.

### 2) To management
I agree with your diagnosis more than with your tone. But you still need to be careful not to turn “closures cannot be ruled out” into a negotiating posture with no quantified endpoint.

If you want investor support, you need to show:
- the exact cost gap,
- what can be closed via concessions and productivity,
- what requires structural footprint reduction,
- and the time profile for getting margins back on target.

Without that, the market will see only uncertainty, and uncertainty is expensive.

### 3) To the state and family holding
I understand the concern about industrial peace and regional stability. But a governance stance that blocks all structural change would be just as damaging as shock therapy.

If the company remains overcapacity, investors will not reward preserved jobs with a stronger valuation. They will punish persistent underperformance. The result is slower erosion, not stability.

## What would move us
We are prepared to back a package only if it delivers three things:

1. **A credible margin recovery path**
   - not just savings in theory, but a bridge to competitive returns.

2. **Durable cost reduction**
   - if that requires limited footprint reduction, then it must remain on the table;
   - if a no-closure package truly closes the gap, then show it.

3. **Implementation certainty**
   - no endless renegotiation,
   - no deal that collapses in strikes or governance vetoes,
   - no cosmetic compromise that leaves the cost base structurally impaired.

## What we would not support
We would not support:
- a deal that preserves every German structure and calls that restructuring,
- a settlement that relies only on temporary concessions while leaving fixed costs untouched,
- or a closure-led approach so aggressive that it destroys trust and execution capacity.

## Our position on the current debate
The expert’s assessment was commercially sensible:  
**test the no-closure scenario seriously, but do not assume it is enough.**  
If the residual gap is still large, then **limited footprint reduction is likely the least bad economic option**.

That is the standard investors should apply. Not ideology, not symbolism — **risk-adjusted value creation**.

## Bottom line
- We support **discipline**, not delay.
- We support **quantified burden-sharing**, not one-sided concessions.
- We support **a hybrid restructuring if it restores competitiveness**.
- We do **not** support a structurally impaired cost base dressed up as compromise.

So: bring the site-by-site numbers. If a no-closure path closes the gap credibly, fine. If it doesn’t, then the company must be honest that some structural footprint change is required.
