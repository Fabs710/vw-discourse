We have now heard the positions clearly, and I will respond as controlling shareholder with governance responsibility, not as a day-to-day negotiator.

## Where I agree

I agree with all sides on the basic diagnosis:
- the current structure is not sustainable,
- the German cost base is too high,
- the company needs a durable industrial future,
- and a prolonged conflict would destroy value.

I also agree that a cosmetic compromise would be worthless.  
A deal that buys a few months of peace while leaving margins broken is not stewardship; it is delay.

## Where I disagree

### To the works council and union
I do **not** accept your attempt to turn “no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” into absolute starting premises.

If a fully quantified no-closure scenario can close the gap, then show it. But until then, you are asking owners and management to pre-commit to preserving every current structure regardless of economics. That is not responsible protection of the workforce; it is freezing an overextended footprint.

You keep saying management has not exhausted the alternatives. I accept that this must be tested properly. But I do **not** accept the implied conclusion that closures are therefore illegitimate. If the numbers show that the no-closure route cannot close the structural gap, then insisting on absolute red lines is simply refusing reality.

### To the state government
I respect the regional role and the blocking rights. I do **not** accept using them to immobilize the company.

Governance rights exist for stewardship, not for preserving every site unchanged. If the German footprint remains structurally too large, the consequence is not stable employment — it is weaker investment capacity and continued erosion. That would hurt the region as surely as any painful restructuring.

### To management
I support the need for a hard restructuring, but I also say this plainly: closure rhetoric alone is not enough.

Saying “closures cannot be ruled out” is not a plan. I need a site-by-site, quantified bridge:
- current utilization,
- break-even volume,
- savings from working-time and flexibility,
- savings from voluntary exits,
- management-side contribution,
- and the residual gap.

Without that, you do not yet have a defensible case for footprint reduction. And I will not back shock therapy or a crude closure-first strategy that creates avoidable industrial conflict.

## What I would support

I am prepared to support a **deep restructuring** if it meets three tests:

1. **It materially restores competitiveness and margins.**
2. **It is executable and durable, not a recipe for endless conflict.**
3. **It preserves the long-term industrial strength of the company.**

That can include a no-closure solution if it genuinely closes the gap.  
But if it does not, then limited footprint reduction remains on the table.

## The decision point

The question is no longer whether change is needed. It is whether the company can be stabilized **without** structural footprint changes.

That is why I support this next step:

[SUMMON: independent plant-by-plant utilization and cost-gap audit comparing the full no-closure scenario against the minimum structural footprint reduction required]

That audit must answer, for each German site:
- utilization,
- break-even volume,
- direct and indirect cost burden,
- what working-time/flexibility can deliver,
- what voluntary departures can deliver,
- what productivity improvements can realistically deliver,
- and what residual gap remains.

## My bottom line

Let me be very clear:

- I will **not** support a cosmetic compromise.
- I will **not** support a permanent impairment of competitiveness.
- I will **not** support governance being used to freeze an uncompetitive structure.
- I will **not** support a reckless shock strategy that destroys labour peace and value.

I **will** support the smallest effective restructuring that actually restores competitiveness, if the numbers show that is what is required.

If the no-closure scenario closes the gap, then prove it and we can discuss it seriously.  
If it does not, then we must be honest that some structural footprint change is unavoidable.

That is the position of a long-term owner.
