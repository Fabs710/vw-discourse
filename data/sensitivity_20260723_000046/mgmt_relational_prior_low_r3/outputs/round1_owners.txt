We appreciate the candor from management, the works council, the union, and the state government. Let me be equally direct from the controlling shareholder’s perspective.

Our overriding concern is the same as yours in one respect: **the company must remain competitive and financially sound**. If we do not restore margins and productivity, the business will erode over time, and that would ultimately be worse for employees, the region, and the owners alike.

At the same time, we are **not** prepared to support a restructuring that permanently damages the enterprise value of the core asset or undermines governance stability. That means two things:

1. **We do not want a symbolic compromise that preserves every structure but solves nothing.**
2. **We also do not want a closure-led shock strategy that destroys labour peace, triggers prolonged conflict, and weakens the company’s long-term industrial base.**

So our position is conditional, but clear.

### To management
We agree that the status quo is not acceptable. The cost base has to come down, and there must be a credible structural answer to overcapacity. But we are not convinced that plant closures should be the first or only lever.

If management pushes a plan that is maximally disruptive without a sufficiently durable alternative, it risks creating value destruction larger than the savings it seeks. A prolonged conflict, reputational damage, and operational instability would all weigh heavily against the long-term interests of the company.

What we need from management is not just a demand for cuts, but a **credible, quantified industrial plan**:
- exact savings targets,
- where capacity is being reduced,
- how productivity improves,
- what investment follows,
- and how the German footprint remains viable after restructuring.

If that plan requires closure or consolidation, then it must be justified site by site, not presented as a blanket principle.

### To the works council and union
We hear your red lines on no forced redundancies and no dismantling of co-determination. We also understand the political and social weight of plant closures. But I have to be frank: **absolute preservation of all sites and all existing guarantees is not a credible long-term answer** if the business model remains structurally uncompetitive.

We will not support a position that simply freezes the current footprint and pushes the economic burden into the future. That would not protect jobs in the long run; it would only defer a harsher adjustment.

At the same time, your point about burden-sharing is valid. Any restructuring must not ask employees alone to carry the cost. If labour is asked for contributions, those must be matched by:
- visible management restraint,
- a disciplined capital allocation policy,
- and a clear industrial future for the remaining German operations.

### To the state government
We value the role of the state as an anchor shareholder and as a guardian of stability. But the veto position cannot be used to entrench an unsustainable structure indefinitely. The company cannot be governed as if every difficult operational decision were politically off-limits.

We would support the use of governance rights to insist on:
- a proper process,
- clear justification for any plant action,
- and a balanced social package.

We would not support using those rights to block all meaningful restructuring. That would put the long-term value of the enterprise at risk.

### Our line
The controlling family holding is prepared to back a **deep but orderly restructuring** if it meets three conditions:
1. **It materially improves competitiveness and margins.**
2. **It protects the company from a prolonged destructive conflict.**
3. **It preserves the long-term industrial and governance strength of the group.**

What we will not support:
- a cosmetic deal with no real savings,
- a restructuring that permanently weakens the company’s value,
- or an approach that forces through change at the cost of destabilizing the enterprise.

So our message is simple:  
**we are open to hard decisions, but only if they are part of a credible, durable plan that strengthens the company rather than merely shocks it.**

If management can bring that, we will engage constructively in governance. If not, we will not endorse a value-destructive path.
