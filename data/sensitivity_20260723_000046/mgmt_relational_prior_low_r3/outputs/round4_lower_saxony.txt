I will be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on one point: **this company needs a real restructuring and a credible industrial future**. The current cost base and utilization profile are not sustainable as they stand. I also agree that a cosmetic compromise would be worthless, and that investment, product allocation, and burden-sharing must be part of any serious settlement.

I also accept the expert’s core framing: the key question is the **residual gap after a genuine no-closure package**. That is the right economic test.

## Where I disagree

### To management
You are still speaking as if closures and compulsory redundancies are the default answer, and that is not acceptable.

Yes, the company has a cost problem. But you have **not yet earned the right** to treat plant closures as necessary. Before any footprint reduction can even be discussed, you must show — site by site — what the company can achieve through:
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- voluntary departures,
- productivity improvements,
- product allocation,
- and management/owner contributions.

You keep saying the German footprint is too large. Fine. Prove the residual gap after non-closure measures. Until then, closure language is pressure, not proof.

And let me be clear: I do **not** accept compulsory redundancies as a bargaining lever. That is not responsible restructuring; it is a threat to the workforce and to regional stability.

### To the controlling family and investors
I agree that a cosmetic deal would be damaging. But you are wrong if you think durability requires keeping closures and forced layoffs permanently on the table from the outset.

A durable settlement is one that preserves industrial capability, skilled jobs, and trust, while restoring competitiveness. If your “smallest effective restructuring” is actually a coded path to shutting plants, then I will not support it.

The burden of proof is not only on labour to accept sacrifice. It is also on capital to show that it is prepared to contribute materially before asking the region to absorb job losses.

### To the works council and union
I support your core red lines:
- no German plant closures as the starting premise,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no erosion of co-determination,
- and no one-sided workforce sacrifice.

I also support the demand for a **quantified no-closure test phase**. That is the correct sequence.

Where I would caution is this: if the audit shows a residual gap that is truly large and recurring, we cannot ignore that forever. But that is a very different proposition from starting with closures or treating them as the first resort.

### To the state government
I agree with your insistence that the state must protect the regional industrial base. But that protection must be operational, not rhetorical.

If management tries to smuggle in closures through terms like “optimization,” “specialization,” or “capacity adjustment,” then your blocking rights and supervisory-board influence must be used. That is exactly why the state is an anchor shareholder. We are not there to watch the regional economy be hollowed out by re-labelling.

At the same time, I also need to see a willingness to engage with the numbers honestly. A blanket political veto without a serious audit would not be sustainable either.

## What I support
I am prepared to support a **time-limited no-closure test phase** with hard conditions:

- no German plant closures during the phase,
- no compulsory redundancies during the phase,
- binding employee contributions that are temporary and proportionate,
- working-time and flexibility measures,
- internal redeployment and retraining,
- transparent management and owner contributions,
- product and investment commitments for German sites,
- and a fixed review date based on a plant-by-plant savings bridge.

That is not concession for concession’s sake. That is disciplined restructuring with a real test of whether the company can stabilize without destroying its regional anchor.

## What I will not support
I will not support:
- a closure-led package from the outset,
- compulsory redundancies as a first-line tool,
- any settlement that weakens co-determination or the state’s effective veto role,
- or a so-called “hybrid” outcome that is really just gradual dismantling of German sites.

## On the expert’s assessment
The expert has said something important and, in my view, correct: the decisive issue is the **residual gap after all realistic non-closure measures**.

That means:
- if the residual gap is modest, a no-closure solution may be viable;
- if the residual gap remains large, then some structural footprint adjustment may become relevant.

But that is **not** the same as saying closures are justified now. It means management must first prove the necessity in a transparent, plant-by-plant way.

So I support the audit, but not as a pretext for a pre-decided closure agenda.

## My bottom line
I will stand for:
- protecting German jobs and plants,
- preserving the company as a regional economic anchor,
- maintaining co-determination and the state’s governance role,
- and allowing a serious, quantified restructuring test.

I will **not** stand for a restructuring that starts by gutting regional employment or by treating closures and forced redundancies as the default solution.
