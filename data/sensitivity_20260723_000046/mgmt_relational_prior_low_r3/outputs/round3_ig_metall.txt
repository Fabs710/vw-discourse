I will be equally direct.

The latest round confirms the core issue: management and investors still want to keep **closure and forced-layoff options** as bargaining leverage, while asking the workforce to accept insecurity first and protection later. That is not a fair restructuring. It is a pressure tactic.

## Where I agree

I agree with the expert and, in part, with management and the controlling family on one point:  
the company needs a **real** industrial future, not a cosmetic fix. The current cost base must come down, utilization must improve, and the business needs binding product and investment commitments.

I also agree that employee contributions can be part of a settlement. We have said that from the start. But those contributions must be:
- temporary,
- negotiated,
- proportionate,
- and matched by binding guarantees.

## Where I disagree

### To management
You still have not answered the central question: **why should workers be the first and main adjustment variable?**

You say the footprint is structurally too expensive. Fine. But that does not justify beginning with closure threats and compulsory redundancy options. Before you talk about footprint reduction, you must show that you have exhausted the alternatives:
- working-time solutions,
- internal flexibility,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- voluntary instruments,
- productivity measures,
- and a serious management and owner contribution.

If you have not done that, then closure rhetoric is not necessity. It is leverage.

And let me be very clear: a “no-closure test phase” is only acceptable if it is a **real test**, not a trap. That means:
- no plant closures during the test,
- no compulsory redundancies during the test,
- full transparency on the savings bridge,
- and a binding commitment that if employees contribute, management and owners also contribute materially.

What I will not accept is this: workers give up protections now, and in return get only a promise that closures will be “reassessed” later.

### To the controlling family and investors
You are right that a cosmetic compromise would be useless. I agree.  
But I reject your implication that the only credible outcome is one that keeps closures permanently on the table.

A durable deal is not one that maximizes short-term financial engineering. It is one that:
- preserves industrial capability,
- keeps skilled workers,
- avoids destructive conflict,
- and restores competitiveness through shared sacrifice.

If your definition of “durable” starts with plant closures and job cuts, then you are not describing balance. You are describing a transfer of the crisis onto labour.

### To the state government
Your support for protecting the regional industrial base is welcome, but it must be operational. If management tries to dress up closures or forced redundancies as “specialization,” “optimization,” or “site adjustment,” then your blocking rights must not remain theoretical.

I want a clear state position:  
**no German plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, and no backdoor dismantling of job security.**

If you are serious about being an anchor shareholder, that is the line you must defend in the supervisory board.

## What we are prepared to discuss
We remain prepared to discuss a negotiated package that includes:
- temporary employee contributions,
- working-time flexibility,
- internal capacity adjustments,
- voluntary departures only,
- retraining and redeployment,
- investment and product commitments,
- and a binding site/job guarantee through 2030.

That is the basis of a serious agreement.

## What we are not prepared to discuss
We are not prepared to discuss:
- closure-led restructuring,
- compulsory redundancies,
- hidden closure logic disguised as “limited footprint reduction,”
- or any package that weakens co-determination or dismantles the collective wage framework.

## On the expert’s assessment
The expert has not justified closures. The expert has said exactly what we have been saying:
- a no-closure scenario must be tested seriously,
- it can work if the gap is manageable and if the company gets real concessions and utilization gains,
- and if it does not work, then the numbers must be shown transparently before any structural move is claimed.

That does **not** give management a blank cheque to restructure by fear. It gives them a burden of proof.

## Our position
We will support a deal only if it starts from these principles:
1. **No German plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Preserve co-determination**
4. **Preserve the collective wage framework**
5. **Employee contributions only in exchange for binding guarantees and fair burden-sharing**

## What must happen next
If management wants a serious negotiation, then put the **full no-closure scenario** on the table with numbers:
- exact savings gap,
- savings from working time and flexibility,
- savings from voluntary departures,
- management and owner contribution,
- product allocation,
- investment commitments,
- and the utilization plan for each German site.

If you can show that the company can stabilize without closures or forced layoffs, then we have a basis for agreement.

If you cannot, then say so openly — but do not expect labour to pre-approve dismantling its own protections.

## Bottom line
We are not here to ratify an imposed restructuring. We are here to defend jobs, wages, and industrial stability while allowing for a fair contribution from the workforce.

If management and owners want a durable settlement, they must stop treating worker sacrifice as the default solution and start sharing the burden in a credible way.

Otherwise, the conflict will escalate.
