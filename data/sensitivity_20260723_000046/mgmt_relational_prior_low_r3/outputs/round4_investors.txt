I’ll be blunt: **capital markets are not interested in preserving every legacy site for its own sake. We are interested in a credible, durable return to competitive margins.** If the current German footprint is structurally too expensive, then the company must fix that — decisively.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family holding, and the expert on the core economic diagnosis:
- the current cost base is too high,
- utilization appears too weak,
- and a cosmetic compromise would be value-destructive.

I also agree with the works council, union, and state on one important point:
- **a reckless shock strategy would destroy value** if it triggers prolonged strikes, governance deadlock, or execution failure.

So I am not backing “closures at any cost.”  
I am backing **whatever closes the cost gap with the lowest execution risk**.

## Where I disagree

### To the employee side
Your position still reads like this: **no closures, no compulsory redundancies, and then trust that the numbers will work out later**. From an investor perspective, that is not yet a credible path to competitive margins.

You keep saying management has not exhausted the alternatives. Fine — then quantify the alternatives. But do **not** assume that a no-closure outcome is automatically sufficient just because it is socially preferable.

If the residual gap after all non-closure measures is still material, then protecting every site simply preserves the problem and erodes value further.

### To management
I agree with your diagnosis more than your tone, but I still want more discipline from you.

“Closures cannot be ruled out” is not enough. It needs to be translated into a **site-by-site savings bridge**:
- current utilization,
- break-even volume,
- direct and indirect site cost,
- savings from working-time changes,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- productivity gains,
- management/owner contribution,
- product and investment commitments,
- and the residual gap.

Without that, markets will see uncertainty, not strategy.

### To the state and family holding
I understand the instinct to protect industrial peace and regional stability. But if governance rights are used to block all meaningful structural change, then investors will conclude that the company is uninvestable at its current cost structure.

That would punish the share price and capital access for years.  
A veto that preserves an uncompetitive structure is not stewardship; it is slow decline.

## What would move capital markets
We would support a package only if it delivers three things:

1. **Credible margin recovery**
   - not just promised savings, but a real bridge to competitive returns.

2. **Durable cost reduction**
   - if that requires limited footprint reduction, keep it on the table;
   - if a no-closure package truly closes the gap, show it.

3. **Implementation certainty**
   - no endless renegotiation,
   - no deal that collapses in strikes,
   - no cosmetic compromise that leaves the cost base structurally impaired.

## What we would not support
We would not support:
- a settlement that preserves every site and calls that restructuring,
- a deal that relies only on temporary concessions while fixed costs remain untouched,
- or a closure-led shock strategy so aggressive that it creates avoidable industrial conflict.

## My position on the current deadlock
The expert’s assessment was commercially sensible:  
**test the no-closure scenario seriously, but do not assume it is enough.**  
If the residual gap remains large and recurring after realistic non-closure measures, then **some structural footprint reduction is likely the least bad economic option**.

That is the standard capital markets will use.  
Not ideology. Not symbolism. **Risk-adjusted value creation.**

## Bottom line
- I support **discipline**, not delay.
- I support **quantified burden-sharing**, not one-sided sacrifice.
- I support **a hybrid restructuring if it restores competitiveness**.
- I do **not** support a structurally impaired cost base dressed up as compromise.

So: bring the site-by-site numbers. If a no-closure path closes the gap credibly, fine. If it doesn’t, then the company must be honest that some structural footprint change is required.
