I will respond clearly and without ambiguity.

## To management
I agree with one thing: the company needs a real industrial future, not slogans.

I do **not** agree that this requires plant closures or compulsory redundancies.

You still have not shown that you have genuinely exhausted the alternatives. You keep repeating “structural overcapacity” as if saying it often enough makes closures necessary. It does not. If you want to argue necessity, then put the full bridge on the table:

- site-by-site utilization
- break-even volumes
- direct and indirect site costs
- what working-time measures can save
- what voluntary instruments can save
- what redeployment and retraining can save
- what management and owners will contribute
- what product and investment commitments are secured

Until then, closure talk is pressure, not proof.

And I am rejecting the idea that compulsory redundancies must remain available as a bargaining tool. That is not a “reality check.” It is a threat against the workforce.

## To investors and the controlling family
I agree that a cosmetic compromise would be worthless.

I do **not** accept your conclusion that a durable solution must keep closures and forced layoffs on the table.

A deal that destroys trust, triggers strike action, and tears up the historic job-security framework is not durable. It is industrial self-harm dressed up as discipline.

If you want durability, then back a solution that preserves all German plants and all jobs from forced layoff, with real burden-sharing from capital and management. Anything else is asking labour to absorb the crisis alone.

## To the state government
I agree with your basic concern for the region. But that concern has to be translated into action, not statements.

If management tries to smuggle in closures by another name — “specialization,” “optimization,” “consolidation,” “capacity adjustment” — then your blocking rights must be used. Otherwise the state is not protecting the regional industrial base; it is watching it be dismantled step by step.

## To the union
We are aligned on the essentials:
- no German plant closures
- no compulsory redundancies
- no weakening of co-determination
- no one-sided workforce sacrifice

I also agree that temporary employee contributions can only be discussed if they are matched by binding guarantees. But let me be very clear: temporary contributions are only acceptable inside a framework that explicitly rules out closures and forced layoffs.

If the framework is “give concessions now and we’ll see later whether jobs survive,” then there is no agreement.

## To the expert
Your audit logic is exactly why I reject management’s rush to structural cuts.

You have said:
- the decisive question is the residual gap after non-closure measures,
- a no-closure path is credible if that gap is modest,
- and only if the gap remains large does footprint reduction become likely.

That means management has **not** earned the right to demand closures today. It means the company must first prove, with site-level numbers, that a no-closure solution cannot work.

So I support the audit — but not as a pretext for pre-decided closure. I support it as the burden of proof management must satisfy before it can even talk about structural cuts.

## My position in this round
I will repeat the non-negotiables:

1. **All German plants remain open**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Co-determination remains fully intact**
4. **The historic job-security framework is preserved**
5. **Employee contributions are temporary, proportionate, and matched by binding guarantees**

## What I am prepared to discuss
I am prepared to discuss:
- working-time flexibility
- internal redeployment
- retraining
- voluntary departures only
- productivity improvements
- negotiated employee contributions
- investment and product commitments

But only if the framework is:
- no closures
- no forced layoffs
- no backdoor dismantling of site security

## What I am not prepared to discuss
I am not prepared to discuss:
- “conditional” no-closure that becomes a trap
- “smallest effective restructuring” if that means one plant shut first
- compulsory redundancies as a supposedly unavoidable tool
- governance changes that weaken the workforce’s veto and co-determination role

## What needs to happen next
If management and capital want a serious settlement, then they must produce the audit and the savings bridge — site by site, in black and white.

[SUMMON: independent plant-by-plant savings bridge and utilization audit showing whether a no-closure, no-forced-layoff solution can close the gap through 2030]

That audit must answer:
- what each German site costs
- what each site produces
- what can be saved without closures
- what capital and management contribute
- what product allocation is guaranteed
- what investment follows
- and what the residual gap is

If the residual gap is truly bridgeable, then we have a basis for a no-closure settlement.

If management still insists on closures and forced redundancies before that is shown, then it is not negotiating in good faith. It is trying to impose a dismantling.

And I will resist that with everything the works council has.
