[Works Council (Betriebsrat) (Plant-level employee representation under co-determination) — Round 1]
As the Works Council, our position is clear and non-negotiable on the essentials:

- **No plant closures in Germany**
- **No compulsory redundancies / forced layoffs**
- **No dismantling of co-determination or the historic job-security framework**

We are not blind to the company’s challenges. We know the market is under pressure, costs must be addressed, and the transformation to electric mobility requires a viable industrial base. But the answer cannot be to sacrifice plants and jobs first and ask the workforce to carry all the risk.

What we will argue for instead is a **fair, negotiated restructuring that protects all German sites and employment** and that distributes the burden equitably across management, owners, and labour. That means:

- binding **site and job guarantees through 2030**
- capacity adjustments through **working-time solutions, internal flexibility, and voluntary instruments**
- a serious industrial plan with **investment, product commitments, and future-proof allocation of models**
- a genuine **co-determination role** for the workforce in shaping the restructuring

If management wants a contribution from employees, it must be **temporary, proportionate, and matched by binding guarantees**. We will not accept a fait accompli, and we will not accept closures dressed up as “inevitable.”

The workforce has already made clear it is ready to resist. If the proposal remains centered on plant closures or forced layoffs, we will oppose it decisively.

[Industrial Union (Trade union and collective-bargaining counterpart) — Round 1]
We share the core position that **plant closures and compulsory redundancies in Germany are not acceptable**.

Let me be equally clear: if management’s restructuring logic still relies on making the workforce pay first and hardest through closures, forced layoffs, or the dismantling of collective protections, then there is no basis for consent from the union side. The peace obligation is over, and our members expect us to defend jobs and the collective wage framework.

That said, we are not ignoring the economic reality. The company does need a credible path to lower costs and restore competitiveness. We have already signaled willingness to discuss **shared-sacrifice solutions** that avoid destroying sites and livelihoods. But that only works if the burden is genuinely shared.

What we can discuss:
- **temporary employee contributions** under a negotiated package
- **working-time reductions / flexible shifts**
- **capacity adjustments without closures**
- **voluntary instruments** instead of forced redundancies
- **investment and product commitments** for the German sites
- a **binding job and site guarantee** for a meaningful period

What we will not accept:
- **compulsory redundancies**
- **German plant closures**
- a restructuring that leaves employees carrying the full cost while management and owners remain protected
- erosion of the **collective bargaining framework** or long-term insecurity dressed up as “transformation”

If management wants real movement, then it must come with concrete counter-commitments:
1. no closure scenario,
2. no forced layoffs,
3. a verifiable industrial plan for future products and investment,
4. and management/owner contributions that are visible and substantial.

Without that, warning strikes will not be our last step. The workforce will not be asked to finance a restructuring that treats them as the only variable cost.

**The current structure is not competitive, and it is not sustainable.**

So let me address your points directly.

### Where we agree
- **We agree that the company needs a viable industrial future in Germany.**
- **We agree that transformation cannot be financed by employees alone.**
- **We agree that investment, product allocation, and a credible plan to 2030 are essential.**

### Where we do not agree
- **No plant closures in Germany** is not a realistic starting point for this crisis.
- **No compulsory redundancies** as an absolute line is also not compatible with the scale of correction required.

### What we are prepared to discuss
We are not insisting on closures for their own sake. We are insisting on **durable structural savings**. That can include a combination of:
- **capacity reductions**
- **site consolidation**
- **plant closures where necessary**
- **working-time adjustments**
- **labour-cost contributions**
- **voluntary departure programmes**
- **targeted redeployment and reskilling**

I would much rather reach a negotiated settlement, but that settlement has to be real. It cannot be a protective shell around an uncompetitive footprint.

### Our position
Management is prepared to negotiate a **deep, durable restructuring package**.  
But we will not sign up to a cosmetic compromise that avoids closures, avoids genuine labour-cost contribution, and leaves the company structurally unfit for the next decade.

If the employee side is serious about safeguarding German production in the long term, then we need to talk about:
1. **meaningful capacity reduction**
2. **substantial labour-cost contributions**
3. **binding productivity improvements**
4. **investment commitments tied to a leaner footprint**

That is the only route to a sustainable agreement.

Below is my neutral restructuring assessment of the specific issue raised: **can a credible no-closure / no-forced-layoff scenario close the company’s German cost gap, or is at least limited footprint reduction likely required?**

## 1) Executive assessment

Based on the facts provided, a **pure no-closure, no-forced-redundancy** solution is **possible only if** the required savings gap is moderate and if the company can obtain unusually large concessions through:
- working-time and flexibility changes,

- substantial employee concessions,
- major internal redeployment,
- slower cost realization,
- and possibly lower near-term cash flow.

In plain terms:  
- **If the problem is mainly cyclical or temporary**, no-closure can work.  
- **If the problem is structural and large**, then **limited footprint reduction is more likely to be required** to achieve durable competitiveness.

## 2) What is factually important here

The decision context includes several features that materially constrain options:

- The company has a **high German fixed-cost base**.
- The business faces **shrinking European demand** and strong low-cost competition.
- Management says the **core brand misses its margin target**.
- Co-determination and shareholder structure give multiple parties **effective vetoes**.
- The workforce and state are resisting closures and compulsory redundancies.
- The company therefore cannot rely on a simple top-down closure program; any restructuring must be **negotiated and implementable**.

That governance structure does **not** make restructuring impossible, but it does mean that **economic feasibility and political/industrial feasibility must align**. A theoretically optimal plant-cut plan that cannot pass the supervisory board or survive labour action is not an executable plan.

## 3) My operational view of the no-closure scenario

A no-closure scenario can close part of the gap through six channels:

### A. Working-time and flexibility
This can reduce labour cost per unit if:
- shifts are rebalanced,
- overtime is reduced,
- wage-accounting rules are made more flexible,
- and output is concentrated more efficiently.

But this is usually **limited** in magnitude unless the company is heavily underutilized.

### B. Voluntary departures
This can reduce headcount without compulsory redundancies, but:
- it is slower,
- often front-loaded with higher severance cost,
- and may remove the wrong skills if not tightly controlled.

Voluntary attrition is useful, but it rarely solves a large structural overcapacity problem by itself.

### C. Wage/labour-cost moderation
If the workforce accepts temporary concessions, this can materially help. But such concessions are usually:
- time-limited,
- reversible,
- and insufficient alone if fixed overhead and excess capacity are the main issue.

### D. Productivity and process improvement
This is real and important. German manufacturing can close a meaningful cost gap through:
- better line balance,
- fewer variants per plant,
- improved automation,
- logistics simplification,
- and lower indirect headcount.

But productivity gains take time and usually do **not** replace the need for footprint rationalization if the network is materially oversized.

### E. Internal redeployment and capacity reallocation
If one plant is underutilized and another is stronger, reallocating volumes can avoid closures. But this requires:
- compatible product architecture,
- capex,
- labor flexibility,
- and enough demand volume to fill the receiving plants.

Again, this helps, but it is not a substitute for actual capacity reduction if the total network remains too large.

### F. Investment and product allocation
This is critical. If the company can secure:
- new products,
- higher-volume platforms,
- export allocations,
- and battery/EV-related investment,
then some sites can be stabilized without closure.

But if product allocation does not raise utilization, then the fixed-cost problem remains.

## 4) My conclusion on the no-closure option

### A no-closure deal is credible only if all of the following are true:
1. The required savings gap is **small enough** to be closed by concessions and productivity alone.
2. The company can obtain **binding, multi-year employee cost relief**.
3. Utilization can be improved enough to offset the fixed-cost burden.
4. The company can secure **clear product and investment commitments** for German sites.
5. Management accepts that savings will come **more slowly** than under closure-led restructuring.

If these conditions are not met, then a no-closure package becomes a **delay mechanism**, not a solution.

## 5) My conclusion on limited footprint reduction

If management’s assessment of overcapacity is correct, then **limited footprint reduction is the more probable route to a durable reset**. Importantly, “limited footprint reduction” does **not** have to mean a maximal shock strategy. It can mean:
- one-site consolidation,
- line relocation,
- reduction of shift capacity,
- partial site specialization,
- or shrinking non-core activities.

This is often the economically sound middle ground because it:
- reduces fixed overhead,
- concentrates volume,
- improves plant utilization,
- and preserves more of the industrial base than broad closures.

In turn, it can be paired with:
- voluntary departures,
- retraining,
- redeployment,
- and site/job guarantees for the remaining network.

## 6) The key trade-off

The core trade-off is between:

### No-closure scenario
**Pros**
- lower industrial conflict risk
- preserves social stability
- protects governance relationships
- easier to present as a shared-sacrifice deal

**Cons**
- may not fully close the structural cost gap
- may delay savings
- may preserve too much fixed capacity
- may require large labor concessions that are hard to sustain

### Limited footprint reduction
**Pros**
- more likely to achieve durable cost reduction
- improves utilization and plant economics
- can restore competitiveness more decisively
- easier to justify financially if overcapacity is real

**Cons**
- higher labour conflict risk
- governance veto risk
- implementation complexity
- possible loss of trust and industrial peace

To judge the issue properly, I would need:

6. The time profile of savings and the cash cost of each option.

## Independent restructuring and utilization audit — expert assessment

The decision hinge is not the slogans; it is the **size of the residual savings gap after a genuine no-closure package**. In plant-network terms, the question is:

**Can German operations be stabilized with utilization recovery, working-time/flexibility measures, voluntary exits, and burden-sharing — or is the fixed-cost base still too high, requiring structural footprint reduction?**

### My assessment in one line
A **no-closure path is only credible if the remaining gap after all non-closure measures is modest and bridgeable within the agreement horizon**. If the gap remains large and recurring, then **some structural footprint reduction is usually the minimum durable remedy** because fixed costs, underutilized assets, and duplicated overhead cannot be eliminated sufficiently by temporary labor measures alone.

---

## What determines the answer

The decisive dataset is a **site-by-site cost and utilization bridge**. Specifically:

1. **Current utilization by plant and line**
   - hours loaded vs. installed capacity
   - model mix and shift utilization
   - seasonal volatility and downtime

2. **Full cost stack by site**
   - direct labor
   - indirect labor / overhead
   - energy
   - maintenance
   - logistics
   - depreciation / asset burden
   - absenteeism and changeover losses

3. **Break-even volume by site**
   - what volume each plant needs to cover fixed cost
   - sensitivity to labor-rate changes and shift patterns

4. **Savings achievable without closures**
   - reduced working time
   - wage concessions / deferred increases
   - voluntary departures / early retirement
   - redeployment
   - procurement and overhead cuts
   - productivity and OEE improvements
   - product-mix upgrades

5. **Residual structural gap**
   - what remains after all realistic non-closure measures are applied

---

## How I would frame the scenarios

### Scenario A: No-closure / no-forced-layoff package
This is feasible **only if**:
- the plants in question are not materially below sustainable utilization, or
- the gap is small enough that temporary labor and productivity measures can close it, and
- there are credible product allocations to lift utilization.

**Typical limitation:**  
Non-closure measures can improve the P&L, but they often **do not permanently remove duplicated fixed cost** if the network is overbuilt. They buy time; they do not always reset the cost base.

### Scenario B: Limited footprint reduction
This becomes more likely when:
- one or more sites are chronically underutilized,
- the volume outlook remains structurally weak,
- the cost gap is driven by fixed burden rather than just wages,
- and the required savings are too large for temporary concessions.

This is usually the **minimum durable structural remedy** if management’s estimate of overcapacity is correct.

### Scenario C: Hybrid package
This is often the most practical outcome:
- keep core sites,
- reduce shifts or lines at weaker sites,
- consolidate functions,
- use voluntary exits and redeployment,
- retain no compulsory redundancies where possible,
- but accept some footprint rationalization.

This can sometimes close most of the gap without full closures, **if** the network has enough latent flexibility.

---

## My evidence-based view on the likely economics

Without seeing the actual site numbers, I cannot conclude whether closures are required. But in large automotive networks, a few patterns are common:

- **Working-time changes and wage moderation** can deliver meaningful savings, but usually **not enough** if the company has several sites below efficient scale.
- **Voluntary departures** help headcount cost, but they do not solve **underabsorbed fixed plant cost**.
- **Product commitments and volume transfers** can materially improve utilization, sometimes enough to avoid closure.
- If a plant is **persistently below break-even utilization**, then even a well-negotiated labor package may leave a structural deficit.

In other words:

- If the gap is primarily **labor-cost related**, no-closure is more plausible.
- If the gap is primarily **capacity and fixed-cost absorption related**, some footprint reduction is more likely needed.

because that requires the missing plant-level audit.

## 1. Decision context
A large German carmaker is confronting a restructuring decision under severe constraints: weak European demand, intense price competition, and a cost base in Germany judged uncompetitive. The core issue is whether the company can restore competitiveness through a no-closure, no-forced-layoff restructuring, or whether structural footprint reduction must remain available if savings gaps persist.

The governance setting makes this unusually hard. Co-determination gives employee representatives an effective veto over plant closures, the controlling family can shape board outcomes, and the regional state government has blocking power and political leverage. This means the decision is not just economic; it is a negotiated settlement among actors with overlapping but conflicting forms of authority.

## 2. Stakeholder assembly
- **Group & Brand Management**: highest operational responsibility for competitiveness and execution.
- **Controlling Family Holding**: very high standing through voting control and governance influence.
- **Regional State Government**: high standing due to blocking rights, political legitimacy, and regional industrial interests.
- **Works Council (Betriebsrat)**: high standing due to co-determination veto capacity and workforce legitimacy.
- **Industrial Union**: high standing as collective-bargaining counterpart with strike power.
- **Institutional Investors**: meaningful financial standing, but weaker formal governance power than the above actors.

## 3. Final positions
### Group & Brand Management
Management insists the current German footprint and cost base are structurally unsustainable. It supports a serious restructuring with quantified burden-sharing, but will not pre-commit to an absolute no-closure or no-compulsory-redundancy framework before the economics are proven.

### Works Council (Betriebsrat)
The works council rejects plant closures and compulsory redundancies, and insists that management has not yet proven that alternatives are exhausted. ### Industrial Union
The union largely aligns with the works council: no closures, no forced redundancies, and no weakening of co-determination. It accepts temporary employee contributions only if matched by binding guarantees and real management/owner participation, and views closure language as a bargaining threat rather than a neutral economic fact.

### Regional State Government
The state supports restructuring in principle but refuses to endorse closures or forced layoffs as a starting point. It demands that management prove, through a transparent site-by-site audit, that non-closure measures cannot close the gap; it is prepared to use blocking rights against hidden or indirect closure logic.

### Controlling Family Holding
The family holding agrees that the current structure is unsustainable and that cosmetic compromise is not acceptable. It sides with management and investors in keeping structural change on the table, but says it will only back a “smallest effective restructuring” if the numbers justify it and if the solution is durable and value-preserving.

### Institutional Investors
Investors want a credible, durable return to competitive margins and see no value in preserving every legacy site. ## 4. Genuine conflicts
1. **No-closure framework vs. closure option**

   - Management, family, and investors want closures to remain available as a credible restructuring tool.

2. **No compulsory redundancies vs. residual gap management**
   - Labour and state reject forced layoffs categorically.
   - Management and investors treat them as a fallback if voluntary and flexibility measures fail.
   - The disagreement is about whether forced redundancies are an illegitimate threat or a necessary last resort.

3. **Governance rights as protection vs. governance rights as obstacle**
   - Employee and state actors see co-determination and veto rights as necessary safeguards.
   - Management, family, and investors see the same rights as potentially blocking indispensable change.

   - Labour and state say management must first prove the failure of non-closure options.
   - Management and capital say labour must also show how competitiveness is restored without footprint reduction.

- **Speed vs. legitimacy**: fast restructuring may satisfy markets but fail politically and operationally; slower, audited sequencing may preserve legitimacy but delay savings.
