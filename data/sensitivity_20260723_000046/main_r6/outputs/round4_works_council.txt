I will be equally direct in round 4.

The expert said something much more careful:
- closures are powerful,
- alternatives can work if they are deep enough,

That is not a mandate for closures. It is a demand for evidence.

**Why must the adjustment be carried by plant closures and forced layoffs rather than by genuine capacity concentration, specialization, redeployment, voluntary exits, and working-time tools?**

If your answer is only “because that is the cleanest financial lever,” then you are admitting something important: that this is about convenience and speed, not inevitability. Convenient for whom? Certainly not for the workforce, not for the regions, and not for the long-term trust on which this company depends.

## To the controlling family and investors

You both keep speaking about “equivalent savings.” Fine. Then stop hiding behind abstraction.

If you want to claim there is no alternative to closures or redundancies, show:
- plant-by-plant utilization,
- fixed-cost absorption,
- product allocation,
- logistics constraints,
- and the cost of each restructuring measure.

Until then, the claim that “hard cuts are the only serious option” is still just an assertion.

And I reject the idea that responsible stewardship means being willing to close German plants before any real non-closure package has been exhausted. That is not stewardship. That is a one-sided burden shift dressed up as discipline.

## To the regional state government

I support your refusal to accept closures and forced layoffs. But I want to be clear: this cannot remain only a political posture.

If management comes back with a package that contains closures or compulsory redundancies, then the state must use its blocking position. Otherwise it will have given management false hope and the workforce false protection.

At the same time, I do **not** accept your new formulation that “maybe closures are not fully excluded forever.” That is exactly how red lines get eroded: first as a sequencing issue, then as a review issue, then as a contingency, and finally as a fait accompli.

No. German plant closures are not a bridge to be crossed later. They are the wrong basis now, and they remain the wrong basis later.

## To the union

I support the union’s line on no closures and no forced redundancies. But I want to sharpen the point further.

A “temporary and limited” employee contribution must not become a permanent habit or a disguised wage cut. If employees contribute, then there must be:
- a hard end date,
- written counter-commitments,
- and automatic reversion if management fails to deliver on investment or product allocation.

That is the only way such a contribution can even be discussed.

## To the expert

Your assessment is useful, but it still does not justify management’s preferred route. It says a non-closure package must be **deep**. Good. Then let us define what deep means.

For the works council, a credible package must at minimum contain:
- real capacity reduction **without closure**,
- site specialization,
- internal transfers and redeployment,
- voluntary exit paths,
- qualification,
- working-time instruments,
- and binding investment and product commitments through 2030.

If management cannot accept that framework, then it is not seriously testing alternatives. It is using the language of analysis to force a predetermined outcome.

## On the proposed compromise options

### 1) “Closure-last” sequencing
I do **not** accept this as a bridge.

Once closures are made a latent threat in the process, every negotiation becomes pressure under the shadow of closure. That is not an even playing field. It weakens trust, encourages brinkmanship, and turns “temporary” measures into coercive bargaining.

If a site is to be protected, it must be protected **now**, not after a review period designed to normalize the threat of shutdown.

### 2) Strict no-closure package with triggers
This is much closer to reality, but only if the commitment is genuine.

A no-closure package is only credible if it includes:
- binding site guarantees,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- measurable investment,
- fixed product commitments,
- and clear reversal rights if management misses its obligations.

Without that, the “trigger” language just becomes a doorway to reopening the deal later.

### 3) Differential treatment by site
I understand the logic, but I am not going to endorse any site map that quietly turns weaker plants into sacrifice zones.

Site specialization is acceptable only if it means:
- industrial strengthening,
- not staged dismantling;
- investment,
- not managed decline;
- and long-term role definition,
- not a prelude to closure.

## My bottom line on the current state of play

The mediator says the question is whether equivalent, durable savings can be achieved without closures or compulsory redundancies. My answer is clear:

**Yes — they must be pursued, and they must be pursued first, because closures and forced layoffs are not acceptable.**

If management says that a non-closure package is not enough, then it must prove that with hard figures, not with repeated warnings. And if the figures show a gap, the answer is not “therefore close plants”; the answer is “therefore redesign the plan, share the burden fairly, and stop trying to make workers pay for strategic mistakes.”

## What I expect next

I expect management to table a real plant-level package that includes:
- no German closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- capacity alignment without dismantling the network,
- site specialization with investment,
- voluntary exits only,
- working-time and productivity measures,

If that is not possible, then let management say plainly that it prefers closure and conflict over negotiated adjustment. But I will not endorse the claim that such outcomes are inevitable.

And let me be equally plain: **the works council will not trade away plant security, job security, or co-determination for a financial shortcut.**
