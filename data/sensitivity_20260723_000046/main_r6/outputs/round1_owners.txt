I appreciate the clarity from all sides. Let me be equally clear from the perspective of the controlling shareholders acting through governance.

We share the core diagnosis from management: **the company needs a durable restoration of competitiveness and margins**. If we do nothing substantive, we will erode value, weaken the brand, and eventually put even more jobs at risk. That would be a failure of stewardship.

At the same time, I do **not** support a reckless solution that creates prolonged conflict, damages the brand, or permanently impairs the enterprise. Nor do I support any approach that undermines the company’s governance stability or ignores the hard reality of co-determination and the state’s blocking position. A solution that cannot survive board and political reality is not a solution.

Where I agree:
- The status quo is not acceptable.
- Structural overcapacity and an uncompetitive cost base must be addressed.
- A temporary, negotiated employee contribution may be part of the answer.
- Investment and future product commitments matter; cuts without an industrial plan are value-destructive.

Where I disagree with the employee side:
- A blanket “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” line is too absolute to be a credible starting point if the company truly has structural overcapacity.
- If every meaningful structural option is taken off the table in advance, then we are left with symbolism instead of competitiveness.
- That said, I do hear the warning: forced confrontation would be expensive, destabilising, and likely self-defeating.

Where I disagree with management:
- A package that is seen as purely imposed from above will likely fail in governance and in the plant.
- You cannot treat labour peace as an afterthought.
- If the restructuring is presented as nothing but closure and job destruction, it may achieve a headline cost reduction while destroying long-term value through strikes, litigation, political backlash, and reputational damage.

Where I agree with the state:
- The company’s role as an industrial anchor matters.
- Co-determination and the special governance structure are not obstacles to be bypassed; they are part of the system we must work within.
- A settlement must preserve the company’s long-term base in Germany, not hollow it out.

My bottom line is this:

1. **We support a decisive restructuring.**
2. **We do not support value-destructive instability.**
3. **We are open to a package that includes structural cost and capacity reduction, but it must be economically real and governance-realistic.**
4. **We want alternatives to blunt closures explored seriously first** — capacity reductions, site specialization, voluntary exits, working-time instruments, and negotiated labour-cost measures.
5. **If closures or deeper measures are truly unavoidable, they must be justified by a credible long-term industrial logic and accompanied by a durable settlement that protects future value.**

So I will not endorse a purely defensive stance that preserves everything unchanged. But I will also not endorse management’s strategy if it ignores the need for labour peace and board-level viability.

The test for me is simple: **does the final package restore competitiveness in a way that can actually be implemented, sustained, and governed without permanently damaging the enterprise?** If yes, I am open. If no, I will not support it.
