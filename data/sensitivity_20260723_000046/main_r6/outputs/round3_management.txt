I’ll be direct.

The expert has confirmed what management has said from the start: **the company needs durable structural adjustment, and alternatives have to be tested against scale, durability, and implementability — not against hopes and slogans.** That is the correct lens.

## Where I agree

I agree with the expert, and partially with the works council, union, state, and owners on one point: **a cosmetic settlement is worthless**. A deal that merely defers the problem, keeps the cost base intact, and buys short-term peace would fail the company and fail the workforce in the medium term.

I also agree that **closures are not automatically the only answer**. I have never claimed that they are desirable for their own sake. What I have said is that they remain on the table because **we need a package that actually closes the structural gap**.

## Where I disagree

### To the works council
You keep repeating “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” as if that alone defines a solution.

It does not.

The expert has just said the crucial thing: the issue is **whether alternatives are deep enough**. So far, you have listed instruments, not shown that they produce the required savings at plant level, with the necessary durability. Capacity reduction without closure can be part of a solution — but only if it is real capacity reduction, not a repackaging of the status quo.

I reject the claim that a closure is somehow proof of management failure. In a structurally overbuilt network, refusing to right-size the footprint can itself be the failure. The German base is not protected by pretending the economics do not exist.

### To the union
You are right that voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time measures, and productivity improvements are real tools. But the expert also said what matters: **these tools often do not fully eliminate fixed cost if the network remains too large and too complex.**

That is exactly the problem here.

A temporary employee contribution is not enough if the underlying plant and capacity structure remains uncompetitive. Temporary concessions are useful only if they lead to permanent improvement. I will not accept a settlement where employees give something for two years and the company is still paying the same structural cost in 2028.

### To the state government
You say you cannot support closures or compulsory redundancies. I hear that. But then you also need to be honest about the consequence: if you rule out the strongest adjustment levers, you are effectively asking management to achieve a large fixed-cost reset with one hand tied behind its back.

That may be politically understandable. It is not economically sufficient by itself.

If the state wants to avoid closures, then it must help produce a package that is **equivalent in scale**. Not just “socially balanced.” Equivalent in savings. Equivalent in durability. Equivalent in competitiveness impact.

### To the controlling family and investors
You are right to insist that the savings must be real. I agree.

But I also want to push back on any assumption that the only credible way to prove seriousness is by announcing closures first. The expert is right: the test is plant-by-plant, cost-center-by-cost-center, and measure-by-measure. That is what management is prepared to do.

What I will not accept is a process that starts with a blanket veto against the most effective instruments and then demands that management somehow produce the same outcome anyway.

That is not stewardship. That is wishful thinking with governance cover.

## What the expert has made clear
The expert’s assessment strengthens the management case in three ways:

1. **Closures are a powerful structural lever.**
   - Yes, they are disruptive.
   - But they remove fixed cost in a way that softer measures often cannot.

2. **Alternatives can work only if they are deep enough.**
   - Capacity concentration, site specialization, and working-time tools may substitute.
   - But only if they actually reduce the footprint and complexity meaningfully.

3. **The decisive question is quantified comparison.**
   - Exactly how much savings?
   - How durable?
   - How implementable?
   - What is the residual cost per vehicle?

That is the right framework. And it is exactly why I am not willing to sign up to any predetermined exclusion of closures or forced reductions.

## My position remains unchanged

Management’s minimum requirement remains:

1. **A substantial, durable reduction in costs and capacity.**
2. **A meaningful labour-cost contribution.**
3. **A package that restores margins and competitiveness through 2030.**
4. **No cosmetic settlement that leaves the current cost base fundamentally intact.**

## What I am prepared to discuss
I remain open to:
- site specialization,
- real capacity concentration,
- working-time adjustments,
- voluntary exit programs,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- productivity improvements,
- and other structural savings,

**if and only if** they are proven to be equivalent in scale and durability.

## What I am not prepared to accept
I am not accepting:
- a blanket “no closures” line as a substitute for economics,
- a blanket “no compulsory redundancies” line if it makes sufficient adjustment impossible,
- or a package that protects every site and every guarantee regardless of cost base.

## Where we go from here
The expert has asked for transparent numbers. I agree. That is the next step.

I want a plant-level, measure-by-measure comparison of:
- closure-led restructuring,
- no-closure capacity reduction,
- and hybrid options,

showing:
- fixed-cost savings,
- implementation risk,
- transition cost,
- and the residual competitiveness position by 2030.

If the employee side wants to rule out closures and compulsory redundancies, then it must prove — not assert — that the alternative delivers comparable savings. Until then, I will not withdraw those options from the table.

That is the reality. And I will not dress it up.
