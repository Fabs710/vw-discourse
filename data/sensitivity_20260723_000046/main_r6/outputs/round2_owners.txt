I will respond plainly from the controlling shareholder’s perspective.

## Where I agree

I agree with management, the investors, and to a lesser extent the state on the core diagnosis: **the current cost base is not sustainable**, and a cosmetic settlement would be value-destructive. The company needs a **durable** restoration of competitiveness and margins, not a delay tactic.

I also agree with the works council and the union on one important point: **a long, destabilising conflict would destroy value**. Strikes, political escalation, and legal deadlock would damage the company, the brand, and ultimately the long-term value of the family holding’s core asset.

So the ownership position is not “cut at any price,” and it is not “status quo at any price.” It is: **real restructuring, but governed, durable, and implementable**.

## Where I disagree

### 1) With the works council and union: absolute vetoes are not credible
A blanket “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” line may be understandable, but it is too rigid to be a serious starting point if the company truly has structural overcapacity.

If every meaningful structural tool is ruled out in advance, then you are not negotiating a solution — you are defending the current footprint regardless of economics. That may be politically satisfying, but it is not stewardship.

That said, I also do **not** think closures and forced redundancies should be treated as the first or only answer. They are serious measures, and they carry heavy implementation risk. But I will not endorse a framework that rules them out categorically before the savings question has been answered.

### 2) With management: do not confuse structural necessity with execution blindness
Management is right that the problem is structural. Where I disagree is in the tone of inevitability around closures and blunt job cuts.

If a package is perceived as imposed, it may technically announce savings while failing in practice because of:
- co-determination constraints,
- political resistance,
- strike risk,
- and reputational damage.

That is not a theoretical risk; it is the core implementation risk. A restructuring that cannot survive governance reality is not value-creating.

So management must come back with a package that is not just economically rational on paper, but **governable**.

### 3) With the state: blocking all hard measures is not a solution
I respect the state’s role and the special governance reality. But if the state’s position becomes “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, regardless of alternatives,” then it risks freezing an uncompetitive structure and pushing the problem into the future.

That would not protect the regional industrial base; it would weaken it over time.

## What would move me

What would move the controlling shareholder is a package that does three things at once:

1. **Delivers real, durable cost and capacity reduction**
2. **Is implementable under co-determination and political reality**
3. **Preserves labour peace and avoids value-destructive instability**

That means I am open to alternative solutions first, but only if they are **equivalent in scale and durability**.

So I would want to see, in concrete terms:
- capacity reductions,
- site specialization,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- productivity measures,
- and a meaningful, time-limited workforce contribution,

paired with:
- investment commitments,
- product allocation,
- and a credible path back to margin improvement through 2030.

## My limit

I will not support an outcome that:
- permanently impairs the enterprise’s value,
- preserves an uncompetitive cost base,
- or turns the restructuring into prolonged conflict.

I also will not support any solution that undermines the company’s governance stability or ignores the special shareholder and co-determination structure. Control and stewardship are not abstract concepts here; they are part of why this company can take a long view.

## To the parties directly

### To the works council and union
Your insistence on job security, co-determination, and site continuity is understandable. But you have not yet shown how the required savings can be achieved with the current footprint and without deeper structural change.

If you want to avoid closures, then bring a package that is not symbolic:
- measurable,
- durable,
- and large enough to actually restore competitiveness.

Temporary sacrifices alone are not enough if the underlying structure remains unchanged.

### To management
I support the objective, but not a simplistic execution. Bring me a package that is economically serious **and** politically survivable. If the plan depends on overreach, it will fail and damage value.

### To the state
Protecting the regional base matters, but so does competitiveness. Help shape a solution that is viable, not merely defensible.

### To investors
You are right that a cosmetic deal is unacceptable. But you should also recognise that a purely maximalist restructuring can be self-defeating if it cannot be implemented.

## Bottom line

The controlling shareholder supports **decisive restructuring**, but only if it is:
- durable,
- implementable,
- and stable.

We are open to alternatives to closures and compulsory redundancies, but only if they deliver **equivalent, lasting savings**. If they do not, then I will not pretend otherwise: the company still needs structural correction.

So the next step is not a general appeal for compromise. It is a concrete package that shows:
- where the cost reduction comes from,
- how much is saved,
- how long it lasts,
- and why it can actually be implemented.

Without that, we are just debating preferences.
