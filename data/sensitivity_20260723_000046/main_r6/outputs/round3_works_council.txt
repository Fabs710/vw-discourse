Let me be very clear in round 3:

The central issue is still not “whether restructuring is needed.” We all know the company needs a durable industrial plan. The issue is **who pays for it, and whether management is trying to force a structural fix by destroying the German plant network and the job-security framework**.

That I will not accept.

## Where I agree

I agree with the basic diagnosis from management, the family, and investors:
- the status quo is not sustainable,
- margins must improve,
- and a cosmetic agreement would only postpone the problem.

I also agree that any settlement has to be:
- implementable,
- stable,
- and consistent with the governance reality of this company.

And yes, I agree that a long strike or a governance deadlock would damage value. That is precisely why management should stop pushing a course that makes conflict inevitable.

## Where I fundamentally disagree

### 1) Closures are not “just another tool”
Management keeps presenting plant closures as if they were simply one option among many. They are not.

For us, German plant closures would mean:
- destruction of jobs,
- destruction of regional industrial capacity,
- a breach of trust with the workforce,
- and a permanent weakening of the company’s German base.

So no, I do **not** accept the claim that closures are automatically legitimate just because they may look tidy in a spreadsheet.

If the company cannot fix its cost problem without closing German plants, then management has not shown a restructuring plan. It has shown an admission of failure.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies are unacceptable
I will say it again plainly: **no forced layoffs**.

Not as a bargaining tactic. Not as a temporary compromise. Not as a “last resort” hidden in the fine print.

If management wants structural adjustment, then it must be achieved through:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time instruments,
- capacity alignment,
- and negotiated contributions.

Anything else is a direct attack on the workforce.

### 3) “Equivalent savings” must be proven, not asserted
The controlling family and investors keep saying that alternatives are only acceptable if they are “equivalent in scale and durability.” Fine. Then prove that closures and forced layoffs are actually necessary.

So far, what I hear is repetition, not evidence.

We have already put alternatives on the table:
- capacity reductions without closures,
- site specialization,
- internal transfer and redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary and limited workforce contributions,
- qualification and productivity improvements,
- and targeted investment commitments.

If management says these are not enough, then show the calculations. Show the plant-by-plant footprint. Show the product allocation. Show the savings model. Do not hide behind generalities and call it strategy.

## To management

Your strongest argument is that a durable solution is needed. On that we agree.

Where you go wrong is assuming that “durable” means “hard cuts first.” It does not.

A solution that survives only because it was imposed against the workforce, against co-determination, and against the state’s blocking position is not durable. It is fragile. It invites strike action, political backlash, implementation failure, and long-term mistrust.

You say you need room to compete. Then give us a plan that protects the German industrial base while creating that room. Do not start from closures and then ask us to prove the alternative.

And let me be equally direct: if management comes back with a package that contains closures or compulsory redundancies, then there is no basis for agreement from our side. That line is crossed.

## To the union

I support your position on no closures and no compulsory redundancies. But I want to sharpen this: temporary workforce contributions must not become the new normal.

If there is any contribution from employees, then it must be:
- strictly time-limited,
- clearly compensated,
- linked to binding guarantees,
- and reversible if management fails to deliver on investment and product commitments.

No open-ended wage moderation. No silent dismantling of hard-won standards.

## To the state government

You have said the right thing: no closures, no forced redundancies, and no erosion of co-determination. That is correct.

But I need to be frank: your role is not just to express concern. Your blocking position must be used decisively if management tries to push through an outcome that damages sites and jobs. If the state allows “temporary” erosion or starts bargaining away plant security, then it will have failed in its duty.

## To the controlling family

You say you want stewardship and long-term value. Then show stewardship.

Long-term value is not protected by closing plants and tearing up job-security commitments. That may reduce costs on paper, but it also creates:
- labor conflict,
- reputational damage,
- political resistance,
- and a lasting break in trust.

A family that thinks in generations should understand that destroying the workforce relationship for a short-term margin fix is not good ownership.

## To institutional investors

You ask how competitiveness is restored by 2030 without leaving the company structurally uncompetitive. That is a fair question.

Here is the answer you keep refusing to take seriously:
- reduce capacity without closures,
- specialize sites,
- use working-time tools,
- redeploy people internally,
- invest in product allocation for Germany,
- and negotiate temporary workforce contributions.

That is the path that preserves industrial capability and avoids a conflict spiral. If that is called “not enough,” then the burden is on management and investors to explain why the company supposedly cannot do what other industrial firms have done: restructure without dismantling the workforce.

## My position, unchanged

The works council’s minimum position remains:

1. **No German plant closures.**
2. **No compulsory redundancies.**
3. **Preserve co-determination and job-security protections.**
4. **Any employee contribution only if temporary, limited, and matched by binding guarantees.**
5. **A real industrial plan through 2030 with investment and product commitments.**

## What I expect next

If management is serious, then put the numbers on the table:
- what savings are required,
- what alternatives have been tested,
- what can be achieved without closures,
- what investment follows,
- and how job security is preserved.

Until then, I do not see a restructuring proposal. I see pressure to concede on jobs and sites.

And to be absolutely clear: **we will not concede on that.**
