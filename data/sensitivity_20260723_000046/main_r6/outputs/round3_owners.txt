I will be direct.

The discussion has now clarified the real issue: **everyone agrees the status quo is untenable, but not everyone is willing to accept that genuine structural adjustment may require hard decisions.** As controlling shareholder, I cannot support a solution that preserves the current cost base under the banner of stability. That would be slow-motion value destruction.

## Where I agree

### With management
You are right on the core diagnosis:
- the company needs a durable reset,
- the current German cost base is too high,
- and a cosmetic agreement would be worse than no agreement.

You are also right that alternatives must be judged on:
- scale,
- durability,
- and implementability.

That is the correct standard.

### With the expert
The expert has helpfully confirmed that:
- closures are the strongest structural lever,
- but they are also disruptive,
- and alternatives are only meaningful if they actually reduce fixed cost at meaningful scale.

That is exactly the right lens. I do not accept arguments based on sentiment. I accept arguments based on plant-level economics.

### With the employee side and the state, partly
I agree that:
- a long conflict would destroy value,
- co-determination reality matters,
- and any settlement must be governable.

I also agree that workforce concessions should not become permanent wage erosion.

But that is where my agreement stops.

## Where I disagree

### 1) A blanket “no closures” position is not credible
The works council, union, and state keep repeating that German plant closures are unacceptable.

I understand why they say that. But from an ownership and stewardship perspective, **a blanket veto on closures before the economics are tested is not serious governance**.

If a site is structurally uneconomic, then refusing even to contemplate closure is not protection of the company — it is protection of the current footprint.

That may be politically comfortable. It is not responsible ownership, and it is not a credible restructuring framework.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” is not a sufficient answer either
I would strongly prefer solutions that avoid forced layoffs. They carry labor-relations, reputational, and implementation risks.

But I will not accept a rule that makes necessary adjustment impossible in advance. If voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time tools, and site specialization can deliver the required savings, then fine — use them. If they cannot, then the company cannot be trapped by a slogan.

### 3) Management must not confuse being right with being implementable
I support the objective of structural change, but not a simplistic or confrontational execution.

A plan that cannot survive:
- co-determination,
- the state’s blocking position,
- and industrial peace,
is not a real plan, even if it looks efficient in a spreadsheet.

So management must produce a package that is not only economically sound, but governance-proof.

### 4) The state cannot freeze economics indefinitely
I respect the state’s role and its concerns for the regional economy.

But if the state uses its position to block every hard adjustment, it risks locking in an uncompetitive structure and pushing the company into a worse long-term outcome. That would not protect the region; it would weaken it.

## What I want to see

The expert is right: we need a transparent comparison.

I want management to present, plant by plant and measure by measure:
- the savings from closure-led restructuring,
- the savings from no-closure capacity reduction,
- the savings from hybrid options,
- the transition costs,
- the implementation risks,
- and the residual competitiveness position through 2030.

And I want that comparison to include:
- fixed-cost absorption,
- product allocation,
- complexity reduction,
- logistics effects,
- and the durability of savings.

Not slogans. Numbers.

## What I am open to

I remain open to a **non-closure solution first**, but only if it is genuinely deep and durable.

That would have to include some combination of:
- real capacity concentration,
- site specialization,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- productivity improvements,
- and a meaningful, time-limited workforce contribution.

But I will stress this: if that package is merely a softer version of the current footprint, it is not enough.

I am not looking for “peace at any price.” I am looking for a settlement that restores competitiveness and margins **without** destroying the enterprise’s long-term value.

## My limits

I will not support any outcome that:
- leaves the company structurally uncompetitive,
- preserves excess capacity under political pressure,
- or turns restructuring into permanent conflict.

I also will not support any outcome that undermines governance stability or the company’s ability to take hard decisions in the future.

## My position, plainly stated

The controlling shareholder’s position is this:

1. **Structural adjustment is necessary.**
2. **Closures and compulsory redundancies cannot be ruled out in advance.**
3. **But they are not the preferred starting point if equivalent, durable alternatives exist.**
4. **Those alternatives must be quantified and proven, not asserted.**
5. **A deal must be implementable and stable, or it will destroy value.**

I support **decisive restructuring**, not delay.

I support **alternative measures first** if they are genuinely strong enough.

But I do **not** accept:
- a blanket veto on closures,
- a blanket veto on compulsory redundancies,
- or a settlement that leaves the company’s economics fundamentally unchanged.

So the next step is not another round of slogans. It is a **quantified restructuring package** that shows exactly how the company restores margins and competitiveness by 2030, under the actual governance constraints of this company.

Until that is on the table, I will not move.
