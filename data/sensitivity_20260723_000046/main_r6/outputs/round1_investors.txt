From the perspective of dispersed institutional shareholders, the key question is not whether every party gets what it wants, but whether the final package **credibly restores competitive margins and protects enterprise value**.

So let me be direct.

## Where I agree

I agree with management, and to a degree with the family owners, on the core diagnosis:

- the cost base is too high,
- structural overcapacity is real,
- and a cosmetic settlement will not be enough.

I also agree with the state, the works council, and the union on one important point: a restructuring that triggers prolonged conflict, strikes, and political escalation will destroy value quickly. That is not a path we would support either.

## Where I disagree

### 1) With the works council and union: “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” is too rigid
I understand the political and social logic, but from an investor standpoint, a blanket ban on the tools management says it needs is not credible unless you can show equivalent savings elsewhere. Right now, I do not see that.

If the end result leaves the company with:
- the same footprint,
- the same fixed-cost structure,
- and only temporary labour concessions,

then margins will remain structurally impaired. That would be unacceptable.

We are not interested in “saving every site” if the cost is preserving an uncompetitive business model.

### 2) With management: the answer cannot be imposed confrontation
At the same time, management should not confuse “decisive” with “maximalist.” A strategy that looks efficient on paper but cannot survive co-determination, the state’s veto position, and industrial relations reality is not a real strategy. It would likely produce delay, conflict, and value leakage.

If management cannot secure implementation, then the announced savings are not savings. We would view that very negatively.

### 3) With the state: regional employment concerns are real, but value discipline still matters
The regional economic role matters, yes. But protecting every domestic job at all costs is not a shareholder objective. If political constraints prevent the company from fixing its cost base, then the company’s long-term competitiveness will weaken further, and that ultimately endangers more employment and more value.

## What would move us

We would support a package only if it is **specific, durable, and financially credible**. That means:

- a clear and measurable reduction in fixed costs,
- genuine capacity rationalisation,
- not just temporary working-time measures,
- a labour-cost contribution that is meaningful, not symbolic,
- and a credible implementation path that does not rely on endless renegotiation.

We would also want:
- concrete investment commitments tied to the restructuring,
- clear milestone tracking,
- and evidence that the plan improves return on sales within a reasonable horizon.

## Our bottom line

We are **not** here to defend a particular site, and we are **not** here to endorse a deal that simply preserves employment while leaving the company uncompetitive.

We would support a negotiated solution if it delivers:
1. durable cost reduction,
2. structural capacity adjustment,
3. and a credible path back to competitive margins.

But we would **not** support:
- a cosmetic deal,
- a settlement that merely postpones the problem,
- or an outcome that keeps the cost base structurally above competitors.

If the discussion stays in the frame of “no closures, no layoffs, no meaningful cost reset,” then we will view that as value-destructive and will say so plainly.
