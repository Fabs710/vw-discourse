Let me answer directly, because we are now at the point where clarity matters more than repetition.

## To the works council

You keep saying “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” as if stating it firmly makes the cost problem disappear. It does not.

I agree with you on one point: we need a real industrial plan through 2030, not a symbolic statement. But I do **not** accept your attempt to turn site preservation into a prior condition for any restructuring. If the current footprint is structurally too expensive, then protecting every site at any cost is not responsible governance — it is avoidance.

You ask us to show a plan that reduces costs without closures or forced layoffs. That is exactly what I have been saying we need to examine — but only if it is **actually capable of delivering durable savings at scale**. So far, you have offered principles, not a cost base solution.

Binding guarantees that all German plants remain open through 2030 are not compatible with a serious restructuring if overcapacity remains. I will not pretend otherwise. If the workforce wants us to rule out the only measures that can create genuine structural relief, then we are not negotiating a solution — we are negotiating delay.

## To the union

I appreciate that you are at least speaking about capacity reduction, working-time flexibility, voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, and temporary contributions. That is the first thing in this round that actually resembles a restructuring toolkit.

But let’s be honest: the package you are describing may help at the margin, but it may still be insufficient if the scale of the cost gap is as large as everyone here acknowledges. Temporary wage moderation and voluntary programs do not automatically fix a permanently uncompetitive footprint.

So I disagree with your implied premise that closures and compulsory redundancies are never needed. They may be needed if the alternatives do not close the gap. Management has a fiduciary duty to insist on that reality.

I also reject the notion that management is “choosing conflict” by insisting on structural change. Conflict is created when the company’s cost base is left intact while the market moves on. Strikes are a risk, yes — but prolonged inaction is a worse one.

## To the state government

You are right to insist that any settlement must be politically and socially viable. I do not dismiss that. But I do need to say plainly: if your veto position is used to block all serious capacity correction, then you are not defending the industrial base — you are freezing an uncompetitive structure.

The company is not asking for deindustrialization. It is asking for room to survive as an industrial base in Germany. Those are not the same thing.

If the state wants to avoid closures, then it must help produce an alternative that is not just a promise of peace. It must be a package with real cost reduction, real volume alignment, and real productivity improvement. Otherwise, we will have protected the appearance of employment while weakening the company’s ability to invest, compete, and keep jobs in the longer run.

## To the controlling family owners

You are right that strikes, reputational damage, and prolonged conflict destroy value. I agree. That is precisely why I am insisting on a durable solution rather than a cosmetic one.

But let’s not pretend that the only risk to value is conflict. A structurally uncompetitive cost base also destroys value — quietly, but persistently. It weakens margins, delays investment, and erodes our ability to compete with lower-cost rivals.

So when you say you want competitiveness and stewardship, I agree with the objective. Where I disagree is on the idea that we can get there without hard structural decisions. We cannot.

## To the institutional investors

You have put your finger on the central point: the company needs measurable, durable fixed-cost reduction and capacity rationalisation. That is exactly right.

What I would add is this: implementation risk is real, and I am not blind to it. That is why I am not demanding a fantasy unilateral approach. But neither can management accept a settlement that preserves the existing cost base and calls it progress.

If the employee side can produce equivalent savings through alternative means, then I am open to that. But “equivalent” has to mean equivalent in scale, durability, and credibility. Not temporary optics. Not deferred pain. Not another round of promises that leave us uncompetitive in 2027 or 2030.

## My position, stated plainly

Management’s minimum requirement remains:

1. **A substantial, durable reduction in costs and capacity.**
2. **A meaningful labour-cost contribution.**
3. **A package that restores margins and competitiveness through 2030.**
4. **No cosmetic settlement that leaves the current cost base fundamentally intact.**

What I am open to:
- closures,
- production shifts,
- site specialisation,
- working-time adjustments,
- voluntary exit programs,
- redeployment,
- productivity measures,
- and other structural savings,

provided they are sufficient in scale and durable in effect.

What I am **not** open to:
- a blanket veto on closures and redundancies,
- an arrangement that preserves every site and every guarantee regardless of economics,
- or a deal that trades short-term peace for long-term decline.

## Final point

I am not here to provoke strikes for their own sake. I am here to restore competitiveness, margins, and investment capacity. But I will not pretend that this can be done without real structural change. If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then it must bring an alternative that is just as effective, not just more comfortable.

That is the test. Anything less is not a solution.
