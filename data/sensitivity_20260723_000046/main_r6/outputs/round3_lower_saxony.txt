I will speak plainly as the regional state government and anchor shareholder.

## Where I agree

I agree with management, the family, investors, and the expert on one central point: **this company needs a durable restructuring, not a cosmetic one**. The status quo is not sustainable, and temporary patches will not solve the competitiveness problem.

I also agree that:
- a long conflict would destroy value,
- implementation risk is real,
- and any settlement must be governable under co-determination and the special shareholder structure.

So no, I am not defending inaction.

## Where I disagree

### 1) German plant closures are not acceptable as the central instrument
I do **not** accept a restructuring that relies on closing German plants. That would damage the regional economy, undermine trust in the company’s governance model, and create political and social consequences that would outlast any short-term balance-sheet gain.

The expert is right that closures are powerful. But “powerful” does not mean “acceptable” or “smart” in this governance setting. A measure can be economically tidy and still be strategically reckless.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies are also not acceptable
I will not support forced layoffs as the path forward. We can discuss adjustment, but not a solution that shifts the burden primarily onto workers while treating the regional industrial base as expendable.

### 3) “Equivalent savings” must be demonstrated, not demanded by assertion
Management says alternatives must be equivalent in scale and durability. Fine. That is a fair test. But the burden is not on the state to bless closures because management prefers them. The burden is on management to show, with real numbers, that non-closure measures cannot deliver a viable solution.

And that evidence has not yet been made convincing enough.

## Where the expert helps — and where it does not settle the question
The expert has clarified something important:
- alternatives can work,
- but only if they are deep enough,
- and the real issue is fixed-cost absorption, capacity concentration, and product allocation.

That is exactly why I am not satisfied with vague assurances from either side.

What I take from the expert is this:
- capacity reduction without closure is not fantasy;
- site specialization can be a real lever;
- working-time, voluntary exits, and redeployment can contribute materially;
- but all of this must be built into a package that is actually large enough.

That means the next step is not to threaten closures louder. It is to put the **plant-level numbers** on the table and test a serious alternative.

## What I am prepared to support

I am prepared to support a package built around:
- capacity reductions without German closures,
- site specialization,
- working-time instruments,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment and qualification,
- temporary, limited workforce contributions,
- product allocation and investment commitments,
- and a clearly defined path to margin improvement through 2030.

But I will only support that if it comes with:
1. **No German plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Binding investment and product commitments for the German base**
4. **Preservation of co-determination**
5. **A fair burden-sharing arrangement involving management, owners, and workforce**

## To management
If you want my support, bring a package that is:
- concrete,
- quantified,
- and politically survivable.

Do not come back with a presentation that says “our preferred lever is closure, therefore closure.” That is not a strategy. It is a preference.

I am open to hard restructuring. I am not open to a restructuring that destroys the very base that makes this company an anchor for the region.

## To the works council and union
Your rejection of closures and forced redundancies is understandable, and in its core I share it. But I also need to be direct with you: saying “no” is not enough.

If we are to defend jobs and sites, we need a package that shows real adjustment without breaking the plant network. That means engaging seriously on:
- capacity alignment,
- specialization,
- flexibility,
- productivity,
- and temporary contributions.

But I repeat: **only if those are matched by binding guarantees**. There will be no open-ended sacrifice from the workforce.

## To the controlling family and investors
I hear your concern that preserving every site may preserve the problem. That is a legitimate concern.

But I also reject the idea that the only serious ownership is the willingness to close plants. Serious ownership also means understanding the company’s governance reality and not forcing a solution that triggers industrial conflict, legal blockage, and political backlash.

A settlement that is not implementable is not value-preserving.

## My limit
Let me be explicit about my boundary:

I will not support any outcome that:
- guts regional employment,
- removes co-determination leverage,
- or uses plant closures and mass layoffs as the default instrument.

That is not a negotiable preference for me; it is a line.

## What I want next
I support the expert’s request for a transparent comparison. Bring me:
- plant-by-plant savings,
- capacity scenarios,
- fixed-cost implications,
- implementation risk,
- and the residual competitiveness position under a no-closure option and under a closure-led option.

But I want the comparison framed honestly:
- not just “what saves the most on paper,”
- but “what can actually be implemented in this company without breaking its social and governance foundations.”

If management can show a real alternative that protects sites and jobs while restoring competitiveness, I will work to build it.

If not, then we are not at agreement — and I will not pretend otherwise.
