I will speak plainly.

As the regional state government and anchor shareholder, I **cannot support** a restructuring that uses **German plant closures** or **compulsory redundancies** as the central instrument. That would damage the regional economy, erode trust in the governance model, and cross a line that is not acceptable from our side.

At the same time, I also do **not** support a pure status-quo defense that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive. So I am not here to block every adjustment. I am here to insist that adjustment must be **socially balanced, economically credible, and implementable within our governance reality**.

## To management
I agree with you on one point: the company needs a **durable** solution, not a cosmetic one.

But I strongly disagree with your framing that closures and forced reductions are the only serious tools. That is too narrow, and it ignores the political, legal, and social constraints that are part of this company’s reality. A plan that cannot survive co-determination, the state’s blocking position, and industrial relations is not a real plan.

What I can support instead is a package built around:
- capacity reductions without closures,
- site specialization,
- working-time instruments,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment and qualification,
- temporary employee contributions,
- and binding investment commitments for future products.

If you want our support, you need to show that this alternative can deliver **real scale** and **durable savings**. But I will not move to closures simply because management insists they are convenient.

## To the works council and union
I agree with your core line: **no compulsory redundancies** and **no German plant closures as a starting point**.

I also support the insistence on:
- co-determination,
- job security,
- and a real industrial plan through 2030.

That said, I need to be equally clear: saying “no” is not enough if the company’s competitiveness is not addressed. The workforce is right to reject being the only adjustment variable, but the employee side must also be prepared for a **serious contribution** if that is what it takes to preserve the company in the long term.

I would consider a temporary, limited workforce contribution only if it is matched by:
- hard guarantees on sites and jobs,
- clear investment commitments,
- and a credible path to future volume and products in Germany.

What I will not accept is a permanent erosion of standards disguised as partnership.

## To the controlling family and investors
I understand your concern about margins, overcapacity, and value destruction. You are right that the company cannot drift.

But you must also understand this: a strategy that destroys regional employment, triggers labor conflict, and sidelines the special governance structure is not value-preserving. It may produce a headline cost reduction, but it risks long-term damage to the enterprise, its reputation, and its ability to operate in Germany.

So I reject the idea that the only way to show seriousness is through closures. Seriousness can also mean:
- measurable capacity adjustment,
- a fair distribution of sacrifice,
- and commitments that make the German base viable for the future.

## My position
The state will support a settlement only if it satisfies these conditions:

1. **No German plant closures.**
2. **No compulsory redundancies.**
3. **Preservation of co-determination and the state’s veto role.**
4. **A credible restructuring that reduces costs and capacity through alternatives.**
5. **Binding investment and product commitments through 2030.**
6. **A fair burden-sharing arrangement involving management, owners, and workforce.**

If management comes back with a package that respects those conditions and still achieves real competitiveness, I am prepared to work for it.

If the answer remains “closures and layoffs first,” then I will say clearly: **that is not acceptable, and it will not pass with us.**
