I will be equally direct.

## To management
I agree on one point: **the status quo is not an option**. We need a durable settlement, not a symbolic one.

But I do **not** accept your central implication: that German plant closures and compulsory redundancies are the necessary path to seriousness. That is not a neutral technical conclusion; it is a choice to shift the burden mainly onto the workforce.

You keep saying “equivalent in scale and durability.” Fine. Then prove that your preferred measures are actually the only way to achieve it. Until then, I treat closures and forced layoffs as **unacceptable**, not as a baseline bargaining chip.

What we are prepared to discuss is:
- capacity reduction **without closures**,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exit programmes,
- redeployment and qualification,
- site specialization,
- productivity improvements,
- and a **temporary, limited employee contribution**.

But only if the package is matched by:
- binding investment commitments,
- product allocation for Germany,
- protection of collective bargaining standards,
- and a clear end date / reversal logic for employee concessions.

If you want the workforce to contribute, then management and owners must contribute visibly too. No one here gets to claim austerity only for labour.

## To the works council
I support your core line:
- **no closures**,
- **no compulsory redundancies**,
- **no erosion of co-determination**,
- and no open-ended sacrifice by employees.

Your point that temporary contributions must be limited and matched by guarantees is exactly right. I would sharpen it further: any employee contribution must be **precisely defined, time-limited, and tied to measurable counter-commitments**. If the company misses those commitments, the concessions must end.

Where I would caution is this: we should not let the other side force a false binary between “closures” and “doing nothing.” There are real restructuring instruments that preserve jobs and still deliver savings. The burden is on management to show why those are insufficient.

## To the state government
Your position is important and, in substance, I support it: no compulsory redundancies, no German plant closures as the basis of a deal, and protection of the industrial base.

But support has to become leverage. If management keeps pushing a plan that depends on breaking sites and workforce trust, then the state must use its blocking position consistently, not just rhetorically. Otherwise the company will read resistance as negotiable.

At the same time, the state should push for a package that is not only defensive. We need:
- investment,
- future product commitments,
- qualification,
- and a credible industrial roadmap through 2030.

## To the controlling family
I agree with you on one thing: **a long conflict would destroy value**.

But I reject the idea that this means workers should accept closures or forced redundancies to “avoid worse later.” That logic has been used too often to make employees pay for management’s strategic failures.

If you want stewardship, then show it in the distribution of pain:
- management contribution,
- owner contribution,
- capital discipline,
- and workforce contribution only as one part of the package.

A one-sided burden shift is not stewardship. It is short-termism with a nicer label.

## To institutional investors
You are right to demand a credible, durable solution. We are not offering a cosmetic one.

But you are wrong if you think only closures and layoffs can create real credibility. A credible industrial settlement can also come from:
- capacity alignment without site closures,
- internal transfers,
- voluntary exits,
- working-time measures,
- productivity improvements,
- and targeted concessions matched by hard guarantees.

What is not credible is a “clean” financial fix that detonates industrial peace and then assumes the company can simply absorb the fallout. That is not value creation; that is deferred damage.

## Our position, stated clearly

The union’s minimum position remains:

1. **No German plant closures.**
2. **No compulsory redundancies.**
3. **Preserve collective bargaining standards and co-determination.**
4. **Any employee contribution must be temporary, limited, and fairly matched.**
5. **Binding investment and product commitments through 2030.**
6. **Shared sacrifice from management and owners, not labour alone.**

## What we can negotiate
We are prepared to negotiate:
- temporary wage moderation,
- reduced bonuses,
- working-time adjustments,
- flexible capacity use,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary exit frameworks,
- and productivity measures.

But only if:
- jobs and sites are protected,
- the burden is shared,
- and the company commits in writing to a future in Germany.

## What remains unacceptable
We will not accept:
- closures disguised as restructuring,
- forced redundancies as “last resort,”
- permanent wage dumping,
- or a deal where employees finance the entire adjustment.

If management insists on that route, then I am not going to decorate it with polite language: **that is conflict, not settlement**. And the union has already shown it is prepared to act.
