I will respond plainly.

## To management

I agree with one part of your diagnosis: **the company needs a durable solution, not a cosmetic one**. And yes, the market is tougher, costs are too high, and the transition to EVs is not happening in a comfortable environment.

But I **do not** accept the conclusion you are trying to draw from that diagnosis.

A restructuring that depends on:
- German plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- and a one-sided transfer of the burden onto the workforce

is **not** a durable solution from our perspective. It is a high-conflict strategy that may cut costs on paper but will create industrial, political, and social damage that ultimately rebounds on the company.

If you want a serious settlement, then the burden has to be **shared**:
- management contribution,
- owner contribution,
- capital discipline,
- and a workforce contribution that is **temporary, limited, and compensated by binding guarantees**.

You keep saying “real scale.” Fine. Then show us a real package that does **not** start from closures and forced layoffs. If your only answer is to break sites and jobs, then we are not negotiating a future — we are managing a conflict.

## To the works council

I am with you on the core line:
- **no plant closures,**
- **no compulsory redundancies,**
- **no dismantling of the collective framework,**
- and no open-ended sacrifice by employees.

Your emphasis on binding guarantees, investment, and product allocation is exactly right. I also agree that employees must not be the only buffer for management mistakes or strategic delays.

Where I want to sharpen the position is this: if we are prepared to discuss temporary workforce contributions, they must be tied to:
- a defined timeframe,
- a clear scope,
- hard protection against forced job loss,
- and visible contributions from the other side.

That means no “temporary” measure that quietly becomes permanent wage erosion.

## To the state government

Your intervention matters, and I support your insistence that the industrial base and co-determination cannot simply be brushed aside.

But I will also say: if the state opposes closures and forced redundancies, then that position has to be backed by concrete pressure for a **socially balanced industrial plan**, not just a political refusal. We need a package that blocks destruction **and** forces the company to contribute on investment, volume, and future production commitments.

So yes: defend the sites. But defend them through a settlement that actually secures their future, not through vague assurances.

## To the controlling family owners

You say you want competitiveness and value protection. I agree with that in principle. But value is also destroyed by:
- strikes,
- prolonged confrontation,
- broken trust,
- and a settlement that tears up the collective wage framework and destabilises the workforce.

If you want the workforce to contribute, then owners and management must also make a visible contribution. Otherwise this is not stewardship; it is shifting the burden onto labour while preserving the top side of the company.

And let me be direct: a package that includes closures and forced redundancies may look “clean” financially, but it is likely to be expensive in implementation, reputation, and labour peace.

## To the institutional investors

You are right that a cosmetic deal is not enough. We are not offering one.

But you are wrong if you think that only closures and compulsory redundancies can produce seriousness. There are other structural measures:
- capacity reduction,
- working-time adjustments,
- redeployment,
- voluntary exit programmes,
- qualification and internal transfer,
- temporary wage moderation or contribution,
- and a negotiated productivity package.

What is not acceptable is pretending that layoffs are the only credible lever while ignoring the bargaining and implementation risk that such a path creates.

## Our position

The union’s minimum position remains:

1. **No German plant closures.**
2. **No compulsory redundancies.**
3. **Preserve the collective bargaining framework.**
4. **Any employee contribution must be temporary, limited, and fairly matched.**
5. **Any restructuring must include binding investment and product commitments through 2030.**
6. **Management and ownership must contribute visibly and materially.**

## What we are prepared to discuss

We are prepared to discuss:
- capacity reductions **without** closures,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exit programmes,
- redeployment across plants,
- qualification and transition measures,
- temporary financial contributions from employees,
- and productivity improvements.

But only if they are part of a package that:
- protects jobs and sites,
- avoids forced redundancies,
- and creates a credible industrial future.

## What we are not prepared to accept

We will not accept:
- closures dressed up as “reorganisation,”
- compulsory redundancies,
- permanent wage dumping,
- or a restructuring where workers finance the whole adjustment.

If that is the direction management insists on, then I will say it clearly: **there is no agreement at this point, only conflict.** And if conflict is chosen, the union has the means and the mandate to respond accordingly.
