We share the diagnosis that the status quo is not acceptable, and I appreciate that several parties are at least talking about durable rather than cosmetic change.

But I need to be equally direct: **from a capital-markets perspective, “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” is not a solution unless it can be shown to deliver equivalent, durable savings at scale.** So far, I have not seen that evidence.

## Where I agree

### With management, the family, and partly the state
- The cost base is too high.
- Structural overcapacity is real.
- A temporary patch would be value-destructive.
- A credible restructuring must restore margins and free up investment capacity.

That is the right framing.

### With the works council, union, and state
- A prolonged strike, political escalation, or governance deadlock would destroy value.
- Any deal has to be implementable under co-determination.
- Investment and product commitments matter; cuts without an industrial plan are not credible.

That is also right.

## Where I disagree

### 1) With the employee side: absolute red lines are too rigid
I understand the social and political logic of rejecting closures and compulsory redundancies. But as shareholders, we are not here to preserve every site or guarantee in place if the economic structure no longer supports them.

If the final package leaves:
- the same footprint,
- the same fixed-cost burden,
- and only temporary workforce concessions,

then margins remain structurally impaired. That is unacceptable.

### 2) With management: “decisive” cannot become “imposed”
At the same time, management cannot treat closures and blunt job cuts as automatically implementable just because they are financially attractive on paper.

If the plan cannot survive co-determination, the state’s blocking position, and industrial relations reality, then the announced savings are not real savings. A technically elegant but ungovernable plan would be a poor outcome for shareholders too.

### 3) With the state: regional protection cannot freeze economics
I respect the regional role, but shareholder value cannot be subordinated indefinitely to preserving every domestic job. If political constraints make the cost base permanently uncompetitive, the company will weaken further, and that ultimately endangers more value and more employment.

## What would move us

We would support a package only if it is **specific, durable, and measurable**. That means:

- a clear reduction in fixed costs,
- real capacity rationalisation,
- a meaningful labour-cost contribution,
- and an implementation path that does not rely on endless renegotiation.

We would also want:
- investment commitments tied to the restructuring,
- product allocation for the German base,
- and milestone-based tracking of margin improvement.

## What we would not support

We would **not** support:
- a cosmetic settlement,
- a deal that simply postpones the problem,
- or an outcome that leaves the cost base structurally above competitors.

And we would also not support:
- a maximalist confrontation that triggers extended strikes and destroys value in the process.

## Bottom line

We are open to alternatives to closures and compulsory redundancies, but only if they are **equivalent in scale and durability**. Temporary concessions, voluntary programs, and working-time measures may help, but if they do not close the structural gap, they are not enough.

So the burden now is on the other stakeholders to show one thing clearly:

**How exactly does this restructuring restore competitive margins by 2030 without leaving the company structurally uncompetitive?**

If that answer is not credible, then capital will continue to pressure management for harder action.
