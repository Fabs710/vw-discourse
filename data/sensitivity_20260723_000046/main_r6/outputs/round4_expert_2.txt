Yes. From a restructuring finance and automotive cost perspective, the key answer is:

A **non-closure restructuring can be economically credible**, but only if it is **deep enough to remove a large share of fixed cost and capacity permanently**. If it relies mainly on temporary employee concessions, it will usually **not** be equivalent to closure-led restructuring.

## My assessment by option

### 1) Closure-led restructuring
**Financial effect:**
- Highest permanent fixed-cost reduction.
- Best for eliminating underutilized capacity and duplicated overhead.
- Usually improves plant utilization at the surviving sites.

**Costs / risks:**
- High one-time restructuring charges.
- Large severance, shutdown, remediation, logistics, and transfer costs.
- High industrial-relations and implementation risk.
- Potential loss of skills and production flexibility.

**Bottom line:**  
This is the strongest pure cost lever, but also the most disruptive.

---

### 2) No-closure restructuring
This can work only if it includes:
- major **capacity concentration**,
- **site specialization**,
- substantial **voluntary exits**,
- **redeployment and qualification**,
- **working-time reductions/flexibility**,
- and a meaningful **labor-cost contribution**.

**Financial effect:**
- Can produce material recurring savings.
- But usually leaves more legacy overhead, plant-fixed costs, and complexity than closures.
- Savings are often smaller and slower unless the footprint is redesigned aggressively.

**Bottom line:**  
Possible, but only if it removes enough headcount, overhead, and complexity to create a true structural reset. Temporary wage moderation alone is not enough.

---

### 3) Hybrid restructuring
This is often the most realistic in a co-determined German automotive context.

Typical structure:
- Use voluntary exits, transfers, specialization, and capacity shifts first.
- Protect strategic sites.
- Keep closures as a fallback or review-point option if targets are missed.

**Financial effect:**
- Can approach the economics of a closure-led plan if the capacity reduction is deep.
- Usually has lower implementation risk than immediate closures.
- Still needs hard, quantified savings targets.

**Bottom line:**  
Often the best balance between feasibility and cost reduction, but only if the “non-closure” part is not merely symbolic.

---

## What would make a no-closure plan “equivalent”?
For a large German OEM, I would expect the plan to show all of the following:

1. **Permanent fixed-cost reduction**
   - Not just temporary wage cuts.
   - Overhead and plant costs must fall structurally.

2. **Meaningful capacity concentration**
   - Fewer sites doing more volume.
   - Better utilization at the remaining plants.

3. **Substantial headcount reduction**
   - Through voluntary exits and redeployment at scale.
   - If the workforce doesn’t shrink enough, savings usually won’t match.

4. **Lower product complexity**
   - Fewer variants, platforms, and redundant processes.
   - Complexity cost is a major hidden burden in automotive.

5. **Binding investment and product commitments**
   - Otherwise the remaining footprint is not secure enough to justify the sacrifice.

6. **An enforcement mechanism**
   - If savings, volume, or margin targets are missed, there must be a credible review or escalation rule.

---

## My judgment
If the question is whether **equivalent, durable savings** can be achieved **without closures or compulsory redundancies**, the answer is:

**Yes, in principle — but only with a very deep operational redesign and large workforce adjustment.**

If the non-closure package is mainly:
- temporary wage moderation,
- limited voluntary exits,
- and promises of future specialization,

then **no**, it is unlikely to match closure-led restructuring on durability or scale.

## Practical conclusion
From a financial and operational standpoint, the minimum credible non-closure package would usually require:
- strong capacity reduction,
- significant headcount reduction,
- site specialization,
- long-term investment commitments,
- and measurable cost targets through 2030.

Without those, it is more a **delay mechanism** than a restructuring equivalent.
