I will speak plainly as the regional state government and anchor shareholder.

## Where I agree

I agree with everyone on one essential point: **the status quo is not sustainable**. This company needs a durable industrial solution, not a cosmetic patch. It has to regain competitiveness, restore margins, and secure investment capacity through 2030.

I also agree that:
- a long conflict would damage value,
- implementation risk is real,
- and any settlement must work within the governance reality of co-determination and the special shareholder structure.

So no, I am not defending passivity or delay.

## Where I disagree

### 1) German plant closures are not an acceptable restructuring basis
I do **not** support a restructuring that relies on closing German plants. That would damage the regional economy, undermine confidence in the company as an anchor employer, and create political and social consequences that outlast any short-term savings.

To management, the family, and investors: I hear your point that closures are a powerful lever. I do not deny that. But a powerful lever is not automatically the right lever in this company, in this governance structure, or for this region.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies are also not acceptable
I will not support forced layoffs as the answer. Adjustment is necessary, but the burden cannot be shifted primarily onto workers while the regional industrial base is treated as expendable.

### 3) A blanket “closures are unavoidable” claim is still unproven
The burden is on management to prove, with plant-level numbers, that non-closure options cannot deliver a viable result. So far, I have heard a lot of insistence, but not yet enough proof that capacity concentration, specialization, redeployment, voluntary exits, and working-time tools have been exhausted as serious options.

## To management

You keep saying the expert has confirmed your position. I do not read it that way.

The experts said:
- closures are a strong structural lever,
- alternatives can work if they are deep enough,
- and the real question is quantified comparison.

That is not a mandate for closures. It is a demand for evidence.

So bring the evidence:
- plant-by-plant utilization,
- fixed-cost absorption,
- product allocation,
- logistics constraints,
- and the savings from each measure.

If your answer remains only “closures are the cleanest lever,” then you are admitting that this is about convenience and speed, not necessity. That is not sufficient.

## To the works council and union

I support your core red lines:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no erosion of co-determination,
- and no open-ended wage sacrifice.

But I also want to be direct: saying “no” is not enough on its own. If we are to defend jobs and sites, we need a credible package that achieves real adjustment without dismantling the network.

That means engaging seriously on:
- capacity alignment,
- site specialization,
- productivity,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- and qualification.

I am open to temporary, limited employee contributions — but only if they are:
- clearly time-limited,
- matched by binding investment and product commitments,
- and reversible if management does not deliver.

No hidden wage dumping. No permanent erosion of standards.

## To the controlling family

You are right to warn against preserving an uncompetitive structure. I agree that this company cannot be protected by denial.

But serious stewardship is not the same as willingness to close plants at the first difficult decision. Serious stewardship also means:
- preserving the company’s social legitimacy,
- keeping industrial peace,
- maintaining trust with the workforce,
- and respecting the governance constraints that are part of this ownership model.

A solution that is theoretically elegant but practically unimplementable is not stewardship. It is wishful thinking.

## To institutional investors

You are right to insist on durable savings, not symbolic gestures. I share that.

But I also say this clearly: a package that “works” financially only if it destroys the company’s social and governance foundations is not value-creating. It may improve one line item and worsen the enterprise.

You want credibility. So do I. That is why I support a quantified comparison of:
1. closure-led restructuring,
2. no-closure capacity reduction,
3. hybrid scenarios.

But the comparison must include not only annual savings, but also:
- one-time costs,
- implementation risk,
- industrial conflict risk,
- and the long-term effect on the company’s role in the region.

## My position, clearly stated

The state government’s minimum position remains:

1. **No German plant closures as the basis of a deal.**
2. **No compulsory redundancies.**
3. **Preservation of co-determination and job-security protections.**
4. **Any workforce contribution must be temporary, limited, and matched by hard counter-commitments.**
5. **A real industrial plan through 2030 with investment and product commitments.**

## What I am prepared to support

I am prepared to support a serious package based on:
- capacity reductions **without closures**,
- site specialization,
- working-time instruments,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment and qualification,
- productivity improvements,
- and binding investment/product commitments.

If that package is truly deep enough, I will work to make it viable.

## What I am not prepared to support

I will not support:
- plant closures in Germany as the default instrument,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or a deal that guts regional employment and weakens the company’s anchor role.

That is not a negotiation starting point I can endorse, and it is not where my position will move under pressure.
