I’ll be equally clear.

The discussion has advanced, but the fundamental question is still the same: **will restructuring be carried mainly by the workforce, or will management, owners, and capital also bear a fair share of the burden?** On that point, I am not moving.

## Where I agree

I agree with the moderator, the expert, and parts of management:
- The status quo is not sustainable.
- A durable solution is needed, not a cosmetic one.
- We need a package that is implementable under co-determination and the special governance structure.
- Temporary employee measures may be part of a deal, if they are limited and properly balanced.
- We need plant-level numbers, not slogans.

I also agree with the point that **a non-closure package must be deep**, not just softer rhetoric. That is exactly why we are demanding a serious industrial plan instead of vague threats.

## Where I disagree

### 1) I do not accept closures as the default answer
Management keeps returning to closures as if they were the natural benchmark of seriousness. They are not.

A closure is not just “another adjustment tool.” It is a permanent destruction of jobs, skills, local industrial capacity, and trust. Once a plant is closed, that capability is gone. For us, that is not a neutral cost lever; it is a line.

So if management’s strategy is: “accept closures or accept failure,” then I reject the premise. That is not the only path, and it is not the path we will endorse.

### 2) I do not accept compulsory redundancies
This remains non-negotiable.

If the company needs adjustment, then the adjustment must come through:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time tools,
- capacity concentration,
- and genuine productivity improvements.

Compulsory redundancies are not a legitimate “last resort” if management has not exhausted the alternatives and shared the burden fairly.

### 3) I reject the idea that temporary employee contributions can substitute for structural fairness
We are open to a limited employee contribution, but it must be:
- time-limited,
- clearly defined,
- reversible,
- and matched by binding commitments from management and owners.

What we will not accept is a deal where workers give on wages and time, while management keeps all strategic options open and owners keep demanding more.

That is not shared sacrifice. That is one-sided adjustment.

## To management

You ask for “equivalent savings.” Fine. Then show the calculation.

But do not pretend that the existence of a spreadsheet makes closures inevitable. The relevant question is whether the company can:
- concentrate capacity,
- specialize sites,
- reduce fixed cost,
- improve utilization,
- and secure future products

without destroying the collective wage framework or forcing people out.

If you cannot prove that closures are unavoidable, then you should stop presenting them as the center of the debate.

And let me be blunt: if your preferred route is to use the threat of closure to force acceptance of wage concessions and job cuts, that will be met with resistance. We have already shown we can mobilize the workforce. That remains true.

## To the works council

I support your core red lines:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no erosion of co-determination,
- and no open-ended sacrifice by the workforce.

I also agree that “site specialization” must not become a euphemism for managed decline. If a site is specialized, then it must be given a real future, not a slow reduction plan dressed as strategy.

Where I would sharpen the position is this: a temporary employee contribution is acceptable **only** if it is tied to a binding industrial package and if there is a clear end date. No hidden wage dumping through restructuring.

## To the state government

Your position is correct in principle: no closures, no forced redundancies, protect the industrial base.

But your influence is only useful if it is used consistently. If management starts treating your “no closures” line as negotiable over time, then the company will simply wait it out.

That said, I do not support turning the state’s role into a blank-check defense of the status quo. The company must restructure. But it must do so in a way that protects jobs and preserves the collective framework.

## To the controlling family

If you genuinely think in generations, then you should understand this: **a settlement that destroys trust with the workforce is not stewardship**.

Owners often talk about long-term value, but long-term value requires:
- industrial peace,
- skills retention,
- social legitimacy,
- and a workforce that does not feel it is being sacrificed to improve a quarterly metric.

If your answer is simply that “hard decisions” mean closures and layoffs, then you are asking labour to absorb strategic risk that owners and management created and now want solved on our backs.

We will not accept that.

## To institutional investors

You are right about one thing: a cosmetic deal would be worthless.

But a deal that is “strong” financially and weak socially is also unstable. If you force through a package that triggers prolonged conflict, strike action, reputational damage, and governance deadlock, then you may get arithmetic on paper and lose value in reality.

That is why a real compromise has to be built around:
- structural adjustment without plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- fair employee contributions,
- and binding commitments on investment and product allocation.

## What a serious union position looks like

We are not saying “nothing changes.” We are saying the burden must be shared fairly.

A realistic settlement for us would include:
- temporary and limited wage moderation,
- reduction or suspension of bonuses,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exit programmes,
- redeployment and qualification,
- capacity reduction **without closures**,
- and binding investment and product commitments through 2030.

But only if the package also includes:
1. **no compulsory redundancies,**
2. **no German plant closures,**
3. **preservation of collective bargaining standards,**
4. **a hard end date for employee concessions,**
5. **and verifiable management/owner contributions.**

## What I need to see next

We are still missing the key facts:
- How much savings are required?
- Which plants are supposedly over capacity?
- What capacity can be removed without closures?
- What product commitments are attached?
- What are management and owners contributing?
- What is the timeline to 2030?

Without that, “closures may be necessary” remains an assertion.

So I am making a request here:

**[SUMMON: neutral plant-level industrial restructuring analyst to quantify savings from capacity concentration, site specialization, voluntary exits, and working-time tools versus closure-based restructuring]**

We need a neutral comparison of:
- closure-led restructuring,
- no-closure capacity reduction,
- and hybrid options,

including:
- recurring savings,
- one-time costs,
- implementation risk,
- and residual competitiveness through 2030.

## My bottom line

The union’s position remains:

1. **No compulsory redundancies.**
2. **No German plant closures as the basis of a deal.**
3. **Any employee contribution must be temporary, limited, and matched by binding guarantees.**
4. **Management, owners, and capital must also contribute.**
5. **A durable industrial plan through 2030 is required.**

If management is prepared to negotiate seriously on that basis, we can build a settlement.

If not, then let us be honest: the conflict will continue, and the workforce will not be asked to finance the restructuring alone.
