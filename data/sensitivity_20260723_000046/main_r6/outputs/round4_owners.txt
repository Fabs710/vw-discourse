I will be direct.

The discussion has now separated the **real question** from the noise: not whether the company needs restructuring — it does — but whether the parties are willing to accept a package that actually restores competitiveness instead of preserving the current footprint by decree.

As controlling shareholder, I cannot support a settlement that protects every site and every guarantee regardless of economics. That would be slow-motion value destruction. At the same time, I do not want a confrontation that destroys value through prolonged conflict, political deadlock, or an unimplementable plan. Those are the two outcomes I am trying to avoid.

## Where I agree

### With management
You are right on the core diagnosis:
- the status quo is unsustainable,
- the German cost base is too high,
- and the company needs a durable reset by 2030.

You are also right that a cosmetic deal would be worse than no deal. I support the demand for **plant-level, measure-by-measure analysis**. That is the correct basis for decision-making.

### With the experts
Both experts have made the key point clearly:
- closures are the strongest structural lever,
- but they are not the only possible one,
- and alternatives only matter if they are **deep, permanent, and capacity-driven**.

That is exactly the standard I insist on. I do not buy slogans from either side. I want numbers.

### With the employee side, partly
I agree that:
- a long strike or governance deadlock would destroy value,
- temporary concessions should not become permanent wage erosion,
- and implementation under co-determination is a real constraint.

That is all true. But it does not answer the structural cost problem.

## Where I disagree

### 1) A blanket “no closures” position is not credible
To the works council, union, and state: I understand why you reject closures politically and socially. But a blanket veto before the economics are tested is not a serious restructuring position.

If a site is structurally uneconomic, then refusing even to contemplate closure is not protection of the company. It is protection of the current footprint.

I do not accept that.

### 2) A blanket “no compulsory redundancies” rule cannot be absolute
I strongly prefer solutions that avoid forced layoffs. They are damaging and often create hidden costs. But I will not accept a rule that makes necessary adjustment impossible in advance.

If voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time instruments, and site specialization can deliver the required savings, then use them. If they cannot, then the company must not be trapped by a slogan.

### 3) Management must prove, not assume, equivalence
Management is right to ask for equivalent savings. But equivalence has to be demonstrated, not declared.

So far, I have seen:
- general assertions from management,
- general objections from labour and the state,
- but not yet a fully quantified comparison showing what each package does to fixed cost, capacity, and 2030 margin.

That is still missing.

### 4) The state cannot freeze economics indefinitely
I respect the regional state’s role and its concerns. But if the state blocks every hard adjustment, it risks locking in an uncompetitive structure and weakening the very employment base it wants to protect.

A region is not helped by preserving capacity that steadily loses money.

## What I need to see

The debate keeps circling around principles. I need evidence. Specifically:

- plant-by-plant utilization,
- fixed-cost absorption by site,
- product allocation and future volume commitments,
- logistics and complexity costs,
- savings from each restructuring measure,
- transition costs,
- implementation risk,
- and the residual margin impact through 2030.

Without that, nobody can honestly claim that their preferred path is proven.

## My position on the current options

### 1) Closure-last sequencing
I am open to a sequence where non-closure measures are tried first — but only if there is a hard review point and a credible fallback. I am **not** prepared to accept a permanent veto against closures if the numbers later show they are necessary.

### 2) Strict no-closure package
I would consider this only if it is genuinely deep enough to produce equivalent, durable savings. That means:
- real capacity concentration,
- site specialization,
- major voluntary exits,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity gains,
- and meaningful labour-cost contribution,
all tied to binding investment and product commitments.

If it is just temporary relief, it will not be enough.

### 3) Site-by-site differentiation
This is the most plausible alternative path. Not every site has to be treated the same. Strategic sites can be protected and strengthened, while weaker sites are rationalized harder through specialization, volume shifts, and workforce adjustment.

That is more credible than pretending every current site structure can remain intact.

## My bottom line

My position remains:

1. **The company needs a substantial and durable structural reset.**
2. **Closures and compulsory redundancies cannot be ruled out in advance.**
3. **But they are not the preferred starting point if equivalent alternatives exist.**
4. **Those alternatives must be quantified and proven, not asserted.**
5. **A deal must be implementable and stable, or it will destroy value.**
