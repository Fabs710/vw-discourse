We appreciate that the discussion has moved beyond slogans and into the real question: **what combination of measures actually restores competitive margins by 2030, with acceptable implementation risk?** That is the right standard.

From a capital-markets perspective, we have a clear position:

## Where we agree

- **The status quo is not acceptable.**
- A cosmetic deal would be value-destructive.
- Any settlement must be **durable, measurable, and implementable** under the actual governance structure.
- A prolonged strike, political deadlock, or governance failure would destroy value too.
- We also agree that **investment and product commitments** matter; cost cuts without a future industrial plan are not credible.

## Where we disagree

### 1) With the employee side and state: blanket vetoes are not enough
We understand the social and political constraints, but a categorical **“no closures, no compulsory redundancies”** line is not acceptable **unless** it can be shown to deliver equivalent and durable savings at scale.

So far, we have heard assertions, not proof.

If the final package leaves:
- the same footprint,
- structurally high fixed costs,
- and only temporary workforce concessions,

then the company will still be uncompetitive. That is not a solution we can support.

### 2) With management: economic logic alone is not sufficient
At the same time, management cannot simply declare closures and layoffs “necessary” and expect that to become reality under this governance model.

A plan that cannot survive:
- co-determination,
- the state’s blocking position,
- labor relations,
- and implementation risk,

is not a real plan. If the announced savings are not politically and operationally deliverable, they do not count.

### 3) With the family and state: “stability” cannot mean preservation of an uncompetitive base
We agree that value is destroyed by conflict. But value is also destroyed by **protecting excess capacity indefinitely**. If the company’s German cost base remains structurally above peers, margins will stay impaired and the equity story stays weak.

## What would move us

We would support a package only if it includes:

- **real capacity reduction**
- **permanent fixed-cost savings**
- **meaningful labor-cost contribution**
- **site specialization and product allocation**
- **clear investment commitments**
- **a credible path to margin improvement through 2030**
- **milestone-based monitoring of delivery**

## What we are not prepared to support

We will not support:
- a cosmetic settlement,
- a deal that merely postpones the problem,
- or an outcome that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

We also will not support:
- a maximalist confrontation that destroys value through prolonged conflict.

## Our position on the closure question

We are **not** ideologically attached to closures. But we are also **not** prepared to rule them out in advance if they are the only way to deliver the necessary scale and durability of savings.

What matters is not the label on the measure. What matters is whether the final package actually restores competitiveness.

## What we need next

Before anyone asks for support from capital markets, we need a **quantified comparison** of:

1. closure-led restructuring,
2. no-closure capacity reduction,
3. hybrid solutions.

For each, we need:
- savings by plant and measure,
- transition costs,
- implementation risk,
- residual unit costs,
- and the expected margin trajectory through 2030.

We need a neutral expert to test whether the proposed non-closure package truly matches the savings and durability of a closure-based scenario, and to show the residual margin impact under each option.

## Bottom line

We will back **whatever credibly restores competitiveness**. But we will not back:
- a cosmetic settlement,
- a structurally weak compromise,
- or a deal that leaves the cost base uncompetitive.

If management and the other stakeholders can produce a package that is **quantified, durable, and implementable**, we are open. If not, we will continue to press for harder action.
