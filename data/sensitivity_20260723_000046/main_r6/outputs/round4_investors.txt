We are aligned with the basic diagnosis, but not with the degree of refusal coming from labour and the state.

## Where we agree
- The status quo is not acceptable.
- A cosmetic deal would destroy value.
- The company needs a durable industrial reset through 2030.
- Temporary conflict would be value-destructive.
- Investment and product commitments matter, but only if they sit on top of a credible cost and capacity fix.

## Where we disagree

### To the works council and union
We understand your red lines politically and socially, but from a capital-markets perspective a blanket **“no closures, no compulsory redundancies”** stance is not acceptable **unless** you can demonstrate, with numbers, that it still delivers equivalent and durable savings at scale.

So far, we have not seen that proof.

Saying “capacity reduction without closures” is possible is not the same as showing that it is sufficient. If the final package leaves:
- structurally high fixed costs,
- excess footprint,
- and only temporary labour concessions,

then margins will remain impaired and the equity story remains weak. That is not a solution we can support.

### To management
At the same time, management cannot just assert that closures and layoffs are necessary and expect the governance structure to yield. If the plan cannot survive co-determination, the state’s veto, and industrial relations, then it is not a real plan.

We support hard restructuring, but only if it is **implementable** and **economically durable**.

### To the state government
We respect your role and your concerns for the region. But protecting every site by principle is not the same as protecting long-term employment. If the company remains uncompetitive, the damage will spread across the whole base anyway.

### To the controlling family
We agree that the company cannot preserve an uncompetitive cost base forever. But stewardship also means not forcing through a solution that is dead on arrival under the governance reality.

## Our position
We will back **whatever credibly restores competitiveness** by 2030. That means:

- real capacity reduction,
- permanent fixed-cost savings,
- meaningful labour-cost contribution,
- site specialization and product allocation,
- binding investment commitments,
- and measurable milestones.

We will **not** back:
- a cosmetic settlement,
- a deal that only postpones the problem,
- or an outcome that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

## On the key dispute
We are **not ideologically attached** to closures. But we are also **not prepared to rule them out in advance** if they are the only way to deliver the needed scale and durability of savings.

What matters is not the label of the measure. What matters is whether the package actually restores competitiveness and margin discipline.

## What we need next
We need a **quantified, plant-level comparison** of:
1. closure-led restructuring,
2. no-closure capacity reduction,
3. hybrid compromise.

For each, we need:
- recurring annual savings,
- one-time transition costs,
- implementation risk,
- residual unit cost,
- and the 2030 margin trajectory.

Until that is on the table, we will continue to press for hard action and we will not support a deal that leaves the cost base uncompetitive.
