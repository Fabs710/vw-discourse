## To the works council

You keep repeating “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” as if that were a restructuring plan. It is not. It is a **veto**.

I agree with you on one point: the company needs a durable industrial solution, not a cosmetic patch. But durability also means the cost base must actually be reset. If the German network remains structurally overbuilt and expensive, then we have not solved the problem — we have postponed it.

You say closures are “destroying the German base.” What destroys the base in the long run is an uncompetitive structure that keeps eroding margins, investment capacity, and ultimately jobs across the whole network. Protecting every site in place is not the same as protecting the company.

I am prepared to discuss non-closure alternatives, but only if they are **deep enough**:
- real capacity concentration,
- site specialization,
- major voluntary exits,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity gains,
- and a meaningful labour-cost contribution.

What I will not accept is a package that simply preserves the current footprint and calls that a solution.

## To the union

You are right that temporary employee concessions cannot become permanent wage dumping. I accept that. But your position still does not answer the core issue: **how do you remove enough fixed cost, fast enough, to restore competitiveness by 2030?**

A temporary contribution helps only if it is part of a structural reset. If it leaves the same overcapacity in place, then we are back here in two years with the same crisis and less time left.

You say no compulsory redundancies. Fine. Then show me a combination of instruments that achieves the required savings **without** leaving us with an uncompetitive structure. If you cannot, then you are not offering an alternative — you are offering a delay.

## To the regional state government

I respect your role and the governance reality. I do not respect the idea that the state can simply say “no closures” and expect the economics to disappear.

You are right that implementation matters. A deal that cannot survive co-determination, politics, and industrial relations is worthless. But a deal that protects every site regardless of economics is also worthless, because it leaves the company uncompetitive.

If the state wants to rule out closures, then it must support a package that is **equivalent in scale and durability**. Not “socially balanced.” Equivalent. That means permanent cost reduction, permanent capacity adjustment, and a verifiable path to margin recovery.

## To the controlling family

You are right about one thing: we cannot preserve an uncompetitive cost base indefinitely. On that, we agree.

Where I disagree is your apparent assumption that the only serious test of ownership is readiness to close plants. I am not defending closures as a principle. I am using them because they remain part of the option set when alternatives do not close the structural gap.

That said, I do agree with you that management must produce a package that survives the real governance structure. A technically elegant plan that cannot be implemented is useless. So yes — we need plant-level numbers, measure-by-measure, and a package that is governance-proof.

But I will not accept the inverse logic either: that because closures are hard, we should pretend softer measures are sufficient when they are not.

## To institutional investors

You are right to demand more than rhetoric. A cosmetic deal would destroy value. So would a plan that keeps the cost base intact.

What you need to hear from management is this: we are not chasing symbolism. We are chasing **fixed-cost reduction, margin repair, and investment capacity through 2030**.

Your demand for quantified comparison is exactly right. And here is my point back to you: if the evidence shows that only deep footprint changes can deliver the savings, then you should be willing to support them. If the evidence shows a serious non-closure package can match that, I will support it. But until then, blanket rejection of hard measures is not serious capital discipline.
