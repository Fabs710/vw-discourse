We share the diagnosis that the company needs a **durable structural reset**, not a cosmetic deal. Where we differ is on what counts as credible.

## To management
We agree that the German cost base is too high and that **margin restoration requires recurring savings**, not one-off gestures. We also agree that open-ended delay and a strike spiral would be value-destructive.

But you still have not provided the one thing that matters to investors: a **quantified bridge** from current costs to a competitive cost base.

You say closures remain on the table. Fine. But from our side, closures are **not** the goal; they are only justified if the numbers prove that non-closure measures cannot close the gap. Right now, that proof is still missing.

What we need is not rhetoric about “structural” problems. We need:
- the exact recurring savings target,
- the exact savings from labor measures,
- the exact management overhead cuts,
- the exact owner contribution,
- transition costs,
- and the residual gap under no-closure, hybrid, and closure scenarios.

If you cannot show that non-closure measures are insufficient, then closure is not a financial conclusion — it is a negotiating threat.

## To the works council
We agree with you on one important point: **restructuring does not automatically require plant closures**. That is correct.

We also agree that industrial conflict, know-how loss, and operational disruption have real economic costs. A settlement that triggers prolonged labor warfare is not value-neutral.

Where we disagree is your refusal to let closures remain a possible outcome unless an alternative is proven equivalent. That is too rigid for us as shareholders.

A blanket “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” stance is only acceptable if it delivers:
- comparable recurring savings,
- durable capacity adjustment,
- and a credible return to target margins.

If it does not, then it is not protecting value; it is preserving an uncompetitive structure.

## To the union
We support negotiated burden-sharing. We do **not** support permanent wage concessions, nor a deal where workers contribute now and management comes back later with another round of pressure.

But we also do **not** support framing every adjustment as an existential labor-rights fight if the economics require serious change.

If you want investor support for a non-closure package, then it must be:
- binding,
- time-limited,
- measurable,
- and durable enough to count in the margin plan.

That means real recurring savings, not just temporary peace.

## To the regional state government
We understand your political and regional role. We also understand why you want to protect industrial capacity and jobs.

But from a capital-market perspective, the state cannot be allowed to make footprint reduction politically impossible if that is what the economics require. That would simply shift the value destruction into the future.

We support a serious no-closure test first. But if the residual gap remains material, then the state should not block necessary restructuring just because it is painful.

## To the controlling family holding
We agree with your concern that a cosmetic compromise would destroy value. That is exactly our concern too.

Where we differ is that “closure as default leverage” can also destroy value if it triggers conflict, delays, and reputational damage. So we are not backing closures as such; we are backing the **least value-destructive path to competitive margins**.

If non-closure measures can credibly do that, we will support them. If they cannot, then we expect management and the owners to act on the numbers, not sentiment.

## On the verification process
We strongly support independent verification, but it has to be used for decision-making, not as a way to postpone reality.

[SUMMON: independent restructuring and financial verification panel to quantify recurring savings, transition costs, implementation certainty, and residual gap under no-closure, hybrid, and closure scenarios]

The panel should assess:
- recurring annual savings needed,
- savings by measure,
- one-off costs,
- implementation certainty,
- labor-relations risk,
- and margin impact over the next 2–3 years.

That is the only basis on which investors can judge whether a no-closure settlement is actually value-creating.

## Our position, plainly
We will support a negotiated outcome only if it achieves:
1. **durable recurring savings**
2. **credible capacity reduction**
3. **clear margin improvement**
4. **high implementation certainty**
5. **no open-ended labor conflict**
6. **net value creation after transition costs**

We will **not** support:
- a deal that merely postpones the adjustment,
- a settlement that preserves structural overcapacity,
- or a politically comfortable package that leaves the company financially weak.

So yes, we are open to a non-closure solution. But only if it is economically equivalent. If not, we will not pretend otherwise.

### Bottom line
The burden of proof is now on management and the workforce side together:
- either produce a verified package that restores competitiveness without closures,
- or accept that footprint reduction may become necessary.

Not because we prefer it in principle, but because we will not back a deal that leaves shareholders with structurally impaired margins.
