## Controlling Family Holding — Round 4

I will be direct.

### To management
I agree with the core diagnosis: this company needs a **durable structural improvement in cost and capacity**. I also agree that a cosmetic settlement that leaves the German footprint uncompetitive would destroy value over time. That is unacceptable.

Where I disagree with you is the way you keep using closures as the implied default before the economics have been fully tested. In a governed company with co-determination, a blocking state stake, and a controlling owner responsible for the long term, that is not a serious first principle. It may be a possible outcome. It is not yet a proven necessity.

You still have not produced the information that would justify making closure the basis of the settlement:
- the exact recurring savings target,
- the savings already available from non-closure measures,
- the management contribution,
- the owner contribution,
- and the residual gap **net of transition costs**.

Until that exists, closure is a claim, not a conclusion.

I do **not** support a symbolic compromise. I do **not** support open-ended delay. But I also do **not** support letting a closure-first posture harden into policy without proof.

---

### To the works council
I agree with you on one important point: restructuring does **not automatically mean closures**. You are right to reject the idea that the workforce must accept plant destruction and forced layoffs simply because management wants faster leverage.

Where I disagree is with any attempt to make “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” a complete answer by itself. It is not. If the cost gap remains large after all non-closure measures, then the enterprise keeps eroding, and that eventually harms the workforce, the company, and the owners alike.

So I support:
- verified savings,
- management restraint,
- owner contribution,
- site protection where it is economically sustainable,
- and a serious test of alternatives first.

But I do **not** accept a framework that excludes every meaningful footprint adjustment in advance regardless of the numbers.

---

### To the union
I share your concern that temporary concessions must not become permanent wage dumping. That is legitimate. Any workforce contribution has to buy something durable:
- job security,
- site security,
- investment,
- and a credible future for the company.

Where I disagree is your attempt to make a blanket no-closure position the starting governance assumption. It may be a bargaining line, but it is not an adequate ownership position if the residual gap remains substantial.

I am open to a non-closure route if it is:
- quantitatively verified,
- recurring,
- enforceable,
- and large enough to restore margins.

I am **not** open to a settlement that protects every legacy feature while leaving the business structurally weak.

---

### To the regional state government
I agree that closures are not desirable and should not be treated lightly. I also agree that industrial capacity in Germany matters.

Where I disagree is with the idea that the state can simply block hard restructuring logic by insisting on no closures as a political premise. The state’s role should be to ensure that alternatives are thoroughly tested and that the regional impact is properly assessed — not to pre-judge the outcome.

So I support:
- a no-closure scenario being tested first,
- binding contributions from management and owners,
- investment and qualification commitments,
- and serious review of the regional consequences of any footprint reduction.

But I do **not** support making closures politically impossible before the numbers are tested.

---

### To institutional investors
You are right to demand durable savings, implementation certainty, and a credible return to target margins. I agree with that.

Where I disagree is any implication that economic discipline is synonymous with immediate closures. It is not. The question is whether the final package restores competitiveness on a durable basis.

If a non-closure package can do that, it should be supported. If it cannot, then a refusal to face the residual gap would be irresponsible.

---

## What I want now
We are past slogans. We need proof.

[SUMMON: independent restructuring verification panel to assess recurring savings, transition costs, management and owner contributions, and the residual gap under no-closure, hybrid, and closure scenarios]

That panel must answer, in hard numbers:
- recurring annual savings required,
- savings achievable without closures,
- management overhead reductions,
- owner-side support,
- transition costs and disruption risk,
- residual gap under each scenario.

Only then can anyone responsibly say whether closures are truly unavoidable or merely being used as leverage.

---

## My position, unchanged
I will support a **non-closure restructuring** if it is:
- quantitatively verified,
- durable,
- large enough to restore competitiveness and margins,
- and paired with labour peace and investment discipline.

I will **not** support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- an open-ended industrial conflict,
- a permanent impairment of value,
- or a settlement that preserves all protections while leaving the company structurally uncompetitive.

I am not moving off that line. Bring the numbers.
