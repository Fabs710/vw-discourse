Let me respond clearly.

## To the works council
You keep saying management is turning a strategy problem into a workforce sacrifice problem. That is not accurate. The problem is that the company has a **structural competitiveness gap**. If the solution does not materially reduce that gap, then it is not a solution.

I agree with you on one point: **restructuring does not automatically have to mean closures**. But I do **not** agree with your conclusion that therefore closures can be ruled out in advance.

What I will not accept is this position:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no meaningful labour-cost contribution,
- and no structural capacity reduction.

That is not restructuring. That is preservation of an uncompetitive cost base.

If you want to avoid closures, then you need to show a package that is **equivalent in recurring savings, scale, and durability**. Not politically nicer. Economically equivalent.

## To the union
I agree that vague closure threats are not a substitute for numbers. That is precisely why I have been calling for a hard package, not generalities.

But I disagree with your repeated attempt to make closures sound like an arbitrary management preference. They are not. They remain on the table because **the economics may require footprint reduction**. If non-closure measures close the gap, I will consider them. If they do not, then you are asking us to live with structural underperformance.

You say you will not accept compulsory redundancies or plant closures as the primary instrument. Fine. Then tell me which combination of:
- labour-cost moderation,
- working-time changes,
- voluntary exits,
- capacity reductions,
- overhead cuts,
- and productivity measures

actually closes the gap, and by how much.

A temporary contribution is not enough if the cost problem is permanent.

## To the regional state government
I respect the fact that you are pushing for industrial continuity. But I reject the idea that your blocking power should be used to pre-empt any serious restructuring option.

You say closures should not be the default. Agreed. They should not be the default. But nor can you declare them politically impossible and still demand a financially credible turnaround.

If you want a non-closure path, then back a package that delivers:
- real labour-cost relief,
- real capacity reduction,
- real productivity improvement,
- and a real investment path to 2030.

Protection without competitiveness is just delay.

## To the controlling family holding
You are right that a cosmetic deal would destroy value. I agree. You are also right that a destructive closure-first strategy would be bad if it goes beyond what is necessary. I agree with that too.

Where I disagree is your apparent assumption that the workforce can be asked to contribute meaningfully while closures are treated as a remote last resort with no practical leverage. That weakens our negotiating position and risks ending up with a compromise that is too soft to fix the business.

We need the ability to say: if the numbers do not add up, then the footprint must shrink. Otherwise, we are not negotiating from economic reality.

## To institutional investors
You are right to demand durable savings and implementation certainty. That is exactly why I am not interested in a symbolic compromise.

But let me be equally clear: a settlement that avoids closures while keeping the company structurally uncompetitive is **not** value creation. It is deferred erosion.

If you want a deal, it must include:
- structural cost reduction,
- structural capacity adjustment,
- and a credible return-on-sales improvement.

Not just peace. Not just optics. Real economics.

## Where I stand
Here is the line:

### I am prepared to consider a non-closure restructuring if it delivers:
- quantified recurring savings,
- a meaningful labour-cost contribution,
- capacity reductions with operational effect,
- management overhead restraint,
- and a credible 2030 investment path.

### I am not prepared to accept:
- a blanket no-closure pledge,
- a no-compulsory-redundancy pledge that prevents necessary workforce adjustment,
- a package that preserves every guarantee while leaving the cost base unchanged,
- or a settlement that postpones the problem and calls it social responsibility.

## [SUMMON: independent verification of savings bridge and residual gap]
We now need an independent, site-level verification of:
- the exact savings gap,
- the savings achievable from non-closure measures,
- the residual gap after labour, overhead, and capacity measures,
- and the timing and durability of each element.

Without that, this discussion keeps circling between slogans. I want hard numbers:
- annual recurring savings,
- transition costs,
- impact on margins,
- and the remaining structural shortfall if closures are excluded.

If the residual gap is small, fine — we can discuss how to close it without plant shutdowns. If it is large, then closures remain a necessary part of the solution.

## Bottom line
I am not moving off the core requirement:

**We need a structurally durable reduction in cost and capacity.**

That means:
- no cosmetic deal,
- no open-ended conflict,
- no solution that protects every status quo element while claiming to restore competitiveness.

If the workforce side wants to avoid closures, it must bring an alternative that is economically equivalent, not just politically more comfortable. If it cannot, then management will not pretend otherwise.
