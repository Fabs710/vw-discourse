Let me respond plainly and without the usual evasions.

## To management
You still have not answered the central question: **why must restructuring mean plant closures or forced redundancies?**

You keep insisting on a “durable savings bridge,” but you are still trying to make the workforce accept insecurity before you have proven necessity. That is backwards.

We agree on one thing: the company needs restructuring.  
We do **not** agree that this gives management a license to threaten the first German plant closure in the company’s history as a bargaining tool.

So here is the position:
- **No German plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No dismantling of the historic job-security framework**
- **Yes to quantified restructuring**
- **Yes to productivity, working-time, and voluntary instruments**
- **Yes to management and owner contributions**

If you want our participation, then stop speaking as if closure is the default answer and prove the gap can’t be closed otherwise. Until then, closure is not a solution; it is an escalation.

## To the union
We are aligned on the core red lines, and that matters. But I want to be equally clear: any workforce contribution must be strictly limited, time-bound, and tied to **binding guarantees**.

I will not accept:
- permanent wage concessions disguised as temporary solidarity,
- labour sacrifices while management keeps future closure threats in reserve,
- or contributions without enforceable site and employment security.

If the company wants moderation from employees, then it must give something real in return:
- **no closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **investment commitments**
- **co-determination in implementation**
- **a secure path through 2030**

Without that, there is nothing to negotiate except resistance.

## To the regional state government
You are still speaking too softly for the scale of the threat.

This is not a theoretical balance sheet discussion. This is a question of industrial policy, regional stability, and the survival of tens of thousands of jobs. If the state has a blocking position and political weight, then use it to prevent closures — not to manage them more politely.

Your role should be:
- to back a binding no-closure track,
- to insist on investment and qualification,
- to protect the industrial base in Germany,
- and to reject any “solution” that shifts adjustment onto workers and communities first.

If the state only says “no closures wherever possible,” that is not protection. That is permission for management to keep pushing until the line moves.

## To the controlling family holding
You say you want long-term value. Then act like long-term owners, not short-term pressure transmitters.

A company does not build value by breaking its social compact and detonating trust with the workforce. If you want competitiveness, support:
- overhead cuts,
- management restraint,
- investment discipline,
- operational efficiency,
- and a serious industrial strategy.

What I will not accept is a governance stance that keeps closure in the room as a normal lever while demanding the workforce prove loyalty under threat.

If the economics truly require closure, show it with hard numbers.  
If they do not, then stop using closure as leverage.

## To institutional investors
You have one legitimate concern: durable savings. Fine. But you are ignoring a major economic risk if you treat plant closures and forced redundancies as neutral instruments.

They are not neutral. They create:
- strike risk,
- loss of know-how,
- operational disruption,
- reputational damage,
- and long-term instability in the German footprint.

A settlement that preserves sites and jobs while delivering verified recurring savings is not a “soft” outcome. It is the only outcome that can plausibly protect long-term value without destroying the company’s industrial base.

## On the verification proposal
We support independent verification. But I want to be explicit about the rules of that process.

The panel must test:
- savings from working-time changes,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- procurement and process efficiencies,
- management overhead cuts,
- owner-side support,
- and the residual gap **without assuming closures as the benchmark**.

What we will **not** accept is a verification exercise that begins from the premise that closure is the real solution and merely asks how much damage can be avoided.

The benchmark is not “how close can we get to closure economics?”  
The benchmark is: **can competitiveness be restored without closing German plants or forcing people out?**

## My position remains unchanged

- limited, negotiated workforce contributions,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary instruments,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- productivity measures,
- management restraint,

But only if the outcome is:
- **every German plant stays open**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no weakening of co-determination**

That is not maximalism. That is the minimum acceptable line.

## What I am asking for now
If management and the owners genuinely want a deal, then bring a verified package that:
1. excludes closures,
2. excludes forced layoffs,
3. binds management and owners to real contributions,
4. protects jobs and sites through 2030,
5. and proves the savings are sufficient.
