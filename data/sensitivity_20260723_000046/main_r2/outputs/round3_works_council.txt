Let me be very clear: the repeated attempt to frame this as “security first versus economics first” is misleading. **We are not denying the need for restructuring. We are rejecting the false claim that restructuring must mean plant closures and compulsory redundancies.**

## To management
You still have not proven that closures are unavoidable. You have said the cost base is too high, the market is weak, and margins are under pressure. We know that. That is not the same as demonstrating that a German plant must be closed.

What you keep doing is this:
- declare the savings target,
- keep closures on the table as leverage,
- and then ask the workforce to accept insecurity in advance.

That is not a serious basis for negotiation. It is pressure politics.

If you want credibility, then produce:
- the exact recurring savings gap,
- the exact savings from non-closure measures,
- the exact management contribution,
- the exact owner contribution,
- and the exact residual gap after all alternatives.

Until then, **closure is a threat, not a necessity**.

## To the union
We are aligned on the core red lines:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of protections,
- no unilateral management diktat.

Where I want to sharpen the line is this: **temporary labour contributions must not become permanent wage concessions while management keeps the option to attack the plants later.** Any contribution by the workforce must be legally and politically tied to:
- binding site guarantees,
- binding job guarantees,
- binding investment commitments,
- and a real say in implementation.

If those conditions are not met, then there is nothing to bargain over except resistance.

## To the regional state government
You are right to say that this is not just a financial exercise. It is an industrial and regional policy question. But then act like it.

Your blocking power and shareholder position should be used to prevent plant closures, not to manage them politely. “No closures wherever possible” is too weak if it leaves the door open to shutting plants once the pressure rises.

We need a **binding no-closure track for all German plants through 2030**. Anything less is not security; it is a postponement of insecurity.

## To the controlling family holding
You say you want competitiveness and long-term value. Good. Then stop treating closure as a normal bargaining instrument.

If the economics really require closure, prove it with numbers. If they do not, then support a solution built on:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- labour-cost moderation within a protected framework,
- management overhead cuts,
- and investment in the German footprint.

What I do not accept is a “balanced” position that keeps the threat of closure alive while asking the workforce to prove loyalty under duress.

## To institutional investors
You keep saying you need durable savings and implementation certainty. So do we. The difference is that you appear willing to buy certainty at the price of destroying sites and trust.

That is a bad investment decision if it produces:
- prolonged industrial conflict,
- loss of know-how,
- damage to brand reputation,
- and a broken social partnership that will haunt the company for years.

A stable, negotiated settlement with guaranteed sites and jobs is not “soft.” It is the only credible basis for long-term value.

## On the expert verification proposal
I support independent verification. But I want to be absolutely precise about what it must test.

It must verify:
- savings from working-time changes,
- voluntary exits and attrition,
- management overhead reduction,
- procurement and efficiency measures,
- owner-side support,
- and the residual gap **without assuming closures**.

What I will not accept is an expert process that starts from the assumption that closures are the benchmark and then asks whether the workforce can match them. The benchmark is not “closure economics.” The benchmark is whether competitiveness can be restored **without destroying German plants and jobs**.

## Our position, unchanged and explicit
1. **No German plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **No unilateral dismantling of co-determination or job-security guarantees**
4. **Yes to restructuring**
5. **Yes to negotiated, socially responsible productivity and capacity measures**
6. **Yes to binding investment and 2030 security**
7. **No acceptance of closure as a fallback bargaining weapon**

## What we will support
We are prepared to discuss:
- working-time solutions,
- voluntary instruments,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- productivity improvements,
- management restraint,
- and a time-limited workforce contribution

**only if** the outcome is a binding settlement that keeps every German plant open and rules out forced layoffs.

## What we will not support
We will not support:
- a “conditional” no-closure promise that can be reopened at the next downturn,
- a deal that strips the workforce to buy time for management,
- or any package that weakens the historic job-security framework.

So let me close with this: if management wants a deal, it must abandon the idea that plant closures are its default solution. If it cannot do that, then there is no shared basis here — only conflict, and we are ready for that conflict.
