## To management
I agree with you on one point: **the company needs a durable restructuring, not a cosmetic patch**. I also accept that the German cost base must come down and that the business cannot simply preserve every historic arrangement unchanged.

Where I **do not** agree is with your continued attempt to keep closures and forced redundancies as the “normal” fallback while others are asked to move first. That is the wrong sequencing for this company.

You still have **not** shown:
- the exact recurring savings gap,
- what non-closure measures already cover,
- what management itself will contribute,
- what owners will contribute,
- and what the residual gap is **net of transition costs**.

Without that, “closures may be necessary” remains an assertion, not a proven necessity.

My position remains:
- **no German plant closures as a bargaining premise**
- **no compulsory redundancies as the default instrument**
- **yes to quantified restructuring**
- **yes to management overhead cuts**
- **yes to working-time, productivity, and voluntary measures**
- **yes to burden-sharing from owners and management**

If your package truly restores competitiveness without industrial hollowing-out, I will examine it seriously. If it does not, then I will not back a closure-first logic simply because you repeat it often enough.

## To the works council
I agree with you that restructuring does **not automatically mean closures**. That is a crucial point, and management has not disproven it.

I also agree that the workforce cannot be expected to give concessions while closure is held over its head as leverage. That would poison trust and make a negotiated solution much harder.

Where I want to be careful is this: a blanket “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” stance is only sustainable if it is backed by a **credible, quantified alternative**. I am not asking you to surrender your red lines. I am asking you to make them economically durable.

So I support:
- site protection,
- job protection,
- voluntary instruments,
- working-time solutions,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- and a time-limited workforce contribution

**provided that** management and owners also contribute and the result is genuinely bankable.

## To the union
I agree with your criticism of closure being used as pressure politics. That is exactly why I want the savings gap verified independently rather than argued in slogans.

But I will also say this plainly: a temporary workforce contribution is only acceptable if it is **strictly limited, enforceable, and tied to binding guarantees**. I will not accept a deal where employees pay now and the company keeps future closure options open as an ongoing threat.

So yes to:
- labor-cost moderation,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification,

but only if they are embedded in:
- binding site security,
- binding employment security,
- real investment commitments,
- and co-determination in implementation.

That is the minimum for legitimacy.

## To the controlling family holding
I agree with you that a cosmetic compromise would destroy value. I also agree that the company cannot remain structurally uncompetitive and simply hope for the best.

Where I disagree is with any posture that treats closure as the default expression of seriousness. In this company, closure is not a neutral technical move. It has regional, industrial, and governance consequences, and the special statute exists for a reason.

Your duty is not to maximize pressure; it is to secure the company’s long-term value **without sacrificing its industrial anchor function unnecessarily**.

So I support a serious verification process that tests:
- the no-closure route first,
- then a hybrid route,
- and only then footprint reduction if the residual gap is still substantial.

That is responsible ownership, not weakness.

I remain open to a negotiated restructuring **only if** it protects the company’s role as a regional economic anchor.

That means:
1. **No plant closures as the starting assumption**
2. **No compulsory redundancies as the default tool**
3. **Binding management and owner contributions**
4. **A serious no-closure scenario tested first**
5. **A workforce contribution tied to site and job security**
6. **No weakening of co-determination or the state’s veto role**

- a deal that makes closures the implicit threat behind every negotiation,
- a package that shifts the burden mainly onto employees,
- or a settlement that preserves financial optics while hollowing out the company’s long-term industrial base.
