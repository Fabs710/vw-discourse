We agree on the central diagnosis: **the cost base has to come down materially, and the solution has to be durable**. From a capital-market perspective, that is non-negotiable. If the restructuring only produces temporary relief, or preserves an uncompetitive structure, then it does not create value and we would not support it.

Where we differ is on the acceptable means and on the risk of a package that becomes too diluted to matter.

### What we agree with
- **Management is right** that the current German cost base is too high relative to the market reality.
- **The family and the state are right** that a purely destructive “closure-first” approach can destroy long-term value if it overreaches.
- **The workforce side is right** that a credible settlement needs to be negotiated, not just imposed, because protracted conflict destroys value too.

### Where we disagree
I do **not** support an outcome that simply protects all sites and existing guarantees while asking the company to “somehow” regain competitiveness. That would be a cosmetic settlement, and cosmetic settlements are unacceptable. If the cost base remains structurally uncompetitive, then margins stay impaired and so does the equity value.

I also do **not** support prolonged industrial confrontation as a substitute for restructuring. Strikes, uncertainty, and delayed implementation erode cash flow, damage delivery reliability, and push customers and investors toward alternatives.

### Our threshold
What would move us is not rhetoric about partnership, but a **quantified, credible path back to competitive margins**. Specifically:

1. **A clear savings target**
   - How much annual cost reduction is required?
   - What share is locked in structurally, not just for one contract cycle?

2. **A credible implementation path**
   - Which measures are binding?
   - What is the timetable?
   - What happens if savings are not delivered?

3. **Evidence that the plan restores margins**
   - We want to see a realistic route back to the return-on-sales target.
   - If the numbers do not close, then the plan is not financeable in the market.

### On closures
We are not ideologically attached to plant closures. But we also do not accept them being ruled out in advance if they are the only way to achieve the required savings. The key issue is not whether a closure is politically uncomfortable; it is whether the final package actually solves the economic problem.

If equivalent structural savings can be achieved through:
- capacity reduction,
- working-time changes,
- voluntary exits,
- labour-cost moderation,
- redeployment,
- and binding productivity gains,

then that is preferable. But “equivalent” means equivalent in scale, durability, and certainty — not “roughly helpful.”

### On employee contributions
If the workforce is asking for site and job security, then the contribution must be real and measurable. We would view as credible:
- meaningful and durable labour-cost reduction,
- concrete flexibility on working time,
- voluntary rather than forced staffing reductions,
- and a disciplined productivity programme.

What would not be credible is a deal that preserves all guarantees, avoids all hard choices, and merely postpones the restructuring problem.

### Our bottom line
We are prepared to back a negotiated solution **if and only if** it produces:
- **material, durable cost reduction**
- **a credible return to competitive margins**
- **deal certainty without endless conflict**

If the proposal remains vague, politically softened, or economically underpowered, then we would regard it as value-destructive and would not support it.

So the question back to management and the other stakeholders is simple:  
**Show us the numbers, show us the binding measures, and show us how the plan restores competitiveness.** Otherwise, this is not a restructuring — it is delay.
