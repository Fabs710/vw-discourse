Management is still trying to turn a structural strategy problem into a workforce sacrifice problem. That will not work.

I will respond directly to each party.

### To management
You say the cost base is too high and that “equivalent” savings to closures must be shown. Fine — show them. But do not pretend that closures and forced layoffs are the only serious instruments. That is simply not true, and the burden is on management to prove otherwise.

What I reject is this framing:
- first announce the threat of closures,
- then demand that the workforce “prove” an alternative under pressure,
- and finally keep the right to close plants anyway if we do not surrender enough.

That is not a negotiation. That is coercion.

If you want a serious discussion, then put the exact figures on the table:
- required annual savings,
- required capacity reduction,
- the share attributable to labour,
- the share expected from management overhead,
- the share expected from owners,
- and the investment commitments attached to any deal.

Until then, your closure talk is a threat, not an argument.

### To the union
We are aligned on the red lines: no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no dismantling of protections. I support your readiness to discuss working-time solutions, voluntary instruments, and labour-cost moderation only if they are part of a binding package that protects sites and jobs.

But I want to be very clear: “temporary contributions” must not become a permanent extraction from the workforce while management keeps the strategic freedom to cut later. Any labour contribution must be matched by:
- binding site guarantees,
- binding job guarantees,
- binding investment,
- and a real co-determination role in implementation.

### To the regional state government
I appreciate that you are not endorsing plant destruction. But I will not let this be turned into a managed retreat. “Capacity reductions without closures wherever possible” is only acceptable if it truly means no German plant closure and no forced layoffs. If that guarantee is not explicit, then it is too weak.

If the state is serious, then use your shareholder and political leverage to insist on:
- a 2030 site and employment pact,
- investment in German plants,
- and protection of industrial competencies in the region.

### To the controlling family holding
You say you do not want value destruction through closures. Good. Then stop leaving the closure option as a standing threat in the room.

A “credible industrial settlement” does not require us to concede plant closure as a normal bargaining chip. If the economic case for closure is so weak that you prefer alternatives, then those alternatives should be built around **binding guarantees**, not around keeping the sword over the workforce’s head.

### To institutional investors
You are right about one thing: delay and vague promises destroy value. But a settlement that destroys jobs, tears up historic protections, and provokes prolonged conflict also destroys value. You do not get sustainable margins on top of industrial unrest and broken trust.

If you want financial credibility, then support a package that actually stabilises the company:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- negotiated capacity and working-time solutions,
- real investment,
- and long-term labour peace through fair treatment.

### Our position
Let me restate it plainly:

1. **No German plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **No unilateral breaking of the job-security framework**
4. **Yes to negotiated, socially responsible restructuring**
5. **Yes to productivity improvements, redeployment, qualification, and voluntary instruments**
6. **Yes to a genuine industrial strategy with investment and co-determination**
7. **No acceptance of closures as an “if needed” fallback**

If management believes there is a savings gap, then it must close that gap with non-destructive measures and shared responsibility. We will not sign off on destroying sites and jobs to solve a problem created by strategic and market misjudgments at the top.

And let me be explicit: if this continues down the path of unilateralism, we will continue to mobilise the workforce. We have already shown that we can do that. The company should not mistake restraint for weakness.
