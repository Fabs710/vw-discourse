I will respond plainly.

## To management
I agree with you on the **need for structural cost reduction**. I also agree that a cosmetic deal that leaves the German cost base fundamentally uncompetitive would destroy value over time. That would be unacceptable.

Where I disagree is your tendency to keep **closures as the implicit default answer** before the economics have been fully tested. In a company like this, with co-determination, state involvement, and a controlling owner responsible for long-term value, a closure-first posture is not just politically difficult — it is governance-poor unless it is proven necessary.

You still have **not** given a properly verified bridge:
- exact recurring savings target,
- what non-closure measures already deliver,
- what management overhead cuts deliver,
- what owner-side contribution is realistic,
- and what residual gap remains **net of transition costs**.

Without that, “closures may be necessary” is still an assertion. I am not prepared to let an assertion become the basis for permanent industrial damage.

## To the works council
I understand and respect your red lines around no closures and no compulsory redundancies. I also agree that the workforce cannot be made the sole adjustment variable for management’s strategic mistakes.

Where I disagree is with the implication that those red lines alone can resolve the economic problem. They cannot. If the business still cannot reach competitiveness after all non-closure measures are counted, then the enterprise will continue to erode, and that ultimately hurts everyone, including the workforce.

So I support your insistence on:
- quantified savings,
- management and owner contributions,
- investment commitments,
- and site protection,

but I do **not** accept a framework that excludes any serious footprint adjustment in advance, regardless of the numbers.

## To the union
You are right that temporary contributions must not become permanent wage dumping. A workforce contribution has to buy something durable:
- site security,
- job security,
- investment,
- and a real future for the company.

Where I disagree is your attempt to make “no closures” a categorical starting point. That may be your bargaining line, but it is not a sufficient governance position for the owners if the residual gap remains large.

If a non-closure package can close the gap with enough certainty and durability, I would prefer it. But preference is not the same as proof. We need proof.

## To the regional state government
I agree that closures are not a desirable first resort and that regional industrial capability matters. I also agree that a destructive move would damage trust and the company’s ecosystem.

But I do **not** agree that the state can simply block any serious restructuring logic by insisting on no closures as a political premise. The state’s role should be to ensure that alternatives are thoroughly tested, not to pre-judge the outcome.

If non-closure measures can deliver the required recurring savings, then yes — that is the better path. If they cannot, then refusing to face that reality would be irresponsible stewardship, not protection.

## To institutional investors
You are right to demand durable savings and implementation certainty. You are also right that vague promises and prolonged conflict destroy value.

Where I disagree is any notion that you can get a stable outcome by simply demanding hard cuts without regard to implementation reality. This is a governed industrial company, not a purely financial asset. A settlement that looks clean on paper but detonates labour peace will not protect value.

So I support a hard financial test, but not an ideologically closure-driven one.

## What I want now
We need to stop debating slogans and test the numbers properly.

[SUMMON: independent restructuring verification panel to assess recurring savings, transition costs, management/owner contributions, and the residual gap under a no-closure scenario]

That panel must determine:
- the recurring annual savings target,
- the savings achievable from labour measures,
- the savings from management and overhead measures,
- the contribution expected from owners,
- the transition costs of each scenario,
- and the remaining structural gap if closures are excluded.

Only then can anyone responsibly say whether plant closure is truly unavoidable or simply being used as leverage.

## My position
I am prepared to support a **non-closure restructuring** if it is:
- quantitatively verified,
- durable,
- large enough to restore margins,
- and accompanied by labour peace and investment discipline.

I am **not** prepared to support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- an open-ended industrial conflict,
- a permanent impairment of the company’s value,
- or a settlement that preserves all protections while leaving the business structurally uncompetitive.

And I am also **not** prepared to support a rushed closure-first move without proof that the non-closure path cannot work.

That is the line. Bring the numbers.
