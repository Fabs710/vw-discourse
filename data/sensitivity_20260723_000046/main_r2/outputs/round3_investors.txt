We appreciate the repeated emphasis on verification, but we need to be very clear about our position: **a non-closure outcome is acceptable only if it is economically equivalent in recurring savings, durability, and certainty**. We are not here to defend closures as a principle, and we are not here to defend preserving every legacy protection either. We are here to protect risk-adjusted equity value.

## Where we agree

### With management
We agree that:
- the cost base is structurally too high,
- the company needs durable savings,
- temporary fixes or symbolic concessions are not enough,
- and the return-on-sales target cannot be restored by wishful thinking.

We also agree that management should not be forced into a settlement that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

### With the controlling family holding
We agree that:
- a cosmetic compromise would destroy value over time,
- permanent industrial damage is undesirable,
- and any move toward closures must be economically justified, not used as theater.

### With the expert
We strongly agree that the dispute cannot be resolved responsibly without a quantified bridge:
- current cost base
- target sustainable cost base
- savings by measure
- transition costs
- residual gap

That is the right basis for decision-making.

## Where we disagree

### With the works council and union
We do **not** accept a blanket “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” posture as a starting point. From a shareholder perspective, that is only acceptable if you can prove that alternative measures deliver:
- comparable recurring savings,
- operationally credible capacity reduction,
- and durable margin improvement.

At present, we have not seen that proof.

We also do **not** accept prolonged strike risk as a negotiating tactic. Industrial conflict directly destroys cash flow, customer confidence, and equity value. A deal that avoids closures but triggers extended disruption may still be value-destructive.

### With the regional state government
We understand the political and regional importance of the German footprint. But we do **not** support treating closures as politically off-limits in advance if the economics ultimately require footprint reduction. The state can insist on scrutiny and safeguards; it cannot manufacture competitiveness by vetoing hard choices.

## What we need to see
The debate needs to move from posture to numbers.

### [SUMMON: independent restructuring and value-impact verification panel]
We want an independent panel to verify:
- recurring annual savings required to restore competitiveness,
- savings from labour measures,
- savings from overhead and operational efficiency,
- savings from owner-side support and governance restraint,
- transition and restructuring costs,
- and the residual gap under a no-closure scenario.

Crucially, that panel should also assess:
- timing of savings,
- certainty of delivery,
- and the margin impact over the next 2–3 years.

## Our financial test
We would support a negotiated non-closure settlement only if it meets all of the following:
1. **durable recurring savings**
2. **material capacity reduction**
3. **credible implementation certainty**
4. **clear return to competitive margins**
5. **no open-ended labour conflict**
6. **net economic improvement after transition costs**

If the package cannot meet those tests, then it is not a shareholder-friendly compromise, even if it avoids plant closures.

## Our position on the possible path forward
We are open to:
- voluntary exits,
- working-time changes,
- targeted labour-cost moderation,
- management overhead cuts,
- operational simplification,
- and a time-limited, verified restructuring package.

We are **not** open to:
- a settlement that preserves structural overcapacity,
- a package that is financially soft but politically comfortable,
- or a deal that trades temporary peace for lasting margin impairment.

## Bottom line
We are not ideologically attached to closures.  
We are attached to **economic outcomes**.

If the non-closure route can genuinely restore competitiveness, it deserves support. If it cannot, then refusing to face that fact would be irresponsible. The only acceptable basis for judgment is a verified, recurring-savings comparison — not slogans, not moral pressure, and not bargaining theater.
