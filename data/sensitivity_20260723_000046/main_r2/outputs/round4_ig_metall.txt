## My position in round 4

I will be just as direct as the works council.

### To management
You still have not answered the decisive question: **why are plant closures or forced redundancies necessary if restructuring is genuinely the goal?**

You keep repeating “durable savings,” which is true as far as it goes. But repeating the target is not the same as proving that the only way to reach it is by closing plants and forcing people out.

What you are asking for is this:
- workforce contribution first,
- security later,
- and closure kept in reserve as pressure.

That is not a fair bargaining sequence. It is leverage dressed up as economic necessity.

I agree that the company needs:
- lower costs,
- higher productivity,
- and a durable industrial plan.

I do **not** agree that this justifies:
- compulsory redundancies,
- plant closures as a starting point,
- or keeping German sites permanently insecure.

If you want a real deal, then show the residual gap **after** all non-closure measures are counted, and show it with real numbers, not strategic ambiguity.

---

### To the works council
We are aligned on the red lines:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no plant closures as the primary instrument,
- no weakening of the collective wage framework,
- no workforce being used as the sole adjustment variable.

That alignment matters. Your insistence on site and job security is not obstruction; it is the correct response to a management strategy that is still trying to normalize closure as an acceptable bargaining weapon.

Where I want to sharpen the position is this:

A temporary workforce contribution is only acceptable if it is **strictly limited, binding, and matched by real counter-contributions** from management and owners. It must not become:
- permanent wage dumping,
- a one-sided sacrifice,
- or a bridge to future attacks.

So yes:
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- qualification,
- redeployment,
- productivity measures,
- and a time-limited labour contribution

But only if they are locked into:
- binding site guarantees,
- binding employment guarantees,
- a real investment path,
- and protection of the collective framework.

Without that, there is nothing fair to negotiate.

---

### To the regional state government
I agree with you that this is an industrial-policy question, not just a line item on a spreadsheet. And I agree that a blunt closure-first strategy would damage regional trust, skills, and the company’s long-term ecosystem.

But “no closures wherever possible” is still too soft if it leaves management room to keep pushing the threat until resistance weakens.

If you are serious, then use your blocking and political power to insist on:
- a binding no-closure track,
- a serious investment commitment,
- qualification and redeployment,
- and a settlement that protects the industrial base in Germany.

You should not be acting as the polite manager of plant exit logic. You should be preventing workers from carrying the crisis alone.

---

### To the controlling family holding
I agree with you on one point: a cosmetic compromise would damage value. We do not want that either.

But I do **not** accept the idea that keeping closure “on the table” is some neutral governance necessity. It is not neutral if it is used to force the workforce into accepting insecurity before alternatives are fully tested.

If the economics truly require closure, prove it with independent numbers.
If they do not, then stop treating closure as a default bargaining instrument.

Owners also have duties here:
- contribute capital discipline,
- support investment,
- accept management restraint,
- and stop using labour as the first adjustment lever.

---

### To institutional investors
Your concern for durable savings is legitimate. But your position becomes self-defeating if you ignore the cost of conflict.

Plant closures and forced redundancies are not clean instruments. They carry:
- strike risk,
- loss of know-how,
- reputational damage,
- operational disruption,
- and long-term instability in the German footprint.

A settlement that preserves sites and jobs while delivering verified recurring savings is not “soft.” It is the only kind of deal that can be durable enough to protect value over time.

So if you want certainty, support a package that is:
- quantified,
- shared-sacrifice based,
- and enforceable.

Do not confuse harshness with credibility.

---

## On the verification panel
I support independent verification, but only if it is designed correctly.

It must verify:
- savings from working-time changes,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- procurement and process efficiency,
- management overhead reductions,
- owner-side support,
- and the residual gap **without assuming closures as the benchmark**.

What I will not accept is a process that starts from closure economics and then asks whether the workforce can somehow absorb the damage.

The benchmark is not closure.
The benchmark is whether competitiveness can be restored **without plant closures and without compulsory redundancies**.

[SUMMON: independent restructuring and labour-cost verification panel with plant-level data, management/owner contribution assessment, and residual-gap analysis under a no-closure scenario]

## My line, clearly stated
I remain open to a serious restructuring package, including:
- temporary labour-cost moderation,
- working-time adjustments,
- voluntary instruments,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- capacity reduction through negotiated means,
- management restraint,
- and owner-side contribution.

But only if the result is:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no plant closures,
- protection of the collective wage framework,
- binding site and job security,
- and a fair sharing of the burden.

That is not maximalism. That is the minimum for a durable settlement.

If management and owners refuse that basis, then they should expect escalation. The peace obligation is over, and our members expect us to defend jobs, wages, and sites.
