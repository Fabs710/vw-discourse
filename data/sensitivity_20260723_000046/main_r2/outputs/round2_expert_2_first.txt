## Independent restructuring assessment

The specific question is whether management has already established, on the information given, that **plant closures are economically necessary**, or whether a **non-closure restructuring could still plausibly close the savings gap**.

### Short answer
**No verified conclusion yet that closures are unavoidable.**  
Based on the record provided, management has stated a structural problem, but it has **not yet supplied the quantified bridge** needed to prove that non-closure measures are insufficient.

## What is verified from the scenario
1. **A restructuring need exists.**  
   The company faces pressure from:
   - weak European demand,
   - intense competition,
   - an elevated German cost base,
   - and missing profitability targets.

2. **The conflict is about instruments, not the existence of a problem.**  
   All parties appear to accept that adjustment is necessary.

3. **The governance structure makes forced implementation difficult.**  
   Co-determination, the controlling shareholder block, and the state shareholder all create effective veto points. So any durable solution must be negotiated.

4. **The workforce side is rejecting closures and compulsory redundancies.**  
   That is a hard bargaining constraint, not a minor objection.

## What is not yet verified
Management has **not** shown:

- the exact annual recurring savings gap,
- how much of that gap is already covered by non-closure measures,
- the net savings from labour-cost moderation,
- the savings from management overhead cuts,
- the impact of voluntary exits and redeployment,
- the residual gap after those measures,
- and whether that residual gap is large enough to justify closures.

Without that decomposition, “closures are necessary” remains **an assertion, not a demonstrated fact**.

## Economic assessment
From a restructuring perspective:

- **Non-closure measures can be material** in German automotive restructuring.
- But they are often **limited in speed, certainty, and magnitude**.
- If the company truly has **large, persistent overcapacity**, then labour measures alone may not be enough.
- If the gap is **moderate**, a negotiated package with working-time changes, voluntary exits, wage moderation, overhead cuts, and investment commitments could plausibly substitute for closures.

So the decisive issue is not ideology; it is whether the **residual gap after all alternatives** remains large and durable enough to require footprint reduction.

## Verification standard I would apply
A closure case would require, at minimum:

1. **Current cost base by site and function**
2. **Target cost base required for competitiveness**
3. **Recurring savings by measure**
4. **Transition costs**
5. **Volume and utilization forecasts**
6. **Sensitivity analysis under downside demand scenarios**

Only if the residual gap remains substantial after all realistic non-closure measures would closures become economically credible as a last-resort instrument.

## My conclusion
At this stage, the available information supports the following expert view:

- **Restructuring is necessary.**
- **Closure may be one possible instrument.**
- **But closure is not yet proven to be unavoidable.**
- A **quantified non-closure alternative** could still be feasible if it delivers comparable recurring savings, sufficient durability, and credible implementation.

## What I would need to verify next
To move from debate to conclusion, I would need:

- site-level headcount and cost data,
- labour-cost assumptions,
- expected savings from each proposed alternative,
- planned capacity reductions,
- severance and transition costs,
- and the company’s volume forecast through 2030.

If you provide that, I can test whether the proposed package is sufficient or whether a residual structural gap remains that materially supports closure.
