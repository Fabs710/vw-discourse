We share the assessment that the company is under real pressure and that a durable solution has to be negotiated, not imposed.

But I need to be equally clear: **closures, compulsory redundancies, and the dismantling of the collective wage framework are not acceptable starting points**. If management is presenting those as the only route, then that is not a restructuring plan we can support — it is a transfer of the entire crisis burden onto the workforce.

What we can discuss, and what we have already signalled openness to, is a **fair shared-sacrifice package** that protects jobs and sites while delivering real cost relief. That can include:

- **temporary or structured labour-cost contributions**
- **reduced capacity through socially responsible measures**
- **voluntary instruments instead of forced layoffs**
- **working-time solutions**
- **renunciation of bonuses or other cost components**
- **binding investment commitments for future products and technologies**

But any such contribution from employees must be matched by **management, owners, and the company itself**. It cannot be the case that workers give up pay, security, and guarantees while others demand “competitiveness” without making comparable concessions or taking responsibility for prior strategic decisions.

So our position is:

1. **No plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **No unilateral breaking of existing protections**
4. **Yes to negotiated capacity and cost measures, if and only if they come with site security, job security, and a credible industrial future**

If management wants a settlement, it must present a package that preserves the German industrial base and treats the workforce as a partner, not as the only cost factor to be cut. If not, it should expect resistance.
