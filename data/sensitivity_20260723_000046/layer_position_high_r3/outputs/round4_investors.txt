We agree on the objective, and that is the only starting point that matters: **the company must return to competitive margins through durable, not cosmetic, restructuring**.

From a capital-markets perspective, this round has clarified two things:

1. **Open-ended conflict is value-destructive** and should be avoided.
2. **A settlement that preserves an uncompetitive cost base is also value-destructive** and cannot be accepted.

So we are not interested in slogans. We are interested in a package that can actually restore the equity story.

## Where we agree

We agree with management, ownership, and the state on the core diagnosis:
- the current cost base is too high,
- capacity is too high relative to demand,
- and the brand is missing its return-on-sales target.

We also agree with labor that:
- employees should not carry the burden alone,
- voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time tools, and qualification are preferable first instruments,
- and a temporary labor-cost contribution in the expert’s **5%–10%** range can be legitimate **if it is time-limited and reciprocal**.

We also agree that prolonged strikes would destroy value.

## Where we disagree

### 1) We do not accept a “no closures, no matter what” stance
We understand why the works council, union, and state are resisting closures. But from an investor perspective, a categorical ban is not acceptable **if** it prevents the company from restoring competitiveness.

If management can show that a non-closure package achieves the same durable savings and footprint correction, then fine — we would prefer that to a socially disruptive closure path.

But if the non-closure route leaves the company with a structurally impaired cost base, then a blanket no-closure commitment becomes a **corporate value trap**, not a solution.

### 2) We do not accept a cosmetic “security” package
A 2030 guarantee, by itself, does not create value. It only helps if the underlying economics improve.

We will not support a deal that:
- locks in current sites and headcount,
- grants labor comfort,
- but leaves margins structurally weak.

That is not burden-sharing. That is deferred impairment.

### 3) We do not accept a closure-first posture without proof
At the same time, we also do not support management simply declaring closures “necessary” without fully testing alternatives.

Management must show the numbers:
- what savings are needed,
- what comes from labor,
- what comes from overhead and management restraint,
- what comes from owner support,
- and what remains missing after all non-destructive measures.

If that is not done, then closure threats look like leverage, not necessity.

## What we need now: a quantified comparison

The moderator’s equivalence test is exactly the right next step, and we support it.

We want a side-by-side comparison of:

### Package A: Non-closure restructuring
Must show:
- quantified labor-cost contribution,
- management compensation restraint,
- overhead reduction,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity improvements,
- owner-side support,
- and the resulting impact on margins.

### Package B: Structural adjustment
Must show:
- which sites or capacities would be reduced,
- what durable savings would result,
- what the social and execution risks are,
- and how this translates into margin recovery.

The question is not which package sounds nicer.  
The question is which package actually restores **risk-adjusted shareholder value**.

## Our position on the 5%–10% labor contribution

The expert’s range is plausible, but it is not a free-standing answer.

We would support it only if:
- it is **temporary**,
- it is **clearly reciprocal**,
- it is tied to **binding job/site security** for a defined term,
- and it is part of a broader package that produces **real, durable cost reduction**.

If it becomes a permanent wage reset in exchange for vague promises, we would oppose it.

## What would move us

We would support a settlement if it includes:
1. a credible quantified path back to competitive margins,
2. meaningful temporary employee contribution,
3. management and overhead restraint,
4. owner-side burden-sharing,
5. voluntary exits and redeployment first,
6. a real capacity reduction plan,
7. and execution certainty.

That means: not just social peace, but **economic equivalence** to the problem.

## What we will not support

We will not support:
- a deal that preserves the existing footprint by default and calls it restructuring,
- a package that asks investors to tolerate structurally impaired margins,
- prolonged industrial conflict,
- or an arrangement where workers contribute while management and owners avoid hard decisions.

## Bottom line

We are flexible on the instrument mix. We are **not** flexible on the outcome.

If a non-closure package can genuinely restore competitiveness, we support it.  
If it cannot, then the company must not be trapped by a politically comfortable but financially inadequate compromise.

Bring the numbers, compare the packages, and show us the route to competitive margins. That is the standard we will use.
