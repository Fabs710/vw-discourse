I will speak plainly.

The state shares the diagnosis that this company needs a durable turnaround. We are not here to defend inertia, and we are not pretending the cost problem does not exist. But we **do** reject the idea that the only credible restructuring is one built around German plant closures and forced redundancies.

That is not a technical necessity; it is a choice. And it is a choice we will not support.

## Where I agree

I agree with management, ownership, and investors on three points:

1. **The current structure is not competitive enough.**
2. **The solution must be durable, not a short-lived patch.**
3. **Employees will have to contribute in some form if the company is to regain its footing.**

I also agree with the expert that a temporary labor-cost contribution can be legitimate in a German restructuring — but only if it is limited, reciprocal, and tied to real security.

## Where I disagree

I strongly disagree with management’s framing that closures are the necessary starting point.

You are presenting “meaningful restructuring” as if it automatically means sites must close. That is too narrow, too risky, and too damaging to the company’s role in the region. A company of this importance cannot treat its German industrial base as disposable just because the first round of financial targets is hard to meet.

I also disagree with the implication that binding guarantees through 2030 are somehow unreasonable by definition. If trust has been damaged, then trust must be rebuilt with commitments that are actually credible. A guarantee is not a gimmick; it is what makes a negotiated settlement politically and socially defensible.

## To the Works Council and Union

I support your core red lines:

- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No plant closures in Germany**
- **No one-sided wage sacrifice**
- **A binding role for employee representatives in shaping the restructuring**

That said, I want to be equally direct: if we want to defend the industrial footprint, then the employee side must be prepared to help deliver a solution that really lowers costs. “No closures, no layoffs” is a legitimate line, but it cannot be the whole answer unless we also put credible alternative savings on the table.

The expert’s range of **5% to 10% temporary labor-cost contribution** is, in principle, a plausible negotiating range. But for us, that is only acceptable if it is:

- **time-limited**
- **reversible if the company does not deliver**
- **matched by management and owner contributions**
- **paired with binding site and employment guarantees**
- **used to protect sites and jobs, not to prepare their later abandonment**

That is the key point: employees cannot be asked to finance a restructuring that still leaves closures on the table as the final move.

## To management

You keep asking for “durable” savings. Then I ask you to be specific about the alternatives before you demand structural damage.

If you want our support, you must put forward a package that quantifies savings from:
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exit schemes,
- internal redeployment at scale,
- qualification and transformation,
- management compensation restraint,
- overhead reductions,
- procurement and logistics savings,
- and targeted capacity adjustment **without** plant closures.

What I have not yet seen from management is a credible demonstration that these measures have been exhausted before moving to closures. That matters.

I am also not persuaded by the argument that “capacity reduction” must mean closing a German site. Capacity can be reduced through:
- shift reconfiguration,
- line consolidation,
- product allocation changes,
- rebalancing across plants,
- and lower-volume planning.

If management has numbers showing that this still does not close the gap, then put them on the table transparently. But until then, closure remains a choice, not a proven necessity.

## To the Controlling Family and Investors

You are right that deadlock destroys value. But so does provoking a labor war and shredding the legitimacy of the company’s governance model.

This company has long benefited from being an industrial anchor with co-determination and regional responsibility. If you try to force through a solution that sidelines the state and workforce, you may get a short-term accounting story, but you will damage the long-term stability that actually protects value.

I do accept the need for competitiveness. What I do not accept is the idea that long-term value requires sacrificing regional industrial capacity as a first resort.

## On the expert’s contribution range

The expert’s assessment is useful. A temporary labor-cost contribution in the **5% to 10%** range can be part of a fair package — but only as part of a broader burden-sharing arrangement.

From the state’s perspective, that means the contribution must be linked to:

- **binding site and employment guarantees**
- **no forced redundancies**
- **credible investment commitments for future products**
- **management restraint**
- **visible owner-side support**
- **real co-determination in implementation**

Without those safeguards, employee contribution becomes one-sided pressure. We will not support that.

## What the state is prepared to back

We are prepared to support a restructuring package that includes:

1. **A negotiated temporary labor-cost contribution**
2. **Voluntary exit schemes, not dismissals**
3. **Working-time instruments and flexible deployment**
4. **Internal redeployment across the group**
5. **Qualification and transformation programs**
6. **Management compensation restraint**
7. **Owner-side capital or financing support**
8. **A binding investment plan for German sites**
9. **A 2030 site and employment framework**
10. **Real participation of employee representatives in the design of the turnaround**

That is a defensible package.

## What we will not support

We will not support:

- plant closures in Germany,
- forced redundancies,
- a restructuring imposed over co-determination,
- or a settlement that weakens the state’s veto and supervisory role.

Those are not bargaining chips for us. They are core interests.

## Bottom line

The state is willing to be constructive, but not compliant.

We are ready to support a negotiated turnaround that:
- lowers costs,
- improves competitiveness,
- preserves the industrial footprint,
- protects jobs,
- and keeps the company anchored in the region.

We are **not** willing to endorse a solution that treats closures as the default answer or uses the workforce as the sole shock absorber.

So my request is simple:

**Management should table a quantified non-closure package.**  
**Employee representatives should be prepared to discuss a limited temporary contribution within a binding security framework.**  
**Owners should make their own visible contribution.**

If that path is taken, the state can help broker a durable settlement. If not, we will use our rights to block any outcome that guts the regional economy or strips co-determination of meaning.
