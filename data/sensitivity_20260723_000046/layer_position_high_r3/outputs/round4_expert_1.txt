## Expert assessment

From an industrial-relations and bargaining-design standpoint, the key question is not whether a temporary labor-cost contribution is “fair” in the abstract, but **what level is plausibly negotiable as part of a balanced restructuring package**.

### Short answer
A **plausible temporary employee contribution** in a contested German automotive restructuring is typically in the range of:

- **about 5% of direct labor cost** as a lower bargaining floor,
- **up to roughly 8%–10%** as an upper plausible range,
- **only if** it is clearly **time-limited, reciprocal, and linked to binding security and investment commitments**.

If the package offers **weak guarantees**, no visible management/owner burden-sharing, or leaves plant closures and compulsory redundancies fully open, then even **5%** may be too much to ask.  
If the package includes **strong no-closure / no-forced-layoff commitments**, owner support, and management restraint, then **5%–8%** is much more realistic than 10%.

## How I would frame “fair” in bargaining terms

A fair temporary labor contribution is usually judged against four criteria:

1. **Magnitude**
   - Large enough to matter financially.
   - Not so large that it becomes a disguised permanent wage reset.

2. **Duration**
   - Temporary, typically **24–48 months** in restructuring practice.
   - Longer terms become much harder to justify without very strong reciprocal benefits.

3. **Reciprocity**
   - Workers give up something only if they receive something concrete:
     - site security,
     - no forced redundancies,
     - voluntary-only exit rules,
     - investment commitments,
     - retraining/redeployment,
     - management pay restraint.

4. **Reversibility / conditionality**
   - If management breaches the deal, the labor concession should expire or roll back automatically.

## What the 5%–10% range means in practice

This range should be understood as **direct labor-cost relief**, not necessarily as a simple cut in base wages. It can be assembled from different tools, for example:

- delayed or reduced wage increases,
- temporary working-time reduction without full compensation,
- suspension of allowances or bonuses,
- shorter hours with partial pay adjustment,
- conversion of one-off payments into restructuring funding,
- deferred vacation or shift-premium changes,
- temporary reduction of special payments.

That matters because in German bargaining practice, a package that is politically acceptable often comes as a **mix of instruments**, not a blunt wage cut.

## My view on where the fair point likely sits

If I had to distinguish likely bargaining zones:

### 1) Low-concession, strong-security package
If labor gets:
- binding no-closure commitments,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- genuine investment pledges,
- management restraint,
- and owner support,

then a labor contribution around **5%** of direct labor cost is plausibly fair.

### 2) Medium-concession, medium-security package
If security is credible but not absolute, and the company still needs measurable flexibility, then **6%–8%** is more realistic.

### 3) Weak-security package
If management insists on keeping closures and layoffs available as active tools, then even **5%** becomes difficult to defend internally. In that scenario, labor would reasonably argue that it is being asked to pay for uncertainty without getting security in return.

### 4) Very high concession package
Above **8%–10%**, the burden starts to look less like a temporary contribution and more like a structural wage reset. That would normally require:
- unusually strong guarantees,
- a clear crisis context,
- and highly visible contributions from management, owners, and possibly the state.

## Comparison with management-side and owner-side measures

A fair settlement in a German restructuring is rarely judged solely by labor’s concession. The broader test is whether the burden is **shared**.

### Management-side measures that should normally accompany labor concessions
A credible management-side package usually includes:
- executive pay restraint,
- reduced bonus pools,
- overhead cuts,
- simplification of layers and functions,
- procurement and non-labor savings,
- reduced external consulting and contractors,
- capital discipline and deferred nonessential spending.

In bargaining logic, if employees are asked for a 5%–10% temporary contribution, management should usually show a **visible, quantified contribution too**, not just symbolic restraint.

### Owner-side measures
Owners can contribute through:
- capital support,
- liquidity backstops,
- tolerance for lower near-term distributions,
- governance commitments supporting the negotiated turnaround,
- acceptance of a longer recovery horizon.

In a contested restructuring, owner-side measures are important because they signal that the burden is not being pushed downward entirely onto labor.

## What would be a balanced package?

A bargaining-plausible package would often have this shape:

- **Employees:** temporary contribution of **5%–8%**, possibly up to **10%** if security is strong;
- **Management:** visible overhead and compensation restraint;
- **Owners:** financial support and willingness to absorb lower returns for a period;
- **Company:** voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, and working-time tools;
- **Guarantees:** no forced redundancies, no closures for a defined period, or at minimum a tightly controlled review mechanism.

## Bottom line

My expert judgment is:

- **5%** is a credible **starting point** for a temporary employee contribution.
- **6%–8%** is often the most plausible **settlement zone** in a serious restructuring.
- **8%–10%** can be justified only if the reciprocal protections are strong and the company is genuinely in a severe turnaround situation.
- Beyond that, the proposal begins to look less like a temporary labor-cost contribution and more like a permanent transfer of restructuring risk onto employees.
