I will respond directly, because we are no longer in the phase for rhetorical red lines. We are in the phase where this company either agrees a **real structural reset** or drifts into prolonged conflict and value destruction.

## First: what I agree with

I agree with all parties on three points:

1. **The company needs a durable turnaround, not a cosmetic patch.**
2. **Employees should not carry the full burden alone.**
3. **Open-ended conflict would damage output, reputation, and enterprise value.**

I also acknowledge the expert’s point: a **temporary labor-cost contribution in the 5%–10% range of direct labor cost** can be a legitimate bargaining basis **if** it is time-limited, reciprocal, and tied to real security and implementation.

So let me be clear: I am not rejecting employee contribution. I am rejecting a package that is **too small, too vague, or too protective of an uncompetitive footprint** to solve the actual problem.

## Where I disagree — sharply

### 1) I do not accept “all plants stay open” as a starting condition
That is not a restructuring principle. It is a veto on restructuring.

We have a structural overcapacity problem. German factory costs are far above target. The European market is shrinking. Competition is intensifying. The brand is missing its return-on-sales target.

In that context, saying “all German plants remain open” before any quantified alternative is even presented is not a serious economic answer. It is a demand to preserve today’s footprint regardless of whether it can compete.

If the workforce and the state want no closures, then they must show how the same permanent savings and capacity correction are achieved by other means. So far, I have heard assertions, not a bankable equivalence.

### 2) I do not accept a blanket ban on compulsory redundancies
I prefer voluntary tools first. I have said that before, and I repeat it. But I will not sign away the company’s ability to act if voluntary measures fail to close the gap.

If management is forced to rule out every form of compulsory adjustment in advance, then the restructuring loses credibility and execution power. That is not a sustainable settlement; it is a freeze.

### 3) I do not accept a 2030 guarantee if it locks in an uncompetitive structure
A guarantee has value only if it is compatible with competitiveness. Otherwise it becomes a promise to protect cost and capacity indefinitely, which simply postpones the next crisis.

Trust must be rebuilt, yes. But trust without performance is not a turnaround. It is sentiment.

## On the requested quantified alternatives

The moderator asked for concrete packages. That is the right direction. Here is the truth:

### Package A: Non-closure restructuring
This can be considered only if it delivers:
- a **meaningful temporary labor-cost contribution**,
- **measurable overhead and management cost reductions**,
- **voluntary exits at scale**,
- **internal transfer of volumes and labor**,
- **working-time changes**, and
- **clear, enforceable capacity reductions**.

But I will be frank: unless this package produces **durable footprint and cost effects comparable to closure-based adjustment**, it will not be enough.

### Package B: Structural adjustment with footprint reduction
This is the more direct route to restoring competitiveness if Package A cannot close the gap. It would combine:
- targeted plant or line closures, or equivalent structural downsizing,
- stronger and more certain cost reduction,
- voluntary exits and social cushioning,
- a clear investment plan for the retained footprint,
- and tighter execution discipline.

I am not saying Package B is pleasant. I am saying it may be necessary. And I will not disarm management’s ability to pursue it if Package A is not economically equivalent.
