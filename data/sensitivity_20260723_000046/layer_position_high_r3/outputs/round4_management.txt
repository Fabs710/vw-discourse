I will respond plainly.

## Where I agree
I agree with the union, works council, state, owners, and investors on one central point: **this company needs a durable restructuring, not a cosmetic patch**.

I also agree that:
- open conflict would destroy value,
- employees should not carry the full burden alone,
- voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, and working-time tools must be part of the answer,
- and a **temporary labor-cost contribution in the 5%–10% range** can be a legitimate bargaining basis **if** it is reciprocal and time-limited.

So let me be clear: I am not rejecting contributions from management, owners, or employees. I am rejecting a package that is **too small, too vague, or too protective of an uncompetitive footprint** to fix the business.

---

## Where I disagree — sharply

### 1) I do not accept “all plants stay open” as a starting condition
That is not a restructuring principle. It is a veto on restructuring.

We have a structural overcapacity problem. German factory costs are far above target. The European market is shrinking. Competition is intensifying. The brand is missing its return-on-sales target.

In that context, saying “all German plants remain open” before any quantified alternative is even presented is not a serious economic answer. It is a demand to preserve today’s footprint regardless of whether it can compete.

If the workforce and the state want no closures, then they must show how the same permanent savings and capacity correction are achieved by other means. So far, I have heard assertions, not a bankable equivalence.

### 2) I do not accept a blanket ban on compulsory redundancies
I prefer voluntary tools first. I have said that before, and I repeat it. But I will not sign away the company’s ability to act if voluntary measures fail to close the gap.

If management is forced to rule out every form of compulsory adjustment in advance, then the restructuring loses credibility and execution power. That is not a sustainable settlement; it is a freeze.

### 3) I do not accept a 2030 guarantee if it locks in an uncompetitive structure
A guarantee has value only if it is compatible with competitiveness. Otherwise it becomes a promise to protect cost and capacity indefinitely, which simply postpones the next crisis.

Trust must be rebuilt, yes. But trust without performance is not a turnaround. It is sentiment.

---

## To the Works Council
You say closures are unacceptable under any label, including “equivalent structural reduction.” I understand why you say that. But from management’s perspective, the issue is not the label. It is whether the company can right-size its footprint and costs to market reality.

You ask for:
- binding closure bans,
- binding no-compulsory-redundancy commitments,
- and security through 2030.

That would be acceptable only if it still delivers:
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- materially lower labor costs,
- and a credible return to competitiveness.

So here is the point where I do **not** concede: a promise of security cannot come at the price of preserving excess structure. If all German sites remain open and no compulsory reductions are possible, then the burden on the rest of the package must be substantial enough to close the gap. Not symbolic. Substantial.

You have also said management has not shown the numbers. Fair criticism. But you have likewise not shown a quantified alternative that delivers the same durable savings without closures or compulsory measures. “Capacity reduction through internal allocation” is not enough unless you can show, in hard numbers, how that replaces structural footprint reduction.

---

## To the Industrial Union
You are right that employees should not finance the turnaround alone. I agree. Management, owners, and the company side must contribute visibly.

But I strongly disagree with your attempt to convert “no closures” into a precondition for any meaningful labor contribution. That is backwards. If the company is asked to offer binding security, the workforce must offer binding flexibility and a real economic contribution.

A temporary 5%–10% labor-cost contribution can be acceptable only if it is part of a package that materially improves the cost base. It is **not** acceptable if the result is simply a slower decline of the same uncompetitive structure.

You talk about management and owner restraint. Fine. But restraint on our side cannot substitute for structural correction. I need:
- lower direct labor cost,
- lower overhead,
- lower footprint cost,
- and lower capacity relative to demand.

Without that, we are just buying time.

---

## To the Regional State Government
You are right to care about industrial continuity. But continuity requires a viable company, not just preserved sites. If the current structure is left intact, the risk is not only a weak result in 2025; it is a worse crisis in 2027 or 2028 when the next market shock arrives.

You say closures are a political choice. In a narrow legal sense, perhaps. In an economic sense, if a site is structurally uneconomic, refusing to address it is also a choice — a choice to accept a weaker company.

I respect your role and the governance constraints. But I will not pretend that public pressure can override the economics. The company has to earn its future.

---

## To the Controlling Family and Investors
You are right on one thing: deadlock destroys value. But so does a settlement that is socially neat and economically insufficient.

You ask for quantified packages. Agreed. Then let’s be honest about what counts as enough.

A credible package must achieve:
- durable labor-cost relief,
- measurable capacity reduction,
- overhead simplification,
- and sufficient margin improvement to restore the brand’s return-on-sales path.

If a “non-closure” package cannot do that, then it is not a solution. It is a delay mechanism.

I support your insistence on numbers. But numbers must reflect the scale of the crisis, not the minimum politically acceptable gesture.

---

## What I am prepared to support
I will be explicit.

Management is prepared to discuss a package built around:

1. **A temporary employee contribution** in the expert’s range, limited in time and reciprocal.
2. **Management compensation restraint** and overhead reductions.
3. **Voluntary exits and internal redeployment first.**
4. **Working-time flexibility and shift redesign.**
5. **Qualification and transformation programs.**
6. **Owner-side support for the transition.**
7. **Investment commitments in future products and technology.**
8. **Capacity reduction through reconfiguration where it genuinely delivers savings.**
9. **Closure options only if the numbers and implementation reality leave no credible alternative.**

That is the package architecture.

What I am **not** prepared to support is:
- all plants open as a permanent principle,
- no compulsory redundancies under any circumstance,
- a guarantee that freezes the structure through 2030,
- and an employee contribution so limited that the business remains structurally uncompetitive.

---

## Directly on the question of “equivalence”
If you want a non-closure package to replace closures, then it must be **economically equivalent** in durable effect.

That means it must deliver, at minimum:
- a real and temporary labor-cost contribution,
- a substantial reduction in overhead,
- measurable productivity gains,
- clear capacity reduction,
- and a credible path to margin recovery.

If it cannot do that, then no amount of political language makes it equivalent. It is not equivalent. It is simply less painful in the short term and more dangerous in the long term.

---

## Bottom line
I hear the demands for security. I hear the concern about trust. I hear the objection to social disruption.

But my responsibility is to restore competitiveness, hit the return-on-sales target, and free funds for the technologies and products that keep this company viable to 2030.

So my position remains:
- **A real, durable cost reduction is non-negotiable.**
- **A meaningful employee contribution is necessary.**
- **Voluntary tools come first, but compulsory measures cannot be ruled out.**
- **Capacity must be reduced in a way that genuinely fixes the cost base.**
- **A settlement that preserves an uncompetitive structure is unacceptable.**

If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then bring a quantified non-closure package that is economically equivalent. If it cannot do that, then we must not exclude the structural measures that the business requires.
