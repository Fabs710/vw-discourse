From an industrial-relations and bargaining-practice perspective, the key issue is not whether employees should contribute at all — in a contested restructuring, they usually do — but **what a fair, temporary, and negotiable contribution looks like relative to the scale of the problem and relative to what management, owners, and the state also put on the table**.

I can assess that in general terms. I cannot verify the company’s internal numbers, so the figures below are **plausible bargaining ranges**, not factual findings about this specific employer.

## 1) What “fair” typically means in a German restructuring settlement

In German manufacturing restructurings, a fair labor-cost contribution is usually judged against four tests:

1. **Temporary, not permanent**  
   It should bridge a restructuring period, not permanently depress wage standards.

2. **Reciprocal**  
   Employees give something only if they receive meaningful counterconcessions:
   - no compulsory redundancies,
   - site or employment guarantees,
   - investment commitments,
   - redeployment and qualification,
   - and usually some management-side restraint.

3. **Proportionate to the gap**  
   It should help close a real competitiveness gap, but not be so large that it becomes an employer-led wage reset.

4. **Broadly shared burden**  
   In accepted bargaining practice, the workforce is not expected to be the sole financing source of restructuring.

## 2) What a plausible temporary labor-cost contribution looks like

For a large German carmaker under restructuring pressure, a **credible temporary labor-cost contribution** would commonly fall in the range of roughly:

- **5% to 10% of direct labor cost** for a defined period, or
- an equivalent package of measures delivering a similar annual savings effect.

That is the range I would regard as **plausible and defensible** if:
- it is time-limited, typically **2 to 4 years**,
- it is tied to enforceable guarantees,
- and it is paired with management/owner contributions.

### What that can include in practice
A fair package often mixes several instruments:

- **temporary wage moderation** or one-off wage postponement
- **reduced special payments / bonuses**
- **working-time reductions or flexible time accounts**
- **suppression of overtime premiums through scheduling changes**
- **voluntary partial-retirement or exit programs**
- **temporary suspension or deferral of agreed increases**
- **lower entry wage progression for new hires, limited and negotiated**
- **concessions on allowances or shift premia only if narrowly targeted**

## 3) Rough magnitude in annual savings terms

Because I do not have verified payroll data, I can only give a method and plausible order of magnitude.

If a large domestic carmaker has, for illustration, **very substantial German direct labor costs** in the several-billion-euro range, then:

- a **5% contribution** could generate savings on the order of **hundreds of millions of euros per year**
- a **10% contribution** could approach **high hundreds of millions to around one billion euros per year**, depending on base wage bill and coverage

But in bargaining practice, once you move much beyond this, you are no longer talking about a “temporary contribution” in the usual sense; you are talking about a **structural wage reset**.

## 4) What would be considered too much

From a labor-relations standpoint, I would be cautious about any package that implies:

- **double-digit permanent wage cuts**
- removal of core protections without strong reciprocal guarantees
- open-ended wage sacrifice while closure options remain live
- employee-financed restructuring with no management or owner burden-sharing

If employees are asked for more than about **5% to 10% temporary labor-cost relief**, the package should usually be justified by:
- exceptionally strong employment security,
- clearly measurable investment commitments,
- and visible non-labor sacrifices by the company side.

## 5) What management-side measures should accompany it

A fair package is usually more credible if management also contributes in visible, quantifiable ways. In practice that means:

### Management-side contributions
- **executive pay restraint**
- lower variable compensation until targets are restored
- **headcount restraint in management layers**
- procurement, logistics, and overhead reductions
- postponement of non-essential capex outside the turnaround plan
- tighter working-capital management
- transparency on plant-level cost bases and targets

A labor-cost contribution looks much more acceptable if management can demonstrate it is also taking a real share of the pain.

## 6) What owner-side contributions should accompany it

Owners do not normally “negotiate wages,” but in major restructurings they are often expected to contribute through governance and capital decisions. A fair package may include:

- **capital injections or backstop financing**
- reduced short-term dividend pressure if needed
- support for investment in future platforms
- acceptance of lower near-term returns
- coordination to avoid forcing a purely labor-financed solution

In a co-determined setting, this is especially important: if labor is asked to give up part of its wage bill, owners are usually expected to help fund the transition as well.

## 7) My assessment of the package balance

If I were comparing bargaining fairness, I would see a balanced restructuring roughly like this:

### Plausible balanced structure
- **Employees:** 5%–10% temporary labor-cost contribution
- **Management:** visible compensation restraint plus overhead reduction
- **Owners:** capital support / weaker dividend pressure / financing for transformation
- **Company:** binding no-forced-redundancy or at least strong redeployment commitment, plus site protection for a defined period
- **State:** support for training, transition, and regional investment instruments

That is the kind of burden-sharing that is usually defensible in German industrial relations.

## 8) Relation to closures and forced layoffs

A fair labor-cost contribution does **not** by itself eliminate the possibility of structural capacity reduction. But in bargaining terms:

- **if labor gives 5%–10% temporarily**, management should have a harder time justifying immediate plant closures as the first and only answer
- if closures are still deemed necessary, the labor contribution should generally be **smaller, shorter, and better compensated**
- if management insists on closures and redundancies, employee concessions usually become much harder to sustain politically and institutionally

So the presence or absence of closures matters a great deal to what counts as “fair.”
