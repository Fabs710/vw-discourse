Management, owners, and investors are still trying to recast this as if the workforce must choose between surrender and decline. I reject that completely.

We are not the problem to be “right-sized.” We are the people who built the value that management now wants to cut away.

## Where I agree
I agree on the need for a durable turnaround.
I agree the cost base must improve.
I agree employees can contribute if the deal is fair.
I agree open conflict would damage the company.
I agree the expert’s 5%–10% temporary labor-cost range is a legitimate bargaining reference point.

But that agreement stops exactly where you keep trying to move the goalposts.

## Where I disagree — and why

### 1) I do not accept plant closures in Germany
Not as a bargaining option.
Not as a fallback.
Not as an “equivalent structural reduction.”

When management says “capacity reduction,” it is often just a polite way of saying closure. I will not let that language laundering pass.

A German plant closure is not a technical adjustment. It is a rupture of trust, a destruction of livelihoods, and a signal that co-determination is only tolerated when it is convenient.

That is unacceptable.

### 2) I do not accept compulsory redundancies
Forced layoffs are not a restructuring tool we will endorse.
Voluntary exits, early retirement, redeployment, qualification, working-time instruments — yes.
Dismissals as a pressure instrument — no.

If the business model cannot be adjusted without firing people, then management has not done its job properly.

### 3) I do not accept “all plants stay open” being dismissed as a veto
Yes — it is a veto.
A legitimate one.
That is what co-determination and the supervisory-board rights are for.

You keep describing our red line as if it were unreasonable. It is not. It is the minimum requirement for a negotiated settlement after management broke trust by moving against the historic job-security pledge.

### 4) I do not accept a labor contribution without binding reciprocal security
The expert’s range may be plausible. But a contribution is only legitimate if it buys something real and enforceable.

Not promises.
Not “good faith.”
Not political declarations.

It must buy:
- binding no-closure commitments for all German plants,
- binding no compulsory redundancies,
- real co-determination in implementation,
- management restraint,
- owner-side burden-sharing,
- and investment commitments.

Without that, a labor contribution is just wage extraction dressed up as responsibility.

## On the moderator’s “equivalence test”
I support a comparison of packages only if it is honest about one thing:  
**a non-closure package is the baseline we require, not a fallback after accepting closures as normal.**

So yes, let management publish its numbers.
Yes, let an independent review compare packages.
But no, this must not become an exercise in legitimizing closures by definition.

The equivalence test has to ask:
- Can the required savings be achieved without closures or forced layoffs?
- Can they be achieved while preserving co-determination and site security?
- Can management and owners contribute visibly enough to make the burden-sharing fair?

If the answer is yes, then closure talk is unnecessary.
If the answer is no, then management has still not exhausted alternatives — and we are not accepting closures as the default consolation prize.
