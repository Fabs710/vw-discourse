## Where I agree

I agree with all sides on one central point: **this company needs a durable restructuring, not another postponement**.

I also agree that:
- the current German cost base is too high,
- capacity is out of line with market reality,
- a purely temporary patch would be irresponsible,
- and open-ended industrial conflict would destroy value for everyone.

The expert’s contribution range is also useful. A **temporary employee contribution in the 5%–10% range of direct labor cost**, if it is truly time-limited and reciprocal, is within the realm of serious bargaining.

## Where I disagree

### To the Works Council and Union
I cannot support the position that:
- **all German plants must remain open**, and
- **no compulsory redundancies can ever be part of the solution**, and
- **binding guarantees through 2030** should be treated as a starting condition.

That package is not a restructuring; it is a demand to preserve the current footprint regardless of economics.

I understand why you are drawing a hard line. But from an ownership perspective, a line that prevents any meaningful footprint adjustment is not stewardship. It is a recipe for continued erosion of competitiveness and margin.

I also do not accept the framing that any closure discussion is “social destruction.” If a plant is structurally uncompetitive, refusing to address that reality can destroy far more jobs and value over time than a managed adjustment would.

### To the Regional State Government
I respect the state’s role and the governance framework, but I do not agree that plant closures are simply a political choice that can be ruled out in advance.

If the economics require structural adjustment, governance cannot be used to freeze an uncompetitive structure indefinitely. That would damage not only the company’s future, but also the long-term interests of the region and the state’s own stake.

### To Management
I support the need for real structural change, but I also think management must be careful not to turn closure threats into a blunt instrument that destroys trust before alternatives are fully tested.

If management wants support for difficult measures, it must show:
- quantified savings,
- credible implementation,
- and a clear explanation of why alternatives are insufficient.

## What would move me

I would support a package if it combines:
1. **meaningful capacity reduction**
2. **substantial labor-cost relief**
3. **voluntary exits and redeployment first**
4. **working-time flexibility**
5. **management compensation restraint**
6. **visible owner support for the turnaround**
7. **a credible investment plan for future products**
8. **an outcome that avoids prolonged conflict and protects enterprise value**

But I need to be clear: I will **not** support a package that:
- leaves the cost base fundamentally unchanged,
- preserves all capacity by default,
- and relies only on symbolic employee concessions.

## My position on the expert’s range

A temporary contribution of **5%–10%** can be a reasonable bargaining band, but only if:
- it is limited in duration,
- it is linked to binding commitments,
- and it is part of a broader package that actually closes the competitiveness gap.

If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then the non-closure package must be **financially credible**, not just politically preferable.

## What I want to see next

I want the parties to stop repeating red lines and put forward **two concrete packages**:

### Package A: Non-closure restructuring
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- temporary labor-cost contribution,
- working-time and redeployment measures,
- management restraint,
- owner support,
- quantified savings and capacity adjustment through reconfiguration.

### Package B: Structural footprint adjustment
- targeted closures or equivalent structural reductions,
- stronger cost reset,
- stronger implementation certainty,
- protections for affected employees,
- and a clear investment path for the retained footprint.

Then we can compare them on:
- savings,
- implementation risk,
- industrial peace,
- and long-term value.

## Bottom line

My bottom line is simple:

- I support a **real** turnaround.
- I do **not** support a solution that locks in structural uncompetitiveness.
- I do **not** support prolonged conflict.
- I do **not** support value destruction through deadlock.
- I am open on the method, but not on preserving an uncompetitive structure as a default.

If the employee side and the state want to avoid closures, they need to bring a package that is economically equivalent, not merely politically more comfortable. If they cannot, then management must be able to pursue the structural measures needed to restore competitiveness.
