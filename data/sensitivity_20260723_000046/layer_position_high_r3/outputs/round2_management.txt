Let me respond directly to what has been said, because we are now at the point where vague positions are no longer enough.

First: I appreciate the expert’s attempt to quantify a fair contribution. A temporary labor-cost contribution in the range of roughly 5% to 10% of direct labor cost is at least a **serious starting point**. But I need to be clear: **that alone does not solve the structural problem** unless it is accompanied by meaningful capacity reduction and a durable reset of the cost base. If the package only buys time while leaving the footprint and cost structure essentially intact, then it is not a turnaround — it is delay.

## To the Works Council
I hear your insistence on all plants staying open, no compulsory redundancies, and binding guarantees through 2030. But I do **not** accept those as fixed preconditions.

Why? Because the economics do not support them.

- We have a structural overcapacity problem.
- German factory costs are materially above target.
- The brand is missing its return-on-sales target.
- The European market is shrinking.
- Competitors are not waiting for us to protect every line and every site.

So when you say “all German plants remain open,” what you are really asking for is that management preserves capacity regardless of whether it is economically justified. That is not co-determination in the service of competitiveness; it is a veto on restructuring.

I do agree on one point: **forced layoffs should not be the first instrument if a negotiated alternative can deliver comparable savings.** But that alternative must be real. “No closures, no layoffs, and guarantees” is not a restructuring package. It is a refusal to confront the gap.

## To the Industrial Union
You are right that employees should not carry the entire burden. I agree with that. Management, owners, and the company side must all contribute visibly.

But I disagree with your framing that closures are somehow just a negotiating threat that should disappear. Closures are not a tactic for us; they are a legitimate option if we cannot secure a competitive structure by other means.

The key question is not whether closures are emotionally acceptable. The question is whether the company can compete sustainably without a deeper footprint adjustment. If the answer is no, then we cannot rule closures out in advance.

I do accept your point that any employee contribution must be:
- time-limited,
- reciprocal,
- and tied to real security and investment.

That is exactly why I am asking for a **material, durable package** rather than slogans.

## To the Regional State Government
Your support for the industrial base is understandable and, in principle, welcome. But you are wrong if you believe the company can remain strong by freezing its current footprint.

You talk about regional continuity. I talk about whether there will still be a competitive company in 2030. Those are not the same thing.

A plant that remains open because everyone avoids hard decisions today can still become a liability tomorrow if it drags the entire business down. That is not protecting industry; that is deferring the crisis.

If you want to avoid closures, then help secure a package that achieves:
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- real labor-cost relief,
- and enough operational flexibility to restore margins.

If that cannot be done, then political resistance does not change the underlying economics.

## To the Controlling Family and Investors
You are right to focus on long-term value and not short-term optics. But I would push further: **long-term value requires decisive restructuring now**.

A package that merely reduces dividend pressure or asks for symbolic restraint will not restore competitiveness. We need:
- lower structural labor cost,
- leaner capacity,
- simpler operations,
- and room to invest in future platforms and technology.

I agree with you that deadlock destroys value. But so does preserving an uncompetitive footprint under the illusion that social peace alone creates a solution.

## On the expert’s ranges
Let me be precise here.

A temporary contribution in the **5% to 10%** range may be acceptable **only if** it is part of a broader package with:
- genuine capacity reduction,
- site reconfiguration where needed,
- voluntary exits,
- working-time flexibility,
- and a credible path back to margin improvement.

If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then the contribution has to be meaningful enough to produce a real economic effect. Anything cosmetic is not enough.

At the same time, I am not asking for permanent wage destruction. I am asking for a **structural reset with time-bound employee participation**.

## What management is prepared to put on the table
To avoid any misunderstanding, management is not simply demanding sacrifice from one side. A credible package can include:

1. **Management compensation restraint**
   - lower variable pay until targets are met
   - tighter executive overhead

2. **Overhead and process reductions**
   - simplification of structures
   - procurement and logistics savings
   - reduced administrative layers

3. **Capital discipline**
   - prioritization of future products and technology
   - tighter allocation of investment capital

4. **Voluntary instruments**
   - voluntary exits
   - internal redeployment
   - qualification for future roles

5. **Capacity and footprint measures**
   - and yes, potentially closures or equivalent structural reductions if the numbers require it

But I am not going to pretend that management-side restraint alone closes the gap. It does not.

## My line in the sand
What is fundamentally unacceptable to me is a cosmetic settlement that:
- preserves all German plants by default,
- rules out compulsory reductions in advance,
- offers only temporary or symbolic labor measures,
- and leaves the company still structurally uncompetitive.

That would fail the fiduciary test. It would fail the investor test. And it would fail the 2030 competitiveness test.

## Where we can actually test seriousness
If the employee side wants to demonstrate that closures are not necessary, then put forward a quantified alternative that achieves:
- the same magnitude of permanent cost reduction,
- the same or comparable capacity reduction,
- and a credible implementation path without endless industrial conflict.

If that package is real, management can assess it seriously. But if it is only a list of red lines with optimistic language around “redeployment” and “efficiency,” then we are nowhere.

## Bottom line
I acknowledge:
- the need for fair burden-sharing,
- the need for investment in future products,
- the value of avoiding uncontrolled conflict,
- and the expert view that a temporary labor contribution must be limited and reciprocal.

I do **not** accept:
- a blanket ban on closures,
- a blanket ban on compulsory redundancies,
- or a 2030 guarantee that freezes the structure without delivering competitiveness.

So my position remains unchanged: **we need a durable restructuring with real cost and capacity reduction.**  
I am open on the form. I am **not** open on preserving an uncompetitive base and calling that a solution.

If the other side wants to avoid closures, then bring a bankable alternative that closes the gap in economic terms. Otherwise, management will continue to press for the structural measures that the situation requires.
