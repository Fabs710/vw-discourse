As institutional investors, we are looking at this through one lens: **does the final package credibly restore competitive margins and protect long-term equity value?** Everything else is secondary.

### Where we agree
- **Management is right** that the current cost base and capacity situation sound structurally uncompetitive.
- **The workforce and public stakeholders are right** that a purely destructive, closure-first approach can burn value through strikes, disruption, and execution risk.
- **The family owners are right** that temporary fixes are not enough; this needs to be durable.
- **Investment in future products and technology is essential.** A turnaround that cuts costs but starves the business of future competitiveness is not acceptable.

### Where we disagree
I am not prepared to support an outcome that simply preserves the existing footprint and calls that a solution. If the restructuring leaves the company with:
- an uncompetitive German cost base,
- structurally impaired margins,
- or only symbolic savings,

then it will not be value-creating. In that case, the share price will remain under pressure and capital will migrate elsewhere.

At the same time, I also do **not** think a blunt closure-driven approach is automatically value-maximising. Prolonged industrial conflict, legal blockage, and political confrontation can destroy savings through delay, reputational damage, and execution failure. So “deep cuts” only make sense if they are actually deliverable.

### What we need to see
We would support a deal if it contains:
1. **A credible, quantified path back to competitive margins**
2. **Durable cost reduction, not just temporary optics**
3. **Capacity reduction that is economically meaningful**
4. **A settlement that is deliverable without open-ended strikes**
5. **Visible management and owner contribution**
6. **A clear investment plan for the future product mix**

### On the employee side
I understand the resistance to closures and compulsory redundancies, but from a capital-markets perspective that is not enough on its own. If the alternative package does not produce comparable savings, then it is not a real answer. We would need to see:
- labor-cost moderation,
- meaningful flexibility on working time,
- voluntary exits,
- internal redeployment at scale,
- and genuine structural simplification.

If the proposal is only “no closures, no layoffs, and guarantees,” then it does **not** solve the problem.

### On management
You need to be careful not to overplay the closure card as a negotiating tactic if it merely hardens resistance and delays reform. What matters to us is not the headline of closures, but the **net financial outcome**. Show us:
- the size of savings,
- the timing,
- the implementation certainty,
- and the impact on return on sales.

### On the controlling family and state
We value long-term ownership stability, but not if it becomes a shield against necessary restructuring. Governance protections are useful only if they do not lock the company into structurally weak economics. The question is not whether every site survives in its current form; the question is whether the company can earn its cost of capital.

### Bottom line
We are **not** committed to plant closures as an end in themselves.  
We are **not** committed to preserving the status quo.  
We **are** committed to whichever package credibly gets the business back to competitive margins, with durable savings and manageable execution risk.

So my challenge to all sides is simple:
**Put a quantified, bankable restructuring on the table that closes the competitiveness gap without destroying value through deadlock.**  
If you cannot, then the market will continue to discount this business heavily.
