A fair reading of the bargaining logic here is:

## 1) The plausible temporary labor-cost contribution
For a large German manufacturing restructuring, a **temporary contribution of roughly 5% to 10% of direct labor cost** is a **credible bargaining band**.

That is not a moral judgment; it is a practice-based estimate. It is plausible when:
- the company shows a real restructuring need,
- the contribution is **time-limited**,
- it is tied to **binding security** and investment commitments,
- and management, owners, and sometimes the state also make visible contributions.

### What that means in practice
A fair package at the lower-to-middle end of the range would usually include some mix of:
- deferred or reduced wage increases,
- temporary reduction of allowances or special payments,
- longer working time with partial compensation adjustment,
- reduced paid time off or banked time measures,
- lower overtime premiums or more flexible scheduling,
- a temporary “solidarity” or “turnaround” contribution.

A **10%** labor-cost reduction is already quite substantial in German collective-bargaining terms and would normally require strong reciprocal guarantees. It is closer to a crisis measure than a routine concession.

## 2) What makes the contribution fair or unfair
A contribution in that range is **fairer** if it is:
- **temporary**, with a clear sunset clause,
- **reversible** if the company breaches commitments,
- matched by **no compulsory redundancies**,
- matched by **no plant closures for the term of the deal** or at least a serious standstill,
- accompanied by **management pay restraint**,
- accompanied by **owner-side support**,
- and linked to **investment in future products, qualification, and redeployment**.

It is **less fair** if:
- it becomes a permanent wage reset,
- the company keeps closure options fully open,
- employees give up pay while management and owners do little,
- or the package lacks any enforceable job/site security.

## 3) Comparison with management-side and owner-side measures
In accepted restructuring practice, a balanced package would not rely on labor alone. A realistic burden split would look more like this:

### Employee side
- temporary direct-labor-cost contribution: **5%–10%**
- working-time flexibility
- voluntary exits and redeployment
- qualification and internal mobility

### Management side
- executive compensation restraint
- overhead reduction
- simplification of layers and functions
- procurement and administrative savings

### Owner side
- transitional financial support
- willingness to absorb lower near-term returns
- governance support for a negotiated rather than destructive solution
- possibly capital backing for investment and transformation

### State side, where relevant
- help on training, labor-market transition, and site transformation
- not usually direct wage funding, but support for adjustment capacity

## 4) What the bargaining comparison suggests
If management is asking for **deep permanent structural savings**, then a temporary 5%–10% labor contribution is only enough if it is part of a broader package that also delivers:
- capacity reduction,
- productivity gains,
- overhead cuts,
- and credible investment.

If management cannot show that **non-closure measures** and other non-labor measures are sufficient, then the labor contribution alone is not the whole answer. But equally, if management wants a labor contribution, it normally has to offer something material in return.

## 5) My assessment in one sentence
A **temporary 5%–10% direct labor-cost contribution** is a plausible and fair bargaining range in this kind of restructuring **only if it is reciprocal, time-limited, and matched by binding job/site security plus management and owner burden-sharing**.
