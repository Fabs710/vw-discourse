Management keeps repeating “structure,” “durable savings,” and “competitiveness,” but you are still not answering the central question: **why should the workforce pay for a crisis by losing plants, jobs, and guarantees while others frame that as unavoidable?**

I’ll be equally direct.

### Where I agree
Yes, the company needs a credible industrial plan.
Yes, investment in future products and technology matters.
Yes, the German footprint has to stay competitive.

We do not deny that change is necessary.

### Where I fundamentally disagree
I **do not accept** that the only credible restructuring is one that keeps plant closures and compulsory redundancies on the table.

That is not “realism.” It is a decision to shift the entire adjustment burden onto employees. And that is precisely what we will not allow.

Management says every site cannot be preserved as-is. Fine. But there is a very large difference between:
- **capacity reduction**, redeployment, working-time flexibility, qualification, and negotiated temporary contributions  
and
- **closing plants and forcing people out of work**.

The first is a restructuring. The second is a rupture.

### On the arguments from investors and ownership
You all talk about “value,” “margins,” and “governance risk.” I hear that. But let me remind everyone: **this company’s value is not created by financial engineering alone. It rests on the workforce, on know-how, on local sites, and on social stability.**

If you destroy trust, you destroy the conditions for performance.

The family holding says it does not want value-destructive conflict. Good. Then do not support a plan that triggers exactly that conflict by threatening closures and compulsory redundancies from the outset.

The institutional investors say they do not care about the instrument, only the economics. Then take the alternative seriously instead of lazily defaulting to the harshest measure. If the economics are the issue, show us the numbers:
- How much savings are required?
- How much can be achieved through internal redeployment?
- How much through shifts, working time, and productivity?
- How much through management overhead and executive discipline?
- How much through a fair, temporary employee contribution?

Do that before talking about closures.

### On management’s “durable effect” argument
You say temporary contributions are not enough. I agree that a one-off fix is not enough.

But your solution cannot be to permanently weaken employment protections and break historic guarantees as if that were the only path to durability.

Durability can also come from:
- a binding site and job guarantee to 2030,
- a restructuring agreement with clear investment commitments,
- productivity and capacity adjustments negotiated with the works council,
- qualification and internal deployment,
- and a fair contribution from all sides, including management and owners.

If management wants social peace and durable savings, then the company must put something substantial on the table, not just demand sacrifice.

### Our non-negotiables
Let me be unambiguous:

- **No German plant closures.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No dismantling of the historic job-security framework.**
- **No deal that sidelines co-determination.**

These are not bargaining chips to be worn down by repetition. They are the line.

### What could move us
If the company wants a negotiated solution, then the conditions are clear:
- binding security for every German site,
- job protection through 2030,
- meaningful co-determination in shaping the restructuring,
- future-product and investment commitments,
- and a burden-sharing package that is genuinely shared and temporary.

Capacity adjustments can be discussed.
Working-time measures can be discussed.
Redeployment and training can be discussed.
A temporary employee contribution can be discussed.

But not plant closures. Not forced layoffs. Not a fait accompli.

### Bottom line
If management and capital want a solution, stop presenting employee rights as the obstacle. They are not the obstacle.  
**The obstacle is the refusal to accept that a competitive carmaker can be restructured without sacrificing its people and sites.**

We are ready for a serious package. We are not ready to negotiate away the workforce’s future.
