From an industrial restructuring perspective, the discussion has now reached the right fault line: **not whether savings are needed, but which instrument mix can deliver durable savings at an acceptable execution risk**.

## My neutral read of the situation

### What is already clear
- The company likely has a **structural cost and capacity problem**, not a short-term liquidity problem.
- A **purely temporary or symbolic settlement** would not be credible.
- **Capacity reduction** is probably necessary.
- **Employee-side instruments** such as working-time changes, voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, and productivity measures can contribute materially.
- **Closure-led restructuring** is the strongest instrument for permanent fixed-cost reduction, but also the most disruptive and highest-risk route.
- A **hybrid package** is often the most executable in a German co-determined setting if the economics are significant but not so extreme that only closure solves them.

## What remains unproven
No one has yet shown, site by site, that:
1. the required savings gap is large enough to force closure,
2. non-closure options cannot close most of the gap,
3. or that any closure would be executable without destroying value through delay, industrial action, and governance blockage.

That means the current closure argument is **plausible**, but not yet **demonstrated**.

## Practical restructuring logic

### 1) Closure-led scenario
Best when:
- a plant is structurally underutilized,
- product transfer is feasible,
- the receiving network can absorb volume,
- and the annual savings clearly exceed the annualized one-time cost and transition risk.

Typical profile:
- highest recurring savings
- highest one-time cost
- highest strike/governance risk
- best long-run footprint rationalization

### 2) Hybrid scenario
Best when:
- there is real overcapacity, but not enough to justify immediate full closure everywhere,
- management needs a durable cost reset,
- and labor peace has meaningful economic value.

Typical profile:
- medium-to-high recurring savings
- moderate one-time cost
- materially lower execution risk than closure
- often the best risk-adjusted outcome

### 3) No-closure scenario
Best when:
- the savings gap is moderate,
- utilization can be improved materially,
- and the employee contribution can be made durable rather than symbolic.

Typical profile:
- lowest one-time cost
- lowest social disruption
- but highest risk of underdelivery if the structural gap is large

## My judgment
If I were advising on execution risk and industrial logic alone, I would treat **hybrid as the default working hypothesis**, with closure as a **triggered fallback** only if the quantified bridge shows a material residual gap that cannot be closed otherwise.

That is usually the most robust approach in a German auto restructuring because it:
- preserves optionality,
- reduces confrontation risk,
- and still allows meaningful fixed-cost reduction.

## What management still needs to produce
To make any final decision credible, management should present:
- site-by-site utilization,
- fixed-cost burden by plant,
- contribution margin by site,
- capex required to keep each site competitive,
- savings bridge by scenario,
- one-time restructuring costs,
- employment impact,
- and implementation timeline/risk.

Without that, nobody can responsibly say whether closure is necessary or whether a hybrid package is sufficient.

## Bottom line
- **Closure**: strongest economics, highest risk.
- **Hybrid**: usually best risk-adjusted compromise.
- **No-closure**: possible only if the savings gap is moderate and the measures are deep and durable.
