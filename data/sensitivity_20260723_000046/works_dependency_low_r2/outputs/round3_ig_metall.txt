I’ll respond directly.

## Where I agree
I agree with the works council, the state government, and the advisor on the main point: **this company needs a durable industrial solution, not a cosmetic one**.

I also agree that:
- investment in future products and technology is essential,
- the German footprint likely needs capacity reduction,
- savings must be quantified instead of asserted,
- and a package built on **shared sacrifice** is more credible than one that dumps the burden on employees alone.

So yes: we are not denying the need for restructuring.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept management’s attempt to keep **plant closures** and **compulsory redundancies** as routine “options” at the starting line.

That is not neutral realism. It is a bargaining threat dressed up as business logic.

And I do **not** accept the investors’ position that if the numbers are “good enough,” the instrument does not matter. The instrument absolutely matters, because a deal that destroys trust, breaks co-determination, and forces layoffs can easily destroy more value than it creates.

## On the advisor’s assessment
The advisor’s analysis is useful, and it confirms several things we have said from the beginning:
- a **no-closure package can work** if the savings gap is moderate and the package is strong;
- a **hybrid compromise** is often the most realistic execution path;
- closures are the most decisive tool economically, but also the highest-risk socially and industrially.

That is important. It does **not** prove that closures are necessary. It proves only that they are the harshest instrument.

And let me be explicit: **“most decisive” is not the same as “required.”**

## To management
If you want to keep closure threats alive, then show the evidence:
- site-by-site utilization,
- contribution margins,
- fixed-cost burdens,
- capex required to keep each site competitive,
- net present value of each scenario,
- and the quantified savings gap that cannot be closed by alternatives.

Until you do that, your position is not a business case. It is pressure.

You say a temporary employee contribution is not enough. Fine. We can discuss more than a one-off measure. But that does not magically justify closures or forced layoffs. It means we need a **real negotiated package**:
- capacity reduction,
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- overhead discipline,
- management restraint,
- owner contribution,
- and a temporary but meaningful employee contribution.

That is the route to a durable deal.

## To the works council
I support your line that the workforce should not be asked to bear the pain alone. That is correct.

I also support your rejection of compulsory redundancies and your insistence on co-determination. Those are not decorative principles; they are the basis for a legitimate settlement.

Where I would sharpen the point is this: we should not allow the debate to become only “no closures” versus “management freedom.” The real issue is whether there is a **quantified alternative** that preserves jobs and sites while still delivering durable savings. The advisor says yes, if the package is strong enough. That is the opening we should exploit.

## To the state government
You are right to emphasize regional stability and long-term legitimacy. If this process turns into a closure fight, the company will lose much more than a few cost points.

You should use your influence to insist on:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding investment commitments,
- and a restructuring agreement that preserves the industrial base.

That is not sentimentality. It is value preservation with social legitimacy.

## To the family holding
You are right to reject a weak deal that merely postpones the crisis. I agree.

But do not confuse “strong” with “closure-led.” A strong settlement can be built through:
- production concentration,
- shift rationalization,
- voluntary exits,
- temporary labour-cost contribution,
- and stricter management and owner contributions.

If you back closure threats too early, you will provoke exactly the industrial conflict and reputational damage you claim to want to avoid.

## Our non-negotiables remain unchanged
Let me be unambiguous again:

- **No German plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No dismantling of the collective wage framework**
- **No deal where employees carry the burden alone**

That is our line.

## What could move us
What could move us is a **serious shared-sacrifice package** with binding guarantees:
- site security,
- job protection through 2030,
- meaningful co-determination in implementation,
- future product and investment commitments,
- and a fair, temporary employee contribution matched by management and owners.

Capacity reduction can be discussed.
Working-time flexibility can be discussed.
Redeployment and qualification can be discussed.
A negotiated employee contribution can be discussed.

But not closures. Not forced layoffs. Not a blank check to management.

## Bottom line
The workforce is prepared to contribute to a durable solution. We are **not** prepared to finance competitiveness by sacrificing our jobs and sites while others call that “necessary restructuring.”

If management wants cooperation, it should stop threatening closures and start presenting a genuinely quantified alternative that preserves employment and respects co-determination.

If that does not happen, then the conflict will intensify. We have the means to do that, and we will use them.
