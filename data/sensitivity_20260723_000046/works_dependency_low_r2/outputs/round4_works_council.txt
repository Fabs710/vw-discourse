Let me be equally direct in Round 4.

The discussion has clarified the economics, but it has **not** changed the core issue: management still wants to keep closure and compulsory redundancies as bargaining weapons, while asking the workforce to trust that this is somehow “necessary.” I do not accept that.

## Where I agree
I agree with the expert and with parts of what the state and family holding said:

- The company needs a **durable industrial plan**, not a cosmetic patch.
- Capacity reduction and cost adjustment are unavoidable.
- The decision must be based on **numbers**, not slogans.
- A weak compromise that merely postpones the problem would be irresponsible.

I also agree that **hybrid measures** and **strong non-closure instruments** can create substantial savings. That is precisely why closure is not the default answer.

## Where I disagree
I disagree with the whole attempt to make closures and forced layoffs sound like “normal” options that simply belong on the table in an abstract way.

They are not normal instruments in a company with:
- co-determination,
- a historic job-security framework,
- strong German plant legitimacy,
- and credible alternatives already identified.

A closure is not just another lever. It is a rupture. A compulsory redundancy is not just another adjustment. It is a breach of trust. If management treats them as routine tools, then it is management that is escalating this conflict.

## To Group & Brand Management
You say the cost base is too high and that temporary measures are insufficient. That may be true in the abstract. But you have **not** proven that the same structural savings cannot be achieved without closures and forced layoffs.

The expert was clear: before anyone claims closure is necessary, management must show:
- site-by-site utilization,
- contribution margins,
- fixed-cost burdens,
- capex needs,
- and the NPV of each scenario.

So bring the actual comparison. Not the threat. Not the rhetoric. The comparison.

Until then, “closure cannot be ruled out” is not an argument. It is pressure.

## To the investors
You keep saying you support whatever restores margins. Fine. Then support the package that restores margins **without destroying the workforce and the company’s social legitimacy**.

Do not pretend that the highest immediate cost-cutting weapon automatically creates the highest value. If closure triggers strikes, governance deadlock, production disruption, reputational damage, and loss of trust, then you may destroy more value than you save.

The risk-adjusted answer may be a hybrid package. It may even be a strong no-closure package if it is sufficiently deep and durable.

But what it is **not** is a blank check for management to threaten closures until the workforce surrenders.

## To the controlling family holding
You said you do not want a destructive settlement. Good. Then do not enable one.

If the family wants a durable solution, it should back:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding site guarantees,
- and a real negotiated restructuring with co-determination.

You also said closures are not yet proven necessary. Correct. Then stop speaking as if they are the natural end state. They are not.

## To the state government
You have a blocking role and a responsibility to the regional industrial base. Use it.

A state that allows closures and forced redundancies here, when alternatives exist and when no hard proof of necessity has been produced, would be failing its duty.

The state should insist on:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding investment,
- and a restructuring plan that preserves the industrial base.

## To the union
I agree with you that the burden cannot simply be dumped on the workforce. That is non-negotiable.

But I want to sharpen the point: any employee contribution must be **temporary, negotiated, and matched by management and ownership**. If the contribution becomes a one-way extraction, then it is not a restructuring agreement. It is a transfer of risk onto workers.

## On the expert’s compromise ideas
The expert and the moderator laid out “hybrid first, closure only by trigger,” “no closures but binding structural depth,” and “site-by-site differentiation.”

I understand why these are being floated. But I will not accept any formula that smuggles closure back in later as an evergreen threat. A trigger-based compromise is only acceptable if the trigger does **not** include forced plant closure as the automatic fallback.

Otherwise, we are just signing a delayed surrender.

## What is actually acceptable
I remain open to discussing:
- shift reductions,
- model concentration,
- working-time changes,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary exits,
- overhead cuts,
- management restraint,
- and temporary, fair employee contributions.

I am not open to:
- German plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- a weakening of co-determination,
- or dismantling the job-security framework.

That line has not moved.

## What would move me
Very little would move me, and nothing in this discussion has met that standard yet.

Only this could change my position:
- binding guarantees that every German plant stays open,
- no forced layoffs,
- job security through 2030,
- a serious co-determination role in shaping implementation,
- and a genuine shared-sacrifice package.

Without those conditions, there is no basis for compromise on the core issue.

## Bottom line
The company needs restructuring, yes.  
But **not by sacrificing German plants and forcing workers out** while calling that realism.

If management wants a durable solution, it should stop using closures and compulsory redundancies as the starting line and start building a serious non-closure package that respects jobs, sites, and co-determination.

If it does not, then the works council will continue to resist — in the supervisory board, in the plants, and in public.
