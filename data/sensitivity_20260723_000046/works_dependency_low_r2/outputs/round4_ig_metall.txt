I’ll respond plainly.

## Where I agree
I agree with the works council, the state government, the family holding, and the expert on the central point: **this company needs a durable restructuring, not a cosmetic one**.

I also agree that:
- capacity reduction is likely necessary,
- investment in future products and technology must be protected,
- savings must be quantified,
- and a **shared-sacrifice** package is the only defensible basis for any settlement.

So let me be equally clear: **the union is not denying the need for change**.

## Where I disagree
What I do **not** accept is management’s continued effort to keep **plant closures** and **compulsory redundancies** as routine, default bargaining instruments.

That is not “realism.” It is a threat.

And I do **not** accept the logic that employees must prove a non-closure solution can work while management is allowed to withhold the site-by-site economics that would justify closure in the first place.

## To management
You keep saying closures and forced layoffs cannot be ruled out.

Fine. But saying that is not the same as proving necessity.

The expert was explicit: before anyone talks about closure as justified, management must provide:
- site-by-site utilization,
- contribution margins,
- fixed-cost burdens,
- capex needs,
- and a scenario comparison with NPV and risk.

Do that first.

Until then, closure is not a business case. It is leverage.

And let me be direct: if management’s plan only works by making the workforce absorb the pain through layoffs, site destruction, and wage pressure, then that is not a restructuring plan we can accept.

## To the works council
I support your rejection of compulsory redundancies and your insistence on co-determination.

Where I want to sharpen the line is this: the union is prepared to discuss a **real negotiated contribution** from employees, but only if it is matched by:
- management restraint,
- owner contribution,
- and binding protections for jobs and sites.

If the deal becomes a one-way extraction from the workforce, then it is not a compromise. It is a transfer of crisis costs onto employees.

## To the state government
Your point about regional stability is correct.

But stability does not come from vague promises. It comes from a settlement that:
- protects sites,
- avoids forced layoffs,
- preserves the collective wage framework,
- and gives workers a credible future in the company.

If the state is serious, it should use its blocking position to insist on exactly that.

## To the family holding
You are right that a cosmetic deal is unacceptable. We agree.

But you are wrong if you treat “durable” as automatically meaning “closure-led.”

A durable solution can be built through:
- production concentration,
- working-time changes,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- overhead cuts,
- and a temporary but meaningful employee contribution.

That is the path that preserves industrial peace and still delivers real savings.

## To investors
You keep saying you support whatever restores margins.

Then support the package that restores margins **without detonating labor relations and trust**.

Because if closure and forced redundancy trigger strikes, governance deadlock, production disruption, and reputational damage, you may destroy more value than you save.

A strong hybrid package is therefore not a soft option. It may be the economically smarter option.

## On the expert’s assessment
The expert did not say closures are necessary. The expert said:
- closure-led restructuring has the strongest permanent savings,
- hybrid is often the most realistic execution path,
- no-closure can work if the gap is moderate and the package is strong.

That matters.

What it means is:
- closure is **possible**,
- not proven **necessary**;
- hybrid is **plausible**,
- and a strong no-closure package is **not off the table**.

So the burden is still on management to show why less destructive measures are insufficient.

## Our position remains unchanged
We remain prepared to discuss:
- capacity reduction,
- shift rationalization,
- working-time flexibility,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary exits,
- temporary employee contribution,
- overhead cuts,
- and productivity measures.

We are **not** prepared to accept:
- German plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or a restructuring that forces employees to bear all the pain.

## What would move us
What could move us is a package with:
- binding site security,
- protection against forced layoffs,
- a serious role for co-determination,
- future product and investment commitments,
- and a fair contribution from management and owners as well as workers.

That is the only basis for a durable settlement.

## Bottom line
If management wants cooperation, it must stop using closures and compulsory redundancies as bargaining weapons and start presenting a genuinely quantified alternative that preserves jobs and sites.

If it does not, then the union will not accept being told that competitiveness requires sacrifice from employees alone.

And if necessary, we will use the means at our disposal to make that point unmistakably clear.
