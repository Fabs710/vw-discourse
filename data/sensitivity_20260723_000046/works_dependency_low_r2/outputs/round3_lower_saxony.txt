I will be direct.

The expert assessments were useful, and they confirm two things I have been saying from the outset:

1. **The company needs a real, durable restructuring.**
2. **A pure cosmetic compromise will not do.**

But they also confirm something equally important: **closures are not automatically necessary**. They are the most decisive instrument economically, yes — but not the only possible route, and not the first one I would support for this company, this region, and this governance structure.

## Where I agree

### With management
You are right that the German cost base is too high, that capacity must come down, and that the company needs a durable margin improvement. I do not dispute the diagnosis.

### With the union and works council
You are right that the burden cannot simply be dumped on the workforce, and that trust, co-determination, and social legitimacy are not decorative. They are part of the company’s operating model.

### With the experts
You are right that:
- a **hybrid compromise** is often the most realistic execution path,
- a **no-closure package** can work if the gap is moderate and the measures are strong,
- and closures are the most reliable way to remove fixed cost, but also the highest-risk route.

That is a fair framing.

## Where I disagree

### 1) I do not accept closures as the default answer
Management keeps saying plant closures “cannot be ruled out.” Fine — but that is not the same as proving they are necessary.

The experts were explicit: before anyone treats closure as justified, we need:
- site-by-site utilization,
- contribution margins,
- fixed-cost burdens,
- capex needs,
- and a scenario comparison.

Until that is on the table, closure is not an evidence-based conclusion. It is a pressure tactic.

### 2) I do not accept compulsory redundancies as a starting point
That is especially important. If the company can achieve adjustment through:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- working-time changes,
- shift reduction,
- qualification,
- overhead cuts,
- and negotiated employee contribution,

then forced layoffs should not be the first instrument reached for. They are socially destructive and politically reckless, especially in a company central to the regional economy.

### 3) I do not accept a restructuring that sidelines co-determination
This is not a side issue. Under this governance arrangement, and under the special statute, the state’s role and the workforce’s role are not ornamental. Any plan that tries to push through a plant decision without real co-determination will run into a wall.

That is not a threat. It is a fact.

## What the expert evidence actually means

The experts did **not** say closures are mandatory. They said:

- closure-led restructuring is **most certain** on savings,
- hybrid is **most plausible** politically and industrially,
- no-closure is **possible**, but only if the savings gap is moderate and the package is strong.

That matters.

So the correct conclusion is not “therefore close plants.”  
The correct conclusion is: **management must prove why a stronger hybrid or no-closure package is insufficient before it asks for irreversible damage to jobs and sites.**

## My position on the scenarios

### Closure-led scenario
I am **not** prepared to endorse this as the starting point, and certainly not without hard site economics proving necessity. It is too destructive, too risky, and too likely to trigger lasting damage to the regional anchor function of the company.

### No-closure negotiated scenario
I am **open to it**, but only if it is genuinely strong:
- real capacity reduction,
- durable labor-cost contribution,
- management restraint,
- owner contribution,
- and measurable margin improvement.

A weak no-closure deal is unacceptable. A strong one could be acceptable.

### Hybrid compromise
This is, at present, the most credible route. But “hybrid” must mean **real structural savings**, not a repackaged freeze.

If it means:
- serious shift rationalization,
- line and model concentration,
- voluntary exits,
- temporary employee contribution,
- overhead cuts,
- and binding investment and job guarantees,

then it deserves serious consideration.

If it is only a soft compromise that preserves overcapacity and kicks the problem down the road, then I will not support it.

## What I would require for support

If the company wants my support, it must bring a package that includes:

- **No German plant closures as the preferred instrument**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Binding site security**
- **Binding co-determination in implementation**
- **A quantified capacity and cost plan**
- **Temporary and fair employee contribution**
- **Comparable contributions from management and ownership**
- **Credible investment commitments for future products and German sites**

That is the standard.

## What I will not accept

I will not accept:
- a plan that guts regional employment,
- a settlement that turns the state’s blocking minority and board role into a formality,
- a restructuring built on fear and threat,
- or a deal where the workforce pays and everyone else merely lectures about realism.

## Bottom line

I am prepared to back a negotiated restructuring that preserves the company as a regional economic anchor and keeps co-determination meaningful.

I am **not** prepared to back plant closures or compulsory redundancies as the default path, and I will use the rights attached to our stake and board position if that is forced forward without a proven case.

So the next step should not be more rhetoric. It should be the quantified comparison the experts called for, with a serious hybrid package presented alongside any closure scenario.

If management can show a durable non-closure or hybrid solution that protects jobs and sites while restoring competitiveness, I will engage constructively.  
If not, then I will oppose a destructive shortcut.
