I will respond clearly.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the experts, the family holding, and the investors on one essential point: **this company needs a durable restructuring, not a cosmetic settlement**.  
I also agree that:
- the German cost base is too high,
- capacity must come down,
- future-product investment is necessary,
- and a deal that simply postpones the problem would be irresponsible.

So the diagnosis is not the issue.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the attempt to make **plant closures** and **compulsory redundancies** the default logic of this restructuring.

That may be economically attractive on paper, but in this company it is not automatically the right answer. We are not dealing with a normal listed company where management can simply impose a footprint decision and move on. We are dealing with:
- a regional anchor employer,
- a special statute with blocking rights,
- co-determination with a real veto,
- and a responsibility to the regional economy and the workforce.

That means the burden of proof is much higher.

## To management
You keep saying closures and forced redundancies cannot be ruled out. I hear that. But that is **not** the same as proving they are necessary.

The expert input was useful precisely because it did **not** say closures are inevitable. It said:
- closure-led restructuring is strongest on permanent savings,
- hybrid is often the most realistic execution path,
- no-closure can work if the gap is moderate and the package is strong.

That is the correct reading. So management must now prove, site by site, why the alternatives are insufficient.

Bring the actual comparison:
- utilization,
- contribution margins,
- fixed-cost burdens,
- capex needs,
- NPV by scenario,
- and the employment impact.

Until that is on the table, closure remains a threat, not a demonstrated necessity.

## To the works council and the union
I agree with you on the core principle that **the workforce cannot be asked to carry the burden alone**. That is right.

I also agree that:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time changes,
- shift rationalization,
- overhead cuts,
- and a fair negotiated employee contribution

must be part of the package.

But I will be equally direct: if you turn “no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” into absolute, unconditional red lines **without** presenting a quantified package that closes the savings gap, then you are not solving the restructuring problem either. You are only rejecting one instrument.

So I support a strong non-closure or hybrid solution **if and only if** it is genuinely capable of delivering durable savings and protecting the industrial base.

## To the family holding and investors
I agree that a weak compromise would be a mistake. A settlement that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive would destroy value in the long run.

But I do **not** accept the conclusion that closure is therefore the natural first answer. The expert’s comparison shows that a **hybrid compromise** may well be the best risk-adjusted path:
- real capacity reduction,
- durable labor-cost contribution,
- productivity improvements,
- overhead discipline,
- management restraint,
- and owner contribution.

That is a serious restructuring path. It is not softness.

If management wants support for harder instruments, it must first demonstrate that a hybrid package cannot close the gap. Otherwise, you are asking us to accept irreversible damage without proving necessity.

## My position
Let me be unambiguous:

### What I can support
- A **strong hybrid restructuring**
- A **no-closure package** only if it is genuinely deep and durable
- Capacity reduction without plant closures where possible
- Voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, productivity gains
- A fair temporary employee contribution, matched by management and owner contributions
- Binding investment commitments for the German sites

### What I cannot support
- German plant closures as the default instrument
- Compulsory redundancies as an opening move
- A restructuring that guts regional employment
- Any process that sidelines the state’s co-determination and veto role
- A settlement where employees carry the burden while others simply declare that “the numbers require it”

## On the expert’s scenario logic
The expert said something important:  
**Hybrid is usually the best risk-adjusted outcome in a German co-determined setting.**

I agree. That is the starting hypothesis that should now be tested seriously.  
Closure is the most decisive economically, yes — but it is also the highest-risk route. If it triggers strikes, governance deadlock, reputational damage, and production disruption, it may destroy the value it claims to create.

That is why I will not endorse closure as a reflex.

## What would move me
What could move me is a **quantified hybrid package** with:
- clear annual savings,
- binding site protections,
- no forced redundancies,
- a meaningful employee contribution,
- management and owner contribution,
- and a credible investment path for future products.

If that package truly closes the gap, then it deserves support.

If it does not, then management should not be surprised that I will oppose a destructive shortcut.

## Bottom line
I am prepared to support a restructuring that preserves the company as a regional economic anchor while restoring competitiveness.  
I am **not** prepared to support a closure-led strategy or forced layoffs as the starting line without hard proof that less destructive measures cannot work.

So the next step is not more rhetoric. It is the quantified side-by-side comparison the expert called for. Until then, no one should pretend closure has been justified.
