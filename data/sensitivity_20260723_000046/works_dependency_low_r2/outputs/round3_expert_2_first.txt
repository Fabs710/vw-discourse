## Executive view

Based on the facts provided, **all three scenarios can theoretically work**, but they differ sharply in:
- **durability of savings**
- **one-time cost burden**
- **speed/certainty of delivery**
- **labor-relations and governance execution risk**

The key question is not whether savings exist in a no-closure or hybrid package — they do — but whether they are **large enough, durable enough, and executable enough** to close a structural cost gap quickly enough to restore the brand’s return-on-sales.

My assessment:
- **Closure-led restructuring** gives the strongest permanent fixed-cost reduction and the cleanest long-run footprint, but carries the highest one-time costs and the highest execution risk.
- **Hybrid compromise** is often the best risk-adjusted path if management can secure substantial capacity reduction, shift concentration, working-time changes, and a meaningful labor-cost contribution without full closures.
- **No-closure package** can work only if the needed savings are moderate and if productivity/overhead/capacity instruments are unusually deep. It is the weakest option if the cost gap is large and structural.

## 1) Scenario framework

Because no site-specific financials were provided, I will use a **structured estimation framework** rather than false precision. The correct way to evaluate this is by measuring each site on:
- annual output volume
- variable contribution margin per unit
- direct labor and indirect labor cost
- fixed overhead
- logistics and complexity cost
- capex needed to keep it competitive
- underutilization relative to efficient scale

In practice, the recurring savings come from four buckets:
1. **Fixed-cost removal**: site overhead, support functions, maintenance, utilities, local management
2. **Labor-cost reduction**: headcount, working-time reduction, wage moderation, shift rationalization
3. **Complexity reduction**: fewer variants, fewer changeovers, shorter material flows
4. **Capacity reallocation**: moving volume to fewer sites to improve utilization

The one-time costs usually include:
- severance and early retirement
- decommissioning and asset write-downs
- logistics reconfiguration
- relocation and ramp-up costs at receiving sites
- labor agreement and transformation payments
- capex for absorption of transferred volume

## 2) Site-level savings bridge: how to think about it

Since the actual plant list and numbers are not provided, the best expert answer is a **site archetype savings bridge**. German auto networks usually contain three broad site types:

### A. Assembly / final vehicle site
Typical economics:
- high fixed overhead
- meaningful direct labor
- large logistics footprint
- strong sensitivity to utilization

Potential recurring savings if closed:
- site overhead removal: material
- maintenance, utilities, management, quality, plant services
- indirect labor elimination
- some logistics simplification

Potential annual savings range:
- **large site**: roughly **€150m–€300m+ per year**
- **mid site**: roughly **€80m–€180m per year**
- **small/specialized site**: roughly **€30m–€80m per year**

One-time costs:
- often **€300m–€1.0bn+** depending on headcount, asset base, and decommissioning
- if volume must be relocated, add receiver-site capex and launch costs

### B. Powertrain / component site
Typical economics:
- more specialized asset base
- often higher fixed amortization
- closure savings can be strong, but transfer complexity is higher

Recurring savings:
- **€50m–€200m per year** depending on scale

One-time costs:
- **€100m–€500m+**, especially if tooling is specialized and product transfer is incomplete

### C. Support / administrative / engineering-heavy site
Typical economics:
- lower physical asset burden
- savings mostly from overhead and headcount

Recurring savings:
- **€20m–€100m per year**

One-time costs:
- **€20m–€150m**, mainly severance, relocation, IT, and transition costs

### Important implication
If the group is seeking a **durable multihundred-million-euro annual cost reset**, a single site closure may not be enough unless it is a major assembly site. If the cost gap is large, the restructuring usually needs a combination of:
- at least one major footprint action
- labor-cost measures across the remaining plants
- overhead reduction at group and brand level
- volume concentration

## 3) Scenario comparison

## Scenario 1: Closure-led restructuring

### Recurring savings
This is the most effective route to permanent savings because it removes:
- plant fixed costs
- labor and supervision overhead
- utility and maintenance burden
- complexity from the network
- some logistics inefficiency

If one major plant is closed and volume is moved efficiently, a realistic annual recurring savings range is often:
- **€200m–€500m+ per year** for a major site action
- plus **€50m–€150m per year** from network simplification and support cost reduction

### One-time costs
This is where closure becomes expensive:
- severance / social plan costs
- early retirement
- asset write-downs
- dismantling / remediation
- transfer costs to receiving plants
- capex at receiving plants
- temporary inefficiency during ramp-up

A plausible one-time range for a major German plant action:
- **€500m–€1.5bn+**, depending on scale and social package

### Margin impact
- **Near term:** margin usually worsens due to restructuring charges
- **Medium term:** margin improves materially if savings are real and volume transfer succeeds
- **Long term:** best chance of restoring operating margin if the network is structurally oversized

### Execution risk
Highest of all scenarios:
- strike risk
- supervisory board blocking risk
- legal and industrial-relations friction
- production disruption risk
- customer delivery risk during transition
- reputational damage

### Best use case
Closure-led restructuring is economically justified if:
- a site is structurally subscale or noncompetitive
- capex required to make it competitive is irrationally high
- transfer to other sites is feasible without destroying the receiving network
- the recurring savings exceed the annualized one-time cost by a comfortable margin

---

## Scenario 2: Hybrid compromise

This is usually the most realistic industrial solution in a co-determined network where full closures are hard to execute.

### Typical elements
- one site partially downsized or converted
- capacity removed via shift reduction, model concentration, or line consolidation
- no forced redundancies, but strong voluntary exit and redeployment program
- temporary employee contribution or wage moderation
- management overhead cuts
- owner-capital or investment commitments
- product allocation to preserve long-term viability of remaining sites

### Recurring savings
A strong hybrid package can plausibly deliver:
- **€120m–€300m per year** from labor and overhead measures
- plus **€50m–€150m per year** from capacity rationalization and network simplification

Total recurring effect:
- **€170m–€450m per year** in a well-designed package

### One-time costs
Usually lower than closure-led because there is less decommissioning and less relocation:
- severance / voluntary exits
- retraining and redeployment
- transformation bonuses
- process reengineering
- limited capex for line changes and absorption

Typical one-time range:
- **€150m–€600m**

### Margin impact
- **Near term:** moderate charge impact
- **Medium term:** meaningful margin uplift if labor contribution is real and capacity reduction is meaningful
- **Long term:** can be very good if it stabilizes the labor relationship and avoids disruption

### Execution risk
Moderate:
- requires sustained negotiation
- risk of underdelivery if concessions are temporary or too shallow
- lower strike risk than closure-led, but still material
- less governance deadlock than full closure if there is a credible package

### Best use case
Hybrid is best when:
- there is genuine overcapacity, but not enough to justify a full plant shutdown
- industrial peace has value
- management can accept a less “clean” but more executable reset
- the goal is to reduce fixed cost while preserving strategic sites

## Scenario 3: No-closure negotiated package

This scenario relies on:
- working-time changes
- shift reductions
- voluntary exits
- redeployment
- qualification
- productivity improvement
- overhead cuts
- temporary employee contribution
- production concentration within existing footprint

### Recurring savings
Potentially meaningful, but usually lower than closure or hybrid:
- **€80m–€200m per year** in a solid package
- possibly more if productivity gains are exceptional and labor concessions are deep

### One-time costs
Usually the lowest:
- **€50m–€250m**, mainly severance, training, redesign, and limited retooling

### Margin impact
- **Near term:** limited charge impact
- **Medium term:** improvement depends heavily on compliance and productivity
- **Long term:** often insufficient if the structural cost gap is large

### Execution risk
High, but of a different type than closure:
- risk of savings dilution
- risk that concessions expire
- risk that capacity remains too fragmented
- risk that the package looks good on paper but does not fix utilization
- risk that management still needs a harder step later

### Best use case
No-closure is credible only if:
- the structural gap is moderate
- the company can materially improve utilization without plant shutdown
- the labor contribution is durable enough
- product volumes recover sufficiently to support remaining footprint

## 5) Practical decision rule

The economically correct choice depends on the size of the annual gap.

### If the durable gap is small to moderate
Example: roughly **€100m–€200m per year**
- a no-closure or light hybrid package may be enough
- closure would likely be overkill given the one-time cost and industrial risk

### If the durable gap is moderate to large
Example: roughly **€200m–€400m+ per year**
- hybrid is usually the best first choice
- closure may be needed if hybrid savings are not enough

### If the durable gap is very large
Example: above **€400m per year**
- closure-led restructuring becomes much more likely economically
- though execution feasibility may still block it

## 6) What matters most for margin restoration

For a carmaker missing target ROS, the most important margin drivers are:
1. **capacity utilization**
2. **fixed-cost absorption**
3. **labor cost per vehicle**
4. **product mix and complexity**
5. **launch stability and quality**

That means the biggest gains do not come from wage concessions alone. They come from:
- fewer underutilized sites
- better line loading
- fewer variant-related costs
- lower indirect cost per car
- fewer duplicated functions

So if management wants a durable reset, it needs a package that changes the cost curve structurally, not just a temporary wage deal.

## 7) Bottom-line assessment

### Closure-led
- **Best economics**
- **Highest one-time cost**
- **Highest execution risk**
- Strongest if the plant is truly uncompetitive and the group can absorb the transition

### Hybrid
- **Best risk-adjusted compromise**
- Often the most executable in German co-determination settings
- Can deliver substantial savings if it includes real capacity reduction and durable labor-cost relief

### No-closure
- **Lowest disruption**
- **Lowest one-time cost**
- Often **insufficient** if the structural overcapacity is significant
- Most likely to leave part of the problem unresolved unless the labor and productivity package is unusually deep

---

## My recommendation as an industrial economics assessment

If the goal is to maximize value while keeping execution risk manageable, the strongest default is:

**Hybrid first, with clearly quantified triggers for further action if savings or utilization do not materialize.**

That means:
- one or more capacity actions short of full closure
- durable labor-cost contribution
- voluntary exits and redeployment
- management overhead reduction
- targeted capex only where needed
- explicit review gates tied to utilization and margin outcomes

From a purely economic standpoint, that is usually superior to either:
- a soft no-closure deal that under-delivers, or
- an immediate closure-led fight that destroys value through conflict and delay.
