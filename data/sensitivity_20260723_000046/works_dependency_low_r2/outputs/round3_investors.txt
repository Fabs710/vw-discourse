We appreciate that the discussion has finally moved from slogans to a more decision-useful frame: **quantified savings, durability, execution risk, and margin impact**. That is the right standard.

Our position remains unchanged in substance:

- **We will not support a cosmetic settlement.**
- **We will not support a deal that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive.**
- **We will support whatever credibly restores margins with disciplined, durable cost reduction.**

## Where we agree

### With management
You are right about the diagnosis:
- the German cost base is too high,
- capacity is too heavy,
- and the company needs a durable reset, not a temporary patch.

You are also right that temporary measures alone are not enough if the structural gap is large. We agree that the outcome must improve return on sales, not just defer pain.

### With the experts
The expert input was helpful because it clarified the real trade-off:
- **closure-led restructuring** is the most reliable way to remove fixed cost permanently,
- **hybrid** is the most plausible executable compromise,
- **no-closure** can work only if the savings gap is moderate and the package is unusually strong.

That is exactly the right lens. We are not interested in ideology; we are interested in risk-adjusted value creation.

## Where we disagree

### With the works council and union
We understand the political and social reasons for rejecting closures and compulsory redundancies. But as shareholders, we cannot accept red lines that may prevent a durable margin reset.

If “no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” mean the company cannot reach a competitive cost base, then that position destroys value. In that case, it is not a viable restructuring position; it is a refusal to confront the economics.

We are not saying closures are desirable. We are saying they cannot be ruled out if they are needed to restore competitiveness.

### With management, to a point
We also do **not** accept management’s current posture as sufficient just because it insists on hard measures.

Saying “closures cannot be ruled out” is not yet a business case. If management wants capital-market support for hard restructuring, it must show the numbers:
- site-by-site utilization,
- contribution margins,
- fixed-cost burden,
- capex needs,
- NPV of closure vs. non-closure,
- and the expected margin impact under each scenario.

Without that, closure is still a threat, not a proven necessity.

### With the state government and family holding
We agree that social conflict and governance deadlock destroy value. But we do not accept the implication that the solution is therefore to avoid hard restructuring at any cost.

That would be just as destructive from a shareholder perspective. A deal that preserves labor peace but leaves the company structurally weak is not value-preserving.

## Our view of the three scenarios

### 1) Closure-led restructuring
We would support this **only if** management proves that:
- the sites are structurally uncompetitive,
- the permanent savings are material,
- and the transition risk is manageable enough not to wipe out the gain.

This is the strongest economic option, but also the highest execution-risk option.

### 2) No-closure negotiated package
We would support this **only if** it delivers a genuine and durable fixed-cost reset:
- not symbolic,
- not temporary,
- not dependent on goodwill.

If it preserves every legacy structure and merely buys time, we would reject it.

### 3) Hybrid compromise
This is currently the most interesting path.

A serious hybrid package could work if it includes:
- real capacity reduction,
- voluntary exits and redeployment,
- shift and working-time changes,
- productivity improvements,
- overhead cuts,
- and a meaningful, multi-year employee contribution.

But hybrid must not become shorthand for “soft compromise.” It has to close the gap in a way that is durable and enforceable.

## What would move us

We would shift support toward the package that best satisfies all three conditions:

1. **Durable savings**
2. **Competitive margin restoration**
3. **Low enough execution risk to preserve value**

On present information, that means we want to see:

- a **side-by-side scenario table** for closure, hybrid, and no-closure;
- savings by year;
- one-time restructuring costs;
- employment effects;
- strike risk;
- implementation risk;
- and time to value realization.

## What we are prepared to back
We are prepared to back a **hard, quantified restructuring** that restores competitiveness.

That could be:
- closure-led, if the economics justify it and the governance risk is contained; or
- hybrid, if it produces comparable durable savings without triggering destructive conflict.

What we are **not** prepared to back is:
- a weak compromise,
- a settlement that leaves overcapacity intact,
- or an outcome where the burden is shifted to shareholders via permanently impaired margins.

## Bottom line
Our standard is simple:

- **No cosmetic deal**
- **No structurally uncompetitive cost base**
- **No prolonged value-destructive conflict**
- **Yes to whatever package credibly restores margins and discipline**

So we are not choosing between management rhetoric and labor rhetoric. We are choosing between value-preserving restructuring and value erosion.

If the employee side wants to avoid closures, it must demonstrate an alternative with equivalent economic effect. If management wants closure authority, it must prove the case numerically. Until then, our support remains conditional on the strongest credible path back to competitive returns.
