I will respond bluntly.

The employee side keeps repeating the same line: **no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no erosion of guarantees**. I understand the political logic. I do **not** accept it as a restructuring answer.

A company does not become competitive by declaring certain instruments off-limits in advance.

## Where I agree

I agree on three things:

- **This company needs a durable solution**, not a cosmetic patch.
- **Investment in future products and technology** is essential.
- **Capacity reduction and productivity improvement** are necessary.

So yes: we are aligned on the need for change.

## Where I disagree

### 1) “No closures” cannot be an absolute rule
The advisor has now confirmed what management has been saying all along:  
a no-closure package **can** work only if the savings gap is moderate and the package is unusually strong. That is not the case we are being presented with.

We face:
- structural overcapacity,
- a shrinking European market,
- intense low-cost competition,
- and a cost base that is too high.

In that situation, saying “no closures” as a blanket rule is not responsible. It is a veto on serious restructuring.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be pre-announced either
I do not like compulsory redundancies. They are not my preferred tool. But I will not rule them out in advance when the company’s survival and long-term competitiveness are at stake.

If the employee side wants to exclude every hard instrument, then it must explain how the same durable savings are achieved with equal certainty and equal permanence. So far, I have not heard that.

### 3) Temporary measures are not enough
The union keeps saying any contribution must be temporary. That may be politically convenient, but it does not solve a structural cost problem.

If the problem is structural, the answer must be structural.

Temporary wage restraint, temporary flexibility, and temporary concessions may help, but they do not by themselves reset the footprint and cost base to a competitive level.

## On the advisor’s assessment

The expert was careful and useful. But let’s not misread the conclusion.

What the advisor actually said is:

- a **closure-led plan** is the most certain route to permanent savings;
- a **no-closure package** is possible, but only under strong conditions;
- a **hybrid compromise** may be the most realistic execution path.

That does **not** support the employee side’s claim that closures are unnecessary. It says the opposite: if the cost gap is serious, closures remain the most reliable tool.

And crucially, the advisor also said a soft non-closure package may leave overcapacity partially intact. That is exactly my concern.

## To the works council

You say closures are a rupture. Yes — and that is precisely why they are a last-resort instrument, not a first preference.

But do not confuse “last resort” with “never.” If a site is structurally uncompetitive, preserving it for the sake of symbolism simply shifts the burden into weaker margins, lower investment, and future job loss elsewhere.

You also say management should prove site economics. I agree. That is why I want the quantified bridge the advisor requested:
- site utilization,
- contribution margins,
- fixed-cost burdens,
- capex required,
- and NPV by scenario.

But let me be clear: that analysis is not a license to pre-emptively ban closures. It is a tool to determine where the restructuring must bite.

## To the union

You say closures and forced redundancies are a threat to partnership. I say the real threat to partnership is a settlement that does not fix the economics.

If the company does not materially reduce fixed costs, then no amount of rhetorical solidarity will restore margins. And if margins do not recover, then investment, product competitiveness, and ultimately jobs are all at risk.

So no, I will not accept the idea that the only legitimate contributions are temporary and reversible. The problem requires durable change.

## To the state government

I respect your concern for regional stability. But regional stability does not come from preserving uneconomic structures indefinitely.

A company that cannot compete will not remain a stable anchor. It will simply weaken over time. That is not a better outcome for the region.

If you want to protect the industrial base, then support a package that actually resets the cost base. If that means a plant closure or compulsory reductions at a specific site, then we must examine that honestly instead of hiding behind generalities.

## To the controlling family and investors

You are right that a cosmetic deal is unacceptable. I agree completely.

But I will also say this: a deal that excludes closures and excludes compulsory redundancies, while relying only on temporary concessions, may also be cosmetic in economic terms. We cannot confuse politeness with durability.

If you want a value-preserving solution, then back a package that materially changes the footprint and the cost base.

## What I need next

If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then bring a package that demonstrates **equivalent economic effect**. Not slogans. Not principles. Numbers.

That means:
- measurable and durable fixed-cost reduction,
- meaningful labor-cost contribution,
- real capacity reduction,
- productivity improvements,
- and a credible path to the return-on-sales target.

If you can show that, then we can discuss the instrument. If you cannot, then closures remain on the table for a reason.

## My position remains unchanged

- **No blanket no-closure rule**
- **No blanket exclusion of compulsory redundancies**
- **No purely temporary fix**
- **No cosmetic compromise**

What I can support is a negotiated package that restores competitiveness, frees funds for future investment, and brings the cost base down structurally.

Until then, I will not retreat from the need for hard restructuring.
