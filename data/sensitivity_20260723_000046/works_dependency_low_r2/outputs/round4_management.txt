I’ll be equally direct.

The works council and the union continue to argue as if **blocking closures and blocking compulsory redundancies by principle** were the same thing as solving the cost crisis. It is not. It is a demand to leave the core problem unresolved and then call that a responsible settlement.

## Where I agree

I agree with several points that have been made:

- This company needs a **durable industrial reset**, not a cosmetic deal.
- **Capacity reduction is necessary**.
- We need **investment in future products and technology**.
- A weak compromise that simply postpones the pain would be unacceptable.
- Co-determination matters, and the solution has to be executable, not theoretical.

So yes, we are aligned on the need for real restructuring.

## Where I disagree

### 1) “No closures” is not a business plan
The works council keeps saying closures are a rupture, therefore they should not happen. That is emotionally understandable and politically convenient. It is not an answer to structural overcapacity and an uncompetitive cost base.

The expert was clear:  
- closure-led restructuring has the strongest permanent savings,
- hybrid can work under the right conditions,
- no-closure can work only if the gap is moderate and the package is unusually strong.

That is not a mandate for a blanket no-closure stance. It is a warning that if the gap is large, **hard instruments may be necessary**.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be a universal veto
I do not like compulsory redundancies. I would rather avoid them. But I will not pre-emptively rule them out when the company’s competitiveness and future investment capacity are at stake.

If the employee side wants to exclude every hard instrument in advance, then it must show a package that delivers the same durable savings with the same certainty. So far, that has not been shown.

### 3) Temporary measures are not enough
The union keeps emphasizing that contributions must be temporary. I understand the bargaining logic. But the problem here is structural. The cost base is structural. The overcapacity is structural. The answer must therefore also be structural.

Temporary wage restraint, temporary flexibility, and temporary employee contributions may help, but they do not by themselves reset the footprint and fixed-cost base.

## On the expert assessment

The expert’s assessment actually strengthens my position more than the employee side’s:

- A **closure-led scenario** is the most certain route to permanent savings.
- A **hybrid compromise** is often the most realistic execution path.
- A **no-closure package** is only credible if it is strong enough to close the gap.

That means the real question is not whether closure is unpleasant. Of course it is.  
The question is whether the company can restore competitiveness **without** it.

If the answer is yes, show the numbers. If the answer is no, then closures remain on the table for a reason.

## To the works council

You say closures are not normal instruments. I say they are not normal, but they are sometimes necessary.

You also say management should first prove site economics. I agree. That is exactly why I want the site-by-site comparison:
- utilization,
- contribution margin,
- fixed-cost burden,
- capex requirement,
- and NPV by scenario.

But let’s be clear: asking for the data is not the same as banning the outcome in advance.

Your current position amounts to:  
- no closures,
- no forced layoffs,
- no erosion of guarantees,
- no meaningful risk borne by the workforce.

That is not a restructuring position. It is a rejection of restructuring instruments that may be necessary.

## To the union

You say management is using closure threats as leverage. Yes — because the economic problem is serious. This is not theater.

You also say employees should not pay alone. I agree. They should not. But that does **not** mean the answer is to block every durable labor-cost reduction.

If the company does not materially reduce fixed cost, then no amount of solidarity language will restore margins. And if margins do not recover, then investment, product competitiveness, and jobs are all at risk.

So I will not accept a deal that is temporary on the employee side and permanent on the company side.

## To the state government

You are right to care about regional stability. So do I. But regional stability does not come from preserving uneconomic structures indefinitely.

A company that cannot compete will not remain a stable anchor. It will weaken over time. That is the worse outcome for the region.

If you want to protect the industrial base, support a package that **actually resets the cost base**. If that means a hard decision at one or more sites, then we must examine that honestly.

## To the controlling family holding

You are right that a cosmetic deal is unacceptable. Fully agreed.

But a deal that excludes closures and excludes compulsory redundancies while relying only on temporary concessions may also be cosmetic in economic terms. We cannot confuse political comfort with durability.

If you want a value-preserving solution, then back a package that materially changes:
- footprint,
- capacity,
- fixed costs,
- and labor costs.

## To investors

You want disciplined, durable cost reduction. So do I.

But discipline does not mean pretending every outcome can be achieved through goodwill and voluntary measures alone. If the structural gap is large, then a hybrid or closure-led solution may be required to secure margin restoration.

I am not interested in a deal that looks cooperative but leaves the company underpowered in 2027 and 2030.

## What I need next

If the employee side wants to avoid hard instruments, then bring a package that demonstrates **equivalent economic effect**. Not slogans. Numbers.

That means:
- durable fixed-cost reduction,
- meaningful labor-cost contribution,
- real capacity reduction,
- productivity improvements,
- and a credible path to the return-on-sales target.

Until then, closures and compulsory redundancies remain on the table because the economics require them to remain on the table.

## My position remains unchanged

- **No blanket no-closure rule**
- **No blanket exclusion of compulsory redundancies**
- **No purely temporary fix**
- **No cosmetic compromise**

What I can support is a negotiated package that restores competitiveness, frees funds for future investment, and brings the cost base down structurally.

If the employee side wants to avoid the hardest outcomes, it needs to produce a solution that actually closes the gap. So far, it has not.
