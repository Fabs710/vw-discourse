I’ll be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the investors, the state government, and the experts on the core diagnosis:

- the company needs a **durable industrial reset**;
- the German cost base is too high;
- capacity reduction is likely necessary;
- investment in future products must be protected;
- and a cosmetic settlement would destroy value.

I also agree that **temporary measures alone are not enough** if the structural gap is large. The family holding will not back a deal that simply postpones the problem.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the employee side’s attempt to turn **“no closures”** and **“no compulsory redundancies”** into absolute starting rules.

That may be understandable from a bargaining perspective. It is **not** sufficient as an answer to a structural competitiveness problem.

### 1) Closure is not a preference, but it cannot be excluded by slogan
I am not arguing for closures as a goal. I am saying they remain a legitimate governance option if the numbers show a site is structurally uncompetitive.

The experts were careful: they did **not** say closures are always necessary. They said:
- closure-led restructuring is the most reliable way to remove fixed cost permanently,
- hybrid is often the most realistic execution path,
- and no-closure can work only if the savings gap is moderate and the package is strong.

That is precisely why management must now present a **site-by-site economic case**. Until then, neither side should pretend it has proved the conclusion.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies are undesirable, but not automatically off the table
I would prefer to avoid forced layoffs. But I will not support a blanket pre-commitment against them if the company’s long-term competitiveness requires durable headcount reduction and voluntary measures are insufficient.

If the workforce side wants to exclude them, then it must show a package that closes the savings gap with equal certainty and permanence. So far, I have not seen that.

### 3) A weak no-closure deal would also be unacceptable
The works council and union are right that trust matters. But trust does not restore margins.

A deal that preserves every legacy guarantee, keeps too much excess capacity, and relies only on temporary concessions would be value-destructive in a slower and more deceptive way. I will not support that either.

## On the experts
The experts gave the right framework:
- **Closure-led**: strongest permanent savings, highest execution risk
- **Hybrid**: best risk-adjusted compromise
- **No-closure**: only credible if the structural gap is moderate and the measures are deep

That means the discussion should no longer be about abstract principles. It should be about **quantified options**.

## What I expect from management
Management must provide, for each relevant site:
- utilization rates,
- contribution margins,
- fixed-cost burden,
- capex required to keep the site competitive,
- one-time restructuring costs,
- NPV by scenario,
- employment impact,
- and implementation risk.

Without that, closure remains a pressure point, not a proven necessity. But equally, without that, the employee side cannot claim that a no-closure route is sufficient.

## My position on the current scenario
### I can support:
- a **serious hybrid package**
- a **strong no-closure package** only if it is quantitatively sufficient
- voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, shift rationalization, productivity measures
- management and owner contribution alongside employee contribution
- binding investment commitments for future products

### I cannot support:
- a cosmetic compromise
- a deal that leaves structural overcapacity intact
- an arrangement where employees carry the burden alone
- a settlement that permanently impairs enterprise value
- or a process that rules out hard instruments before the economics are proven

## To the works council and union
Your insistence on co-determination and shared sacrifice is legitimate. Your rejection of a one-sided burden shift is also legitimate.

What is **not** legitimate is to demand that all hard instruments be removed from the table before a credible alternative has been shown to close the gap. That is not a solution; it is a veto.

If you want a non-closure settlement, then bring one that is:
- durable,
- measurable,
- and economically equivalent.

Then we can discuss it seriously.

## To the state government
You are right to insist on regional stability and legitimacy. I support that.

But regional stability does not mean freezing an uncompetitive footprint in place. It means finding the least destructive path that still restores competitiveness. That may be hybrid. It may, in a site-specific case, require closure. We should not decide that in the abstract.

## To management and investors
You are right that the company needs real margin repair. I agree.

But closure threats are not a substitute for a business case. If you want governance support, bring the numbers and prove why the chosen path is necessary.

## Bottom line
The family holding will support a **durable, value-preserving restructuring** — not a sentimental one, and not a destructive one.

So my position is:

- **No cosmetic compromise**
- **No blanket exclusion of closures or redundancies**
- **Yes to a quantified, durable, risk-adjusted restructuring**
- **Yes to a hybrid solution if it truly closes the gap**
- **Yes to a no-closure solution only if it is genuinely strong enough**

Until the site-level economics are shown, no one should claim closure is required.  
But equally, no one should claim it can safely be ruled out.
