I will be equally direct.

The employee side keeps repeating “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no dismantling of guarantees,” but that does not solve the company’s problem. It only defines what you refuse to bear. It does **not** define how the German cost base becomes competitive.

## Where I agree
I agree on three points:

- This company needs a **durable industrial solution**, not a temporary patch.
- Investment in future products and technology is essential.
- Capacity and workforce adjustments must be discussed in a serious, quantified way.

So yes, we are aligned on the need for change.

## Where I disagree
I **do not accept** the claim that management is simply choosing the “harshest” route for its own sake.

We are facing:
- structural overcapacity,
- a shrinking European market,
- intense low-cost competition,
- and a German cost base that is too high.

In that situation, saying “no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” as absolute rules is not a solution. It is a veto on restructuring.

### 1) No closures as an absolute red line is not credible
If all German sites are untouchable, then you are asking the company to absorb permanent overcapacity and then somehow become competitive anyway. That is not realistic.

I am not saying closures are the preferred instrument. I am saying they cannot be ruled out in advance. If a site is structurally uncompetitive, then management must retain the ability to act. Otherwise the burden is just pushed into weaker productivity, lower investment, and eventual decline.

### 2) “Temporary contributions only” is insufficient
The union says any employee contribution must be temporary. That does not match the scale of the problem.

We need a **durable** cost effect. If contributions expire before the business problem is fixed, then we are back here again in a few years with the same overhang and less credibility.

### 3) Burden-sharing cannot mean only symbolic restraint from management
I accept that management, ownership, and capital must all contribute to the solution. But that does not change the arithmetic. Overhead discipline, leadership restraint, and owner support are helpful; they are not a substitute for structural cost reduction in the footprint and workforce.

## To the works council
You say the workforce should not pay for the crisis. I reject that framing.

This is not about “paying for management mistakes” in a moral sense. It is about whether the company has a viable future. If the cost base is uncompetitive, then everyone’s position is weaker — including jobs, sites, investment, and long-term security.

You also say capacity reductions can be discussed but closures cannot. That is an artificial distinction if the required savings are large enough. Capacity reduction without the ability to close or restructure sites may simply be too shallow to matter.

## To the union
You say closures and compulsory redundancies are a threat that makes partnership meaningless. I disagree.

The real threat to partnership is insisting on a structure that cannot deliver competitiveness. If the settlement does not materially reduce fixed costs, we will not restore margins, and then no one gets stability.

You ask for binding job and site security through 2030. I cannot accept blanket security to 2030 if it prevents the company from making the changes required to survive that period.

## To the state government
I understand your concern for regional legitimacy, and I do not dismiss it. But I will not allow governance to become a shield against necessary adjustment.

If the company is to remain an industrial anchor, it must be competitive. Social legitimacy depends on having a business that survives, not on preserving every legacy structure unchanged.

## To the family holding and investors
I agree with you that a cosmetic settlement would be unacceptable. That is exactly my concern.

But I also need to be clear: if the governance side backs a solution that excludes closures, excludes compulsory redundancies, and only offers temporary concessions, then you are backing a deal that likely does not go far enough. That would undermine investor confidence just as surely as a strike would.

## What I need to see
If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then bring a package that delivers **equivalent economic effect**. That means quantified, not rhetorical.

I want to see:
- a measurable and durable reduction in fixed costs,
- a meaningful labour-cost contribution,
- capacity reduction at a scale that actually changes the footprint,
- productivity improvements,
- and a credible path to the brand’s return-on-sales target.

If you can show that a no-closure package achieves that, then we can discuss the instrument. But if the package is essentially “preserve everything, add temporary concessions, and hope for the best,” then I will reject it.

## Bottom line
I am not prepared to sign up to a deal that preserves the existing cost base under a nicer label.

- **No blanket no-closure rule**
- **No blanket exclusion of compulsory redundancies**
- **No purely temporary fixes**
- **No cosmetic compromise**

What I can accept is a negotiated package that produces durable structural savings and restores competitiveness. That is the standard. If the employee side wants a real settlement, it has to meet that standard.
