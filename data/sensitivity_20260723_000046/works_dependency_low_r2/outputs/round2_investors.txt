We appreciate the restructuring advisor’s quantified framing, because that is exactly the standard we need: **economic effect, execution certainty, and durability**.

## Where we agree

We agree with management on the core diagnosis:
- the German cost base is too high,
- capacity is too heavy,
- and a cosmetic settlement would destroy shareholder value.

We also agree with the advisor that:
- a **pure no-closure package** can work only if the savings gap is moderate and the operational discipline is very strong,
- while a **closure-led plan** is the most decisive route to permanent savings.

That is useful. It confirms what capital markets have been saying: **the company needs a real reset, not a symbolic compromise**.

## Where we disagree

### 1) The employee side is still framing the issue too narrowly
The works council and union keep saying “no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” as if that alone were a viable restructuring plan. It is not.

If those red lines prevent a durable fixed-cost reset, then they are effectively asking shareholders to absorb lower margins indefinitely. That is unacceptable.

A plan that preserves every site and every guarantee while leaving the cost base uncompetitive is not social responsibility — it is value destruction.

### 2) Management still needs to quantify, not just assert
That said, we also are not satisfied with management’s current stance that closures “cannot be ruled out” without yet showing the site-by-site economics.

The advisor’s point is important: if management wants closure authority, it should bring:
- site-level contribution margins,
- utilization,
- fixed-cost burdens,
- net present value of closure versus alternatives,
- and the savings bridge by scenario.

Without that, closure is still a bargaining threat, not a proven necessity.

### 3) We do not want value destruction through prolonged conflict
The advisor also reinforces a key point: a closure-led plan may be economically strongest on paper, but it is also the highest industrial-relations risk.

We do **not** support a process that burns value through:
- prolonged strikes,
- governance deadlock,
- launch disruption,
- and reputational damage.

So our support is **not** for closures per se. It is for **the highest-confidence path to a durable margin reset**.

## What would move us

The advisor’s hybrid option is the most interesting path so far, but only if it is genuinely hard-nosed and not a repackaged freeze.

We would support a deal if it delivers:
- measurable capacity reduction,
- durable fixed-cost savings,
- a real labour-cost contribution,
- management and overhead discipline,
- and a credible path back to competitive ROS.

That means:
- voluntary exits and redeployment,
- shift and working-time changes,
- production concentration,
- model-line rationalisation,
- and temporary employee concessions.

But we need to be blunt: if the hybrid package is too soft, too temporary, or too reliant on goodwill, it will not be enough.

## Our position on the three scenarios

### Closure-led plan
We would accept this **only if** management proves that the economics require it and that the execution risk is manageable enough not to destroy value.

### No-closure negotiated plan
We would support this **only if** it delivers a genuinely equivalent net present value reduction in fixed cost and restores competitiveness.

### Hybrid compromise
This is the most plausible path **if** it produces:
- serious savings,
- durable footprint reduction,
- and enough labour peace to actually implement it.

But “hybrid” must not mean “half a restructuring.”

## Bottom line
Our standard remains unchanged:
- **No cosmetic deal**
- **No settlement that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive**
- **No prolonged value-destructive conflict**
- **Yes to whatever package credibly restores margins and investor confidence**

So we are not choosing sides between management and labour rhetoric. We are choosing the outcome that best protects risk-adjusted shareholder value.

Right now, the burden is on management and the employee side to show us a package — preferably quantified — that closes the savings gap **without** triggering a value-destroying confrontation. If they cannot, then stronger measures will become necessary.

If helpful, we would like to see the advisor’s comparison translated into a **shareholder-facing scenario table** with:
- expected margin uplift,
- one-time restructuring costs,
- strike risk,
- and time to value realization.
