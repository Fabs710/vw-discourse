I will be direct.

The family holding is **not** prepared to back a settlement that preserves the current cost base, protects every legacy guarantee indefinitely, and leaves the company structurally uncompetitive. That would be a slow-value destruction strategy, and we will not support it.

At the same time, we are **also not** prepared to endorse a confrontation-first approach that defaults to plant closures and compulsory redundancies before every serious alternative has been tested. A prolonged industrial conflict, reputational damage, and governance deadlock would also destroy value. So neither extreme is acceptable.

## Where I agree

### To management
You are right that:
- the German cost base is too high,
- capacity has to come down,
- and the company needs a durable improvement in margins and competitiveness.

That diagnosis is credible.

### To the works council, union, and state
You are right that:
- a cosmetic deal is useless,
- labour peace matters,
- investment in future products matters,
- and the company cannot be run as if co-determination were decorative.

Those are real constraints, not slogans.

## Where I disagree

### With management
What I do **not** accept is the claim that closures and compulsory redundancies must remain the default route just because the economics are difficult.

If you want governance support for a hard restructuring, you must show:
- the quantified savings gap,
- why internal redeployment and working-time measures are insufficient,
- why capacity reduction cannot be achieved through less destructive means,
- and why a negotiated labour-cost contribution would not close most of the gap.

At this stage, I do **not** see enough evidence that closures are the only credible instrument. “Cannot be ruled out” is not the same as “necessary.”

### With the works council and union
I also do **not** accept the blanket position of:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no meaningful structural change.

If the company’s footprint is uncompetitive, then some adjustment must happen. If the response is simply to defend every site and every historic protection, then we are just postponing the issue until it becomes worse.

So: absolute preservation is not acceptable either.

### With the state government
You are right to insist on social legitimacy and regional stability. But that cannot become a veto over necessary restructuring. The company must remain competitive, or there will be no stable industrial base to protect.

## What would move us

We would support a **serious negotiated package** if it produces durable economic improvement **without permanent damage to the enterprise**. That means:

- measurable and lasting capacity reduction,
- a real labour-cost contribution,
- productivity and flexibility improvements,
- management and overhead discipline,
- a credible investment path for future products,
- and labour peace.

But I want to be clear: **the instrument matters only insofar as it delivers the economics and avoids value-destroying conflict**.

## My current position

- **No support for a “preserve everything” deal.**
- **No blank cheque for closures or forced layoffs.**
- **No acceptance of a purely temporary fix that leaves the cost base unchanged.**
- **Yes to a durable restructuring that restores competitiveness and protects long-term value.**

## What I need next
Before anyone asks the supervisory board to bless a final package, management should put forward a **quantified side-by-side comparison** of:

1. closures / compulsory reductions, versus  
2. a negotiated alternative with capacity cuts, working-time changes, redeployment, and employee contribution.

We need to see:
- savings,
- employment effects,
- execution risk,
- strike risk,
- and expected impact on margins and future investment.

If the alternative without closures truly delivers the same durable effect, I am open to it. If it does not, then we will not hide behind sentiment. But I will not endorse closures as a reflex.
