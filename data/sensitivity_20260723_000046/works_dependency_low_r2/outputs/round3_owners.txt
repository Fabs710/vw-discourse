I will respond directly.

## First: where I agree

I agree with the central point raised by management, investors, and the experts: **this company needs a durable industrial reset**. A cosmetic agreement that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive would be value-destructive, and the family holding will not back that.

I also agree that:
- capacity must come down,
- investment in future products and technology must be protected,
- and a deal that simply postpones the problem is not acceptable.

So the diagnosis is not the dispute. The dispute is the **instrument**.

## Where I disagree

### 1) I do not accept closure as the default answer
Management keeps saying plant closures “cannot be ruled out.” That may be true in a legal sense, but it is **not yet a proven necessity**.

The experts were careful: they did **not** say closures are required in every case. They said:
- closure-led restructuring is the most decisive for permanent savings,
- hybrid is often the most realistic execution path,
- and no-closure can work if the savings gap is moderate and the package is strong.

That means the burden is still on management to prove, site by site, why a closure is economically unavoidable. Until then, closure is not a conclusion. It is an option.

### 2) I do not accept compulsory redundancies as a starting position
Forced layoffs are a blunt instrument. They may be available in extremis, but I will not support them as the opening move when other tools remain untested:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- shift reduction,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity improvements,
- overhead discipline,
- and negotiated employee contributions.

If management wants governance support for compulsory redundancies, it needs to demonstrate that those alternatives are insufficient.

### 3) I do not accept a narrow “employees must pay” framing
The works council and union are right on one point: a one-sided burden shift would destroy trust and likely trigger conflict that damages value. That is not a social preference; it is an economic fact.

But I also do not accept the opposite extreme: preserving every legacy guarantee indefinitely while expecting the company to recover competitiveness by wishful thinking. That also destroys value.

So the right answer is not “employees pay everything” or “nothing changes.” It is a **real shared-sacrifice package with measurable economic effect**.

## On the arguments from the works council and union

I understand why you reject closures and compulsory redundancies. As bargaining positions, those are coherent. But if they are absolute red lines without a quantified alternative that closes the savings gap, then they become a veto on restructuring.

That is not something I can support.

You say:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no erosion of guarantees,
- strong co-determination,
- site security through 2030.

I can support parts of that **only if** the package delivers the required economics. If it does not, then the line is too rigid.

The question is not whether your concerns are legitimate. They are.  
The question is whether your preferred instruments are sufficient. That is still unproven.

## On management and investors

You are right that a weak compromise would be unacceptable. But you are not yet entitled to conclude that only closures can solve this.

The expert assessments actually point to the more prudent path: **a serious hybrid package first**, with quantified triggers and review points.

That is the route that best balances:
- durable savings,
- execution feasibility,
- labor peace,
- and preservation of the enterprise’s long-term value.

If management jumps too early to closure threats, it risks the very instability it says it wants to avoid. If it jumps too late, it may miss the window to restore competitiveness. Both are bad. That is why I want the numbers, not slogans.

## On the regional state government

Your concern for regional stability is well taken. That is exactly why I am not willing to support a confrontation-first approach.

But I will also say this: regional legitimacy does not mean freezing the footprint in place. It means designing a restructuring that preserves the industrial base **without** gratuitously detonating trust.

That again points toward a hybrid solution, not a reflexive closure strategy.

## What would move me

I am open to a negotiated package if it includes all of the following:
- measurable and durable capacity reduction,
- real labor-cost contribution,
- overhead and management discipline,
- productivity improvements,
- credible investment commitments,
- and enough labor peace to implement the plan.

I am **not** open to:
- a cosmetically softer version of the current cost base,
- a blanket no-change stance from the employee side,
- or closure threats used as leverage before the economics are proven.

## My position

### I can support:
- a strong hybrid restructuring,
- a no-closure package only if it is genuinely strong and quantitatively sufficient,
- temporary employee contributions if matched by management restraint and real operational change.

### I cannot support:
- a weak compromise,
- preserving all legacy protections with no structural adjustment,
- or a closure-first strategy without a demonstrated site-level business case.

## What I want next
Before anyone asks the supervisory board to bless a final plan, I want management to present:
1. site-by-site economics,
2. a quantified savings bridge for each scenario,
3. one-time costs and time to value,
4. employment effects,
5. strike and execution risk,
6. and a clear comparison of:
   - closure-led,
   - hybrid,
   - and no-closure alternatives.

If the hybrid or no-closure package truly closes the gap, I will support it over a destructive closure fight.  
If it does not, then the employee side should not expect us to endorse a deal that preserves value only in appearance.

## Bottom line
The family holding will back a **durable competitiveness solution**, not a sentimental one and not a destructive one.

- No cosmetic compromise.
- No reflexive closure policy.
- No blank cheque for compulsory redundancies.
- Yes to a serious, quantified, value-preserving restructuring.

If management wants our support, it must prove that the chosen path restores margins **and** avoids value-destroying instability.
