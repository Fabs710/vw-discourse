Let me be equally clear from management’s side:

We **do** agree on one point: this company needs a credible long-term industrial plan for Germany, not a cosmetic fix. We also agree that investment, future products, and qualification matter. But we **fundamentally disagree** with the idea that we can preserve every plant, protect every job to 2030, and still restore competitiveness when our German cost base is structurally too high.

That combination does not add up.

### Where I disagree

1. **No plant closures is not a viable starting position**
   
   We cannot rule out closures in advance when the company’s overcapacity is real and when some sites are materially above target on cost and productivity. If we do that, we are guaranteeing that the adjustment falls elsewhere in a weaker, slower, and ultimately more damaging form.  
   A restructuring that leaves every site untouched is not a restructuring. It is postponement.

2. **No compulsory redundancies cannot be the absolute condition**
   
   I understand the concern, but if we exclude all workforce reduction instruments, we lose the ability to make the cost base fit for the market we actually face. Internal redeployment, retraining, attrition, and working-time changes can contribute, but they will not be enough on their own if we are serious about closing the gap.

3. **Temporary employee contributions alone are insufficient**
   
   We need a genuine, durable labour-cost contribution, not only short-term concessions with expiry dates that simply recreate the problem later. This must have structural effect. Otherwise we are asking shareholders and the company to carry the burden while leaving the underlying cost position unchanged.

### What management is prepared to say

We are not here to provoke conflict for its own sake. We are here because the business case is urgent and the timeline is real.

So I will be direct about the conditions under which I can move:

- **A measurable, durable reduction in capacity and fixed cost**
- **A real labour-cost contribution**
- **A settlement that restores the brand’s return-on-sales path**
- **A package that frees funding for future products and technology through 2030**

If the employee side brings a credible package that achieves those outcomes without closures, then we can discuss the instrument. But the **outcome** must be equivalent in economic effect. I will not accept a deal that preserves every structure and merely repackages the same cost base.

### On your points about fairness and burden-sharing

I do not dismiss the issue of shared sacrifice. Management, ownership, and employees all have a stake in this company’s future. But the arithmetic is decisive: if the German footprint stays too expensive, then no amount of fairness language will save jobs in the medium term.

So yes, we are willing to discuss:
- capacity reductions,
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- and a negotiated employee contribution.

But no, I cannot accept:
- a blanket prohibition on closures,
- a blanket ban on compulsory redundancies,
- or a framework that prevents us from achieving the structural savings we need.

### Bottom line

We need a settlement that changes the cost base **for good**, not a temporary peace that leaves the company uncompetitive. If the answer is simply “protect everything,” then we are not solving the crisis.

I am prepared to negotiate the path, but not the necessity of deep structural change.
