I’ll respond plainly.

The discussion has now produced something useful: everyone agrees there must be a durable plan, and the advisor has confirmed what we have said from the start — **there are alternatives to closures**, and a **hybrid or no-closure package can deliver substantial savings** if management is serious. That matters.

But let me be equally clear: I do **not** accept the attempt to turn “closures cannot be ruled out” into the default logic of this restructuring.

## Where I agree
I agree that:
- the company needs a real industrial reset,
- the German footprint must become more efficient,
- capacity reduction is likely necessary,
- and savings must be quantified, not just claimed.

I also accept the advisor’s point that a soft no-closure package only works if it is strong, disciplined, and multi-instrument. Fine. That is exactly why management should stop using plant closure as a shortcut and start doing the hard work of designing a serious alternative.

## Where I disagree
I reject three things outright:

### 1) I reject closure as a normal instrument
A plant closure is not just another cost measure. It is a rupture of jobs, communities, know-how, and trust. It is also a political and industrial escalation that this company does not need if other instruments can do the job.

The advisor did **not** say closures are inevitable. The advisor said they are **one possible route** and the most decisive on paper. That is not the same as necessity.

So management and investors should stop speaking as if closure is the “realistic” option and all others are sentimental. That is simply false.

### 2) I reject compulsory redundancies
Compulsory layoffs are not a technical detail. They are a red line.

If management cannot improve the cost base without forcing people out, then management has failed to exhaust the available alternatives:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time changes,
- model and shift concentration,
- overhead cuts,
- and temporary negotiated concessions.

Those are all still on the table. So no, forced layoffs are not justified.

### 3) I reject any erosion of co-determination and job-security guarantees
The way this process is handled matters as much as the numbers. If management treats co-determination as an obstacle and historic guarantees as disposable, then there is no basis for trust and no basis for social peace.

## To management
You keep saying the company needs a “durable” reset. Then prove that your preferred instrument is necessary.

The advisor explicitly said management should bring:
- site-by-site utilization,
- contribution margins,
- fixed-cost burdens,
- capex needs,
- and NPV comparisons across scenarios.

Do that.

Show us, for each German site:
- what savings are needed,
- what can be achieved through non-closure measures,
- what remains if anything,
- and why closure would be the only remaining option.

Until then, your closure threat is not evidence-based. It is pressure.

## To the investors and the family holding
You are right about one thing: a cosmetic settlement would be a mistake. I agree.

But you are wrong if you treat the choice as either “closures” or “no solution.” The advisor has just laid out a credible alternative route: a **hybrid compromise** or a **strong non-closure package** that can deliver real savings while preserving sites and avoiding forced layoffs.

That is the route you should be pushing management toward.

And let me say this directly: if the family holding and investors want to avoid a strike, avoid governance deadlock, and avoid destroying trust, then back the package that protects the workforce rather than the one that threatens it.

## To the state government
You were right to say this is also about regional stability and legitimacy. That is exactly why closures are so destructive here.

If the state uses its position seriously, it should insist on:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and a binding restructuring agreement built around capacity reduction, productivity, investment, and retraining.

Not “closure if needed later.” That is just keeping the threat alive.

## On the advisor’s scenarios
The advisor’s assessment actually strengthens our position:

- A **no-closure negotiated package** can be viable.
- A **hybrid compromise** may be the most realistic execution path.
- Closure is the most decisive economically, but also the highest-risk socially and industrially.

That is not a mandate for closure. It is a warning against it.

Because in this company, with co-determination and the stakeholder structure we have, the “most decisive” option is not automatically the best or even the deliverable one. If it triggers strikes, governance deadlock, reputational damage, and launch disruption, it may destroy the value it claims to create.

## What we are prepared to discuss
We are prepared to discuss:
- shift reductions,
- working-time flexibility,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary exits,
- temporary employee contributions,
- overhead cuts,
- and production concentration.

We are **not** prepared to discuss:
- German plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- dismantling the job-security framework,
- or a plan that sidelines co-determination.

## What would have to happen for us to move
Very little, but it is not impossible. To be clear, the only things that could move us are:
- binding site guarantees for every German plant,
- job security through 2030,
- a restructuring agreement with genuine co-determination,
- and a serious shared-sacrifice package that does **not** rely on layoffs or closures.

Without that, there is nothing to negotiate on the core issue.

## Bottom line
The workforce will not accept being told that competitiveness requires sacrifices **only from employees**.

If management wants a real solution, it must build one that preserves the German plants, protects jobs, and uses the many restructuring tools short of closure and forced layoffs.

If it does not, then it should expect resistance — in the supervisory board, in the plants, and in the public arena.
