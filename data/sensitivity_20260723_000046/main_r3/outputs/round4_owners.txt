## My position
I support a **durable restructuring that restores competitiveness and margins**. I do **not** support preserving every current structure if the numbers show that the company cannot compete that way.

At the same time, I do **not** support rushing into plant closures or compulsory redundancies as a first move when the independent, site-specific case has not yet been fully tested. A prolonged labour war would destroy value just as surely as an uncompetitive cost base would.

So my standard is simple:
- **no cosmetic deal**
- **no permanent impairment of the enterprise**
- **no governance drift into paralysis**
- **no value-destroying conflict**

## To the works council
I agree with you on one point: management has not yet demonstrated, in a fully transparent way, that closures are the only credible route.

I also agree that burden-sharing cannot mean labour pays while everyone else lectures. That would be unstable and unfair.

Where I disagree is your insistence that closures and compulsory redundancies are categorically off the table in advance. That is not stewardship; it is a veto on any hard adjustment, regardless of the numbers. If the residual gap is large enough, then refusing any footprint reduction would simply postpone the correction and worsen the eventual damage.

What I want to see is the accounting, not slogans:
- savings from voluntary exits
- early retirement
- internal transfers
- working-time changes
- product concentration
- overhead cuts
- management restraint
- owner-side support
- and the residual gap

If, after that, the remaining gap is genuinely modest, then a non-closure solution deserves serious support. If it is not modest, then the employee side cannot just declare the outcome unacceptable and expect the company to absorb the loss forever.

## To the union
You are more constructive than the works council because you at least acknowledge temporary wage restraint, flexibility, and voluntary exits as possible tools.

That said, I will be equally frank: temporary concessions only matter if they produce a durable reset. A two-year bridge that leaves the structure unchanged is not a solution.

I could support a temporary labour contribution if it is tied to:
- quantified structural savings
- real capacity reduction
- management and owner contributions
- transparent monitoring
- and a credible path to restored returns

But I will not support a package where labour gives and the rest of the structure is protected from change.

## To management
I agree with management on the central diagnosis: the company needs a durable reset, not another postponement. I also agree that savings must be measurable and enforceable.

Where I am not yet satisfied is that the case for closures is still being treated more as a bargaining lever than as a proven necessity. That is not good enough for a decision with irreversible consequences.

You should present the comparison cleanly:
1. non-closure package
2. hybrid downsizing package
3. closure-heavy package

For each, I want:
- annual savings
- one-time transition costs
- liquidity impact
- implementation risk
- and timing to margin recovery

If non-closure measures close most of the gap, then fine, that is preferable. If they do not, then we need to face the footprint question honestly. But you do not get to shortcut the analysis and ask governance to bless the harshest path on instinct alone.

## To the regional state government
I understand your political and regional responsibility. I also respect that the special statute gives you real leverage.

But that leverage cannot be used to freeze an uncompetitive structure indefinitely. If the business cannot earn its cost of capital, then all jobs are eventually at risk, only later and more painfully.

So I support a process that protects regional stability, but not one that turns every hard adjustment into a taboo. If the numbers justify selective footprint reduction, you should not pretend otherwise.

## To institutional investors
Your insistence on durability and capital discipline is legitimate. I share it.

What I do not accept is the idea that only closures can produce discipline. They may be necessary in some cases, but they are not automatically the answer. The question is whether the residual gap after alternatives is small enough to bridge.

If a non-closure or hybrid package can produce:
- real fixed-cost reduction
- durable margin improvement
- and implementation certainty

then that is the preferable outcome. If it cannot, then the burden shifts back to those opposing structural change.

## On the expert inputs
The finance and operations experts were useful because they clarified the real decision rule:
- a modest residual gap can be bridged without closures
- a medium gap pushes toward hybrid downsizing
- a large gap makes footprint reduction difficult to avoid

That is exactly why I want the plant-by-plant workup now.

## My governance stance
I will support a settlement only if it meets the following conditions:
- it is economically credible
- it is durable
- it protects long-term enterprise value
- it avoids unnecessary instability
- it shares the burden in a balanced way
- and it does not lock us into permanent underperformance

I will **not** support:
- a cosmetic compromise
- a deal that merely delays the problem
- a settlement that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive
- or a confrontation that destroys value through strikes and reputational damage

## Bottom line
I am not siding with any bloc out of habit.

- The employee side has not yet shown that a no-closure package closes the gap.
- Management has not yet shown that closures are unavoidable.
- The state should not freeze the company’s future.
- Investors should not demand the cheapest accounting answer if it creates a costly conflict.

The right next step is the plant-by-plant, side-by-side financial comparison. After that, we can decide what is genuinely unavoidable.

Until then, my position is:
**support restructuring, insist on hard numbers, and keep closures available only if the evidence shows they are necessary — not because they are convenient.**
