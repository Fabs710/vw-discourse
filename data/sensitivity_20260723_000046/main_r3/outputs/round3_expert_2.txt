## The capacity logic

In manufacturing, the critical issue is whether the plants are:
- **variable-cost efficient but underloaded**, or
- **structurally overfixed** with too much plant, indirect labor, and support cost for the volume they carry.

A plant can be “competitive” on unit labor cost and still be uneconomic if utilization is too low.

### General rule
When utilization falls below the point where fixed cost per vehicle rises sharply, you get a cost cliff.

For large automotive plants, the rough threshold often lies around:

- **70–80% utilization** for a well-balanced site,
- below **~65%** the economics usually deteriorate quickly,
- below **~50–60%** the site often requires either major consolidation, product concentration, or closure logic.

That is not a universal law, but it is a useful screening rule.

## What usually works without closure

The following are the main non-closure levers, in order of likely effectiveness:

### A) Product concentration
Move fewer models onto fewer lines/sites.

- This is often the strongest non-closure lever.
- It can materially improve utilization.
- It reduces indirect cost and complexity.
- It often requires capex and logistics changes, but can save more than wage moderation alone.

### B) Single-shift or shift elimination
If volume does not justify multiple shifts, removing a shift cuts capacity and variable premium cost.

- Good for quick capacity reduction.
- Savings depend on how much fixed cost remains.
- Less effective if the site is heavily fixed-cost loaded.

### C) Voluntary exits / early retirement
This is politically easier and can remove labor cost without forced redundancies.

- Best where age structure supports it.
- Usually expensive upfront.
- Works well if used at scale.

### D) Overhead and management reduction
Important, but usually not enough alone.

- Corporate overhead can be cut.
- Site management layers can be flattened.
- Procurement/logistics/admin can be centralized.

### E) Working-time flexibility and wage restraint
Useful as a bridge, but rarely enough on its own to solve a large structural overcapacity problem.
