I will be equally clear.

## To the works council
I agree with your red lines:
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no plant closures as the default answer**
- **no dismantling of co-determination**

But I need to correct one thing in your framing: a **durable settlement** will not be achieved by repeating “no closures” alone. If we want to keep jobs and preserve the collective wage framework, then we must also put a **serious quantified alternative** on the table. Otherwise management will keep using the threat of closures to split the front.

So I support your position, but I want it backed by a binding package that shows:
- voluntary departures only,
- internal transfers,
- early retirement / partial retirement,
- working-time flexibility,
- bonus restraint or temporary wage contribution,
- and management/owner contributions.

That is the only way to make “no closures” economically credible.

## To management
I hear your insistence on durability and measurable savings. That is legitimate. What is **not** legitimate is turning that into a closure ultimatum before the alternatives have been fully quantified.

You keep saying “closures remain on the table.” Fine. But then you need to prove, with plant-by-plant numbers, that the remaining gap after alternatives is genuinely unbridgeable. Until then, closures are **a threat**, not a necessity.

And let me be explicit:
- We will not accept compulsory redundancies.
- We will not accept plant closures in Germany as a first response.
- We will not accept a restructuring where labour carries the pain while management and capital ask for sacrifice in words only.

If you want labour peace and a real settlement, bring a side-by-side comparison with hard numbers:
1. savings from labour measures,
2. savings from management overhead and executive restraint,
3. savings from owner support,
4. savings from internal capacity reduction,
5. residual gap after all of that.

If that residual gap is still huge, then we can discuss further. But until then, the burden is on you.

## To the expert assessments
The finance and capacity analysis are useful because they confirm what we already know:
- temporary measures alone are not enough,
- but neither is a closure-first policy automatically justified,
- and the key issue is the **residual gap after all alternatives**.

That is exactly why I am not retreating from the union’s position, but I am also not accepting vague optimism. We need **hard numbers**:
- current utilization,
- fixed cost by site,
- cost of voluntary exits,
- cost of working-time measures,
- owner-side capital support,
- and the gap that remains.

### What I take from the experts
If the gap is only moderate, then a negotiated non-closure package is viable.
If the gap is very large, then management must prove why **major downsizing without closures** is impossible before it can demand plant closure.

So yes, I support the call for an independent plant-by-plant assessment.

## To the regional state government
I appreciate that you reject closures and forced redundancies. But I need you to do more than express concern.

If the company tries to force a closure-heavy outcome, the state must use its blocking position to stop it. At the same time, if we are serious about preserving the industrial base, the state should also push for:
- investment commitments,
- qualification support,
- transition finance,
- and pressure on management and owners to contribute materially.

The workforce should not be the only side expected to “be realistic.”

## To the controlling family
Your language about stewardship is only convincing if you accept that stewardship includes **sharing the burden**.

I agree that a cosmetic deal is unacceptable. But so is a deal where the entire adjustment is forced onto the workforce through layoffs, site cuts, and wage pressure.

If you want to preserve long-term enterprise value, then support:
- a temporary labour contribution,
- management pay restraint,
- owner-side capital support,
- and binding site security.

If you refuse those and still demand closures, then you are choosing confrontation, not stewardship.

## To institutional investors
You are right to demand durability and credibility. I agree with that. But durability does **not** automatically mean plant closures. It means a settlement that actually holds.

A durable package must include:
- measurable annual savings,
- real implementation milestones,
- burden-sharing across all stakeholders,
- and a guarantee that the workforce is not the only adjustment variable.

If you want a stable solution, then support a package that preserves jobs and the collective agreement while delivering cost reduction. If the company is forced into a strike and legal conflict because management refuses that, value will be destroyed anyway.

## My position
Let me restate it plainly:

### Non-negotiable
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No plant closures in Germany**
- **Preservation of the collective wage framework**
- **No one-sided burden on labour**

### Open for negotiation
- temporary wage restraint or bonus suspension
- working-time flexibility
- voluntary exit programs
- internal redeployment and retraining
- early retirement / partial retirement
- management overhead and executive restraint
- owner support for transition and investment

That is a real shared-sacrifice package. If management wants labour cooperation, this is the path.

## What would move me
I would consider a temporary labour-cost contribution if, and only if, it is tied to:
- binding job and site security,
- no forced layoffs,
- no closures,
- management and owner contributions,
- and a quantified savings plan with transparent monitoring.

That is the condition. Not a promise, not a phrase, but a binding package.

## What will not move me
- vague claims that closures are “necessary”
- a review clause that is really a trapdoor to later shutdowns
- “shared sacrifice” where workers pay and everyone else frames it as realism
- pressure to surrender the collective wage framework without equivalent counter-concessions

## Bottom line
I am ready for a serious deal. But the deal must be **fair, durable, and shared**.  
If management refuses that and keeps closures on the table as a threat, then we will respond with the means available to us, including industrial action.

Bring the numbers. Bring the burden-sharing. Bring the site security. Then we can negotiate. Without that, there is no basis for asking workers to carry the pain.
