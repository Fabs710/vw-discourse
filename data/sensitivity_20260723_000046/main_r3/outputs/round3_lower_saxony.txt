I will be direct.

## My position
I do **not** accept plant closures in Germany as the starting point, and I do **not** accept compulsory redundancies as a routine restructuring instrument. That is especially true where this company has a regional anchor function, where the state holds a blocking minority, and where co-determination is part of the governance settlement.

I do agree on one thing with management, investors, and the family: **the company needs a durable restructuring**. A cosmetic deal would be irresponsible. But “durable” does not automatically mean “closures.” You have not yet shown that all non-closure alternatives are exhausted or that the residual gap is so large that closures are unavoidable.

## Where I agree
I agree with the union and works council that:
- job security matters,
- voluntary instruments should come first,
- internal transfers, retraining, and early retirement must be used seriously,
- and burden-sharing must not fall on labour alone.

I also agree with management and investors that:
- the current cost base and capacity must be brought into line,
- temporary measures alone are not enough if they do not become structurally effective,
- and the result must be measurable and enforceable.

## Where I disagree
I disagree with the following assumptions coming from management and parts of the capital side:

1. **That closure is the natural or necessary default.**  
   It may be one option, but it is not proven to be the only credible option.

2. **That a preserved site security framework is incompatible with competitiveness.**  
   That depends on the actual savings package, not on ideology.

3. **That labour must first concede the core protections before management quantifies alternatives.**  
   No. The burden of proof is on those demanding irreversible damage.

4. **That the state should tolerate an outcome that weakens regional employment and industrial stability.**  
   I will not support that.

## On the experts
The specialist inputs were useful because they confirmed two things:
- a non-closure package can work if the gap is moderate and all levers are combined;
- but if the fixed-cost overhang is large, then some meaningful footprint reduction is likely required.

What they did **not** establish is that closures are immediately justified. They established only that we need a **plant-by-plant, quantified comparison** before anyone starts declaring inevitability.

So I support the call for hard numbers. But I will not accept the conclusion in advance.

## To management
You keep saying “durable and quantified,” which is correct as far as it goes. But you are still using closures as a pressure device rather than proving they are unavoidable.

If you want the state to consider deeper concessions, then present:
- savings from voluntary departures,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- management overhead cuts,
- executive compensation restraint,
- owner-side capital support,
- and only then the residual gap.

If, after that, you can show a genuine unbridgeable gap, then we can discuss what footprint reduction is necessary. But not before.

I also want to be explicit: **I will not support a deal that uses the state’s vote to rubber-stamp a closure plan without exhausting alternatives.**

## To the works council and union
Your red lines on compulsory redundancies and closures are politically and socially understandable. I support your insistence that labour should not be the only side asked to pay.

But I also want to be frank with you: “no closures” is not enough as a political slogan. If we want to preserve sites and jobs, we need a **credible quantified alternative** that can survive board scrutiny and investor scrutiny.

That means I expect the employee side to engage seriously on:
- voluntary exit programs,
- internal transfers,
- early retirement / partial retirement,
- temporary labour-cost restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- and genuine productivity measures.

If the result is strong enough to preserve sites, I will back it. If it is only a symbolic protest package, I will not pretend that is enough either.

## To the controlling family
You speak of stewardship. Then stewardship should mean:
- not forcing a confrontation that destabilizes the company’s regional base,
- not treating closures as the easiest solution simply because they are easier to present to the capital markets,
- and not ignoring the governance settlement that has held this company together.

If you want legitimacy, then support a negotiated solution that protects the region, preserves co-determination, and still delivers measurable savings. If you insist on closures without exhausting alternatives, you will be creating long-term mistrust and political resistance that the company does not need.

## To investors
Your concern about competitiveness is legitimate. Your demand for durability is legitimate. But I would caution you against treating a closure-heavy plan as automatically superior.

A prolonged strike, legal conflict, and political backlash would destroy value too. If a negotiated non-closure package can close most of the gap with real structural measures, that may be the better long-term outcome.

But again: the burden of proof is on the proponents of each option. Show me the residual gap and the cost of each path.

## What I would support
I could support a package with all of the following:
1. No compulsory redundancies.
2. No immediate plant closures.
3. Binding site security for a defined period.
4. Voluntary and reversible workforce adjustment tools.
5. Material management and owner contributions.
6. Quantified capacity reduction where necessary, preferably through internal restructuring and product concentration rather than closure.
7. Independent monitoring of savings and utilization.
8. A re-opener only on objective triggers, not a political trapdoor.

That is a serious restructuring path. It protects the regional anchor while still allowing the company to regain competitiveness.

## What I will not support
I will not support:
- a closure-first approach,
- a package that sacrifices German employment to solve a management problem,
- a settlement that weakens co-determination,
- or a “shared sacrifice” deal where labour pays and ownership escapes.

## Bottom line
I remain open to a disciplined restructuring, but only one that:
- protects the regional economic base,
- avoids compulsory redundancies,
- preserves the state’s governance role,
- and proves, with hard numbers, that any footprint adjustment is truly necessary.

Until then, I do **not** accept closures as justified.

If the next step is serious, let us see the plant-by-plant accounting and the quantified alternatives. Then we can talk about what is actually unavoidable.
