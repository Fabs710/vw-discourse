From a restructuring finance perspective, the key issue now is **not ideology, but the size of the residual gap after all non-closure levers** and whether that gap is small enough to bridge with temporary burden-sharing.

## What the current bargaining positions imply financially

### 1) If management’s implied gap is modest
A **non-closure package** can work if the remaining annual savings shortfall is roughly in the range of:
- **low hundreds of millions of euros per year**, or
- about **1–2% of domestic cost base**, depending on the size of the German operations.

In that case, the company can often bridge the gap with:
- voluntary exits and early retirement,
- internal redeployment,
- single-shift conversions and line rationalization,
- reduced working time,
- wage/bonus moderation,
- management overhead cuts,
- and possibly owner support for transition costs.

### 2) If the gap is large
If the residual gap is closer to:
- **mid- to high-hundreds of millions annually**, or
- requires a **double-digit percentage reduction in fixed site cost**,

then a no-closure package becomes much harder to sustain. You can still do it, but the required burden-sharing gets heavy:
- larger labor concessions,
- larger owner capital injections,
- and more aggressive plant-by-plant capacity reduction.

At some point, if the cost base is structurally misaligned and utilization remains weak, **major footprint reduction becomes financially hard to avoid**.

## Realistic cost components of a no-closure package

A non-closure restructuring usually has **three cost layers**:

### A) One-time transition costs
Typical items:
- severance / voluntary exit packages,
- early retirement bridging,
- retraining and redeployment,
- tooling and logistics changes,
- production disruption during ramp-down.

For a large manufacturing group, these can easily be in the **low hundreds of millions to over €1 billion**, depending on headcount reduction scale.

### B) Ongoing annual savings
Main sources:
- payroll reduction,
- lower overtime,
- lower shift premiums,
- reduced management layers,
- lower indirect labor,
- overhead rationalization.

A credible package must produce **durable annual EBIT improvement**, not just a one-year bridge.

### C) Liquidity timing effects
Even if the long-run savings are attractive, the deal can be hard on cash in year 1–2 because:
- severance is upfront,
- savings arrive later,
- working capital may worsen during transition,
- strike risk can create additional liquidity drag.

So the company must assess:
- cash runway,
- headroom under revolving facilities,
- covenant sensitivity,
- and the timing of investment needs for EV/product programs.

---

## What “alternative measures” can realistically save

Without plant closures, the biggest quantifiable levers are usually:

1. **Voluntary departures / early retirement**
   - Best for politically acceptable headcount reduction.
   - Cash cost is high upfront.
   - Works best where age structure is favorable.

2. **Working-time reduction / shift reconfiguration**
   - Can remove capacity without immediate site closure.
   - Savings are limited if fixed costs dominate.
   - Often less effective than management expects unless volume is very weak.

3. **Product concentration**
   - Put fewer models on fewer lines/sites.
   - This can create significant savings, but only if logistics and capex are manageable.

4. **Overhead reduction**
   - Usually necessary, rarely sufficient alone.
   - Watch for “paper savings” that do not translate into cash.

5. **Management and owner contribution**
   - Symbolically important.
   - Financially helpful, but usually too small to solve a major structural overhang on its own.

---

## A practical threshold for viability

A no-closure package is usually viable only if:
- the remaining annual gap after alternatives is **small enough to be covered by wage restraint and overhead cuts**, and
- the company can fund the upfront transition cost without stressing liquidity.

As a rule of thumb:
- if the residual annual gap is **below about €100m–€200m**, a negotiated non-closure package is often financeable;
- if it is **€300m–€500m+**, it becomes increasingly difficult without either deeper labor concessions or some form of footprint reduction;
- above that, **closure or equivalent permanent capacity removal** becomes financially more likely.

These are not universal thresholds, but they are useful decision ranges.

## The key stress test the parties need

Management should present a **side-by-side bridge**:

### Option 1: Non-closure package
- annual savings by lever,
- one-time cash cost,
- time to breakeven,
- liquidity impact,
- probability of implementation.

### Option 2: Hybrid downsizing
- selective capacity reduction,
- voluntary exits plus product concentration,
- smaller fixed-cost base,
- lower one-time disruption than full closure.

### Option 3: Closure-heavy package
- highest permanent savings,
- highest labor/political conflict risk,
- likely highest strike/legal disruption cost,
- but possibly strongest long-run fixed-cost relief.

The decision should then be based on:
- **NPV of savings net of transition cost**,
- **cash burn in year 1–2**,
- and **execution risk**.
