I will be equally direct.

I agree with the works council on the **core red lines**: **no compulsory redundancies** and **no plant closures as the default solution**. If management’s idea of restructuring is to make workers pay for a strategy problem by sacrificing jobs, wages, and protections, then we do not have a bargaining basis. We have a conflict.

Where I also agree with management, the family holding, and investors is that **the company does need a durable, measurable restructuring**. Nobody benefits from a cosmetic deal that buys a few months and then comes back with worse numbers. I am not here to defend the status quo at any price.

But I reject the premise that the only serious answer is closures and layoffs. That is not “economic necessity” in the abstract; it is one option among several. Before any closure logic is invoked, management must prove that it has exhausted alternatives and that the remaining gap is genuinely unavoidable.

### My position is this:
- **No forced redundancies.**
- **No plant closures in Germany as a first or default step.**
- **Preserve the collective wage framework.**
- **Any workforce contribution must be fair, time-bound, and matched by management and owners.**
- **Any restructuring must protect future jobs, investment, and site viability.**

### Where I differ from the works council
I am prepared to be more explicit about **shared sacrifice** if that is what it takes to secure jobs and sites. That can include:
- a temporary labour-cost contribution,
- working-time flexibility,
- bonus waivers or suspensions,
- voluntary departure programs,
- early retirement / partial retirement,
- internal redeployment and qualification,
- and capacity reduction through non-coercive means.

But I want to be very clear: **employees will not bear the pain alone**. If labour contributes, then management and owners must also contribute in concrete terms:
- executive compensation restraint,
- overhead reduction at management level,
- credible capital support for transition and investment,
- and binding commitments on future product allocation.

### To management
Your argument is only convincing if you can show, in numbers, that a non-closure package cannot close the gap. So far I hear the assertion that closures are “necessary,” but not yet a fully tested alternative comparison. If you want labour to accept difficult measures, then put the alternatives on the table transparently:
- What savings come from working-time flexibility?
- What comes from voluntary departures?
- What comes from retraining and redeployment?
- What comes from management and owner contributions?
- What gap remains after those measures?

If, after that, you still say closures are unavoidable, then you must justify that with hard evidence, not with pressure.

### To the family holding and investors
I hear your concern about competitiveness and value destruction. That concern is legitimate. But a settlement that simply shifts the burden onto the workforce while preserving the broader distribution of pain will not be stable. And a long industrial conflict will also destroy value. You do not get durable competitiveness by provoking a strike and forcing a confrontation that weakens the industrial base.

If you want a solution that avoids closures, then support a **real burden-sharing package** instead of demanding that labour solve the whole problem. If you want the workforce to accept restraint, then owners and management must give something meaningful in return.

### To the regional state government
Your veto power matters, and I welcome the clear statement that forced redundancies and closures are unacceptable. But we also need you to do more than block the worst option. You should actively support a negotiated package that protects sites and jobs, including if necessary political pressure on the company to match labour’s concessions with investment and transition commitments. The workforce should not be the only actor carrying responsibility for the region’s industrial future.

### On the moderator’s bridge ideas
#### 1) No forced redundancies + limited capacity adjustment
This can be a basis for discussion **only if “capacity adjustment” does not become closures by stealth**. Voluntary programs, internal transfers, early retirement, and working-time solutions are acceptable tools. Forced layoffs are not.

#### 2) Time-bound site security with conditional review
I am not enthusiastic about a weak, short-term pause that simply postpones the next attack on jobs. But I can discuss a **binding period of site security** combined with clearly defined savings measures and a serious review of execution — provided the review is about whether the package is working, not a backdoor to shut plants later.

#### 3) Shared-contribution restructuring package
This is the only direction that has a chance of holding. But “shared contribution” must mean exactly that:
- labour contributes in a limited and reversible way,
- management contributes materially,
- owners contribute materially,
- and the company commits to site and investment security.

### My bottom line
We are ready for a **serious restructuring settlement**, but not for one that:
- destroys collective wage standards,
- imposes forced redundancies,
- or uses closures as the first answer.

If management wants a deal, bring a quantified package that closes the cost gap through shared sacrifice, not workforce sacrifice alone. If that happens, the union can be constructive and disciplined. If not, then we will use our bargaining power accordingly, including industrial action.
