We agree on the diagnosis, but not on the threshold for action.

## Where we agree
- The company’s German cost base is too high.
- Capacity is misaligned with demand.
- A cosmetic settlement would be value-destructive.
- A prolonged strike would also be value-destructive.
- The outcome must be durable, quantified, and enforceable.

That is the correct frame from a capital-markets perspective. We are not interested in preserving inefficiency for sentiment or politics.

## Where we disagree

### 1) On the works council and union
Your insistence on **no closures** and **no compulsory redundancies** is understandable from a labor perspective, but it is not acceptable as a financial outcome if the residual gap remains material.

If the company’s fixed-cost overhang is large enough, then ruling out footprint reduction in advance is not a restructuring position; it is a veto on the mechanism most likely to restore margins.

We do **not** accept:
- a package that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive,
- a settlement that preserves all guarantees while shifting the burden elsewhere,
- or temporary concessions that unwind before competitiveness is restored.

If you want us to support a non-closure route, then show hard numbers proving that the residual gap is genuinely bridgeable. So far, that proof has not been made.

### 2) On management
Management is right that the current situation requires a durable reset, not another delay. But management still has not delivered the comparison we need.

Before invoking closures as necessary, present a proper side-by-side case:
1. non-closure package,
2. hybrid downsizing package,
3. closure-heavy package,

with:
- annual savings,
- one-time transition costs,
- liquidity impact,
- implementation risk,
- strike/disruption risk,
- and timing to margin recovery.

Until that is on the table, “closures are unavoidable” remains an assertion, not a proven conclusion.

### 3) On the state and family
You are right to worry about value-destructive conflict. We agree. But that does **not** justify freezing the footprint in place.

If the plant-by-plant analysis shows that the current structure cannot be carried at a competitive cost, then governance blocks cannot be used to preserve an uneconomic model indefinitely. That would protect optics in the short term and destroy equity value in the medium term.

## What we require
For institutional investors, an acceptable outcome must show:
1. **durable margin improvement**
2. **structural cost reduction**
3. **implementation certainty**
4. **financeable transition costs**
5. **no cosmetic substitution of concessions for real restructuring**

That means a non-closure settlement is only acceptable if it is economically equivalent in effect, not merely politically easier to sell.

## What would move us
We are open to a negotiated package if it contains:
- quantified headcount reduction,
- real fixed-cost removal,
- management overhead and executive restraint,
- owner-side capital support for restructuring and transition,
- and a binding timetable with milestones.

We would also accept a phased plan if the upfront cost is high but the long-run savings are truly equivalent and verifiable.

## What will not move us
- a blanket promise to keep every site open regardless of economics,
- vague “shared sacrifice” rhetoric,
- temporary concessions without permanent savings,
- or a settlement that avoids hard decisions while margins remain impaired.

## Bottom line
We will support whatever credibly restores competitiveness and protects the risk-adjusted value of our holdings.

A non-closure package can be acceptable, but only if the numbers are real and the savings are durable. If the alternative package does not close the gap, then deeper footprint reduction remains on the table. We will not support an outcome that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.
