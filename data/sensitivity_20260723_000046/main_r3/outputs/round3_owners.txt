I’ll be direct.

I agree with several points that have been made:
- The company needs a **durable** restructuring, not a cosmetic deal.
- A prolonged industrial war would be **value-destructive**.
- Management, labour, the state, and owners all have to contribute in some form.
- Temporary measures alone are not enough unless they lead to a structural reset.

Where I disagree is on the idea that the family holding should endorse a framework that effectively **rules out plant closures and compulsory redundancies in advance**, regardless of what the numbers show.

That is not stewardship. That is freezing the problem.

## My position

### On the works council
I respect the insistence on protecting jobs and co-determination. I also accept that the workforce should not bear the entire burden. But your position still reads as:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no weakening of guarantees,
- and only voluntary/internal measures.

That may be politically understandable, but it is not yet an economically sufficient answer to structural overcapacity. If the fixed-cost gap is large, then preserving every site under all circumstances would simply postpone the correction and deepen the eventual damage.

So I do **not** accept a blanket no-closure rule.

### On the union
You are more constructive because you recognize shared sacrifice and are prepared to discuss temporary labour-cost contributions, flexibility, and voluntary exits. That is the right direction.

But I still need to say this clearly: if those measures do not create a **durable** reduction in cost and capacity, they are not enough. A two-year contribution that leaves the footprint untouched is not a solution.

### On management
Management is right to insist on measurable savings and a real restructuring. Where I am not satisfied is that I still do not see a fully transparent, side-by-side comparison of:
- non-closure measures,
- hybrid downsizing,
- and closure-heavy options.

Before you ask the governance side to accept closures as unavoidable, show the actual bridge:
- what savings come from labour measures,
- what comes from overhead cuts,
- what comes from owner support,
- what comes from redeployment and productivity,
- and what residual gap remains.

If the gap remains large, then yes, deeper footprint reduction becomes harder to avoid. But that conclusion has to be proven, not asserted.

### On the state government
I understand the political responsibility to protect the regional base. I also understand the blocking power the state has under the special governance structure.

But I want to be equally clear: blocking any meaningful adjustment simply because it is painful would not be responsible either. If the company remains structurally uncompetitive, then all jobs are eventually at risk.

So I support a process that preserves industrial stability, but not one that locks the company into an uneconomic footprint.

### On institutional investors
Your demand for durable savings and implementation certainty is legitimate. I share it.

What I do not share is the assumption that only closures can deliver that certainty. If a non-closure or hybrid package can be quantified, financed, and enforced, that is preferable to a confrontation. But if it cannot, then the burden of proof shifts back to those opposing footprint reduction.

## What I will support

I would support a package only if it contains all of the following:
1. **Quantified durable savings**
2. **A real capacity reduction**
3. **Meaningful labour contribution**
4. **Management overhead and executive restraint**
5. **Owner support where needed**
6. **Protection of future investment and product allocation**
7. **A clear implementation timetable**
8. **A fallback if targets are missed**

I am not asking for a closure-first approach. I am asking for an economically honest one.

## What I will not support
I will not support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- an open-ended moratorium that simply delays the problem,
- a settlement that preserves all sites and all guarantees while claiming competitiveness is restored,
- or a deal that sacrifices the company’s long-term value to avoid difficult decisions.

## Where this leaves us
At this point, I do **not** accept that plant closures are yet proven unavoidable. But I also do **not** accept the employee-side position that closures are categorically off the table.

The next step has to be hard numbers, not slogans.

That analysis should tell us:
- what can be achieved without closures,
- what cannot,
- and what the minimum economically coherent footprint adjustment is.

Until then, I will not endorse either extreme:
- not the management temptation to rush to closures without exhausting alternatives,
- and not the labour position that no closures can ever be acceptable.

My objective remains the same: restore competitiveness and margins, protect long-term enterprise value, and avoid a destructive conflict that damages the asset we are all responsible for.
