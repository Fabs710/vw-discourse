I’ll be equally direct.

The works council and union keep repeating “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” as if repetition creates an economic solution. It does not. I respect the need for social legitimacy, but legitimacy does not repeal arithmetic.

## Where I agree
I agree on three points:

- The company needs a **durable** restructuring, not a cosmetic one.
- A deal that simply postpones the problem would be unacceptable.
- Management, owners, and the company itself must also contribute. Executive restraint, overhead reduction, and transition support are part of any serious package.

## Where I disagree
I disagree with the core premise that **German plant closures and stronger footprint reduction are categorically off the table**.

That is not a principle of responsible restructuring. It is a veto on the very instrument that may be required to remove structural overcapacity. If the German footprint remains too large for the market reality, then refusing to adjust it is not protecting the company — it is preserving an uncompetitive cost base.

### To the works council
You say closures are “a managerial choice.” That is only half true. Yes, management chooses instruments, but management also has a fiduciary duty to fix the business. If the numbers show that maintaining all sites leaves the company structurally uncompetitive, then your position amounts to asking management to accept permanent underperformance.

You also say the burden must not fall on labour alone. I agree. But the conclusion is not “therefore no closures.” The conclusion is:
- we need **real labour-cost contribution**,
- **real capacity reduction**,
- and **real structural savings**.

Voluntary exits, internal transfers, early retirement, and working-time measures are helpful. But they are not automatically sufficient. If they do not deliver enough permanent savings, then they are not a substitute for footprint adjustment.

### To the union
Your position is more constructive because you acknowledge temporary wage restraint, bonus moderation, flexibility, and voluntary exits. That is the right direction. But let me be clear: **temporary concessions are only acceptable if they produce a durable reset**.

A two-year contribution that leaves the fixed-cost base untouched is not enough. We need a package that closes the gap to 2030, not one that merely buys time to return to the same crisis.

So yes, I am prepared to discuss:
- temporary labour-cost relief,
- flexibility,
- voluntary departures,
- early retirement,
- redeployment,
- and management/owner contributions.

But I am **not** prepared to accept a settlement that:
- preserves every site,
- preserves every guarantee,
- and still expects the company to achieve a structurally lower cost base by magic.

## On the specialists
The finance and capacity assessments confirmed what management has been saying all along: a non-closure solution may work only if the savings gap is modest and if all levers are used together. They also confirmed the other side of the equation: if the fixed-cost overhang is large, then closures or major downsizing become economically hard to avoid.

That is precisely why I will not concede the closure issue in advance.

The works council keeps asking for plant-by-plant numbers. Fine — that is exactly the next step. We need a quantified comparison of:
- voluntary departures,
- working-time changes,
- redeployment,
- management overhead cuts,
- owner capital support,
- and the residual gap after those measures.

If that residual gap is small, then a non-closure path may be possible. If it is not small, then the closure question becomes unavoidable.

## To the regional state government
I understand your political and economic responsibility. But using blocking power to freeze structural adjustment is not stewardship. It is deferral.

If you want to preserve the industrial base, then you must support a solution that actually restores competitiveness. That may involve difficult measures. It may involve site changes. It may involve a smaller footprint. What it cannot involve is insisting that the cost base stay intact while the market shrinks around us.

## To the controlling family
You say you oppose a reckless closure campaign. I agree that a value-destructive industrial war would be bad. But stewardship also means not clinging to legacy structures that no longer fit the business.

We need a settlement that is:
- durable,
- economically credible,
- and capable of restoring margins.

If a non-closure package can do that, I’m open. If not, then the company must be allowed to restructure its footprint. That is not aggression; that is responsibility.

## To investors
You are right to insist on durable savings and implementation certainty. I share that demand. But durable savings do not come from slogans about solidarity. They come from reducing fixed cost, aligning capacity with demand, and lowering the labor-cost base in a way that lasts.

## Bottom line
Here is my position, plainly:

- I do **not** accept a cosmetic deal.
- I do **not** accept a package that preserves all sites and all guarantees while claiming competitiveness is restored.
- I do **not** accept compulsory redundancies as the first response, but I also do **not** accept them being ruled out absolutely before alternatives are quantified.
- I do **not** accept plant closures as a slogan, but I will not rule them out if the numbers show they are necessary.

What I will accept is a quantified, binding package that:
1. delivers durable cost reduction,
2. reduces overcapacity materially,
3. includes genuine labour contribution,
4. includes management and owner contribution,
5. and frees funds for future products and technology to 2030.

If the employee side wants to keep all German sites open, then it must prove that a non-closure package closes the gap in hard numbers. Until then, closures remain a legitimate restructuring option.
