From an industrial-operations and capacity-model standpoint, the disagreement is now correctly framed.

## Core judgment
The key question is **not** whether the company needs restructuring. It almost certainly does.

The question is whether the **residual fixed-cost and capacity gap after non-closure measures** is:
- **small enough** to bridge with voluntary/internal measures,
- **medium enough** to justify hybrid downsizing,
- or **large enough** that some form of permanent footprint reduction becomes financially unavoidable.

Without plant-by-plant numbers, no one can honestly prove that closures are either necessary or unnecessary.

---

## Decision rule
A practical model is:

- **Modest residual gap** → non-closure package can work
- **Medium residual gap** → hybrid downsizing is usually the best compromise
- **Large residual gap** → closure or equivalent permanent footprint reduction becomes hard to avoid

The real determinant is whether the company can remove enough:
- annual fixed cost,
- excess labor cost,
- under-absorbed plant overhead,
- and complexity cost.

---

## What non-closure measures can realistically do
A serious non-closure package usually includes:
- voluntary exits
- early retirement / partial retirement
- redeployment
- working-time reduction or shift rationalization
- wage restraint / bonus moderation
- management overhead cuts
- executive restraint
- owner-side transition support

### Typical economic effect
In large German industrial settings, that kind of package may close roughly:

- **20–40% of the annual structural gap** if executed seriously
- sometimes more if the site is clearly overstaffed
- often less if the main issue is **plant-fixed cost** and **low utilization**

### Limitation
Non-closure measures rarely solve the problem when:
- utilization is structurally low,
- a plant is too small for its cost base,
- product volumes are forecast to stay below efficient scale,
- or duplicated support functions remain embedded in the footprint.

In those cases, you can reduce pain, but not remove enough cost.

---

## Why closures change the math
Closures can remove:
- plant labor
- plant overhead
- utilities
- maintenance
- logistics
- duplicated support cost
- and the under-absorption burden of idle assets

That creates **durable annual savings** that non-closure measures often cannot match.

But closures also bring large one-time costs:
- severance / social plan costs
- pension bridge costs
- asset impairments
- relocation and retooling costs
- dual-running costs
- supply disruption risk
- strike and delay risk

So closures are not a cheap shortcut. They are a high-savings, high-disruption instrument.

---

## Scenario comparison

### 1) No-closure package
**Best when:** the residual gap is modest

**Pros**
- lower one-time cash cost
- lower industrial-relations conflict
- preserves site optionality

**Cons**
- lower annual savings
- may leave the company structurally too expensive

**Financial profile**
- one-time cost: low to medium
- annual savings: low to medium
- margin impact: often insufficient if overcapacity is large

---

### 2) Hybrid downsizing
This is often the most realistic compromise in a co-determined industrial setting.

Includes:
- product concentration
- single-shift conversion
- line consolidation
- voluntary workforce reduction
- functional relocation
- selective capacity removal without full closure

**Pros**
- real structural savings
- less disruptive than full closure
- may preserve key sites

**Cons**
- slower and more complex
- may still face labor resistance
- may not be enough if the gap is very large

**Financial profile**
- one-time cost: medium
- annual savings: medium to high

---

### 3) Closure-heavy restructuring
**Pros**
- highest certainty of deep structural savings
- strongest reset of fixed cost and capacity

**Cons**
- highest one-time cost
- highest execution risk
- highest political and labor conflict risk
- risk of delays and under-delivery

**Financial profile**
- one-time cost: high
- annual savings: high

---

## What would make a no-closure plan credible
For management and investors, a no-closure settlement is credible only if it shows:

1. quantified annual savings
2. durability through 2028/2030 or longer
3. binding workforce contribution
4. management and owner contribution
5. milestone-based execution
6. no rebound in fixed cost
7. sufficient cash savings to stabilize margins and liquidity

If the package is temporary or reversible, capital will discount it.

---

## What data the independent accountant must provide
For each plant/site:

- headcount
- labor cost per unit
- fixed overhead
- utilization rate
- volume outlook to 2030
- capex needs
- one-time restructuring cost under each scenario
- annual savings under each scenario
- residual gap after alternatives
- cash timing by year
- sensitivity to volume decline and wage inflation

That is the only defensible basis for deciding between:
- no-closure restructuring,
- hybrid downsizing,
- or deeper footprint reduction.

---

## Bottom line
At this point, the evidence standard is simple:

- If the **residual gap is small**, a no-closure package may be enough.
- If the **residual gap is medium**, hybrid downsizing is usually the best economic bridge.
- If the **residual gap is large and structural**, some permanent footprint reduction becomes difficult to avoid.

No one has yet shown the site-by-site numbers needed to decide which case applies.
