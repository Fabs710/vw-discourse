From the capital markets’ perspective, the key issue is simple: **does the proposed settlement credibly restore margins, or does it merely postpone the reckoning?**

I agree with management and the controlling family on one point: **the current cost base and capacity footprint are not competitive**. If the company keeps operating with structurally too-high German costs, shareholder value will continue to erode. A cosmetic settlement, or one that preserves all the old guarantees without delivering durable savings, would be unacceptable.

I also agree with the state government and employee representatives on one point: **a prolonged industrial conflict would destroy value too**. Strikes, uncertainty, and reputational damage are not acceptable substitutes for restructuring. Deal certainty matters.

Where I part company with the works council and union is on the idea that **no closures, no compulsory redundancies, and full site guarantees through 2030** can be the starting point. That is not a restructuring framework; it is a veto on material adjustment. If that position prevails, margins remain structurally impaired and the market will penalize the company accordingly.

At the same time, I am not endorsing a crude closure program simply because it is dramatic. If management cannot show that plant closures and layoffs are the only way to close the gap, then it should not insist on them. We would prefer the company to achieve the same or better savings through a **credible alternative package** that is durable, enforceable, and faster to execute:
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- working-time flexibility,
- permanent labour-cost relief,
- internal redeployment where it genuinely reduces cost,
- and a settlement that preserves investment capacity in future products.

But the standard is high: **the savings must be real, measurable, and lasting**. Temporary concessions that unwind in two years do not solve the problem. Nor does a deal that transfers the burden to capital markets through continued margin erosion.

So my position is:

- **We support decisive restructuring.**
- **We do not support a settlement that leaves the cost base uncompetitive.**
- **We prefer non-destructive solutions if they deliver comparable savings with certainty.**
- **We will not back a cosmetic compromise or prolonged conflict.**

To management: bring a package that proves the economics. Show the scale of savings, the timing, and the durability. If closures are necessary, justify them clearly. If they are not, then stop treating them as a default threat and use the bargaining process to secure an enforceable alternative.

To labour: if you want to avoid closures, then you need to put forward more than slogans. You need a quantified contribution that actually closes the cost gap. “No closures” without equivalent savings is not a viable investor answer.

To the state and family owners: your influence is meaningful, but it should be used to force a value-creating settlement, not to preserve an expensive status quo. The market will support tough action if it is disciplined and credible.

Our bottom line is not ideological. It is financial: **we will back whatever package credibly restores competitive margins and lowers structural costs. Anything less will not command our support.**
