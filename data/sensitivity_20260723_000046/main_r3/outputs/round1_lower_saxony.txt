I will respond very directly.

I **share management’s assessment that the company needs a durable restructuring**. Nobody benefits if we postpone hard decisions and return in two years with a deeper crisis. The regional government also cannot defend a strategy that simply preserves inefficiency and shifts the bill into the future.

But I am **not prepared to accept a restructuring built on plant closures in Germany, compulsory redundancies, or the destruction of the co-determination balance**. That would be economically shortsighted and politically unacceptable. It would damage the company’s role as a regional anchor, weaken trust for years, and increase the social and industrial conflict risk rather than reduce it.

So my position is:

- **Yes** to a credible restructuring.
- **No** to a solution that guts German sites or treats the workforce as the main adjustment variable.
- **No** to any attempt to sideline the state’s blocking-minority role or the works council’s effective veto.
- **Yes** to negotiated, reversible, socially balanced measures that protect the company’s long-term health.

To the Works Council and Union: I agree that **forced redundancies and unilateral closures are unacceptable as a first resort**. I also agree that the workforce has already contributed a great deal and cannot be expected to absorb the entire adjustment alone. But I need to be equally clear: if we simply say “no closures, no layoffs, no significant concessions” without offering a viable alternative that actually closes the competitiveness gap, then we are not protecting jobs — we are storing up a larger crisis.

To Management: you are right that the status quo is not sustainable. But your current framing is too narrow if it assumes that structural savings can only come from closing plants and cutting jobs. Before you demand irreversible closures, you must demonstrate that you have fully exhausted other routes:
- capacity consolidation through internal reallocation,
- temporary and targeted working-time reductions,
- qualification and redeployment,
- changes to investment sequencing,
- management and capital contributions,
- and a negotiated labour-cost package that is fair and time-limited.

What I would consider a legitimate path is this: **a negotiated restructuring package that preserves German sites as far as possible, avoids compulsory redundancies, secures future product allocation and investment, and makes all stakeholder groups contribute proportionately**. That includes management, owners, and capital — not just labour.

If management comes back with a plan that still relies on closures as the default instrument, I will not support it. If the employee side refuses every meaningful adjustment, I will also not pretend that is a workable outcome. My responsibility is to the region, the workforce, and the company’s future — in that order together, not one against the others.

So my message is simple: **bring me a real social-partnership restructuring with durable competitiveness, not a closure program dressed up as inevitability.** If such a package is possible, I am willing to work for it. If not, I will use the state’s rights to block any outcome that destroys the company’s regional anchor function.
