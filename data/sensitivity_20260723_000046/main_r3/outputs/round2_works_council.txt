I will respond directly.

To management, the family holding, and investors: your insistence that “something must give” is not disputed. What I dispute is the assumption that the only credible path is plant closures and the tearing up of job-security protections. That is not a law of nature; it is a managerial choice. And given the scale of the workforce’s prior concessions and the importance of German sites to this company’s history, I do not accept closures as the default solution.

Let me be explicit:

- **No plant closures in Germany.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No unilateral dismantling of co-determination or historic job-security commitments.**

That is not a bargaining tactic. It is the condition for any legitimate restructuring discussion.

Where I do agree:
- The company needs a durable answer, not a cosmetic one.
- The cost gap and capacity issue must be addressed.
- Temporary or shared contributions may be part of a responsible package.
- We do need real numbers, real timelines, and real execution discipline.

Where I disagree:
- I reject the idea that closures are “necessary” before alternatives are fully exhausted.
- I reject treating the workforce as the first and easiest adjustment variable.
- I reject a so-called “shared contribution” that in practice means labour pays while management and owners preserve their comfort.
- I reject any bargain that weakens the co-determination balance or turns site security into a conditional slogan.

To the moderator’s bridge proposals:

### 1) “No forced redundancies” + limited capacity adjustment
This is not enough as stated, because it still leaves open the path to closures by other means. If “limited capacity adjustment” means shutting down a plant or hollowing it out site by site, that is simply closures by another name. I will not dress that up as acceptable.

What can be discussed is:
- voluntary departures only,
- internal transfers,
- qualification,
- early retirement,
- working-time solutions,
- and temporary flexibility measures.

But these must be used to **preserve all German sites**, not to prepare them for closure.

### 2) Time-bound site security with conditional review
A time-limited commitment is weaker than what is required. The workforce has already lived through too many “temporary” assurances that later became permanent losses. If you want trust, then the commitment has to be binding through 2030, not a short pause before the next wave of pressure.

I can engage on a structured review of savings and competitiveness, but **not** on a review clause that functionally legitimizes closure later. The review must be about how to strengthen the sites, not whether to abandon them.

### 3) Shared-contribution package
On principle, yes, burden sharing matters. In practice, I want to see concrete, credible contributions from:
- executive pay,
- management overhead,
- capital allocation,
- investment sequencing,
- and owner support.

If labour is asked for flexibility or restraint, then management and owners must commit to:
- no closures,
- no forced layoffs,
- guaranteed investment at German sites,
- and a real say for the workforce in implementation.

Anything less is not shared sacrifice; it is socialized pain.

To the regional state government: I appreciate that you reject closures and compulsory redundancies. But your support must be more than rhetorical. If needed, use your blocking position to enforce a solution that protects the regional industrial base. I expect you to stand with the workforce against a closure program dressed up as “competitiveness.”

To the controlling family: stewardship does not mean pushing irreversible damage onto the workforce to preserve short-term numbers. If you want legitimacy, then you must support a solution that keeps the plants open and protects the company’s industrial foundation. If you back closures, you will be choosing confrontation and long-term mistrust.

To investors: you keep asking for quantified savings. Fine. Then quantify the alternatives properly. Show me that you have fully tested:
- working-time reductions,
- internal relocation,
- temporary labour-cost contributions,
- reduction of executive and overhead costs,
- product and investment sequencing,
- and owner-side support.

If those measures are not enough, the honest answer is not “therefore close plants.” The honest answer is that management has not yet presented a restructuring plan that respects the workforce and the company’s social compact.

So my position in round 2 is unchanged, and I want that recorded clearly:

**We are prepared to negotiate a serious restructuring, but only on the basis of binding German site security, no compulsory redundancies, and preservation of co-determination and job security.**  
If management comes with closures or forced layoffs, we will oppose it with all available means, including industrial action and our veto rights.

If management wants a viable way forward, bring a plan that keeps every German plant open and distributes the burden fairly. Until then, there is no basis for approval from the works council.
