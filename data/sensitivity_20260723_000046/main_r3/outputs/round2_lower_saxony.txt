I’ll be equally direct.

I **agree** with management, the family holding, and investors on one point: the company needs a **durable** restructuring, not a cosmetic one. If the cost base stays structurally uncompetitive, that eventually puts **all** jobs at risk. I am not defending stagnation.

But I **do not agree** that this automatically justifies plant closures in Germany or a restructuring logic that treats the workforce and the state as the only adjustment variables. That would be economically blunt, socially damaging, and politically irresponsible.

## Where I stand

### What I support
- A restructuring that **restores competitiveness**
- Real, measurable cost reduction
- A **shared-contribution** package
- Voluntary departures, internal transfers, retraining, early retirement
- Working-time flexibility and temporary labour-cost relief
- Management overhead cuts and visible owner contribution
- A binding investment and product commitment for German sites where viable
- Preservation of co-determination and the state’s blocking and veto role

### What I reject
- **Plant closures as the default answer**
- **Compulsory redundancies**
- Any attempt to hollow out the state’s role or bypass supervisory-board rights
- A package where labour pays while management and capital remain protected
- “Temporary” measures that are really a backdoor to permanent site destruction

## On the expert input
The specialist analysis is useful, and it confirms an important point: **a non-closure package can work if the gap is moderate and if all levers are used together**. That is exactly why I am not accepting management’s closure-first framing.

What the expert also makes clear is that closures are not the only possible answer, but they may become necessary if management cannot show that alternatives close the gap. That means the burden is on management to **prove**, not merely assert, that closures are unavoidable.

So my position is:

- If management wants closures, it must demonstrate with hard numbers that all alternatives have been exhausted and still leave an unbridgeable gap.
- If management cannot do that, then closures are **not justified**.

## My response to management
You say you need durable savings. Fine. Show the numbers transparently:
- what savings come from working-time changes,
- what savings come from voluntary exits,
- what savings come from redeployment and overhead cuts,
- what management and owner contributions are on the table,
- and what residual gap remains.

Until that is done, I will not accept the claim that closures are “necessary.” Necessity has to be demonstrated, not declared.

## My response to the works council and union
I support your core line on **no compulsory redundancies** and rejecting closures as the starting point. That is consistent with the regional interest and with the company’s role as an industrial anchor.

At the same time, I want to be honest: if we want to avoid closures entirely, then we need a **credible quantified alternative**. That means labour cannot simply say “no closures” and stop there. A durable package must be capable of closing the cost gap in practice.

So I am asking the employee side to engage constructively on:
- temporary labour-cost contribution,
- flexibility in working time,
- voluntary departure instruments,
- and a real, binding redeployment plan.

But that discussion only makes sense if it is paired with:
- site security,
- no forced redundancies,
- and owner/management contributions of their own.

## My response to the controlling family and investors
I hear your concern about value destruction and competitiveness. That is legitimate. But stewardship also means not forcing through a solution that detonates industrial peace and damages the company’s regional legitimacy.

A prolonged conflict, strikes, and reputational damage would destroy value too. So the choice is not “closures or weakness.” The real choice is whether you are prepared to support a **negotiated, enforceable, socially balanced restructuring** that preserves the company’s anchor function while improving performance.

I will not support a crude closure program just because it is the simplest accounting answer.

## My response to the government’s role
To be clear: I will use the state’s blocking minority and board position to prevent any outcome that:
- guts regional employment,
- removes co-determination in substance,
- or turns this company into a footloose cost-reduction exercise.

That is not obstruction for its own sake. It is legitimate stewardship of a regional industrial anchor.

## What I would support as a path forward
A serious package could include:
1. **Binding protection for German sites for a defined period**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Voluntary and reversible workforce adjustment tools**
4. **Meaningful management and owner contributions**
5. **Investment commitments for future products and technology**
6. **A jointly monitored savings target with milestones**

That is a workable path. What is **not** workable is a package that starts with closures and then asks the state and workforce to accept it as inevitability.

## My bottom line
I am open to a disciplined restructuring, but only if it protects:
- jobs,
- sites,
- co-determination,
- and the company’s role in the region.

If management comes back with a closure-heavy plan, I will oppose it. If the other stakeholders are serious about a non-destructive alternative, I am prepared to support and help broker it. But the core principle remains unchanged:

**No outcome that guts regional employment or sidelines the state’s role will get my backing.**

If needed, I can now help draft a **state-holder counterproposal** with clear conditions for a no-closure restructuring.
