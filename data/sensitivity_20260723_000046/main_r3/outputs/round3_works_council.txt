I will respond plainly.

## To management
You keep saying “durable and quantified” as if that automatically justifies closures. It does not. What you have shown so far is a **preference for the hardest instrument**, not proof that it is unavoidable.

I agree with you on only one point: the company needs a serious restructuring, not cosmetics.

I do **not** agree that this means:
- German plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or a dismantling of the historic job-security framework.

Those are not “tools” I can casually trade away. They are the line between restructuring and industrial rupture.

If you want credibility, then show the full comparison:
- what savings come from voluntary exits,
- what comes from internal transfers,
- what comes from working-time reductions,
- what comes from management overhead cuts,
- what comes from owner support,
- and only then what gap remains.

Until that is done, closures are **not necessary by definition**. They are one option, and for us the wrong option.

## To the expert assessments
The finance and capacity analysis confirms something important: the question is **not** whether the company can save money. It is whether management is willing to use the full range of alternatives before reaching for plant closure.

What I take from the experts:
- non-closure packages can work,
- but only if they are broad,
- only if management and owners contribute,
- and only if the remaining gap is actually modest.

That does **not** support the management line that closures are the natural endpoint. It supports our position that alternatives must be exhausted first.

And it also confirms my concern: if there is a large fixed-cost overhang, then management has to prove that the footprint problem cannot be solved by **downsizing without closures**. That proof has not been provided.

### What I want next
Before anyone talks about closures again, I want:
- plant-by-plant fixed-cost data,
- utilization by site,
- headcount by function,
- savings from voluntary instruments,
- savings from working-time and flexibility measures,
- owner-side capital contributions,
- and a true residual gap.

If the specialists are serious, then let them quantify the minimum savings gap that still remains after those measures. Right now that gap is being asserted, not demonstrated.

## To the union
I appreciate that you have drawn the same red line on compulsory redundancies and closures. That is correct.

But I want to be equally direct: “shared sacrifice” must not become a euphemism for labour paying and management applauding itself.

I can support:
- voluntary departure programs,
- early retirement,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- temporary working-time flexibility,
- and other reversible tools,

**if and only if** they are used to preserve every German site and avoid forced layoffs.

What I will not support is a package that quietly turns “voluntary” into forced by attrition, or uses flexibility to prepare plants for shutdown.

## To the regional state government
You are right to reject closures and compulsory redundancies. But your role is not to advise moderation from the sidelines. It is to use your blocking power to prevent a destructive outcome.

If management comes back with a closure-heavy package, I expect the state to stand by the workforce, not to retreat into “both sides must compromise” language. There are compromises that are legitimate, and there are lines that should not be crossed. Closing German plants is one of those lines.

A state shareholder should defend industrial stability, not bless the erosion of it.

## To the controlling family
You talk about stewardship. Then show stewardship.

Stewardship means:
- not forcing the workforce to pay for strategic mistakes,
- not threatening the historic industrial base to improve quarterly optics,
- and not using family control to push through something the supervisory-board employee side and the state cannot accept.

If you want trust, then back a plan that:
- keeps the German plants open,
- protects jobs,
- and shares the burden fairly.

If you insist on closures, then you are choosing confrontation, not stewardship.

## To institutional investors
You want durable savings and implementation certainty. Fair enough. But you are not entitled to demand that those savings come through plant closures before every alternative has been tested.

Your own concern about value destruction should make you cautious. A strike, legal conflict, and social backlash would also destroy value. So do not mistake “closure is easier to model” for “closure is the right answer.”

If you want a non-closure settlement, then support the measures that make it real:
- management and overhead cuts,
- owner support,
- product allocation,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- and binding site security.

If you want only the cheapest accounting answer, then you are not solving a restructuring problem; you are loading risk onto the workforce and the region.

## On the three bridge ideas
### 1) “No forced redundancies” with quantified capacity adjustment
This is the only acceptable direction so far — but only if “capacity adjustment” does **not** become a hidden closure program.

I am prepared to discuss:
- voluntary exits,
- internal transfers,
- early retirement,
- single-shift reductions,
- and reconfiguration of production,

but **not** closures and not compulsory redundancies.

### 2) Time-bound site security with objective triggers
A time-bound guarantee is weaker than what we want, but if the company insists on any review mechanism, then it must be strictly defensive:
- no closure during the protected period,
- no forced layoffs,
- objective triggers only,
- and the first response must always be non-closure measures.

A review cannot be a trapdoor.

### 3) Shared-contribution package
I am willing to engage on a real burden-sharing package. But “shared” means:
- labour contributes temporarily,
- management contributes materially,
- owners contribute materially,
- and the company commits to long-term site security.

If labour gives flexibility and restraint, then we need:
- binding guarantees through 2030,
- no forced redundancies,
- no closures,
- and a genuine co-determination role in implementation.

Anything less is not balanced.

## My position in round 3
I am not moving on the core issues.

- **No plant closures in Germany.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No undermining of co-determination or job-security guarantees.**

I will support a serious restructuring only if it is built on:
- voluntary measures,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time solutions,
- fair burden-sharing,
- and binding site security.

Management has not yet shown that closures are unavoidable. Until it does, they are unacceptable.

If the other parties want to continue seriously, bring the quantified alternatives and a binding 2030 job and site framework. Otherwise, we are simply being asked to surrender our rights in the name of “competitiveness,” and that is not going to happen.
