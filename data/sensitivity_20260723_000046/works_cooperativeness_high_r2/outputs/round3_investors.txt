We appreciate the fact that the discussion is finally moving from slogans to economics.

## Our position in one sentence
We support a **durable restructuring that restores competitive margins and lowers the cost base structurally**. We do **not** support a settlement that preserves overcapacity, delays hard decisions, and calls that “stability.”

## Where we agree
We agree with management, the family, the state, and the expert on several important points:

- This is **not** a cosmetic problem.
- The company needs **real cost and capacity adjustment**.
- **Voluntary measures, redeployment, retraining, working-time flexibility, and productivity improvements** should all be used.
- A **prolonged strike or open conflict** would destroy value.
- We need **site-by-site transparency** before any final decision.

Those are all sensible points, and they are now the minimum basis for an investable outcome.

## Where we disagree with the employee side
We do **not** accept the line that:
- every German plant must stay open,
- compulsory redundancies can never be discussed,
- and binding job guarantees through 2030 are acceptable regardless of the economics.

That is not a restructuring plan from a capital-market perspective. It is a protection plan for the existing footprint. If the footprint remains structurally too large, margins will remain weak and the equity will continue to deserve a discount.

We are not indifferent to social costs, but our priority is risk-adjusted value. A company with a permanently uncompetitive cost base is not preserving jobs in any meaningful long-term sense; it is postponing deterioration.

## Where we disagree with management
We also do **not** simply endorse management’s closure-led framing as a default. The expert report was useful, but it did **not** prove that closures are necessary. It said closures may become justified if the overcapacity is severe and alternatives fail.

That is the correct standard.

So management still needs to show:
- the **site-by-site capacity picture**,
- the **minimum viable utilization** for each plant,
- the **expected savings** from non-destructive alternatives,
- the **conversion capex** where repurposing is proposed,
- and the **reason closures are unavoidable** if that is the claim.

Without that, closure rhetoric looks like leverage, not analysis.

## Where we stand on the scenarios

### 1) Site-preserving scenario
We could support this **only if** it produces:
- material and durable savings,
- a credible path to competitive margins,
- and a footprint that is genuinely viable without recurring support.

That means:
- not just temporary labour concessions,
- but a real, durable reduction in fixed cost,
- and a demonstrable improvement in utilization and productivity.

If the site-preserving scenario requires repeated concessions every bargaining round just to keep the plants afloat, then it is not a solution. It is a delay mechanism.

### 2) Capacity-consolidation scenario
We are open to this if the data show that some capacity is structurally uneconomic.

If so, then we would support:
- shift consolidation,
- product transfers,
- selective site repurposing,
- and, if genuinely necessary, closures or workforce reductions.

We do not say that lightly. But capital cannot support a model that permanently carries excess fixed cost because every irreversible step is politically uncomfortable.

## To the works council and union
Your insistence on social protection is understandable. But it has limits.

A “no closures, no forced layoffs” line is only credible if you can show a complete alternative that closes the economic gap. So far, that has not been demonstrated.

The expert’s answer matters here: voluntary measures and flexibility can **mitigate**, but typically do **not fully solve** a structural overcapacity problem. That is the key point.

So if the employee side wants to avoid closures and forced redundancies, it needs to accept:
- substantial and durable labour-cost contributions,
- real productivity changes,
- more flexibility,
- and a willingness to consolidate where economics require it.

Otherwise the burden is just being shifted into the future, where shareholders will absorb lower returns and possibly further value destruction.

## To the state and the family
We appreciate your insistence on governance stability and social legitimacy. Those matter. But legitimacy is not created by blocking economically necessary change. If the final package leaves margins structurally impaired, then no one wins:
- not the workforce,
- not the owners,
- not the region,
- and certainly not minority capital.

We would support a negotiated settlement that is **economically sufficient**, even if it is socially difficult, because that is what protects long-term enterprise value.

## Our red line
We will not support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a deal that keeps all German plants open regardless of utilization,
- temporary concessions that do not structurally lower the cost base,
- or a package that leaves the company needing another restructuring soon after.

That would be value destruction with a polite label.

## What would move us
A package would become supportable if it includes:
1. **Transparent site-by-site economics**
2. **Meaningful capacity reduction**
3. **Durable labour-cost savings**
4. **A credible margin-restoration path**
5. **A settlement that is final enough to give deal certainty**
6. **No open-ended conflict risk**

If those conditions are met, we can support a tough restructuring. If not, we will continue to argue for stronger action and price the risk accordingly.

## Bottom line
Our vote is for **competitiveness first**.

We are prepared to back a negotiated restructuring, but only one that genuinely restores the cost base. We will not back a settlement that protects every site, avoids hard decisions, and leaves the business structurally impaired.
