I will respond clearly and without pretending that we are closer than we are.

## First: what I accept
I accept that:
- the company needs **real restructuring**,
- the current cost base and capacity situation must be examined **site by site**,
- voluntary measures, retraining, redeployment, working-time flexibility, and productivity improvements are legitimate tools,
- and a prolonged conflict would hurt workers and the company.

So yes: I am not denying the crisis.

## What I do **not** accept
I do **not** accept that the logical answer is:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- tearing up the job-security framework,
- or using “competitiveness” as a blanket justification for shifting the burden onto the workforce.

That remains my line. If management wants me to move, it must show how every German plant stays open, how no forced layoffs are needed, and how the plan is durable through 2030.

---

## To management
You keep asking for structural change, but you still have not proven that structural change must mean closures.

The expert report made an important point: voluntary measures may not fully solve a severe overcapacity problem. Fine. But that does **not** automatically justify the most destructive option. It means you need to show, with hard site-by-site numbers:
- what each plant’s utilization is,
- what the minimum viable utilization is,
- what product or volume can be assigned,
- what can be done through shift changes and internal redeployment,
- and what conversion capex would be needed to keep sites open.

Until you show that, your closure argument is still a claim, not a demonstrated necessity.

And I will say it plainly: a restructuring plan that starts from closures and then asks us to “cooperate” is not a negotiation. It is a demand for surrender.

---

## To the controlling family
You say you want durability and franchise value. Then you should understand that durability cannot be built on a broken trust relationship with the workforce.

If the family really wants long-term stability, it should back a package that:
- keeps all German plants open,
- avoids compulsory redundancies,
- preserves co-determination in substance,
- and secures the German industrial base through investment and product commitments.

A company that destroys its own social legitimacy does not become stronger. It becomes more conflict-prone and less governable.

---

## To the regional state
You are right to insist that closures and forced layoffs should not be the starting point. But that must translate into real pressure on management, not just cautious language.

I need the state to use its position to insist on:
- transparency,
- alternatives to closures,
- and binding guarantees that no German site is sacrificed for a quick accounting fix.

The region cannot be asked to absorb the social cost of management’s strategic errors.

---

## To the union
I agree with your insistence on shared sacrifice. But shared sacrifice must remain **shared**. It cannot become code for permanent worker concessions while ownership and management remain protected.

If labor is expected to contribute, then the contribution must be tied to hard guarantees:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding job and site security through 2030,
- and a serious industrial plan.

Without that, there is no basis for asking workers to give up anything.

---

## To investors
You keep saying the company needs durable savings and a lower cost base. I understand that. But I do not accept the conclusion that all security claims must give way to capital-market logic.

Your own expert input said closures are only relevant if overcapacity is severe and alternatives fail. That means:
- prove severity,
- prove that alternatives are exhausted,
- prove that repurposing is not possible,
- and prove that no site can be kept open viably.

Until then, “closure” is not an economic fact. It is a management preference.

And let me be blunt: a deal that maximizes short-term margin by destroying labour trust will not create lasting value. It will create the next conflict.

---

## On the expert assessment
The expert assessment was useful, but it did **not** move the burden away from management.

Yes, voluntary measures may not fully close a major gap. But that leaves several possibilities:
- more volume allocation,
- more shift optimization,
- stronger management restraint,
- more owner contribution,
- more investment,
- more internal redeployment,
- and stronger product commitments.

It does **not** automatically lead to closures.

The key question is still:
**Can every German site be kept viable without forced layoffs and without closing plants?**

That is the question management must answer.

---

## My position on the two scenarios

### 1) Site-preserving scenario
This remains the only acceptable basis for discussion from my side.

For it to be credible, it must include:
- all German plants remaining open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no tear-up of the job-security framework,
- real product and volume assignments where needed,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary departures only,
- retraining and redeployment,
- and a binding industrial plan through 2030.

That is not a soft option. It is a serious restructuring through utilization, flexibility, and investment.

### 2) Capacity-consolidation scenario
If this means closures or forced layoffs, then I reject it.

If this means squeezing workers harder while preserving management and shareholder comfort, then I reject it.

If it means a plan that leaves some sites disposable, then I reject it.

I am not prepared to call that “necessary” without proof, and I am not prepared to negotiate away the workforce’s security on speculation.

- savings from non-destructive measures,
