I’ll respond plainly.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on one fundamental point: **this cannot be a cosmetic restructuring**.

The company does need:
- a real improvement in competitiveness,
- a durable reduction in fixed costs,
- and a path back to acceptable margins.

I also agree that a **prolonged conflict would be value-destructive**. Strikes, reputational damage, customer uncertainty, and management distraction all hurt the enterprise and, ultimately, everyone’s position.

And I agree that **voluntary measures, redeployment, retraining, working-time flexibility, and capacity optimization** should be tested seriously.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the premise that the only credible route is a closure-led, layoff-led restructuring.

At the same time, I also do **not** accept the employee-side position that every German plant must stay open and compulsory redundancies are categorically off the table regardless of economics. That may be understandable as a negotiating stance, but it is not enough to solve a structural competitiveness problem.

So my position is not “closures at any cost,” and it is not “no structural change.”  
It is: **the package must be economically sufficient, durable, and governable without destroying the franchise.**

## To management
Management is right that the cost base is too high and that the economics must change structurally. But I want to be careful here: **support for restructuring is not a blank cheque for irreversible destruction of value**.

If plant closures are the only instrument management can imagine, then that is a sign the analysis is incomplete. Before any irreversible step, I want to see:
- a site-by-site assessment,
- the exact overcapacity by function and plant,
- the savings from alternatives such as shift changes, product reallocation, internal transfers, and working-time measures,
- and the investment case that shows how the company gets to a stronger future.

If those alternatives do not close the gap, then we cannot pretend otherwise. But management has not yet earned the right to treat closures as the default answer.

## To the works council and union
I understand your refusal of forced redundancies and your concern about site closures. I do not dismiss that lightly. But I have to be direct: **a blanket refusal of any plant closure or any compulsory reduction is not a complete strategy**.

If the result is that the company remains structurally uncompetitive, then the long-term value of the enterprise suffers, and that harms everyone — employees included.

That said, I do support the principle that:
- workers should not bear the entire burden,
- any labour contribution must be matched by management restraint and a credible industrial plan,
- and social peace is worth a lot if it is tied to real economics.

What I would expect from the employee side is not a veto over all change, but **serious engagement on material contributions**:
- productivity improvements,
- capacity flexibility,
- working-time arrangements,
- voluntary departure mechanisms,
- and, if necessary, temporary labour-cost concessions.

But those contributions must lead to a **durable** solution. If the burden is temporary for labour and permanent for the business, then that is not a fair settlement.

## To the regional state
I appreciate your emphasis on social legitimacy and the regional footprint. That matters. But I want to be equally frank: **legitimacy does not come from preserving every structure regardless of economics**.

If the company cannot restore competitiveness, then the industrial base weakens over time anyway. A controlled restructuring is preferable to endless decline.

I agree with your insistence that governance stability and co-determination matter. They are part of the company’s legitimacy. But they are not a substitute for competitiveness. If we protect the governance form while losing the substance of the business, we will have protected very little.

## To investors
I share your focus on credible margin improvement and durable savings. That is the right lens. But I do not support a package that is simply harsh for the sake of appearing decisive.

The goal is not to “win” the negotiation. The goal is to restore the company’s earning power without creating a permanent instability problem. If the deal is too shallow, it fails. If it is too destructive, it also fails.

## What would move us
The family holding would support a settlement if it delivers all of the following:
1. **Real structural savings**
2. **A credible path to margin improvement**
3. **Durable labour peace**
4. **Protection of the company’s long-term franchise**
5. **Governance stability**
6. **A serious industrial future with investment behind it**

What we would **not** support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a settlement that preserves overcapacity and calls it peace,
- or a destructive conflict that weakens the company’s value and reputation.

## Bottom line
I am **not** endorsing a blanket “no closures” line.  
I am **not** endorsing closure as the starting point either.

My threshold is this: bring forward a package that is **economically sufficient, socially manageable, and durable enough to hold**. If it does that, the controlling owners can support it. If it does not, then I will not pretend it solves the problem.
