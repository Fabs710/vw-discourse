I’ll respond directly, because the positions on the table still do **not** solve the underlying problem.

## First: what I agree with
I agree with the expert and with all sides on a few points:
- This is a **real restructuring problem**, not a cosmetic one.
- We need **site-by-site transparency**.
- Voluntary tools, redeployment, retraining, working-time flexibility, and productivity measures all belong in the package.
- A prolonged conflict would damage the company.

So yes: I accept that the solution must be negotiated, and I accept that we need hard numbers.

## Where I disagree — fundamentally
What I **do not** accept is the repeated attempt to turn “site preservation” into the starting point and then ask management to prove why reality should be allowed to intrude.

That is not how a structural crisis is solved.

### To the works council
You say:
- all German plants must remain open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no tear-up of the job-security framework,
- and any closure-led plan is unacceptable.

I have to say plainly: **that position is not compatible with restoring competitiveness if the numbers show persistent overcapacity**.

You are asking us to pre-commit to preserving every site regardless of economics. I cannot do that. If one or more sites are structurally underutilized, then refusing closure or consolidation in advance means preserving excess fixed cost and postponing the next crisis.

I understand your political and social concern. But co-determination is not a veto over economic reality. It requires us to negotiate the path, not deny the problem.

### To the union
You repeatedly call for shared sacrifice, but your definition of “shared” still sounds too much like:
- temporary concessions from labour,
- no irreversible structural change,
- and no hard decisions on capacity.

That is not enough.

The expert report was useful precisely because it showed the issue is **fixed-cost absorption** and **structural overcapacity**. In that situation, temporary working-time tweaks and voluntary departures may help at the margin, but they do **not** close a major competitiveness gap by themselves.

If you want me to move off hard structural measures, then show me how the gap is closed **durably**. So far, that answer is missing.

### To the regional state
I respect your role and I accept your point that governance stability matters. But I must be clear: **stability that preserves uncompetitive structures is not real stability**.

You say closures and compulsory redundancies should not be the starting point. Fine. I can agree with that formulation. They are not my starting point either. My starting point is the economic gap.

But I will not accept a settlement that is designed first to avoid pain and only second to fix the business. If the result is a weak industrial footprint, everyone loses — including the region.

### To the controlling family
You are right that we should not pursue destruction for its own sake. That is not what I want.

But I need your support for a package that is economically sufficient. If the family wants long-term franchise value, then it must support real structural change, not just comfort language about durability. Durability comes from competitiveness.

### To investors
You are right to insist on credible margin improvement and durable savings. That is exactly the point.

But I also need to be blunt: if the workforce side insists on no closures, no forced redundancies, and no meaningful labour-cost contribution, then there is no credible path to a return-on-sales recovery. And if there is no credible path, then the market will continue to discount this business.

## On the expert report
The expert made the key point clearly:
- voluntary measures can mitigate,
- but they usually do **not** fully solve structural overcapacity.

That matters. It means the employee-side demand for a site-preserving outcome is only feasible **if** the numbers support it. We have not yet been shown that they do.

So I repeat: I am not persuaded by blanket resistance to closures or by claims that voluntary tools alone will close the gap.

## On the two scenarios
### 1) Site-preserving scenario
I will not rule it out in principle.

But it must satisfy three conditions:
1. It must deliver **comparable, durable savings** to a closure-led scenario.
2. It must include a **material labour-cost contribution** that is not merely temporary symbolism.
3. It must create a credible path back to the required margin and investment capacity.

If that can be shown with numbers, I am prepared to consider it.

If not, then it is not a solution — it is a delay tactic.

### 2) Invasive capacity-adjustment scenario
This remains on the table, and I will not withdraw it as an option.

If the site-by-site data show that some capacity is structurally uneconomic, then consolidation, shift reduction, and yes potentially closures and workforce reduction are legitimate tools. Not because we want confrontation, but because we need a business that can survive 2030.

## What I need next
I want the discussion to move from slogans to concrete parameters. Specifically, I want for each German site:
- current utilization,
- minimum viable utilization,
- model/product pipeline to 2030,
- savings from shift consolidation,
- savings from wage and working-time measures,
- conversion capex if the site is repurposed,
- and the closure/consolidation alternative if no viable future exists.

Without that, “no closures” is just a political statement, not a restructuring plan.

## Bottom line
Here is my position, clearly:
- I support a **negotiated solution**.
- I support **voluntary measures**, redeployment, retraining, and working-time flexibility.
- I do **not** accept a blanket ban on closures or compulsory redundancies.
- I do **not** accept a package that leaves the company structurally overcosted and undercompetitive.

If the employee side wants security through 2030, then it must be built on a cost structure that can actually sustain it. That is the real issue, and I will not soften on that.
