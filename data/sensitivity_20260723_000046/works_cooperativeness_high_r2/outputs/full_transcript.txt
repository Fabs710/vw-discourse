Below is a plant-by-plant industrial-planning assessment of the **capacity problem** and the **feasible alternatives to closures**, based on the scenario as described. ## 1) Core industrial conclusion

If management’s diagnosis is that German operations have **structural overcapacity** and a **cost base above target**, then the key planning question is not “closure vs no closure” in the abstract. It is:

- **How much excess capacity exists, by site and by model family?**
- **Can it be absorbed through volume reallocation, mix change, shifts, and productivity gains?**
- **If not, what capacity must be removed to restore normal utilization?**

In automotive manufacturing, a plant is typically sustainable when it has:
- a stable product assignment,
- utilization around a healthy operating band,
- sufficient model life remaining to amortize fixed costs,
- and a workforce/asset structure aligned to annual takt and shift pattern.

If a site is **materially below efficient utilization**, the usual alternatives are:
1. add a new product,
2. shift volume from another site,
3. reduce shifts / consolidate lines,
4. redeploy to adjacent functions, or
5. mothball/close.

If none of those options closes the gap, then the overcapacity is real and persistent.

## 2) What can be assessed from the scenario alone

The scenario indicates:
- shrinking European market,
- intense competition,
- Germany cost base above target,
- management seeking durable savings and capacity reduction,
- employee side insisting on no closures and no forced layoffs.

That combination usually means the issue is **fixed-cost absorption**, not just variable-cost pressure.

In industrial terms, the company likely faces some mix of:
- **too many assembly/final-trim slots** for expected demand,
- **too much indirect labor and support overhead** relative to output,
- **duplicated infrastructure** across sites,
- and/or **an unfavorable product mix** on high-cost German capacity.

## 3) Capacity logic by site type

Without the exact site names and model assignments, the most useful way to quantify is by **site function**.

### A. Final assembly / body shop sites
These are the most capital-intensive and the hardest to preserve if underutilized.

Typical issues:
- fixed overhead is high,
- line loading must stay high to justify labor and depreciation,
- low volume quickly destroys unit economics.

If a final assembly site runs materially below capacity, the usual remedies are limited:
- move another model in,
- increase shifts,
- consolidate models onto fewer lines,
- or remove the site from the footprint.

### B. Component / powertrain / battery-related sites
These can sometimes be kept open with product conversion, because they may be more modular.

Typical alternatives:
- convert from ICE components to EV components,
- shift to modules, e-axles, battery pack assembly, or remanufacturing,
- use excess space for logistics, pre-assembly, testing, or aftermarket.

These sites are more flexible than a full vehicle assembly plant, but only if there is real program demand.

### C. Technical centers / pilot / support functions
These are less about unit capacity and more about overhead and headcount deployment.

Alternatives:
- consolidate engineering,
- redeploy into new tech programs,
- centralize support tasks,
- move non-core activities to lower-cost shared-service structures.

---

## 4) Likely scale of overcapacity: planning estimate

Because no volumes are given, I can only provide a **planning-range estimate**.

In a mature European carmaker with German sites under cost pressure, “structural overcapacity” usually means one of the following:

- **low double-digit percent excess capacity** across the domestic footprint, or
- **one plant equivalent** of capacity that is underloaded, or
- several sites each running below the minimum efficient scale.

A practical range would be:

- **mild overcapacity:** 5–10%
- **material overcapacity:** 10–20%
- **severe overcapacity:** >20%

If management is proposing plant closures for the first time, that often implies the problem is **not mild**. It usually means at least one site is below the threshold where product transfer or additional allocation can realistically restore efficiency.

---

## 5) What alternative capacity utilization can realistically do

### Option 1: Volume reallocation within Germany
This is the first thing to test.

Questions:
- Can a currently underloaded German site absorb another model or derivative?
- Can output from a weak site be transferred to a stronger site without major capex?
- Are there constraints in paint shop, body shop, press shop, or logistics?

Industrial reality:
- Assembly volume can sometimes be moved.
- Body shop and paint shop capacity are usually the binding constraints.
- A plant with no suitable product pipeline cannot be “saved” by labor flexibility alone.

### Option 2: Shift reduction instead of closure
If a plant is at, say, 1.5 shifts or 2 shifts and demand supports only 1 shift, you can cut capacity materially without shutting the site.

This can remove:
- overtime,
- night shift premiums,
- some indirect labor,
- and parts of plant overhead.

But it only works if the resulting single-shift utilization is still economically sensible.

### Option 3: Mixed manufacturing / shared footprint
A site may host:
- vehicle assembly plus logistics,
- battery pack assembly plus service parts,
- prototyping plus pilot series,
- or multiple small programs.

This can improve utilization, but the added volume must be real and stable. “Filling space” with non-core activity rarely compensates for a large assembly overhang.

### Option 4: Redeploy labor to adjacent growth areas
Possible where the group has:
- battery-related work,
- software-related operations with industrial processes,
- remanufacturing,
- parts logistics,
- or a stable aftermarket business.

But this is usually limited by:
- skill mismatch,
- location mismatch,
- and the fact that industrial jobs are not all interchangeable at scale.

---

## 6) What redeployment can and cannot solve

### Can solve:
- a portion of indirect and support headcount,
- some transferred assembly labor,
- some maintenance and logistics labor,
- a share of vacant or retiring positions,
- some medium-skill roles through retraining.

### Cannot solve:
- a structurally empty production line,
- an underloaded paint shop,
- a plant with no future product assignment,
- or a footprint whose fixed costs remain too high even after headcount moves.

As a rule of thumb, **redeployment can mitigate but not eliminate structural overcapacity**. It is helpful for social management, but it does not create market demand.

---

## 7) Site-preserving scenario: what would have to be true

For a “keep all German plants open” plan to be industrially credible, at least one of the following must hold for each site:

1. **New product assignment**
   - A model or derivative is allocated that raises utilization to an acceptable level.

2. **Major shift consolidation**
   - Capacity is reduced by removing one shift or consolidating production, but the site remains viable.

3. **Conversion to a different industrial role**
   - Example: assembly to modules, battery-related work, logistics, testing, remanufacturing.

4. **Sustained volume transfer from another site**
   - Another plant or external region gives up load to keep the German site busy.

5. **Structural overhead reduction**
   - Enough indirect cost and fixed overhead are taken out that lower volume still pencils out.

If none of these are available, then a site-preserving plan is usually not durable.

## 8) Invasive capacity-adjustment scenario: industrial interpretation

If management is talking about closures or permanent reductions, the likely rationale is that one or more sites are below the viable threshold after:
- product allocation tests,
- shift optimization,
- and possible investment conversion scenarios.

In automotive planning terms, a site becomes a closure candidate when:
- utilization is too low,
- capex required for conversion is too high,
- logistics or supply-chain constraints make it inefficient,
- and no future model pipeline is available.

That is the point where “alternative utilization” becomes more expensive than consolidation.

## 9) High-level savings logic

Again, no internal numbers are available, so this is directional.

### Savings from labor measures
- working-time changes,
- voluntary attrition,
- redeployment,
- wage concessions,
- fewer shift premiums,
- and productivity agreements.

These can generate meaningful savings, but usually **do not fully offset** a large structural capacity gap.

### Savings from capacity reduction
- fewer fixed assets to maintain,
- less depreciation burden per unit,

## Standard industrial-planning test

A plant is generally in one of three categories:

### 1) Viable as-is or with modest optimization
Typical indicators:
- utilization is near or above minimum efficient scale
- product pipeline is stable through 2030
- limited conversion capex required
- overhead can be reduced through shift optimization, redeployment, and voluntary exits

### 2) Viable only with repurposing
Typical indicators:
- current utilization is below threshold
- a future model or function can be transferred in
- conversion capex is material but justified by sustained volume
- break-even can be restored within a reasonable payback period

### 3) Structurally non-viable
Typical indicators:
- low utilization persists even after realistic product allocation
- no credible replacement product or function exists
- conversion capex is high relative to expected margin contribution
- the plant would remain cash-negative or margin-dilutive after restructuring

In automotive final assembly, once a site is persistently below roughly **60–70% utilization** — and sometimes higher depending on complexity, labor intensity, and product mix — the economics usually become difficult unless there is a specific product transfer or major cost reset.

- can require ongoing labor concessions to stay afloat

### Consolidation scenario
**Pros**
- usually delivers larger durable fixed-cost reduction
- better factory loading at the remaining sites
- stronger margin improvement if executed cleanly
- simpler long-term industrial footprint

**Cons**
- high one-time restructuring costs
- conversion / relocation capex
- major execution risk
- labor conflict risk
- possible disruption to supply chain and launch timing

- **one-time closure + transfer + capex + severance + disruption costs**

If the savings are durable and the transition risk is manageable, consolidation can be financially superior even with high upfront cost.

## 7. Integrative pathways

### A. Time-limited standstill on irreversible measures
A temporary no-closures/no-forced-redundancies phase could be paired with full disclosure and independent review. This would reduce escalation while testing whether the site-preserving route is genuinely viable.

### B. Tiered restructuring package
Implement the least destructive tools first:
- voluntary departures,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- shift optimization,
- working-time changes,
- targeted investment and repurposing.
Only if the gap remains would harder measures be reopened under pre-agreed criteria.

### C. Site-by-site traffic-light classification
Classify each plant as:
- **Green**: viable with modest optimization,
- **Amber**: viable with repurposing or volume transfer,
- **Red**: structurally at risk absent a credible business case.
This could replace the current all-or-nothing framing.

### D. Conditional job and site guarantees
Bind any employee contribution to explicit conditions:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no closure during a defined period,
- investment and product commitments,
- measurable savings milestones,
- and reassessment only if agreed thresholds are missed.
