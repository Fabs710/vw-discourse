## 1. Decision context

The company is facing a high-stakes restructuring under severe competitive pressure: weak European demand, strong Chinese and low-cost competition, and a German cost base that management views as structurally too high. The strategic question is whether the company can restore competitiveness through a negotiated, site-preserving package or whether durable savings require plant closures, forced redundancies, and deeper capacity consolidation.

This decision is unusually constrained by governance. Co-determination gives employee representatives an effective veto over major plant decisions, while the controlling family and regional state also hold blocking power and legitimacy. That means the outcome cannot be reduced to management preference; it must be politically and industrially durable.

---

## 2. Stakeholder assembly

- **Group & Brand Management**: responsible for the restructuring proposal and financial viability.
- **Works Council**: plant-level employee representation with strong co-determination rights.
- **Industrial Union**: negotiates wages and can escalate to strikes.
- **Regional State Government**: anchor shareholder and political guardian of jobs/industrial base.
- **Controlling Family Holding**: majority voting-right holder and long-term governance actor.
- **Institutional Investors**: capital-market shareholders focused on returns and competitiveness.
- **Independent Expert**: plant-by-plant industrial planning and restructuring analysis.

---

## 3. Final positions

### Group & Brand Management
Management argues that the German footprint is structurally overcapacitated and uncompetitive, so the restructuring must produce durable savings, not just temporary relief. It keeps plant closures and compulsory redundancies on the table if the data show that sites cannot be made viable.

### Works Council
The works council accepts that real restructuring is needed, but rejects plant closures, compulsory redundancies, and any erosion of job-security guarantees. It insists that every German plant remain open and viable through 2030, with any workforce contribution tied to hard guarantees and substantial transparency.

### Industrial Union
The union supports negotiated restructuring, voluntary measures, redeployment, working-time flexibility, and a fair employee contribution. But it will not accept closures, forced layoffs, or a settlement that shifts the burden onto labour while preserving management and ownership comfort.

### Regional State Government
The state wants restructuring, but only as a last resort after alternatives are fully tested site by site. It resists closure-led restructuring and forced redundancies, while also acknowledging that some footprint adjustment may be unavoidable if the economics are proven.

### Controlling Family Holding
The family supports a tough, economically serious restructuring that protects long-term franchise value and avoids chronic conflict. It does not support a cosmetic compromise, but it also does not want a reckless closure-first strategy without site-specific proof.

### Institutional Investors
Investors want a durable cost and capacity reset that restores margins and lowers fixed costs. They are open to consolidation if required by the economics, but do not endorse management’s closure framing without evidence; they want plant-by-plant justification and a credible ROI path.

### Independent Expert
The expert does not take a governance position, but repeatedly stresses that voluntary measures can mitigate overcapacity, not necessarily eliminate it. It highlights the industrial reality that low-utilization sites often require repurposing, volume transfer, or consolidation, especially when sustained utilization falls below roughly 60–70%.

---

## 4. Genuine conflicts

1. **Plant closures vs. site preservation**  
   Employee-side and state actors want closures off the table unless proven unavoidable; management and investors keep them as an economically legitimate tool.

2. **Compulsory redundancies vs. job security**  
   Labour-side stakeholders categorically reject forced layoffs; management and investors refuse to rule them out.

3. **Burden-sharing vs. burden shifting**  
   Employees want shared sacrifice with owners, management, and capital; capital-side actors insist labour must make material, durable contributions to competitiveness.

4. **Co-determination as constraint vs. flexibility for restructuring**  
   The workforce and state see co-determination as substantive and protective; management and investors see governance rigidity as a barrier if it blocks necessary industrial change.

5. **Evidence threshold for irreversible steps**  
   Labour and state require full site-by-site proof before any closure discussion; management wants the option to act if the evidence confirms non-viability, but has not yet made that case complete enough to settle the dispute.

---

## 5. Areas of convergence

- All sides agree the company needs **real restructuring**, not cosmetic adjustments.
- There is broad support for **voluntary measures**, **retraining**, **redeployment**, **working-time flexibility**, **productivity improvements**, and **internal transfers**.
- All parties recognize that a **prolonged conflict** would damage the company and its stakeholders.
- There is agreement that a durable settlement must be based on **site-by-site transparency** and hard numbers.
- Most stakeholders accept that any solution must be **economically credible and socially implementable**.

---

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker

1. **Jobs and site continuity vs. durable competitiveness**  
   Preserving all sites may protect legitimacy and labour peace, but may not generate enough fixed-cost reduction.

2. **Negotiated stability vs. structural depth**  
   A compromise could avoid confrontation, but may be too shallow if overcapacity is severe.

3. **Voluntary adjustment vs. compulsory consolidation**  
   Voluntary exits and flexibility are politically and socially easier, but may be insufficient in scale.

4. **Short-term industrial peace vs. long-term credibility**  
   A softer deal might secure peace now, but risk another restructuring later if underlying economics remain weak.

5. **Governance legitimacy vs. management autonomy**  
   The current structure gives multiple actors veto power; the challenge is to achieve a deal that is both governable and sufficiently transformative.

---

## 7. Integrative pathways

### A. Time-limited standstill on irreversible measures
A temporary no-closures/no-forced-redundancies phase could be paired with full disclosure and independent review. This would reduce escalation while testing whether the site-preserving route is genuinely viable.

### B. Tiered restructuring package
Implement the least destructive tools first:
- voluntary departures,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- shift optimization,
- working-time changes,
- targeted investment and repurposing.
Only if the gap remains would harder measures be reopened under pre-agreed criteria.

### C. Site-by-site traffic-light classification
Classify each plant as:
- **Green**: viable with modest optimization,
- **Amber**: viable with repurposing or volume transfer,
- **Red**: structurally at risk absent a credible business case.
This could replace the current all-or-nothing framing.

### D. Conditional job and site guarantees
Bind any employee contribution to explicit conditions:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no closure during a defined period,
- investment and product commitments,
- measurable savings milestones,
- and reassessment only if agreed thresholds are missed.

---

## 8. Open questions

- What is the current and forecast utilization for each German site through 2030?
- Which sites have credible product pipelines, and which do not?
- What is the minimum viable utilization threshold by plant type?
- How much conversion capex would repurposing require at each site?
- What savings can realistically be achieved through voluntary measures alone?
- What labour-cost contribution would be sufficient to make a site-preserving deal durable?
- Which sites, if any, are truly non-viable after realistic optimization?
- What governance concessions, if any, are needed to make a settlement implementable?

---

## 9. Recommended next step

Commission and table an **independent plant-by-plant industrial and financial analysis** before any irreversible decision. The analysis should compare at least two scenarios for every German site:
1. **site-preserving**, and
2. **capacity-consolidation**.

For each site, the report should include utilization, minimum viable utilization, product pipeline, conversion capex, employment effects, and EBITDA/free-cash-flow impact. Negotiations should then proceed on the basis of that shared evidence, with a temporary standstill on closures and forced redundancies during the review period.

---

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction

1. **The central dispute shifted from “whether restructure” to “how to prove non-viability.”**  
   Early rounds showed broad agreement that restructuring is necessary; only later did it become clear that the real impasse is evidentiary, not conceptual.

2. **Voluntary measures are widely accepted, but only as mitigation, not resolution.**  
   The expert input made explicit that flexibility tools can soften overcapacity but may not eliminate it, which sharpened the difference between labour’s preferred route and capital’s expectations.

3. **All sides increasingly demanded the same missing artifact: a site-by-site business case.**  
   Despite incompatible end positions, everyone converged on the need for plant-level utilization, cost, and capex data.

4. **The works council and union are not identical in emphasis.**  
   Both reject closures and forced layoffs, but the union showed somewhat greater openness to a negotiated labour contribution if it is tied to binding guarantees.

5. **The family and regional state are not simply aligned with management.**  
   Both support tough restructuring in principle, but both resisted a closure-first logic without proof, suggesting they may act as moderating governance actors.

6. **The likely compromise space is procedural before it is substantive.**  
   The most realistic bridge is not immediate agreement on closures vs. preservation, but agreement on how to test the options, sequence the measures, and prevent irreversible steps before evidence is shared.

7. **A “site-preserving” plan is no longer treated as purely ideological; it is now an empirical claim.**  
   Management and investors are no longer debating the legitimacy of the idea, but whether it can actually close the economic gap.

8. **The real risk is a false compromise.**  
   Multiple parties warned that a superficial deal could preserve peace briefly while leaving the company structurally weak and forcing another round of conflict later.
