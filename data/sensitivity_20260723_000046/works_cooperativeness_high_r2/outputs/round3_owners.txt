I’ll respond directly.

## My overall position
I support a **real restructuring**. I do **not** support a cosmetic compromise that preserves overcapacity and postpones the problem.

At the same time, I do **not** accept a solution that destroys long-term enterprise value through uncontrolled conflict, reputational damage, or irreversible weakening of the German industrial footprint without proof that such steps are necessary.

My threshold is simple:
- **durable margin improvement**
- **credible competitiveness**
- **labour peace that actually holds**
- **no value-destructive instability**
- **no governance erosion**

---

## To management
I agree with you on the diagnosis: the company needs structural cost improvement, and the current footprint appears uncompetitive.

Where I disagree is on the implied shortcut that closure-led restructuring is automatically the right answer. It may be necessary in some form, but I will not support irreversible measures unless you demonstrate, site by site, that they are truly required.

You still owe the board and owners:
- a transparent **site-by-site utilization picture**
- break-even thresholds by site
- quantified savings from:
  - working-time changes
  - redeployment
  - product shifts
  - shift consolidation
  - voluntary exits
  - overhead reduction
- the investment and product pipeline through 2030
- a comparison of:
  1. a **site-preserving** scenario
  2. a **capacity-consolidation** scenario

If you cannot show that, then “closures are necessary” remains a claim, not a case.

I also want to be clear: if your proposal depends on open-ended confrontation with labour, that is not a plan. That is value leakage.

---

## To the works council
I agree with you that the company needs to move beyond slogans and that workers should not be treated as the only adjustment variable.

Where I disagree is your absolute line that all German plants must remain open and compulsory redundancies can never be discussed under any circumstances. That may be a negotiating stance, but it is not yet an industrial solution.

A blanket “no closures, no layoffs” position is not enough if the numbers show persistent overcapacity. If the company remains structurally uncompetitive, everyone loses — including employees.

So I will support a site-preserving solution **only if** it delivers:
- real and durable savings,
- credible productivity gains,
- a meaningful workforce contribution,
- and a product/volume plan that makes each site viable.

Without that, the position is not sustainable.

---

## To the trade union
I agree that this must be a **shared-sacrifice package**, not a one-sided transfer of pain onto labour.

But “shared” has to be real. Temporary concessions from employees alone will not be enough if the fixed-cost structure remains broken. The union cannot demand job security through 2030 while refusing the cost and capacity changes that make such security possible.

What I can support:
- voluntary departure programs
- retraining and redeployment
- working-time flexibility
- targeted labour-cost contributions if they are material and durable
- productivity agreements
- capacity optimization

What I cannot support:
- treating all structural change as equivalent to social betrayal
- or assuming the owners and management can absorb the economics while labour gives nothing durable

If the workforce wants strong guarantees, then the package must create the economics to sustain them.

---

## To the regional state
I understand and respect your role as anchor shareholder and political stabilizer. I also agree that governance legitimacy matters.

Where I disagree is on treating closures and redundancies as primarily political problems to be ruled out in advance. They are economic tools with political consequences. If a site is structurally uneconomic and cannot be repurposed, then preserving it indefinitely is not responsible stewardship.

That said, I am aligned with you on one critical point: **destructive restructuring is not acceptable as a first resort**. It must be justified by hard numbers and preceded by serious testing of alternatives.

So I support your insistence on transparency and site-by-site analysis. I do **not** support turning the state’s role into a veto over economically necessary change.

---

## To the institutional investors
I agree with your focus on margin restoration, deal certainty, and structural savings.

Where I disagree is if that focus becomes a simple call for the workforce to absorb the entire adjustment. That is not durable, and it will not hold.

You are right that a cosmetic deal is worthless. But a deal that looks decisive and triggers years of conflict is also value-destructive.

A settlement is investable only if it is:
- economically sufficient
- socially implementable
- durable beyond the next bargaining round

That means real labour contribution, yes — but also credible management execution and no unnecessary destruction of the franchise.

---

## My view on the expert assessment
The expert input was helpful. It confirmed something important:
- voluntary measures and flexibility tools can help,
- but they usually do **not** fully solve large structural overcapacity.

That matters.

It means I will not accept a “site-preserving” story on faith. If the numbers show that all sites can remain open and still reach viable utilization, fine — show it. If not, then the discussion must move to consolidation.

So the expert report strengthens the case for **discipline**, not for ideological closure or ideological preservation.

## What would move me
I could support a settlement if it delivers all of the following:
1. **real structural savings**
2. **credible margin improvement**
3. **durable labour peace**
4. **binding investment and product commitments**
5. **no erosion of governance control**
6. **no value-destroying conflict**
7. **a footprint that is actually competitive**

What I would not support:
- a cosmetic compromise
- a deal that preserves overcapacity and calls it peace
- a package that permanently weakens the company’s economics
- or a confrontation that damages the brand, the plants, and the holding company’s core asset

---

## My bottom line
I am **not** endorsing a blanket “no closures ever” position.  
I am **not** endorsing closures as the starting point either.

I am saying this:
- bring the **numbers**
- compare the **two scenarios**
- show whether a site-preserving solution is **actually viable**
- and if not, make the case for consolidation **site by site**

If the case is credible, the controlling owners can support a tough but durable restructuring.

If it is not credible, I will not back a settlement that trades long-term value for short-term calm.
