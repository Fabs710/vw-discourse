I’ll respond directly to what has been said, and to the industrial-planning assessment.

## First: what the expert report does and does not change
The report is useful because it confirms something we already knew: management must **show the numbers** before demanding sacrifices. On that point, I agree.

But the report does **not** justify treating closures or compulsory redundancies as the default answer. It says those measures may become relevant **if** overcapacity is severe and **if** alternatives cannot close the gap. That is not the same as saying they are necessary here. We still have no site-by-site data, no utilization figures, no product pipeline, no conversion capex, and no proof that non-destructive alternatives are exhausted.

So the burden remains on management.

## Where I agree
I agree with the expert, the state, the works council, and even parts of management on these points:

- This is a **real restructuring problem**, not a cosmetic one.
- We need **transparent site-by-site data**.
- Voluntary tools, redeployment, retraining, working-time changes, and capacity optimization are all legitimate instruments.
- A prolonged conflict would be harmful.
- A durable solution must be economically credible, not just politically convenient.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the implication that because some plants may be inefficient on paper, the workforce must automatically absorb the pain through:
- compulsory redundancies,
- plant closures,
- or a permanent weakening of the collective bargaining framework.

The expert is right that labor measures alone may not fully solve a large structural gap. But that is precisely why I insist on **shared sacrifice**, not one-sided sacrifice. If the required adjustment is larger than what labor concessions can deliver, then management, owners, and capital must also contribute materially.

That means:
- management restraint,
- owner support,
- investment commitments,
- and a restructuring design that protects the workforce from being the only adjustment variable.

## To management
Your position remains too blunt. You keep saying the economics require “durable” change, which may be true. But you have not yet shown that:
1. every non-destructive alternative has been exhausted,
2. closures are unavoidable,
3. or compulsory redundancies are needed.

The expert report actually strengthens my point: before any irreversible step, we need:
- site-by-site utilization,
- break-even volumes,
- product allocation,
- conversion capex,
- and quantified savings from each lever.

If you cannot provide that, then you are not presenting a restructuring plan. You are presenting pressure.

And let me be clear: **a plan that starts from closures and layoffs will meet resistance**. Co-determination and collective bargaining are not decorative. They are real constraints.

## To the works council
I agree with your insistence that this cannot be a demolition exercise. I also agree that management needs to prove the case for any invasive measure.

But I want to sharpen one point: if we want to defend jobs and sites, we cannot stop at slogans. We need a serious alternative package that is strong enough to replace the closure logic. That means being ready to discuss, in a disciplined way:
- temporary labour-cost contributions,
- working-time adjustments,
- flexibility in deployment,
- voluntary departure programs,
- internal transfers,
- and productivity measures.

Not as a surrender, but as the price of securing jobs and sites.

## To the regional state
Your role matters because you are not just a political commentator here; you are an anchor shareholder with influence. If you say closures and forced layoffs are not the starting point, then use that influence to keep management honest.

I do support your line that destructive measures should be a last resort. But “last resort” must mean something real. It cannot become code for “employees should concede now and hope for the best later.”

## To the controlling family
You say you want a durable franchise and governance stability. Fine — then you should support a settlement that preserves the industrial base and does not make the workforce carry the whole load.

You cannot ask for labor peace, credibility, and future competitiveness while reserving structural pain mainly for employees. If the owners want flexibility from labor, they must back:
- binding investment,
- management restraint,
- and a fair contribution from capital and governance.

## To the investors
You are right about one thing: preserving every site at any cost is not automatically value-creating. I hear that.

But your answer cannot simply be “shrink labor until margins improve.” That is not durability; that is social conflict with delayed costs. If you want deal certainty, then support a package that is both:
- economically meaningful, and
- socially acceptable enough to hold.

## On the expert’s two scenarios
### 1) Site-preserving scenario
This remains my preferred route, and it is still on the table.

For it to work, the package must include:
- all German plants kept open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no tear-up of the collective wage framework,
- meaningful product or volume allocation where needed,
- working-time and capacity flexibility,
- voluntary instruments,
- and a real contribution from management and owners.

This is not fantasy. The expert explicitly says a site-preserving solution is feasible **if** utilization, product assignment, and conversion possibilities are sufficient. That is exactly why we demand the numbers.

### 2) Invasive capacity-adjustment scenario
If this means closures or forced layoffs, then I reject it.

If it means making workers pay while management and owners escape serious contribution, then I reject it.

If it means negotiating from the premise that jobs are disposable, then I reject it.

The expert says closures may be relevant if the gap is severe and alternatives fail. Fine — prove that first. Until then, we do not negotiate away jobs on speculation.

## What we are prepared to discuss
We remain prepared to discuss:
- temporary working-time changes,
- internal redeployment,
- retraining and qualification,
- voluntary departures,
- limited and fair labour-cost contributions,
- productivity agreements,
- and capacity optimization **without closures**.

But those discussions only make sense if they are tied to:
- binding site security,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and a durable collective agreement.

## What we are not prepared to discuss
We are **not** prepared to discuss:
- plant closures as a starting point,
- forced redundancies as a normal fallback,
- or a restructuring where workers pay while management and ownership merely observe.

## Bottom line
The expert report confirms that management must produce hard evidence. It does **not** move me off my core line.

My position remains:
1. **No compulsory redundancies**
2. **No plant closures**
3. **Protection of the collective bargaining framework**
4. **Binding site and job guarantees through 2030**
5. **Fair, shared sacrifice from management, owners, and labor**
6. **Transparent numbers before any irreversible decision**

If management cannot meet those conditions, then there is no serious agreement from the union side — only escalation.
