We are prepared to be pragmatic, but not at the expense of value.

## Our position
We support a **durable restructuring that materially improves margins, lowers the fixed cost base, and restores competitiveness**. We do **not** support a settlement that preserves structural overcapacity, leaves German operations uncompetitive, and then labels that “stability.”

## Where we agree
We agree with management, the family, and the expert on the following:
- This is a **real structural problem**, not a cosmetic one.
- The company needs **site-by-site transparency**.
- **Voluntary measures, redeployment, retraining, working-time flexibility, productivity gains, and internal transfers** should all be used.
- A prolonged strike or open conflict would be value-destructive.
- Any workable deal must be **durable**, not a temporary patch.

Those are sensible foundations. But they are only the foundation.

## Where we disagree with the employee side
We do **not** accept:
- a blanket commitment that every German plant must stay open regardless of economics,
- a categorical ban on compulsory redundancies in all circumstances,
- or binding job guarantees through 2030 if they are not backed by a cost structure that can actually sustain them.

That is not a restructuring strategy. It is a protection strategy for the existing footprint. If the footprint remains too large, returns will stay weak and equity will continue to be discounted.

The expert input actually reinforces that point: voluntary and flexibility-based measures can **mitigate** the problem, but may not fully solve **structural overcapacity** if it is significant. That is exactly why we cannot accept slogans in place of a hard industrial case.

## Where we disagree with management
We also do **not** endorse management’s closure-led framing as a default answer.

The burden is still on management to show, site by site:
- current and forecast utilization,
- minimum viable utilization,
- product/model pipeline through 2030,
- savings from shift optimization, working-time changes, voluntary exits, and redeployment,
- conversion capex where repurposing is proposed,
- and the EBITDA / free-cash-flow impact of each scenario.

Without that, closure arguments are not yet a decision; they are leverage.

So our position is not “close plants now.”  
Our position is: **prove the economics and then act accordingly**.

## On the two scenarios

### 1) Site-preserving scenario
We could support this only if it delivers:
- **material and durable savings**,
- a credible path back to competitive margins,
- and a footprint that does not require recurring support.

That means:
- not just temporary concessions,
- but a real reduction in fixed cost,
- a credible product and utilization plan,
- and burden-sharing that is actually meaningful.

If a site-preserving deal just defers the issue and preserves excess capacity, then it is not a solution. It is a delay mechanism that will end in a worse position later.

### 2) Capacity-consolidation scenario
We are open to this if the numbers show that some capacity is structurally uneconomic.

If that is the case, then we would support:
- shift consolidation,
- product transfers,
- selective repurposing,
- and, if genuinely necessary, closures or workforce reductions.

We do not say that lightly. But capital cannot back a permanently overcosted structure simply because the alternatives are politically uncomfortable.

## To the works council and union
Your insistence on social protection is understandable. But it has limits.

A “no closures, no forced layoffs” line is only credible if you can show a complete alternative package that closes the economic gap. So far, that has not been demonstrated.

If you want to avoid closure and forced redundancy outcomes, then you need to accept:
- substantial and durable labour-cost contributions,
- more flexibility,
- real productivity changes,
- and a willingness to consolidate where the economics require it.

Otherwise the burden is just being pushed into the future, where shareholders absorb lower returns and the company risks another restructuring round.

## To the state and the family
We appreciate your insistence on governance stability and social legitimacy. Those matter. But legitimacy is not created by blocking economically necessary change. If the final package leaves margins structurally impaired, then no one wins:
- not the workforce,
- not the owners,
- not the region,
- and certainly not minority capital.

We would support a negotiated settlement that is **economically sufficient**, even if it is difficult, because that is what protects long-term enterprise value.

## Our red line
We will not support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a deal that keeps all German plants open regardless of utilization,
- temporary concessions that do not structurally lower the cost base,
- or a package that leaves the company needing another restructuring soon after.

That would be value destruction with a polite label.

## What would move us
A package becomes supportable if it includes:
1. **Transparent site-by-site economics**
2. **Meaningful capacity reduction where required**
3. **Durable labour-cost savings**
4. **A credible margin-restoration path**
5. **Deal certainty**
6. **No open-ended conflict risk**

If those conditions are met, we can support a tough restructuring. If not, we will continue to argue for stronger action and price the risk accordingly.

## Bottom line
Our vote is for **competitiveness first**.

We are prepared to back a negotiated restructuring, but only one that genuinely restores the cost base. We will not back a settlement that protects every site, avoids hard decisions, and leaves the business structurally impaired.
