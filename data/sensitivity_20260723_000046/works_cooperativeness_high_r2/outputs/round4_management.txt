## Where I agree
I agree on three points:
- This is a **real restructuring crisis**, not a cosmetic issue.
- We need **site-by-site transparency** and hard numbers.
- **Voluntary measures, redeployment, retraining, working-time flexibility, and productivity improvements** must be part of the package.

I also agree that a prolonged conflict would hurt everyone. That is exactly why we need a settlement that actually fixes the cost base.

## Where I disagree — and strongly
What I do **not** accept is the repeated attempt to make **“keep every German plant open, no compulsory redundancies, no structural burden on labour”** the default answer.

That is not a restructuring solution. It is a demand that management absorb the full reality of overcapacity while the footprint remains unchanged. I cannot support that.

### To the works council
You keep saying:
- all plants must remain open,
- no forced redundancies,
- no tear-up of job security,
- closures are off the table.

I have to be blunt: **if the numbers show persistent structural overcapacity, then that position is not compatible with restoring competitiveness**.

You say management has not proven the case for closures. Fair enough — that is why we are asking for the detailed site-by-site data. But let me be equally clear: if the data shows one or more sites are below viable utilization and cannot be made viable through reallocation, conversion, or shift optimization, then refusing closure in advance is not responsible industrial policy. It is denial.

### To the union
You say voluntary tools can mitigate the issue. I agree — mitigate. But mitigation is not enough if the cost and capacity gap is structural.

A package built mainly on:
- temporary working-time changes,
- voluntary departures,
- and non-binding flexibility

will not restore the return-on-sales target if fixed costs stay too high.

You also speak of “shared sacrifice.” I support that. But shared sacrifice has to be **material and durable**, not symbolic. Labour must contribute in a way that actually lowers the cost base over time.

### To the regional state
I respect your anchor role and the importance of the region. But saying closures should not be the starting point is not the same as ruling them out.

We should not start with closures. We should start with the economics. If the economics require consolidation, then shielding the footprint from all structural change only postpones a deeper problem.

### To the controlling family
You are right that a destructive conflict is value-destructive. I agree. But the way to avoid that is not to preserve an uncompetitive structure.

If the family wants long-term franchise value, it must support a package that is economically sufficient. That means real structural savings, not merely preserving the appearance of stability.

### To investors
You are right to insist on durable savings and margin restoration. That is exactly the point. A deal that leaves the company overcosted and overcapacity-laden is not investable.

But you should also be clear-eyed: labor concessions alone will not be enough if the footprint remains unchanged. That is why consolidation must remain on the table.

## On the expert assessments
The expert input was helpful because it made the real issue explicit:
- voluntary measures can help,
- but they usually **do not fully solve** structural overcapacity.

That is the key point.

So I am **not** persuaded by the argument that because closures are painful, they should be off limits. Pain is not the criterion. Viability is.

The real question is:
**Can each German site be brought to minimum efficient scale with a credible product and cost plan through 2030?**

If yes, then we should consider that route seriously.  
If no, then the company must consolidate capacity. That includes the possibility of closures.

I want the next round to move from slogans to hard decision data.

- minimum viable utilization,
- product/model pipeline through 2030,
- savings from shift consolidation,
- savings from working-time and labour-cost measures,
- conversion capex if repurposing is possible,

  1. a site-preserving scenario, and  
  2. a capacity-consolidation scenario.

If the site-preserving scenario can truly deliver comparable and durable savings, I will consider it. But if it cannot, then it is not a solution — it is a delay mechanism.

## My position, clearly
I can support:
- voluntary measures,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity agreements,
- and a negotiated labour contribution.

I cannot support:
- a blanket ban on closures,
- a blanket ban on compulsory redundancies,
- or a deal that leaves German operations structurally uncompetitive.

That is the line.

## Bottom line
I am open to a negotiated settlement, but only one that restores competitiveness in a durable way. If the site-preserving route can do that, prove it with numbers. If it cannot, then we must be prepared to consolidate capacity, including difficult plant decisions.

A cosmetic deal that preserves the cost base is not acceptable.
