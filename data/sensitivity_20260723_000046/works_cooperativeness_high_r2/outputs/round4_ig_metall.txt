I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the works council, the state, and the expert on one crucial point: **this is a real restructuring problem, and we need hard site-by-site numbers**. No one should be asked to sign away jobs or wage standards on the basis of vague claims.

I also agree that:
- voluntary measures matter,
- redeployment and retraining matter,
- working-time flexibility matters,
- and a prolonged conflict would be damaging.

So yes, I am still prepared to negotiate a serious settlement.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the repeated attempt to make **plant closures and compulsory redundancies** the default answer.

The expert report did **not** prove that closures are necessary. It said they may become relevant if overcapacity is severe **and** alternatives fail. That is a very different statement.

Until management proves, site by site, that:
- utilization is structurally unviable,
- repurposing is impossible or irrational,
- and non-destructive alternatives cannot close the gap,

there is no justification for demanding workers absorb irreversible losses.

## To management
You keep saying you need durable structural change. Fine. But durability does **not** mean shifting the burden onto labour alone.

If you want the union to move, then bring:
- transparent site-by-site data,
- credible savings from each alternative,
- a binding investment and product pipeline,
- and a package showing how the burden is shared by management and owners, not just by workers.

Right now, what I hear is:
- labour should concede,
- labour should be flexible,
- labour should contribute,
- but management keeps open the option of closures and layoffs as leverage.

That is not shared sacrifice. That is burden shifting.

And let me be clear: **I will not accept a settlement that tears up the collective bargaining framework or leaves the workforce to pay for strategic mistakes alone.**

## To the works council
I support your insistence that the burden of proof is on management. You are right that we should not negotiate away jobs on speculation.

Where I need to sharpen the line is this: we should not let “no closures” become merely a slogan unless we are prepared to back it with a real alternative package strong enough to replace closure logic.

That means we must be ready to discuss:
- temporary working-time reductions,
- voluntary departures,
- internal transfers,
- retraining,
- productivity gains,
- and a fair, limited employee contribution.

But only if the package is tied to:
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **site security**
- and a durable collective agreement

## To the regional state
I support your insistence that closures should not be the starting point. But if you mean that seriously, use your influence to push management toward a non-destructive restructuring.

That means:
- transparency,
- serious product and investment commitments,
- and a refusal to treat German plants as disposable.

What I do **not** want is polite language while workers are slowly cornered into accepting layoffs through pressure.

## To the controlling family
If you want stability, then do not back a strategy that creates years of strikes and destroys trust.

A stable company is not one that simply looks lean on paper. It is one that can actually operate with labour peace, a credible industrial plan, and a workforce that sees a future.

So if you want labour flexibility, then support:
- binding site protection,
- no forced redundancies,
- and a real contribution from management and capital.

## To investors
You are right to demand real savings. But you are wrong if you think the only serious path is to weaken labour until margins improve.

The expert said voluntary and flexibility tools can **mitigate** the problem. That is exactly where I stand. They are part of the solution — not a fig leaf for closures.

If you want an investable deal, it has to be:
- economically credible,
- socially sustainable,
- and durable beyond the next bargaining round.

A deal that provokes permanent conflict is not value creation.

## My position on the scenarios
### 1) Site-preserving scenario
This is still the only acceptable basis for a negotiated settlement from the union side.

Conditions:
- all German sites remain open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of the wage framework,
- meaningful investment and product commitments,
- voluntary and internal adjustment tools first,
- and a fair contribution from labour that is matched by contributions from management and owners.

That is not softness. That is responsible restructuring.

### 2) Capacity-consolidation scenario
If this means closures or forced layoffs, then I reject it.

If it means workers carry the pain while others keep their guarantees, then I reject it.

If it means “we called it restructuring, but the workforce alone pays,” then I reject it.

## What would move me
I could move only if management presents a package that clearly shows:
- how the required savings are achieved **without forced redundancies**,
- how every German site remains viable,
- how the company contributes through management restraint and capital commitment,
- and how the collective bargaining framework remains intact.

Until then, my position stands.

## Bottom line
I am not retreating from my core line.

- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No plant closures**
- **Protection of the collective wage framework**
- **Fair shared sacrifice**
- **Transparency before any irreversible step**

If management can meet those conditions with real numbers, then we can negotiate seriously. If not, then there is no compromise to be found — only escalation.
