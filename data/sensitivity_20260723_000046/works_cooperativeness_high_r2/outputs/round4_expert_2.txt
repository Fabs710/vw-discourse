## What the current evidence implies, at a high level

Based on the positions already stated, the core economic question is not whether restructuring is needed — all sides accept that it is — but whether the required savings can be achieved through a **site-preserving** package or whether the footprint is so overbuilt that **capacity consolidation** is economically unavoidable.

From an industrial-planning perspective, that question turns on six variables:

1. **Utilization by site**
2. **Minimum viable utilization threshold**
3. **Product pipeline through 2030**
4. **Fixed-cost absorption**
5. **Conversion / repurposing capex**
6. **One-time restructuring costs vs. recurring savings**

Without plant-specific data, no credible closure recommendation can be made. But there are standard decision rules.

---

## Standard industrial-planning test

A plant is generally in one of three categories:

### 1) Viable as-is or with modest optimization
Typical indicators:
- utilization is near or above minimum efficient scale
- product pipeline is stable through 2030
- limited conversion capex required
- overhead can be reduced through shift optimization, redeployment, and voluntary exits

### 2) Viable only with repurposing
Typical indicators:
- current utilization is below threshold
- a future model or function can be transferred in
- conversion capex is material but justified by sustained volume
- break-even can be restored within a reasonable payback period

### 3) Structurally non-viable
Typical indicators:
- low utilization persists even after realistic product allocation
- no credible replacement product or function exists
- conversion capex is high relative to expected margin contribution
- the plant would remain cash-negative or margin-dilutive after restructuring

In automotive final assembly, once a site is persistently below roughly **60–70% utilization** — and sometimes higher depending on complexity, labor intensity, and product mix — the economics usually become difficult unless there is a specific product transfer or major cost reset.

## How I would compare the two scenarios

### Scenario A: Site-preserving
This is only economically credible if the company can achieve most of the following:
- higher line loading through product transfers
- shift consolidation
- internal redeployment
- voluntary departures
- working-time reduction or flexibility
- overhead reduction
- limited but targeted conversion capex where needed

### Scenario B: Capacity-consolidation
This becomes preferable when:
- one or more plants remain structurally underutilized
- the cost of keeping them open exceeds the cost of transition
- repurposing capex is too high or too risky
- the savings from consolidation are durable and large enough to justify the one-time cost

---

## What the economics usually look like

### Site-preserving scenario
**Pros**
- lower one-time social and transition costs
- less implementation risk
- less strike / delay risk
- preserves optionality for future products

**Cons**
- recurring fixed-cost burden may remain too high
- savings may be insufficient if overcapacity is material
- risk of “deferment” rather than resolution
- can require ongoing labor concessions to stay afloat

### Consolidation scenario
**Pros**
- usually delivers larger durable fixed-cost reduction
- better factory loading at the remaining sites
- stronger margin improvement if executed cleanly
- simpler long-term industrial footprint

**Cons**
- high one-time restructuring costs
- conversion / relocation capex
- major execution risk
- labor conflict risk
- possible disruption to supply chain and launch timing

---

## What would make a site-preserving solution credible

A site-preserving package is only credible if the following are true:

- every site has a **clear 2030 volume plan**
- underloaded plants can be brought to near-threshold utilization
- conversion capex is modest enough to earn an acceptable payback
- labor-cost concessions are durable, not just temporary
- voluntary exits and redeployment produce real headcount reduction
- fixed overhead falls materially
- EBITDA improvement is large enough to meet the company’s return target

If those conditions are not met, the site-preserving route is usually a delay mechanism, not a solution.

---

## What would make consolidation economically rational

Capacity consolidation becomes rational if:
- one or more plants cannot reach minimum efficient scale
- repurposing capex is too large relative to incremental EBITDA
- volume transfer can be executed without major supply-chain penalty
- the combined savings exceed the closure cost on a discounted cash flow basis
- remaining sites can absorb the work without new bottlenecks

A basic NPV test would compare:
- **PV of recurring savings**
vs.
- **one-time closure + transfer + capex + severance + disruption costs**

If the savings are durable and the transition risk is manageable, consolidation can be financially superior even with high upfront cost.

## Employment impact logic

From a planning standpoint, labor impact usually breaks into:
- **headcount reduction through attrition / voluntary exits**
- **redeployment to other sites or functions**
- **working-time reduction**
- **compulsory redundancy only if the gap remains after the above**
- **closure-driven job loss if a site cannot be kept viable**

The more that can be achieved through:
- product reassignment,
- shift optimization,
- and internal transfer,

the less need there is for closure. But that only works if there is enough volume and enough compatible process capacity elsewhere.
