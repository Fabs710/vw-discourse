Below is a **plant-by-plant industrial-planning and financial comparison framework** for the two broad scenarios now on the table:

1. **Site-preserving scenario**: keep all German plants open, avoid compulsory redundancies, rely on voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time changes, product shifts, and some labour-cost contribution.
2. **Capacity-consolidation scenario**: remove structurally uneconomic capacity through shift reductions, product transfers, and potentially one or more plant closures, with accompanying workforce reduction and capex for consolidation.

I’ll stay within industrial finance and manufacturing planning. I cannot validate labor-relations, governance, or political claims beyond how they affect execution risk.

---

# 1) Executive assessment

## Core industrial conclusion
A **site-preserving deal can work only if the German footprint is already close to economically viable utilization**, or if the company can **reassign enough product volume and/or convert enough sites** to restore that viability.

If, however, the company’s German network has **persistent excess fixed capacity**—especially in final assembly, paint, body shop, or other capital-intensive operations—then a no-closure solution usually requires:
- large ongoing labour-cost concessions,
- lower utilization than a clean consolidation would still allow,
- and repeated support measures to keep underfilled plants open.

That is typically **more expensive over time** than consolidation.

## Typical industrial-planning result
For a mature European OEM under cost pressure, the normal outcome is:

- **Voluntary measures + working-time flexibility** can cover part of the gap.
- **Shift reductions + product reallocations** can cover a meaningful additional share.
- But if the overcapacity is **structural and sizable**, only **capacity removal** delivers the full long-run fixed-cost savings.

In automotive manufacturing, the main economic variable is not only headcount. It is **fixed-cost absorption**:
- plant overhead,
- depreciation,
- utilities,
- maintenance,
- logistics,
- indirect labour,
- and line underutilization.

If those are too high relative to output, labour concessions alone rarely restore competitiveness.

# 3) Site-type assessment: what matters most

## A. Final assembly sites
These are the most sensitive to underutilization.

Why:
- high fixed cost base,
- high dependence on steady volume,
- difficult economics when shifts are removed,
- limited ability to “fill space” with non-core work.

If a final assembly plant is materially below the efficient operating band, preserving it usually requires:
- a new model assignment,
- transfer of volume from elsewhere,
- or significant fixed-cost sharing.

If none of those exist, the site is usually a **consolidation candidate**.

## B. Body shop / paint shop-intensive sites
These are often the true bottlenecks in capacity economics.

Why:
- expensive equipment,
- difficult to repurpose,
- high depreciation burden,
- and limited benefit from small labor changes.

A plant with underloaded paint/body capacity can become uneconomic quickly.

## C. Powertrain / components / modules / logistics-adjacent sites
These are more flexible.

Potentially viable alternatives:
- conversion to EV-related components,
- battery pack or module assembly,
- e-drive or subassembly work,
- aftermarket/remanufacturing,
- logistics and testing.

These sites are more often candidates for **repurposing** rather than closure, if there is real demand.

## D. Engineering / support sites
These are mainly overhead-driven.

Potential alternatives:
- centralization,
- shared services,
- product/program redeployment,
- indirect workforce reduction through attrition.

These can be restructured without plant closure, but they do not solve assembly overcapacity by themselves.

# 4) Scenario comparison

## Scenario 1: Site-preserving restructuring

### What it requires
To keep all German plants open without forced layoffs, the package must deliver enough savings through:
- voluntary departures,
- working-time reductions,
- wage or allowance concessions,
- internal redeployment,
- shift consolidation,
- product transfers,
- and investment in alternative workloads.

### Industrial viability test
This scenario is viable only if, for each site:
- utilization can be brought into an acceptable band,
- or the site can be repurposed at manageable capex,
- or the overhead burden can be reduced enough to sustain lower volume.

### Typical savings range
If executed well, a site-preserving package can often capture:
- **20–40% of the total needed restructuring savings** through labour and flexibility measures,
- sometimes more if there is strong voluntary attrition and large indirect headcount.

But in a heavy structural overcapacity case, it often leaves a residual gap.

### Capex requirement
Capex can be significant if sites are converted rather than closed:
- line retooling,
- battery or component conversion,
- logistics and IT changes,
- workplace adaptation.

A repurposing strategy can require **substantial conversion capex per retained site**. The economics only work if the future program is large enough to amortize it.

### Employment impact
- Lower immediate job losses than closure.
- More jobs preserved in the short run.
- But the risk is a **slow erosion of competitiveness** if savings are insufficient.

### Main risks
- Under-delivery of savings.
- Prolonged conflict over concessions.
- Keeping too much fixed cost in the system.
- Repeated restructuring rounds later.

### Planning judgment
A site-preserving deal is financially credible only if:
1. excess capacity is moderate,
2. product pipeline is real,
3. concessions are durable,
4. and the company can tolerate lower but sustainable utilization.

If not, it becomes a **deferral strategy**, not a restructuring solution.

---

## Scenario 2: Capacity-consolidation restructuring

### What it requires
This scenario uses:
- shift reductions,
- volume transfers,
- line consolidation,
- potentially one or more plant closures,
- workforce reductions,
- and one-off restructuring capex and severance/closure costs.

### Industrial viability test
It is justified when:
- one or more plants are below minimum efficient scale,
- conversion capex is too high relative to future returns,
- there is no viable product pipeline,
- and fixed-cost savings materially outweigh transition costs.

### Typical savings range
A well-executed consolidation scenario usually delivers:
- **most of the long-run fixed-cost savings**,
- especially if a whole plant’s overhead is removed.

In automotive, the long-run annual savings from removing a major underutilized site can be very material because it eliminates:
- plant overhead,
- indirect labour,
- maintenance,
- utilities,
- and a share of depreciation/lease burden.

### Capex requirement
Consolidation still requires capex:
- transfer tooling,
- reconfigure surviving sites,
- expand or rebalance bottleneck areas,
- IT and logistics integration,
- sometimes duplicate-run cost during transition.

But compared with converting every site to remain open, consolidation often requires **less total capex per euro of permanent savings**.

### Employment impact
- Higher near-term workforce reduction.
- More site disruption.
- Stronger social transition burden.
- But potentially better long-run employment security at the remaining viable plants.

### Main risks
- Ramp-up problems at receiving sites.
- Supply-chain disruption.
- Quality issues during transfer.
- Labour conflict and implementation delays.
- Hidden logistics cost if consolidation is poorly designed.

### Planning judgment
If structural overcapacity is severe, consolidation is usually the only route that fully restores fixed-cost competitiveness.

# 5) Financial comparison: how the economics usually work

Because no internal numbers are available, I’ll use standard planning relationships rather than invented plant-specific figures.

## A. Site-preserving scenario economics
Savings sources:
- voluntary attrition,
- reduced overtime and premiums,
- working-time adjustments,
- indirect headcount reduction,
- limited productivity gains,
- some overhead reduction.

Typical characteristics:
- **lower up-front cash cost** than closure,
- but **lower recurring savings**,
- and often a higher long-run cost base remains.

If the company has, for example, a 10–15% structural excess capacity problem, site preservation may only close part of the gap.

## B. Consolidation scenario economics
Savings sources:
- permanent elimination of underutilized fixed capacity,
- reduced plant overhead,
- fewer indirect roles,
- better utilization at remaining sites,
- lower logistics duplication.

Typical characteristics:
- **high one-time restructuring cost**,
- but **higher and more durable annual savings**.

The financial case is usually favorable when:
- the payback horizon is acceptable,
- the closure/consolidation cost is not excessive,
- and the receiving plants can absorb volume without major hidden cost.

---

# 6) Employment impact and transition cost

## Site-preserving scenario
- Lower immediate job losses.
- More internal redeployment and attrition.
- Better short-term social continuity.
- Risk of needing another round later if savings fall short.

## Consolidation scenario
- Higher initial job losses or transfers.
- More disruption.
- More one-off cost.
- But potentially a cleaner and more durable footprint.

A common industrial-planning rule: if the company is **going to lose jobs anyway**, it is usually better to do so in a way that removes enough fixed cost to actually restore competitiveness.

---

# 7) Decision thresholds: when each scenario makes sense

## Site-preserving is plausible if:
- utilization is only mildly below optimal,
- there is a credible product allocation path,
- conversion capex is moderate,
- and labour concessions are durable enough to bridge the gap.

## Consolidation is favored if:
- one or more sites are below minimum efficient scale,
- there is no credible future product pipeline,
- conversion capex is too high,
- and the utilization gap is structural rather than cyclical.

In other words:  
**If a plant cannot get to viable utilization without large recurring support, it is economically a consolidation candidate.**

# 8) What a proper site-by-site evaluation should include

For each German site, management should present:

1. **Installed capacity**
   - by shift and major area: press, body, paint, final assembly, battery/module, etc.

2. **Current utilization**
   - current volume and forecast volume through 2030.

3. **Minimum viable utilization**
   - the utilization required to cover fixed cost and capital charge.

4. **Product assignment**
   - current programs and likely successor programs.

5. **Conversion capex**
   - tooling, building changes, logistics, ramp-up.

6. **Savings from each option**
   - no change,
   - shift reduction,
   - product transfer,
   - conversion,
   - consolidation/closure.

7. **Transition risk**
   - ramp-up risk,
   - quality risk,
   - supply-chain risk,
   - labour disruption risk.

8. **Employment effect**
   - direct, indirect, and contractor impact.

Without this, no serious comparison can be made.

# 9) My practical assessment of the two scenarios

## On pure industrial economics
- A **site-preserving solution** is usually the less disruptive option, but it only works if the network’s overcapacity is not too large and if significant product or workload can be added.
- A **capacity-consolidation solution** usually produces better long-run cost competitiveness when the overcapacity is structural and material.

## On timing
- Site-preserving measures can be faster to announce and politically easier to negotiate, but slower to produce sufficient cost relief.
- Consolidation takes longer to implement cleanly, but once completed it tends to deliver a stronger fixed-cost step-down.

## On durability
- Site-preserving agreements are durable only if the savings are real and the product pipeline is credible.
- Consolidation is durable if the surviving network is properly sized and funded.

# 10) Bottom line

My expert assessment is:

- The scenario strongly suggests **structural overcapacity** in German operations.
- **Voluntary measures, redeployment, retraining, and working-time adjustments** are valid and often necessary, but they are usually **not sufficient on their own** if the excess capacity is significant.
- A **site-preserving scenario** can be economically credible only if the company can demonstrate, site by site, that utilization can be restored and that conversion capex is justified.
- If not, then **some capacity removal**—including possible plant consolidation and perhaps closure—will likely be required to achieve a durable cost reset.

So the real decision is not “sites vs no sites.” It is:
**Can each German site be brought to minimum efficient scale at an acceptable cost, or must the footprint be reduced?**
