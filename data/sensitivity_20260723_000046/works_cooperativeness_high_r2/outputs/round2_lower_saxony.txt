I will respond plainly from the regional state’s perspective: **a restructuring is necessary, but a restructuring built around plant closures and compulsory redundancies is not acceptable as the starting point**.

## To management
I agree with you on the diagnosis that the company needs real cost improvement, not a cosmetic exercise. I also accept that German operations must become more competitive and that the future investment plan has to be financed somehow.

But I disagree with your central framing in one important respect:  
**you are treating closures and compulsory redundancies as if they were merely technical options. They are not.** They are irreversible decisions with major regional, social, and governance consequences.

What I need from management is not repeated insistence that the economics are hard. I accept that. What I need is:
- a **site-by-site transparency of overcapacity and savings assumptions**,
- a serious demonstration that **non-destructive alternatives were actually exhausted**,
- and a restructuring package that does **not** make German plants and jobs the first and main adjustment variable.

If you cannot show that, then you are asking the state to endorse industrial shrinkage without adequate safeguards. I will not do that.

## To the works council
I support your insistence that co-determination is real, that workers should not carry the entire burden, and that forced redundancies should be avoided if there is a negotiated alternative.

Where I am **not** simply aligning myself with you is on the absolute wording. I understand the political and social logic of “no closures, no forced layoffs,” but if we are talking about a viable final settlement, we will need to see whether **limited, very carefully designed capacity adjustments** can be reconciled with site preservation and job security.

That said, I am not prepared to back a package that:
- tears up the job-security framework,
- makes plant closure the default answer,
- or treats the workforce as the disposable cost center.

So your line is broadly right in substance: **site-preserving restructuring first, destructive measures only as a last resort and only if truly unavoidable**.

## To the union
I agree that any solution must be a **shared-sacrifice package** and not a one-sided transfer of pain onto employees. That is exactly the right principle.

But I also need to say this clearly: if the workforce contribution is limited to temporary concessions without durable structural effect, then the company will simply come back to the same problem later. So I do need to see:
- meaningful productivity improvements,
- flexibility where it genuinely helps,
- voluntary instruments,
- and, if necessary, limited and time-bound labour-cost contributions.

What I do **not** support is using “shared sacrifice” as code for workers giving a lot while management and owners contribute only symbolically. That will not be politically or socially sustainable, and I would not endorse it.

## To the controlling family and investors
I understand your focus on competitiveness, margin restoration, and deal certainty. Those are legitimate concerns.

But I want to be equally direct: **a settlement that is economically clean on paper but socially destructive in practice is not a stable settlement** for this company. It will provoke resistance, damage trust, and weaken the company’s regional base. That is also a value risk.

So I support your insistence on real restructuring, but not if it comes at the cost of:
- hollowing out the German industrial footprint,
- destabilizing labour relations,
- or weakening governance legitimacy.

## My position
As regional state and anchor shareholder, my support depends on a package with these elements:

1. **German sites protected wherever economically viable**
   - No closure-first logic.
   - Closure only if there is no credible alternative and if the burden-sharing is fair.

2. **No compulsory redundancies if negotiated alternatives exist**
   - Voluntary, phased, or internally managed workforce reduction comes first.

3. **A credible industrial future**
   - Concrete product, investment, and technology commitments.
   - Savings without a future model are not enough.

4. **Preservation of co-determination and the state’s veto role**
   - This governance balance is part of the company’s stability.
   - It is not open for erosion in the name of speed.

5. **A fair burden-sharing structure**
   - Labour contributions must be matched by management restraint and owner commitment.

## What I can support
I can support a restructuring that includes:
- capacity reduction,
- working-time adjustment,
- redeployment and retraining,
- voluntary departure programs,
- targeted productivity measures,
- and negotiated labour-cost contributions,

**if and only if** it protects the German industrial core and avoids mass job destruction.

## What I cannot support
I cannot support:
- a package built around plant closures in Germany,
- compulsory redundancies as the default tool,
- or a restructuring that weakens the state’s co-determination and veto role.

## Bottom line
I am prepared to help broker a durable settlement, but only one that is **economically credible and socially responsible**. The burden cannot fall mainly on regional jobs and sites. If management wants the state’s support, it must come back with a **site-preserving, negotiated restructuring plan** and not a closure-led one.

If that cannot be done, then I will not provide political or board-level backing for the current direction.
