I will be direct.

## My position
I support a **genuinely tough restructuring** that restores competitiveness, margins, and long-term value. I do **not** support a cosmetic compromise that leaves the company overcosted, overcapacity-laden, and exposed to repeat crisis.

At the same time, I do **not** support a reckless confrontation that destroys value through prolonged strikes, reputational damage, or a governance breakdown. The family holding’s interest is not short-term optics; it is durable enterprise value.

So my standard is simple:
- **durable cost improvement**
- **credible industrial viability**
- **labour peace that actually holds**
- **no value-destructive instability**
- **no erosion of ownership governance**

## To management
I agree with you on the core diagnosis: the current German footprint appears structurally uncompetitive, and the company needs real change.

Where I disagree is with any assumption that a closure-led outcome is automatically justified without a precise, site-by-site case. You still need to prove it.

You have not yet given the board and owners the data needed to decide whether:
- all sites can be made viable through product shifts, utilization changes, and repurposing,
- or whether one or more sites are truly uneconomic and must be consolidated.

So I want the detailed comparison on the table:
- current and forecast utilization by site,
- break-even thresholds,
- product/model pipeline through 2030,
- savings from working-time, redeployment, shift consolidation, and voluntary exits,
- conversion capex where repurposing is possible,
- and EBITDA / free-cash-flow impact under both scenarios.

Until then, “closures are necessary” is not yet a case; it is a claim.

## To the works council
I understand your refusal to accept closures and compulsory redundancies as a starting point. I also understand why you insist on preserving co-determination and job security.

But I need to be clear: a blanket “no closures, no layoffs, no structural burden on labor” position is not enough if the numbers show persistent overcapacity. That may be a defensible bargaining line, but it is not yet an industrial solution.

The expert input has made one thing clear: voluntary measures and flexibility can mitigate the problem, but may not fully solve severe overcapacity. That means the question is not whether pain exists; it is whether the proposed alternative can actually close the gap.

If you want a site-preserving route, then it has to be shown to be economically durable, not just politically preferable.

## To the union
I agree that workers should not be the only adjustment variable. I also agree that permanent labour concessions without counterpart commitments would be unacceptable.

But “shared sacrifice” has to be real and durable. Temporary concessions alone will not restore the cost base if the footprint remains structurally too large.

If the union wants to avoid closures and forced layoffs, then it must engage seriously with measures that materially lower the long-term cost structure:
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary departures,
- redeployment,
- productivity agreements,
- and potentially durable labour-cost contributions.

What I do **not** accept is a package that promises job protection through 2030 while refusing the structural adjustments required to make that promise credible.

## To the regional state
I respect your anchor role, and I agree that the state’s voice matters. I also agree that closures and forced redundancies should not be the starting point.

But I do not accept the idea that the state can simply rule out difficult structural decisions in advance. If a site cannot be made viable despite serious testing of alternatives, then preserving it indefinitely is not responsible stewardship.

Your role should be to insist on:
- transparency,
- serious examination of alternatives,
- and a socially and industrially credible solution.

Not to pre-empt the economics.

## To investors
I agree with your insistence on durable savings and a credible margin-restoration path. A deal that keeps all structural costs in place is not investable.

Where I differ is that I do not want value destroyed by open-ended confrontation or by a rushed settlement that provokes years of conflict. A hard deal only matters if it holds.

So I share your objective, but not a simplistic “cut first, ask questions later” approach. We need a solution that is economically sufficient **and** implementable.

## On the scenarios
### 1) Site-preserving scenario
I am open to it **only if** it truly delivers comparable and durable savings, site by site, with a credible product and utilization plan.

If it can do that, fine. But it must be proven, not asserted.

### 2) Capacity-consolidation scenario
I will not rule it out.

If the evidence shows that one or more sites are structurally uneconomic and cannot be repurposed at reasonable capex, then consolidation is a legitimate tool. Not because I prefer it, but because preserving permanent excess capacity would be value-destructive.

However, any consolidation must be carefully governed:
- no chaotic execution,
- no unnecessary reputational damage,
- no uncontrolled labor escalation,
- and no threat to governance control.

## What would move me
I could support a settlement if it delivers all of the following:
1. real structural savings
2. durable margin improvement
3. credible labor peace
4. site-by-site proof of viability
5. a footprint that is actually competitive
6. no erosion of governance control
7. no prolonged conflict that destroys value

## What I will not accept
I will not accept:
- a cosmetic deal,
- a permanent subsidy of underutilized capacity,
- a settlement that only postpones the problem,
- or a destructive confrontation that weakens the company’s core asset.

## Bottom line
I support a **negotiated but economically serious restructuring**.  
I do **not** support blanket preservation of every site regardless of economics.  
I do **not** support closures as a first reflex either.

Bring the numbers. Compare the scenarios. Prove which sites are viable, convertible, or truly at risk.

If the site-preserving route is genuinely viable, I will consider it. If it is not, then we must be prepared for difficult but necessary consolidation.
