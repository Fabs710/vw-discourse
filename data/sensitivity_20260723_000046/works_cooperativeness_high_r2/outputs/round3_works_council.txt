Management, owners, investors, and state representatives: I will respond directly.

We all agree that this cannot be a cosmetic exercise. Fine. But let us not pretend that “real restructuring” automatically means plant closures, compulsory redundancies, and tearing up the job-security framework. That is not restructuring in my book; that is shifting the crisis onto the workforce.

## Where I agree
I agree that:
- the company needs durable cost improvement,
- voluntary measures, redeployment, retraining, working-time flexibility, and productivity improvements belong on the table,
- and a prolonged conflict would hurt everyone.

I also agree that we need **site-by-site transparency**. Without hard numbers, there is no serious basis for asking workers to give up hard-won protections.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept:
- plant closures as a “necessary option” that is simply waiting to be activated,
- compulsory redundancies as a normal fallback,
- or “material labour contribution” as code for workers paying for management’s strategic mistakes.

If management cannot show how the required savings can be achieved **without closures and without forced layoffs**, then the burden of proof has not been met.

## To management
You keep saying the economics require structural change. Then prove it in detail:
- Which plants are allegedly overcapacity?
- What exactly is the shortfall by site and function?
- What savings come from working-time, internal transfers, product shifts, and capacity optimization?
- What investment commitments secure the German footprint through 2030?

I am not interested in abstract pressure. I need a concrete restructuring plan that preserves every German plant and avoids compulsory redundancies.

And let me be equally clear: if you come here with a closure-led plan and ask for our consent afterward, you will not get it. Co-determination is not decorative. It is a real veto where it matters.

## To the controlling family
You say you want a durable franchise and governance stability. Then use your influence to stop management from turning this into a demolition exercise.

A settlement that destroys trust in the workforce and breaks the job-security framework is not “durable.” It will create years of conflict, strikes, and damaged legitimacy.

If you truly want a stable company, back a site-preserving solution:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding protection through 2030,
- and a negotiated contribution from labour that does not sacrifice the company’s social foundation.

## To the regional state
I welcome your statement that closures and compulsory redundancies are not acceptable as a starting point. That is the right line. But I need more than cautionary language.

If the state means what it says, then it should actively support a package that protects the industrial base:
- enforce transparency,
- pressure management to table non-destructive alternatives,
- and make clear that any attempt to treat German plants as disposable will face resistance.

The state’s role is not to facilitate industrial shrinkage; it is to protect regional value creation and social stability.

## To investors
You want credibility and margin improvement. I understand that. But if your definition of credibility is “close plants and cut workers,” then we fundamentally disagree.

A package that keeps all German plants open, avoids forced layoffs, and preserves co-determination can still be credible if it is built on:
- working-time adjustments,
- voluntary departures,
- internal redeployment,
- training,
- productivity gains,
- management restraint,
- and fair burden-sharing across the group.

What is not credible is a deal that destroys the workforce’s trust and then expects durable peace.

## On the two scenarios
Since the moderator asked for scenario comparison, here is our view:

### 1) Site-preserving scenario
This is the only acceptable basis for discussion from our side.

Conditions:
- all German plants remain open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no tear-up of the job-security framework,
- meaningful investment and product commitments,
- voluntary and internal adjustment tools only,
- and co-determination preserved in substance, not just form.

This is not a “soft” option. It is a serious restructuring through capacity management, flexibility, and investment.

### 2) Invasive capacity-adjustment scenario
If this means closures or forced layoffs, then it is not acceptable.

If it means squeezing the workforce harder while leaving management and owners protected, then it is not acceptable.

If it means that one side permanently bears the pain while others demand patience, then it is not acceptable.

I am not prepared to negotiate away the company’s social foundation in exchange for vague promises of future competitiveness.

## What we are prepared to discuss
We are prepared to discuss:
- temporary working-time changes,
- voluntary departure programs,
- internal transfers across German sites,
- retraining and qualification,
- productivity agreements,
- and a limited, fair employee contribution **only if** it is matched by hard guarantees on sites and jobs.

We are also prepared to discuss how to improve site utilization and how to reallocate volumes and products **without shutting plants down**.

## What we are not prepared to discuss
We are **not** prepared to discuss:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or a settlement that makes “job security through 2030” meaningless.

## Bottom line
If management wants a settlement, it must come back with a plan that:
1. keeps every German plant open,
2. rules out compulsory redundancies,
3. preserves co-determination and the job-security framework,
4. provides binding site and investment guarantees through 2030,
5. and demonstrates, with numbers, how the restructuring works without social destruction.

Until then, I do not see a compromise — I see pressure to concede on core rights. And on that, my answer is no.
