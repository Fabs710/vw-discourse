I will be equally direct.

## To the Works Council
I hear your repeated red lines: no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no changes to job-security guarantees. I understand why you are holding that line. But you are still not addressing the core economic problem.

You keep saying “show us the numbers.” Fair. I am now saying the same back to you: **show me how a no-closure package removes enough fixed cost and overcapacity to restore competitiveness by 2030.** Not in general terms. Site by site. Volume by volume. Cost by cost.

Because if the answer is only:
- working-time tweaks,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary exits,
- and temporary concessions,

then I will tell you plainly: **that is not enough if it leaves the structural cost base intact.** It may soften the blow, but it does not solve the problem.

I am open to avoiding closures **only if** the alternative is economically equivalent in real terms:
- durable capacity reduction,
- durable labor-cost relief,
- and a credible path back to target margins.

If you cannot deliver that, then you are not offering a solution — you are asking management to accept permanent uncompetitiveness.

## To the Trade Union
You say no compulsory redundancies and no plant closures can be the basis of a negotiated package. I disagree with the way you present that as a sufficient framework.

A bargaining framework is not a solution. The solution has to deliver the savings.

I accept that workers should not carry the burden alone. I accept that management and ownership must contribute. But I do **not** accept the idea that the collective wage framework, job-security guarantees, and site protection can simply be preserved first and then somehow the cost gap will disappear.

That sequence is backwards.

If you want real bargaining, then we need a package that includes:
- material labor-cost contribution,
- capacity reduction,
- productivity improvement,
- and a headcount path that is structurally credible.

Temporary moderation is acceptable. Cosmetic moderation is not. And “voluntary” alone is not a serious answer if the numbers required are large.

## To the Regional State Government
I agree with you on one point: a destructive conflict would be bad for the region, bad for the workforce, and bad for the company. That is exactly why I am pushing for a settlement that changes the cost base now.

But I do not accept your position that closures should be effectively ruled out as the default route.

A blocking minority is not a mandate to freeze structural change. If the alternative package does not deliver enough savings, then preventing closures without providing an equivalent solution would simply lock in decline.

You say capacity reduction can be discussed. Good. Then let us discuss **real** capacity reduction, not just internal reshuffling that preserves the same fixed-cost burden.

## To the Controlling Family
You say you want value preservation and not a value-destroying conflict. I agree. But value is destroyed just as surely by maintaining an uncompetitive cost base as by a strike.

This is the central trade-off:
- yes, a closure-driven route carries conflict risk,
- but no, a cosmetic no-closure deal is not “stability” if it guarantees continued margin erosion.

So I need clarity from the ownership side: are you prepared to back a genuinely hard restructuring, including closures if needed, or are you asking management to carry the risk of a weak compromise?

Because I will not accept a governance setup where management is expected to restore competitiveness while being denied the instruments required to do it.

## To Institutional Investors
You are right to insist on durability and real savings. You are wrong if you think that means management should simply wait for a perfect no-closure package that preserves nearly everything.

Your own logic points in the same direction as ours: the package has to be hard enough to matter. If the employee side cannot quantify a no-closure package that closes the gap in a durable way, then closures remain on the table.

That is not a threat for effect. It is a matter of arithmetic.

## On the expert input
The experts have been useful, but let me be precise about what they did and did not establish.

They established that:
- no-closure packages can save real money,
- but they usually deliver only part of the effect of closures when the overcapacity is structural.

That supports my position, not yours.

If the no-closure path is to replace closures, it must be unusually deep:
- shift removals,
- capacity consolidation,
- lower labor cost,
- headcount reduction through more than natural attrition,
- and network reconfiguration with real fixed-cost impact.

If that package is not available, then we are back to the central reality: **structural overcapacity has to come out somehow.**

## What I will not accept
Let me state this clearly:

I will **not** accept:
- a settlement that preserves all sites and all guarantees while leaving the cost base uncompetitive,
- a deal that delays hard decisions until the next crisis,
- or a package that buys industrial peace at the expense of the company’s future.

I am open to a negotiated solution. I am **not** open to a symbolic one.

## What could move me
A no-closure alternative could move me only if it contains:
1. **quantified permanent savings**,  
2. **actual capacity reduction**,  
3. **a clear and credible headcount path**,  
4. **material employee cost contribution**,  
5. **management and owner contributions**, and  
6. **a margin trajectory that restores investment capacity to 2030**.

If those conditions are met, I can support avoiding closures.

If they are not met, then I will continue to argue for harder structural measures, including closures and stronger labor-cost adjustments.

## Bottom line
I am not retreating from my position.

- Doing nothing is not an option.
- A cosmetic compromise is not acceptable.
- A no-closure solution is acceptable **only if it is economically equivalent in substance**.
- If it is not, then the company must use the instruments needed to restore competitiveness.

So the next step is not more declarations. It is the quantified comparison that everyone has already agreed is necessary:
- closure-based scenario,
- no-closure scenario,
- site-by-site,
- with savings, capacity impact, headcount path, one-time costs, and investment needs.

Bring that, and we can decide whether the employee side’s alternative is truly sufficient. If not, the hard options remain on the table.
