## To the Works Council

Where we fundamentally disagree is your claim that **no closures, no compulsory redundancies, and full preservation of existing guarantees can simply be the starting point**. That is not a restructuring solution. That is a demand to preserve the current cost structure while the market deteriorates.

You keep asking us to prove that closures are unavoidable. We do not need to prove closures are the only possible instrument. We need to prove that **the final package must remove enough permanent cost and capacity**. If you can deliver that without closures, I am open. But you have not yet shown how your preferred route gets us to a structurally competitive cost base by 2030.

Your position is still:
- keep every German plant open,
- avoid forced reductions,
- preserve historic guarantees,
- and hope that shift changes, redeployment, and voluntary exits will close a structural gap.

That may reduce pain, but it does **not** yet answer the economics. If the alternative is not equivalent in substance, then it is not enough.

## To the Trade Union
I agree that workers should not carry the burden alone, and I accept that any contribution must be negotiated, time-limited where possible, and linked to a credible future for the German sites.

But I reject the way you keep framing **no closures** as if that alone were a sufficient restructuring concept. It is not. A bargaining framework is not a business plan.

You say you can support:
- temporary labor-cost relief,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- productivity improvements.

Good. But the question is whether that adds up to **durable structural savings**, not just a softer landing. If it does, show the numbers. If it doesn’t, then you are asking management to accept permanent underperformance.

And let me be clear: I will not accept a package where the collective wage framework remains untouched in substance while the company is expected to repair its margin gap from somewhere else.

## To the Regional State Government
I respect your concern for regional stability and the industrial base. That concern is legitimate.

But your position still avoids the central issue: **if the company has real structural overcapacity, capacity has to come out somewhere**. Saying closures are not preferred is not enough. We need a package that actually fixes the cost base.

If the state wants to prevent closures, then it must support a **credible alternative** that removes the same fixed-cost burden through other means. So far, we have not seen that alternative quantified.

I also have to say plainly: the blocking power in governance cannot be used to freeze necessary restructuring. If it is used to block hard decisions without offering a credible substitute, then it protects decline.

## To the Controlling Family
I agree with you that value destruction through prolonged conflict would be harmful. I also agree that a weak compromise would be a bad outcome.

Where I disagree is your implication that closures are merely a tactical choice. They are not. They are a response to structural overcapacity when the economics require permanent removal of cost and capacity.

You say you are open to a no-closure path if it is deep enough. Fine. That is exactly the test. But so far, I have not heard a package that matches the needed scale. If the no-closure alternative leaves the fixed-cost base too high, then it is not a real alternative.

So my message is simple: I am open to avoiding closures **only if** the alternative is economically equivalent in substance. Otherwise, we will not protect the company’s future by preserving every legacy structure.

## To Institutional Investors
You are right to insist on durability, execution certainty, and real savings. That is exactly my position too.

Where I disagree with you is if you treat closures as the benchmark of seriousness in and of themselves. Closures are not automatically value-maximizing. They are only justified if they are the best way to close the economic gap.

What matters is not the symbol of a closure. What matters is:
- annual recurring savings,
- lower fixed cost,
- capacity reduction,
- and restored margin trajectory.

If a no-closure package can deliver that, I will support it. If it cannot, I will not pretend otherwise just to avoid conflict.

## On the moderator’s phased compromise
I understand the logic of a phased approach. But I will be blunt: a phase-one deal that locks in concessions while keeping closure disputes alive for later is not enough.

We cannot accept a structure where:
1. management gets immediate labor concessions,
2. the cost problem remains unresolved,
3. and the same closure argument returns six months later.

If there is to be a phased solution, it must be built around **measurable savings and verifiable capacity reduction**, not around indefinite postponement. Otherwise it just delays the same fight.
