## Our position

As capital-market shareholders, we support **a restructuring that credibly restores competitive margins**. We do **not** support a deal that preserves an uncompetitive cost base, protects every legacy arrangement, and calls that “stability.” That would be value destruction, not compromise.

At the same time, we do **not** want a prolonged closure fight if a negotiated alternative can deliver materially similar savings with less disruption. We are pragmatic, but only within the constraint that the economics actually work.

## Where we agree

### With management
You are right about the core problem: the company needs **durable** savings and a lower structural cost base. A cosmetic settlement would be unacceptable, and we would view it as another delay in margin repair.

You are also right that the employee side has not yet produced a fully quantified alternative that proves it can close the gap. The burden is on the no-closure camp to show:
- annual savings,
- capacity reduction,
- headcount path,
- and implementation certainty.

### With the family holding
We agree that the company needs a **real restructuring**, not a value-destroying stalemate. A prolonged strike or governance crisis would damage cash flow, brand perception, and execution credibility.

We also agree that closures are not a value-neutral tool. If they trigger avoidable conflict and delay, the market will penalize the company before any savings arrive.

### With the state government, in part
We agree that deindustrialisation is not the goal and that regional and political damage matters. But the state’s instinct to protect every site by default is not enough on its own. If the resulting structure remains uncompetitive, then the region gets a slower decline instead of a fix.

### With the works council and union, in part
We accept your point that a negotiated package can be real. We do not dismiss working-time measures, redeployment, qualification, and voluntary exits. Those can be part of a serious solution.

## Where we disagree

### To the works council and union
Your repeated red lines — **no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no meaningful dilution of guarantees** — are understandable politically, but they are **not sufficient** from a shareholder perspective unless you can demonstrate that the alternative closes the cost gap.

Right now, the position sounds like:
- preserve every site,
- preserve most guarantees,
- accept only temporary concessions,
- and assume competitiveness will follow.

That is not evidence-based restructuring. That is hope.

If the no-closure package can truly deliver permanent savings and lower overcapacity, fine — show it. But if not, then insisting on those red lines while rejecting harder measures means you are asking shareholders to finance structural underperformance.

### To management
We do not give management a blank cheque for closures either. If you push closures as the default tactic without proving the no-closure route fails, you risk:
- industrial escalation,
- delay,
- reputational damage,
- and a worse financial outcome than the one you wanted to avoid.

You need to show the numbers, not just the threat.

## Our assessment of the options

### 1) Closure-based restructuring
This is the more decisive route to fixed-cost reduction. From a shareholder standpoint, that matters. If the company truly has persistent overcapacity, then permanent capacity removal has real economic logic.

But closures come with:
- one-time costs,
- political backlash,
- strike risk,
- implementation delay,
- and possible damage to future labor relations.

So we would support closures only if management can show that:
- the no-closure alternative is insufficient,
- the execution risk is manageable,
- and the savings justify the disruption.

### 2) No-closure alternative
We are open to it, but only if it is **hard enough** to be economically equivalent in substance.

Based on the expert inputs, that would require:
- shift reductions,
- capacity consolidation,
- headcount reductions through attrition/voluntary exits/redeployment,
- temporary labor-cost relief,
- productivity gains,
- and meaningful investment reconfiguration.

If the package only softens the pain while leaving the fixed-cost base largely intact, we will not support it.

## What would move us

We need a package that gives us:
1. **Quantified annual savings**
2. **Durable cost reduction**
3. **A credible capacity plan**
4. **A believable headcount path**
5. **Deal certainty**
6. **No open-ended industrial conflict**

If the no-closure route can deliver those, it is acceptable. If it cannot, tougher measures become unavoidable.

## What we will not support

We will not support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a deal that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive,
- or a prolonged conflict that burns value without improving the outcome.

We also will not support a settlement in which labor keeps all protections while investors absorb the downside through lower returns.

## Bottom line

We are **not** here to choose between “closures at any cost” and “no change.”  
We are here to choose between:
- a **credible restructuring** that restores competitiveness and margins, and
- a **weak compromise** that preserves political comfort and erodes shareholder value.

If the no-closure package can be shown, in numbers, to close the gap durably, we can support it. If not, then management must be prepared to use harder structural measures.
