I will be equally direct in round 3.

## First: what I agree with
I agree on one point that is now beyond dispute: **doing nothing is not an option**. This company needs restructuring, and it needs a real economic answer, not a press-release compromise.

I also agree with the repeated point that **a cosmetic deal is unacceptable**. If the numbers do not work, then pretending otherwise would only postpone the next crisis.

And yes: the discussion has now reached the right level of seriousness. We need a **quantified comparison**. Fine. Let us quantify it.

## Where I strongly disagree
What I do **not** accept is the idea that closures and forced redundancies remain “normal options” simply because management, investors, or owners prefer them.

They are not normal to the workforce. They are a breach of trust, a destruction of sites, and a direct attack on the job-security framework that made this company stable for decades.

So let me state the employee position clearly again:

- **No plant closures in Germany.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No dismantling of job-security guarantees as the price of restructuring.**

Those are not bargaining chips for us. They are red lines.

## To management
You keep saying the employee side is not answering the cost question. That is not correct. We are answering it: **the company must restructure through a negotiated no-closure package**.

What you have not yet done is prove that closures are unavoidable. The expert input did not prove that. It said the opposite: a no-closure package can produce meaningful savings and may be economically credible if properly designed.

So your claim is not “we need restructuring.” We already agree on that.

Your real claim is: **we want the right to close plants and cut people if we prefer that route.**  
That is what I reject.

If you want a no-closure solution rejected, then bring the actual site-by-site numbers and show why the alternative fails. Not abstract warnings. Numbers.

## To the family holding
You say you do not want value destruction, and I take that seriously. Then stop treating closures as an acceptable fallback in reserve. That shadow threat poisons every negotiation.

The real value destroyer is not a workforce defending its plants. The real value destroyer is management trying to force a structural rupture while expecting the workforce to finance it with jobs.

If you want stability, you should support:
- a binding no-closure settlement,
- a contribution from management and ownership,
- and a genuine industrial plan for German sites.

If you keep closures on the table, you are not preserving value. You are manufacturing conflict.

## To the state government
Your position is closer to ours, but it must be backed by action. Saying closures are not the preferred route is not enough if management still believes it can pressure the board toward them.

Use the blocking power. That is what it is for.

The state should not become a bystander while a regional industrial base is dismantled. If the company needs capacity reduction, then the first responsibility is to design it **without closures and without forced layoffs**.

## To institutional investors
You want “durable savings.” So do we. But you are making a dangerous simplification when you treat closures as the benchmark of seriousness.

The expert was clear:
- closures remove fixed costs more decisively,
- but no-closure packages can still deliver a large share of the savings,
- and closures bring major conflict and execution risk.

So the question is not whether closures are mathematically stronger in isolation. Of course they are. The question is whether they are **necessary** given the political, legal, industrial-relations, and long-term trust costs.

My answer is: **not proven**.

If you want economics, then consider the full economics:
- strike risk,
- delay,
- reputational damage,
- loss of trust in future bargaining,
- supply-chain disruption,
- and the erosion of the co-determination model.

Those are not side effects. They are part of the cost.

## On the expert comparison
The expert said a no-closure package can be credible if it includes:
- shift reduction,
- headcount decline via attrition and voluntary exits,
- temporary labor-cost relief,
- productivity gains,
- and capacity reallocation.

Good. That is the path we are prepared to discuss.

But let me be absolutely clear:  
**That does not mean we accept closures if the package is merely “not deep enough” by management’s preference.** It means management must work within the bounds of what is negotiable.

The workforce should not be forced to pay for strategic mistakes with plant shutdowns.

## What a serious no-closure package must include
Since everyone insists on specifics, here are the conditions under which we would even continue discussing a settlement:

### Non-negotiable
- Every German plant remains open.
- No compulsory redundancies.
- Co-determination remains fully effective.
- Historic job-security commitments are preserved.

### Open to discussion
- Working-time adjustments.
- Internal redeployment across German sites.
- Qualification and reskilling.
- Temporary moderation of bonuses or special payments.
- Productivity measures.
- Voluntary exit options, not forced ones.

### What must be in return
- Binding site and job guarantees through 2030.
- A written management contribution.
- A real owner-side contribution.
- Investment commitments for future products and technologies at German sites.

That is a fair bargain. It is also the only acceptable one.

## What we are not accepting
Let me say this as plainly as possible:  
We are **not** accepting a package where:
- management keeps all strategic freedom,
- owners keep all options open,
- investors get their margin repair,
- and the workforce loses plants, jobs, and guarantees.

That is not restructuring. That is social dumping dressed up as strategy.
