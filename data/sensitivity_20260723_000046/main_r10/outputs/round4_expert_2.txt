Below is a neutral, industrial-planning comparison of the three restructuring paths, using **illustrative but bounded estimates** because no site-level data have been provided. I am focusing on **capacity, fixed-cost removal, headcount, costs, timing, and execution risk**.

## Executive view

- A **closure-based** plan is the fastest way to remove fixed cost and overcapacity, but it carries the highest conflict and implementation risk.
- A **no-closure / capacity-consolidation** plan can be credible, but only if it is **deep**: shift cuts, line consolidation, indirect-labor reduction, voluntary exits, and labor-cost moderation together. On most large automotive networks, such a package usually delivers **about 70–85%** of the recurring savings of a closure-led plan, not 100%.
- A **phased compromise** is often the most implementable in a co-determined German setting, but it needs hard milestones and verification; otherwise it simply delays the same dispute.

# 1) Illustrative scenario comparison

Assumptions:
- Large German passenger-car network with several plants.
- Current domestic overcapacity is material.
- Target is a durable cost reset over 3–5 years.
- Numbers below are **ranges**, not facts.

### A. Closure-based restructuring

| Metric | Indicative range |
|---|---:|
| Annual recurring savings | **€1.0–1.6 bn** |
| One-time restructuring cost | **€1.5–3.5 bn** |
| Capacity reduction | **15–25%** of German output capacity |
| Headcount impact | **8,000–15,000** jobs |
| Time to capture major savings | **18–36 months** |
| Required capex | **€0.3–0.8 bn** mainly for transfer, tooling, ramp-up elsewhere |
| Execution risk | **High** |

**What drives the savings**
- Removal of plant-specific fixed costs
- Elimination of some indirect labor and overhead
- Fewer shifts and less duplicated infrastructure
- Potentially stronger network specialization

**Main drawbacks**
- High strike risk
- Political and governance resistance
- Supply-chain disruption
- Large one-time charges
- Risk of delay eroding the economic case

---

### B. No-closure / capacity-consolidation restructuring

This means no plant shutdowns, but meaningful reductions in shifts, lines, indirect labor, and internal duplication.

| Metric | Indicative range |
|---|---:|
| Annual recurring savings | **€0.7–1.3 bn** |
| One-time restructuring cost | **€0.8–2.0 bn** |
| Capacity reduction | **10–18%** of German output capacity |
| Headcount impact | **4,000–10,000** jobs, mostly attrition/voluntary/redeployment |
| Time to capture major savings | **24–48 months** |
| Required capex | **€0.8–2.0 bn** |
| Execution risk | **Medium** |

**What drives the savings**
- Shift removal and line consolidation
- Lower indirect labor
- Less overtime and lower utilization of excess space
- Voluntary exits, early retirement, redeployment
- Productivity measures and some labor-cost moderation

**Important caveat**
A no-closure plan usually does **not** remove as much fixed cost as a closure plan unless it is very deep. If it only trims shifts or uses temporary labor concessions, savings are usually too small.

**Why capex can be higher**
To keep all sites open while reducing cost, companies often need:
- new tooling and re-layout
- automation
- internal logistics redesign
- product transfers between halls or plants
- digital planning and quality systems

That is why the no-closure route can require more up-front investment even while preserving employment better.

---

### C. Phased compromise with milestone review

This is a hybrid:
- phase 1: no closures, immediate savings package
- phase 2: review after verified milestones
- possible stronger measures if targets missed

| Metric | Indicative range |
|---|---:|
| Annual recurring savings | **€0.8–1.4 bn** if targets met |
| One-time restructuring cost | **€1.0–2.5 bn** |
| Capacity reduction | **10–20%** |
| Headcount impact | **5,000–12,000** jobs |
| Time to capture major savings | **24–42 months** |
| Required capex | **€0.8–1.8 bn** |
| Execution risk | **Medium–High** unless milestones are very clear |

**Strength**
- Best chance of being negotiated and implemented without immediate rupture

**Weakness**
- If triggers are vague, the process becomes a defer-and-reopen mechanism
- If triggers are strict, it can still collapse into a closure conflict later

# 2) Site-by-site logic: what usually happens in a German auto network

Without actual site data, the normal restructuring pattern is:

## Plant types and likely cost role

### 1. High-volume final assembly site
- Biggest fixed-cost leverage
- A shift cut can save substantial labor and overhead
- But closing a final-assembly site is politically hardest

### 2. Powertrain / component site
- Often easier to consolidate than final assembly
- Savings can come from product transfer or line removal
- Higher risk if EV transition leaves legacy ICE capacity underused

### 3. Body shop / paint / logistics-heavy site
- Large overhead burden
- Capacity consolidation can create meaningful savings
- Internal footprint reduction can be substantial even without closure

### 4. Small satellite or specialty plant
- Usually the weakest economics if utilization is low
- Often the first candidate for consolidation or closure in pure cost terms

---

# 3) What a no-closure package must include to be credible

A no-closure plan is only economically serious if it combines **at least four** of the following five levers:

1. **Shift elimination / operating hours reduction**
2. **Indirect-labor cut**
3. **Voluntary exits / early retirement / attrition**
4. **Product and line consolidation within the network**
5. **Temporary labor-cost moderation**

If it relies mainly on:
- wage moderation alone, or
- voluntary exits alone,

then it usually falls short of a structural fix.

---

# 4) Headcount effects: realistic ranges

For a large German carmaker, the following is a reasonable planning range:

- **Closure scenario:** 8,000–15,000 jobs eliminated, including direct and indirect roles
- **No-closure scenario:** 4,000–10,000 jobs reduced, with a much higher share handled through attrition, redeployment, and voluntary exits
- **Phased compromise:** 5,000–12,000 jobs over a longer period, depending on milestone outcomes

A key point: **headcount reduction is not the same as cost reduction**.  
You can cut 5,000 jobs and still not remove enough fixed cost if the network remains overbuilt in halls, shifts, logistics, and overhead.

---

# 5) Investment requirement

This is often underestimated in no-closure deals.

## Closure-led
Capex is often lower because the network is being simplified outright:
- tooling moves
- ramp-up at retained sites
- logistics adjustments

Indicative: **€0.3–0.8 bn**

## No-closure
Capex is often higher because you are trying to preserve sites while reconfiguring them:
- new product allocation
- automation
- internal logistics
- footprint redesign
- digitalization and quality systems

Indicative: **€0.8–2.0 bn**

That capex is not “extra” in a strategic sense; it is the cost of avoiding closure while still cutting capacity.

---

# 6) Implementation timing

## Closure scenario
- Announcement to legal/operational completion: often **18–36 months**
- Savings start earlier, but full impact may be delayed by industrial action and transfer logistics

## No-closure scenario
- Usually **24–48 months**
- Slower because redeployment, voluntary exits, and network consolidation take time

## Phased compromise
- First savings can come in **6–12 months**
- Full economic test occurs after **18–24 months**
- Risk is that unresolved triggers create a second conflict

---

# 7) Execution risk comparison

### Closure-based
Highest risk of:
- strikes
- political intervention
- reputational damage
- supply disruption
- delayed implementation

### No-closure
Risks are more operational:
- savings may be insufficient
- cost targets may slip
- excess capacity may linger
- investment may be needed before savings are realized

### Phased compromise
Main risk is governance:
- milestone definitions
- verification
- what happens if the target is missed
- whether “review” becomes a disguised closure threat

---

# 8) Practical conclusion

If I were comparing these strictly as industrial restructuring instruments:

- **Closure-led**: best for maximum fixed-cost removal, worst for conflict and disruption.
- **No-closure**: best for social stability and governance acceptability, but only works if the package is deep, quantified, and investment-backed.
- **Phased compromise**: often the most feasible politically, but only if milestones are hard and independently verified.

## My bounded estimate
If the closure scenario is the 100% benchmark for recurring savings, a serious no-closure package can often achieve about:

- **70–85% of closure-scenario savings**
- with **lower conflict risk**
- but usually requiring **more capex** and **more time**

If the company needs **full 100% savings**, no-closure is usually too weak unless the network has exceptionally large hidden overhead that can be removed without shutdowns.
