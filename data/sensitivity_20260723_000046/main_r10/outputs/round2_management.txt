I appreciate the expert clarification, because it confirms the central point: **a no-closure package may save money, but it is unlikely to eliminate a deep structural overcapacity problem unless it is very substantial, durable, and economically equivalent to closures.** That is exactly why I am not willing to accept a purely symbolic “jobs and sites” solution.

### To the Works Council
I hear your refusal of closures, compulsory redundancies, and job-security changes. I understand the political force of that position. But I need to be equally direct: **your red lines do not solve the cost problem**.

You keep asking us to produce a “quantified non-closure plan.” Fair enough — but the numbers have to close. A package of working-time changes, redeployment, qualification, attrition, and temporary concessions may reduce pain, but it does not automatically remove the structural cost base. If the result is that we preserve every site and every guarantee while only trimming around the edges, then we have not restructured the company; we have postponed the crisis.

What I can say clearly is this:
- I am open to **avoiding closures** if — and only if — the alternative delivers **real, durable, and material** savings.
- I am **not** open to a deal that locks in all plants and all jobs first and then asks management to figure out competitiveness later.

If you want to avoid closures, then you need to engage on the hard economic question, not just the political one: **what permanent cost and capacity reduction do you actually put on the table?**

### To the Trade Union
You are right that a negotiated solution is better than open conflict. You are also right that labour should not be the only side contributing. But I reject your framing that any meaningful labour-cost adjustment is somehow a breach of social partnership.

Social partnership cannot mean that one side carries the adjustment burden while the other side vetoes every structural change. If the company’s return-on-sales target is missed and the cost base is too high, then a material employee contribution is not optional; it is part of restoring viability.

I also do not accept the idea that job-security guarantees through 2030 can be declared first and then everything else negotiated around them. That sequence is backwards. **Guarantees are the outcome of a viable restructuring, not the precondition for one.**

So yes:
- working-time tools,
- temporary moderation,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- productivity measures,

are all part of the discussion.

But no:
- no forced redundancies forever,
- no closures ever,
- and no cost contribution from labour

is not a serious basis for a settlement.

### To the Regional State Government
I agree with you on one point: a destructive conflict would damage regional stability and the company’s governance. But I strongly disagree that your blocking power should be used to freeze necessary change.

You say plant closures should not be the default. I agree. But they must remain **available** if the alternative does not close the gap. You cannot ask management to restore competitiveness and simultaneously remove the only instruments that reliably reduce structural overcapacity.

The state should be pushing for:
- a credible restructuring,
- an investable future,
- and a deal that avoids unnecessary conflict,

not for a blank prohibition on the hardest decisions.

### To the Controlling Family
You say you want value preservation, not value destruction. On that, we agree. But the path to value preservation is not to preserve every legacy arrangement indefinitely.

Your caution about escalation is valid. A prolonged strike would be damaging. But the way to avoid that is not to rule out closures categorically; it is to get to a serious package fast enough that everyone understands the economic reality.

If the ownership side wants credibility, it should back a restructuring that achieves:
- lower fixed costs,
- lower excess capacity,
- and a stronger margin structure.

That may include closures if no other path is sufficient. I am not using closures as a political trophy; I am saying they remain on the table because **structural overcapacity has to be taken out somehow**.

### To Institutional Investors
Your position is the most economically disciplined, and I largely share the diagnosis. You are right that we cannot accept a cosmetic compromise. You are right that the savings must be economically real, not rhetorically real.

Where I differ from the employee side is this: investor discipline is not compatible with endless exemptions. If the company needs a durable return to competitiveness, then capacity must be reduced and labour costs must come down in a way the numbers can support.

That can happen through closures, or through an alternative package if — and only if — it truly delivers equivalent results.

### On the expert input
The expert is right that no-closure packages can work in some cases. But the key line is also right: **if the problem is deep structural overcapacity, no-closure packages usually need unusually large concessions to match a closure-based restructuring.**

That is the heart of the matter. I am not asking for theory. I am asking for an outcome:
- a structurally lower cost base,
- materially lower excess capacity,
- and a path back to target margins.

If a no-closure package can do that, show it.
If it cannot, then we must have the courage to act accordingly.

### My bottom line
Let me be explicit about where I stand:

**I agree**
- restructuring must be negotiated,
- the company must remain investable,
- and a prolonged conflict would be harmful.

**I disagree**
- with any blanket ban on closures,
- with any blanket ban on compulsory redundancies,
- and with any settlement that protects every site and every legacy guarantee regardless of economic reality.

**I will consider**
- a no-closure alternative only if it produces durable, quantified, economically equivalent savings,
- and I mean equivalent in margins, capacity, and cost base — not equivalent in political comfort.

So the next step is not another round of general declarations. The next step is a **hard package comparison**:
1. what savings and capacity cuts closures would deliver, and  
2. what exact concessions, timing, and guarantees a no-closure package would require to match them.

If the employee side cannot produce that, then they are asking me to accept a weaker restructuring and call it compromise. I will not do that.
