From the perspective of dispersed institutional investors, the key issue is straightforward: **this company needs a credible, durable return to competitive margins**. We are not interested in a cosmetic deal, and we are not interested in a prolonged conflict that destroys value while everyone claims victory.

### Where we agree
We agree with management, the family holding, and the state on the core diagnosis:
- the German cost base is too high,
- capacity is too much for the market,
- and the current margin structure is not good enough.

We also agree that:
- doing nothing is not an option,
- a negotiated solution is preferable to an open-ended confrontation,
- and the company needs room to keep investing in future products and technology.

### Where we disagree
We do **not** support a settlement that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive.

That means:
- we will not back a “no-closures, no-tradeoffs, no-real savings” outcome,
- we will not support a deal that merely postpones the problem,
- and we will not accept an arrangement where management, owners, and labour each claim constraint while margins continue to erode.

At the same time, we also do **not** see value in a strategy of maximum confrontation for its own sake. If plant closures and forced redundancies trigger a long strike, legal conflict, and operational disruption, that can destroy more value than the savings justify in the near term. So we are not endorsing management simply because it is tougher.

### What would move us
We would support whichever path credibly delivers:
- durable cost reduction,
- lower excess capacity,
- a realistic improvement in return on sales,
- and deal certainty.

That could be:
- plant closures, if they are genuinely necessary and executed in a way that restores competitiveness without a prolonged shutdown of the business,
- or an alternative package, if it delivers comparable savings through capacity reduction, labour-cost relief, flexibility, and productivity gains.

But the bar is high: **the alternative must be economically equivalent, not rhetorically equivalent**.

### Our limit
What would be fundamentally unacceptable to us is a settlement that:
- preserves every site and every legacy guarantee,
- avoids the hard choices,
- and leaves shareholders holding a structurally impaired business.

We are willing to be patient for a credible negotiation, but not for indefinite delay. If this process produces only symbolic concessions, we will view that as value-destructive and treat capital allocation accordingly.

### Bottom line
Our position is:
- **Yes** to a negotiated restructuring that restores competitiveness.
- **No** to a cosmetic compromise that protects optics but not margins.
- **No** to prolonged conflict that burns value.
- **Yes** to tough measures, if they are clearly tied to a durable and credible financial improvement.

Management needs to show the numbers. If the employee side wants to avoid closures, it must produce savings that are real enough to convince the market.
