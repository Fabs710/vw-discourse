I will be direct.

## My position
As the regional state government and anchor shareholder, I can support a **serious restructuring**. I cannot support a restructuring that **guts jobs in the state, closes plants as the default answer, or sidelines our co-determination and veto role**.

That remains the line.

Doing nothing is not an option. But neither is treating closures and forced redundancies as if they were the natural first choice. For a company that is a regional industrial anchor, that would be reckless economically and politically.

## Where I agree

### With management
You are right that the company needs durable savings, lower excess capacity, and a credible path back to competitiveness. I do not dispute the diagnosis that the cost base is too high.

You are also right that a cosmetic compromise would be irresponsible. If a deal only postpones the problem, it helps no one.

### With the works council and union
You are right that this cannot be imposed as a one-sided cut. If workers are expected to contribute, then the package must be negotiated, balanced, and tied to real guarantees.

You are also right that a destructive conflict would damage the company, the workforce, and the region.

### With the family holding and investors
You are right that a weak deal is not acceptable. The company must remain investable and competitive. Any package that leaves the structural problem untouched would be irresponsible.

## Where I disagree

### To management
I disagree with your continued insistence that closures remain a normal fallback option.

I do **not** accept that plant closures in Germany should stay on the table as if they were just another efficiency lever. For us, closures are not a neutral technical adjustment. They are a severe regional-economic shock, and they would have lasting consequences for:
- employment,
- supplier networks,
- skills,
- trust,
- and the company’s role in the region.

If you want the state to support restructuring, then you must first show that you have exhausted serious non-closure alternatives, not simply declare them insufficient because they are harder to execute.

### To the works council and union
I also cannot accept the absolute framing that treats every meaningful capacity adjustment as illegitimate because it is painful.

If the company has real structural overcapacity, then capacity must come out somewhere. If the employee side refuses to discuss any substantial adjustment beyond very limited measures, then we will not have a solution; we will have a stalemate.

So I am not endorsing rigidity on either side.

### To the family holding and investors
You both keep saying that closures may be necessary if the numbers demand it. I understand the logic, but I will be blunt: that is not a governance answer, it is a threat.

If you want stability, then do not keep closure pressure hanging over the process from the start. That erodes trust and makes a negotiated solution harder, not easier.

## What I can support
I can support a package that has all of the following:

1. **No plant closures as the preferred path**
   - Capacity reduction can be discussed.
   - Site consolidation inside the network can be discussed.
   - But closure should not be the first instrument.

2. **No compulsory redundancies as the primary mechanism**
   - Headcount adjustment should, as far as possible, come through attrition, redeployment, early retirement, qualification, and voluntary instruments.

3. **A real, quantified no-closure restructuring**
   - It must show credible savings, not slogans.
   - It must show where capacity is reduced.
   - It must show how the job path looks over time.

4. **Binding guarantees**
   - If workers contribute, they need protections in return.
   - That includes site protection, job security, and a meaningful role for co-determination.

5. **Shared burden**
   - Labour contributes.
   - Management contributes.
   - Owners contribute.
   - The state is prepared to facilitate, but not to subsidize a one-sided solution.

## What I will not support
I will not support:
- plant closures in Germany as the default restructuring tool,
- compulsory redundancies as the starting point,
- or a deal that weakens the state’s co-determination and veto role.

Those are not bargaining chips for us.

## On the expert assessments
The experts have been helpful. They confirm something important: **a no-closure package can be economically meaningful** if it is deep enough and properly designed.

That matters because it means closures are not the only serious option. But the experts also confirm that a no-closure package usually needs:
- shift reductions,
- redeployment,
- attrition and voluntary exits,
- productivity gains,
- and temporary labour-cost relief.

That is the route I want tested first, because it best protects regional employment and industrial capability.

## On the moderator’s phased compromise
I am cautious here.

A “phased no-closure restructuring with trigger-based review” sounds tidy, but it becomes dangerous if the trigger is just a backdoor to closures later. The workforce will not accept a process where:
1. concessions are made now,
2. closures remain in reserve,
3. and later the same threat is simply reintroduced.

So if there is to be a phased solution, it must include:
- a binding initial no-closure commitment,
- a clear savings target,
- a defined review mechanism,
- and no automatic reopening of closure demands absent truly new facts.

Otherwise, it is not a compromise; it is delayed pressure.

## What I need next
We are now past general positions. I want the side-by-side comparison everyone has asked for:

- closure-based scenario,
- no-closure scenario,
- site by site,
- with:
  - annual savings,
  - one-time costs,
  - capacity reduction,
  - headcount impact,
  - investment requirements,
  - and the guarantees attached.

Without that, the discussion is not serious enough.

## My bottom line
I am open to a negotiated restructuring. I am **not** open to deindustrialisation disguised as restructuring.

If the no-closure path can deliver durable savings while protecting the state’s industrial base, I will support it. If management insists on closures before proving that alternative solutions fail, then I will oppose that course and use the rights attached to our stake and governance position.
