## My position
As controlling shareholder, my test is simple: **does the package restore competitiveness and margins without triggering value-destructive instability or weakening governance control?**

Doing nothing is not acceptable. A weak compromise that preserves structural uncompetitiveness is also not acceptable.

So I agree with management, investors, and the state on the need for a **real restructuring**. I also agree with the works council and union that a purely imposed solution would be reckless and likely destroy value through conflict.

But I do **not** accept the employee side’s blanket red lines as the starting answer. A no-closure solution is only supportable if it is **economically equivalent in substance** — not symbolically “better,” but materially capable of closing the cost gap.

## Where I agree

### With management
You are right that the company needs **durable** savings and a lower structural cost base. I do not dispute that.

You are also right that the alternative package must be quantified and must show real fixed-cost and capacity reduction, not just temporary labor relief.

### With investors
You are right to insist on durability, execution certainty, and margin repair. A cosmetic settlement would only postpone the problem and depress value further.

### With the state government
You are right that a prolonged conflict would damage the company, the region, and the industrial base. I share the concern that avoidable escalation would be value-destructive.

### With the works council and union
You are right that the company cannot simply impose restructuring and expect peace. A negotiated path is preferable, and labor cannot be the only party contributing.

## Where I disagree

### To the works council
Your position is still too absolute.

You say:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- preserve historic guarantees,
- preserve co-determination.

That is a labor position, not yet a restructuring solution.

The key issue remains: **how do you remove enough fixed cost and overcapacity?**  
Temporary moderation, redeployment, and voluntary exits may help, but by themselves they may not be enough.

You keep asking management to prove closures are unavoidable. Fair enough. But the burden cuts both ways: you must also prove that your no-closure route can restore competitiveness by 2030, site by site, with quantified savings and a credible headcount path.

### To the union
I agree with no forced redundancies as a principle, but I do not accept the implication that a no-closure outcome is automatically the right answer.

If the company’s structural overcapacity is real, then the package must remove real capacity and real cost. If your alternative does not do that, then it is not a solution — it is a delay.

### To management
I am not giving you a blank cheque for closures.

A closure-led strategy can easily become value-destructive if it:
- hardens resistance,
- triggers strikes,
- delays implementation,
- damages the brand and supply chain,
- and burns more value than it saves.

So closures are not a default bargaining weapon. They are only acceptable if the economics truly require them and the no-closure alternative fails under scrutiny.

### To the state government
I respect your role and your concern for the regional base, but I do not accept any reading of the blocking position as a mechanism to freeze necessary restructuring.

If the state wants no closures, it should support a **credible alternative** that actually removes the cost burden. Concern alone is not a restructuring plan.

## On the phased compromise idea
I am cautious.

A phased no-closure arrangement can be sensible **only if** it is structured around measurable performance and not used as a backdoor to endless delay or repeated bargaining rounds.

I would not support a phase-one deal that:
1. gives concessions immediately,
2. leaves the structural problem unresolved,
3. and then reopens the same closure fight six months later.

If there is a phase-based approach, it must include:
- verified savings milestones,
- a clear capacity-reduction path,
- and enforceable governance discipline.

Otherwise, it is not a compromise; it is postponement.

## What would move me
A no-closure package could move me only if it contains all of the following:

1. **Quantified permanent savings**
2. **Real capacity reduction**
3. **A credible headcount path**
4. **Material labor-cost contribution**
5. **Management and owner contribution**
6. **A margin trajectory that restores investment capacity to 2030**

That is the test. Not rhetoric, not slogans, not trust alone.

## What I will not support
I will **not** support:
- a settlement that preserves every legacy structure while leaving the company uncompetitive,
- a deal that buys short-term peace at the expense of long-term viability,
- or a package that turns “shared sacrifice” into mostly symbolic labor moderation while the burden on the company remains essentially unchanged.
