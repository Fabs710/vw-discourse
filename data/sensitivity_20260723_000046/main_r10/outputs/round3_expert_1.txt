From an industrial-relations and collective-bargaining perspective, the debate has now converged on the right question: **can a negotiated no-closure package deliver enough permanent savings and capacity reduction to be economically credible, given the company’s structural overcapacity?**

## What is typical in a case like this
In large German manufacturing firms, especially auto, the bargaining outcome usually depends on four institutional constraints:

1. **Co-determination and supervisory-board blocking positions**  
   Plant closures and deep restructuring are difficult to impose if employee representatives and other blocking shareholders oppose them.

2. **Collective bargaining peace and strike leverage**  
   Once the peace obligation ends, the union can raise the cost of delay through industrial action, which makes “hard” restructuring more expensive in practice.

3. **Management’s need for durable cost relief**  
   If the company has persistent overcapacity and an above-target cost base, management will usually insist that temporary concessions alone are insufficient.

4. **The employee side’s insistence on employment and site security**  
   Works councils and unions typically seek to exchange labor-side flexibility or cost moderation for binding guarantees.

## The core economic trade-off
A closure-based route usually gives:
- faster and larger fixed-cost removal,
- clearer capacity reduction,
- but higher one-time costs,
- greater conflict risk,
- and higher political/reputational damage.

A no-closure route usually gives:
- lower conflict and better labor relations,
- preservation of sites and local capabilities,
- but it must be much deeper to offset overcapacity:
  - shift reductions,
  - redeployment,
  - voluntary exits and attrition,
  - working-time changes,
  - temporary wage moderation,
  - productivity gains,
  - and possibly production reallocation within the network.

If the no-closure package does **not** produce durable reductions in fixed costs and headcount, it is typically viewed by management and investors as a delay, not a solution.

## What would make a no-closure package credible
In bargaining terms, it would normally need:

- **quantified annual savings** large enough to materially improve margins,
- **permanent capacity reduction**, not only temporary underuse,
- **a credible headcount path** through attrition, voluntary exits, and redeployment,
- **limited and time-bound labor-cost concessions**,
- **investment commitments** for future products and technologies,
- and **binding site/job guarantees** for the period of labor sacrifice.

## Likely bargaining dynamics
Given the blocking positions you describe, the likely outcome is not an all-or-nothing decision, but a **package deal**:
- some labor-side contribution,
- some management/owner-side contribution,
- a reduction in capacity short of full closures if that can be made credible,
- and guarantees in return.

However, if the site-by-site numbers show that a no-closure package cannot close the cost gap, then management will continue to press for closures or equivalent hard measures. The employee side will then try to raise the conflict cost high enough that a negotiated alternative becomes preferable.

## Bottom line
The decisive question is not whether closures are “better” in the abstract. In German automotive restructuring, the decisive question is:

**Can a no-closure package remove enough fixed cost, capacity, and labor expense to restore competitiveness without triggering a deeper conflict that destroys the value it is meant to save?**
