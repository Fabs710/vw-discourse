I appreciate the clear positions from management, the works council, the union, and the state.

Let me be equally clear from the ownership side: **we support a restructuring that restores competitiveness and margins, but we do not support a value-destructive confrontation or a solution that permanently weakens the company’s industrial base.**

### Where I agree
- **Doing nothing is not an option.** The cost base and capacity problem cannot be wished away.
- **A negotiated solution is preferable to escalation.** A prolonged strike or governance crisis would destroy value for everyone.
- **A durable path back to competitiveness matters more than optics.** We need something that actually improves returns and preserves investment capacity.

### Where I disagree
- I do **not** accept a solution that simply preserves every site, every job, and every legacy guarantee unchanged while expecting shareholders to absorb the downside.
- I also do **not** support a “closures first, bargaining later” approach if that means triggering a prolonged conflict, reputational damage, and avoidable destruction of know-how before alternatives are seriously tested.

The ownership position is therefore not a blank cheque for management, and it is not a veto on change either. What we need is a **credible, durable restructuring package** that meets three conditions:

1. **Real economic effect**
   - The package must materially reduce cost and improve productivity.
   - Symbolic concessions will not be enough.

2. **Stability and labour peace**
   - Any path that leads to open-ended industrial conflict is value-destructive.
   - We need a settlement that can actually hold.

3. **Protection of long-term enterprise value**
   - No outcome should permanently impair the company’s industrial capability or governance stability.
   - We will not support measures that solve one quarter and weaken the enterprise for years.

### On the current positions
To the works council and union: I hear the red lines on compulsory redundancies and plant closures. I understand why you are drawing them. But from a stewardship perspective, if those red lines make it impossible to achieve sufficient savings, then we have a problem that must be solved with a serious alternative, not with slogans.

To management: I share the concern about competitiveness, but I would caution against assuming that the only route is maximal confrontation. If you push too hard and too fast, you may achieve the opposite: delayed savings, litigation, strikes, and a damaged brand. That is not good governance.

To the state: I agree that regional stability and co-determination matter. But they cannot become a permanent shield against necessary adaptation. The company must remain viable in a brutally competitive market.

### What I would regard as a credible path
I am open to a package built around:
- capacity reductions,
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment and qualification,
- targeted labour-cost relief,
- and, if needed, tightly governed site adjustments **only if no other economically credible solution exists**.

What I am **not** open to is a settlement that preserves everything in name while leaving the company structurally uncompetitive.

### Bottom line
The family holding wants a solution that is:
- **economically real,**
- **socially stable,**
- and **governance-respectful**.

If the employee side can produce a serious alternative that delivers comparable savings without plant closures, we will take it seriously. If management can only present a plan that invites a destructive stand-off, we will not endorse that either.

We are prepared to back a tough restructuring. We are **not** prepared to back value erosion disguised as compromise.
