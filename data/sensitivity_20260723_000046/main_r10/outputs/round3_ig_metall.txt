I will be equally direct.

## To the works council
I agree with your insistence that **doing nothing is not an option** and that a **cosmetic deal would be irresponsible**. I also agree that the discussion must now be quantified, not rhetorical.

But I do **not** accept the way you are trying to turn “no closures” into the only legitimate starting point while leaving the economic question unresolved. A no-closure outcome may be the right answer — but only if it is **real, durable, and substantial** enough to protect the company’s future. If it is not, then it is not a solution; it is a delay.

Where I stay firm is this:
- **No compulsory redundancies**.
- **No plant closures as the bargaining method**.
- **No dismantling of the collective wage framework**.

Those are not bargaining ornaments. They are core protections. If management wants a workforce contribution, it must come through a **fair, negotiated package**, not through job destruction.

## To management
You are right about one thing: the company needs **durable savings**, not symbolic optics. But your argument still has a serious flaw: you keep treating closures and layoffs as if they were the default proof of seriousness.

They are not.

The burden is on management to show that a **no-closure restructuring cannot close the gap**. The expert input has not shown that. It showed the opposite: a no-closure package can be meaningful if it combines:
- shift and capacity reduction,
- redeployment,
- voluntary exits,
- productivity measures,
- and temporary labor-cost relief.

That is what you should be testing honestly.

What I will not accept is a setup where:
- workers are told to give up pay and security,
- but management retains full strategic freedom,
- and owners/investors are shielded from meaningful contribution.

That is not shared sacrifice. That is one-sided adjustment.

## To the family holding
I hear your concern about value destruction and conflict risk, and I share it. A prolonged strike would damage the company.

But that is exactly why you should not keep closures as a shadow threat while asking the workforce to concede. That is how you manufacture the very conflict you say you want to avoid.

If you want stability, then support:
- a negotiated no-closure settlement,
- a real owner-side contribution,
- and binding job and site security.

Do not ask workers to trust a process that still has their plants on the chopping block.

## To the state government
Your position matters because co-determination and blocking rights are not symbolic here — they are real. If the state believes closures should not be the default, then it should use its position to force a serious alternative evaluation, not just repeat concern.

I agree that regional stability is vital. But stability is not preserved by making workers carry the risk alone. If there is a restructuring, it must be structured so the burden is shared fairly.

## To institutional investors
I understand your insistence on quantified savings and durability. That is legitimate.

But I reject the implication that closures are the only economically serious benchmark. The expert was clear that a no-closure package can still deliver a large share of the savings if it is designed properly. That means the real question is not “can you make pain disappear?” It is:
- Can we reduce capacity?
- Can we reduce cost?
- Can we protect jobs?
- Can we do it without destroying trust and the collective bargaining architecture?

That is the test.

## Our union line remains clear

### Non-negotiable
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No plant closures.**
- **No dismantling of the collective wage framework.**

### Open to trade
- temporary wage moderation,
- reduced bonuses/special payments,
- working-time flexibility,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and reskilling,
- voluntary exits,
- and a finite, negotiated employee contribution.

### Must be matched by others
- management contribution,
- owner-side contribution,
- investment commitments,
- and binding site/job guarantees.

## What I can support
If management brings a package that:
1. quantifies the savings,
2. shows how capacity is reduced without closures,
3. limits job losses to voluntary and internal measures,
4. shares the burden across management, owners, and labor,
5. and protects the collective wage framework,

then I am prepared to bargain seriously.

## What I cannot support
I will not support a deal that:
- uses “competitiveness” as a pretext for forced layoffs,
- threatens closures to extract unilateral concessions,
- or asks workers to pay for restructuring while others stay insulated.

I remain open to a **shared-sacrifice, no-closure settlement**. But it must be real:
- no forced layoffs,
- no closures,
- fair employee contribution,
- and real contributions from management and owners.

If the numbers show that this can close the gap, good — we have a path.

If management insists on closures before proving the alternative fails, then it is not negotiating in good faith, and the workforce will respond accordingly.
