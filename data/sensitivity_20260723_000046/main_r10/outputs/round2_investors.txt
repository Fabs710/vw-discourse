I appreciate the expert assessments, because they sharpen the real issue: **the company needs durable savings, not slogans**. From our side, that remains the only test that matters.

## Where we agree

### With management
You are right that a **cosmetic compromise is unacceptable**. If the cost base remains structurally too high, margins will keep eroding and the equity story deteriorates further. We agree that the company needs:
- material cost reduction,
- lower excess capacity,
- and a credible path back to return-on-sales targets.

### With the family holding
You are right that we should avoid a **value-destructive confrontation**. A prolonged strike or governance crisis would burn cash, damage the brand, and delay the very savings everyone says they want.

### With the state and employee side, partially
The expert confirms that **a no-closure package can be real**, but only if it includes actual capacity reduction, headcount decline, and labor-cost relief. So yes: closures are not the only conceivable route.

## Where we disagree

### To the works council and union
Your blanket “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no job-security changes” line is **not enough** unless you can show the numbers. We do not invest in principles; we invest in returns.

If your position is:
- every German plant stays open,
- every legacy guarantee stays intact,
- labor contributes only marginally,

then that is not a restructuring. It is postponement. And we will not support a settlement that leaves the company **structurally uncompetitive**.

That said, we also do **not** support management turning closures into a negotiating weapon if the alternative package is genuinely capable of delivering comparable savings with less disruption.

## Our position on the alternatives

### 1) Closure-based restructuring
We accept that closures can deliver the most decisive fixed-cost reduction. If that is the only way to restore competitiveness, we would not oppose it in principle.

But it comes with a warning:
- if it triggers a long conflict, the near-term destruction may outweigh the savings,
- and the market will punish delay, uncertainty, and operational disruption.

### 2) No-closure alternative
We are open to it, **but only if it is economically equivalent in substance**.

From the expert input, that means it must include:
- real capacity reduction,
- shift and working-time changes,
- headcount reduction through attrition/voluntary exits/redeployment,
- temporary labor-cost relief,
- productivity gains,
- and a credible implementation timetable.

If the package merely softens pain while preserving the fixed-cost base, we will view it as value-destructive.

## What would move us

We need a package with:
1. **Quantified annual savings**
2. **A durable capacity reduction**
3. **A credible headcount path**
4. **Deal certainty**
5. **No open-ended conflict**

A no-closure solution can work if it is **hard enough**, not because it is politically comfortable.

## What we will not support

We will not support:
- a settlement that keeps all sites open, preserves all guarantees, and pretends the margin problem has been solved,
- nor a closure strategy that destroys value through avoidable escalation and delay.

## Bottom line

Our position is simple:

- **Yes** to a restructuring that credibly restores competitiveness and margins.
- **No** to a cosmetic compromise.
- **No** to prolonged conflict.
- **Yes** to either closures or a no-closure package, **if and only if** it delivers real, durable, quantified savings.

So the next step is not more declarations. It is a **fully quantified comparison**:
- what closures would save,
- what the no-closure package would save,
- how quickly,
- and with what employment and execution risks.

If the employee side can produce a no-closure package that truly closes the cost gap, we will support it. If not, then tougher measures become necessary.
