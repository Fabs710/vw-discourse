Below is a neutral industrial-planning assessment of the **specific question**: whether a **no-closure restructuring** can realistically match a **closure-based restructuring** in a large German automotive network, and what the quantified trade-offs usually look like.

## 1) Executive assessment

**Short answer:** a no-closure package can often recover a **material share** of the savings, but it usually **does not fully replicate** the permanent fixed-cost removal of a plant closure unless the underlying problem is only moderate overcapacity or the no-closure package is unusually deep and long-lasting.

In large automotive networks, closures matter because they remove:
- site fixed overhead,
- indirect labor,
- utilities and maintenance,
- logistics complexity,
- duplicate support functions,
- and future capex burden.

A no-closure package can reduce:
- direct labor cost,
- overtime,
- agency labor,
- shift premiums,
- headcount through attrition,
- and some overhead via consolidation.

But it usually leaves a **residual fixed-cost base** at each site, so its savings are often **temporary or partial** unless paired with:
- strong volume reallocation,
- major working-time changes,
- headcount reduction by attrition/voluntary exits,
- and a multi-year wage/bonus concession.

## 2) Practical comparison: closure-based vs no-closure

Because I do not have the company’s actual network model, I will state this as a **scenario framework** with illustrative ranges typical for a German passenger-car network.

### A. Closure-based restructuring
A full closure of one medium-sized vehicle or component plant usually delivers:
- **direct annual cash savings:** roughly **€250m to €500m per site** after ramp-down, depending on site size and what remains in the network,
- **one-time restructuring cost:** often **€300m to €1bn+** per site, including severance, transfer costs, decommissioning, and write-downs,
- **permanent headcount effect:** often **1,500 to 5,000 jobs** per site directly, with additional supplier and local indirect effects outside the company.

A closure is economically powerful because it removes not only labor but also **structural fixed cost**.

### B. No-closure alternative
A negotiated alternative without closures can realistically deliver:
- **annual savings:** often **€100m to €300m per major site-equivalent intervention**, sometimes more if the network is very labor-heavy and underutilized,
- **headcount reduction:** mainly through attrition, redeployment, early retirement, and voluntary exits; commonly **5% to 15% of domestic workforce over several years** in a large network, occasionally more with aggressive packages,
- **implementation cost:** lower than closures in headline terms, but still substantial if financed through incentives, training, and transfer structures.

However, the no-closure package often leaves:
- the plant’s fixed operating skeleton intact,
- excess logistics and support complexity,
- and a slower path to the same margin target.

## 3) Site-by-site logic: what can be reduced without closure

For a German automotive network, the economically relevant question is not “closures or no closures” in the abstract, but **where volume, people, and fixed costs sit**.

A site-by-site planning lens typically asks:

### Site type 1: final assembly / vehicle plant
Typical levers without closure:
- remove a shift,
- reduce line speed,
- reduce model complexity,
- move volume to another plant,
- cut overtime and agency labor,
- reduce indirect headcount.

Typical savings:
- **€50m to €150m/year** if one shift is removed or utilization falls materially,
- more if the site has large indirect structures.

### Site type 2: powertrain / component plant
Typical levers:
- consolidate product families,
- move work to another internal site,
- convert to EV-related components,
- reduce internal logistics and tooling duplication.

Typical savings:
- **€30m to €120m/year** per plant-equivalent intervention.

### Site type 3: engineering / support / administration
Typical levers:
- centralize functions,
- reduce duplicated overhead,
- shared-service migration,
- headcount freezes and attrition.

Typical savings:
- **€20m to €80m/year** per major function cluster, depending on scale.

These figures are not company-specific; they are planning ranges. The actual number depends heavily on:
- current utilization,
- labor intensity,
- local wage structure,
- existing fixed assets,
- and whether volume can be reallocated.

## 4) What a credible no-closure package would need to include

To be economically credible, a no-closure package must be more than wage moderation. It usually needs **all** of the following:

### 1. Capacity reduction without site closure
- removal of one shift at selected plants,
- lower output targets,
- simplified model mix,
- and transfer of some volume to higher-utilization sites.

### 2. Headcount reduction without forced redundancies
- hiring freeze,
- non-replacement of retirees,
- voluntary severance,
- early retirement bridges,
- internal redeployment to growth areas,
- qualification and retraining.

### 3. Temporary labor-cost relief
Possible items:
- deferred wage increases,
- reduced bonuses/special payments,
- time-limited working-time concessions,
- flexible hours with lower premium pay.

Typical magnitude:
- **3% to 8% of domestic payroll cost** for a time-limited concession package,
- occasionally higher if it includes structural shift-pattern changes.

### 4. Management and ownership-side contribution
If the workforce is asked to contribute, the package usually needs visible burden-sharing:
- management bonus restraint,
- overhead reduction,
- investment commitment,
- and a credible product pipeline for the sites.

### 5. Binding implementation rules
- no forced redundancies,
- site guarantees,
- defined end date,
- and measurable triggers for review if demand worsens.

Without these elements, a no-closure plan is usually too weak to be considered a true substitute for closures.

## 5) Can no-closure savings equal closure savings?

### My assessment:
- **If overcapacity is moderate:** yes, possibly.
- **If overcapacity is structural and broad-based:** usually not fully.
- **If the network has several underutilized sites:** no-closure can buy time, but not usually the full permanent cost reduction.

Why? Because a plant closure removes a **whole fixed-cost block**. A no-closure plan reduces the **variable and semi-fixed** part of cost much more effectively than the hardest fixed part.

In practice:
- a no-closure package may achieve **40% to 80% of closure-equivalent annual savings**,
- but often at the price of **longer duration**, **more complexity**, and **greater dependence on continued labor cooperation**.

## 6) Employment effect comparison

### Closure-based
- Direct job losses are larger and more immediate.
- Reskilling and transfer demands increase sharply.
- The company can lower headcount faster and with more certainty.
- Social and political disruption is significantly higher.

### No-closure
- Fewer immediate layoffs.
- Employment declines more gradually.
- Better preservation of site capability and local know-how.
- But savings accumulate more slowly and depend on sustained bargaining discipline.

A typical no-closure headcount path in a large German automotive group might be:
- **Year 1–2:** hiring freeze, no replacement of leavers, stop agency labor
- **Year 2–4:** voluntary exits, early retirement, internal transfers
- **Year 4–6:** residual workforce reduction via productivity gains and mix changes

This can reduce domestic employment materially, but usually not as fast as a closure.

## 7) Capex and opex implications

### Closure-based
- **Capex** may be lower at the closed site, but the network often needs capex elsewhere to absorb volume.
- **Opex** drops more decisively.
- There is a one-time restructuring bill.

### No-closure
- Often requires **more operational capex**:
  - line reconfiguration,
  - product/tooling changes,
  - digitalization,
  - logistics optimization,
  - battery or EV-component conversion if relevant.
- Planning range for network adaptation capex: often **€100m to €500m+** depending on whether volumes are reallocated and whether plants are converted.
- Opex savings are slower and sometimes partially offset by complexity.

So a no-closure solution is not “free”; it often substitutes **capex and complexity management** for **closure and write-off**.

## 8) Transition timing and risk

### Closure-based timeline
- announcement to shutdown: often **12 to 36 months** depending on labor/legal/investment complexity,
- high strike and disruption risk,
- but once executed, the savings are durable.

### No-closure timeline
- negotiation and implementation can start faster,
- but full savings often take **3 to 6 years** to materialize,
- and the outcome is more exposed to:
  - labor drift,
  - demand changes,
  - and incomplete delivery on productivity assumptions.

## 10) Bottom line

### Industrial-planning conclusion
- **Closures** are the most decisive way to remove structural fixed cost and overcapacity.
- A **no-closure package** can still be economically serious, but it usually needs:
  - shift reduction,
  - headcount reduction through attrition/voluntary exits,
  - labor-cost moderation,
  - productivity improvement,
  - and capex-funded network reconfiguration.
- In many real automotive networks, the no-closure option is **credible as a bridge or partial substitute**, but **not always a full economic equivalent** to closure unless the overcapacity problem is limited or management accepts a slower, more negotiated adjustment.
