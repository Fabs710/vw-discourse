I will be just as direct.

## To the works council
I agree with you on the essentials:
- **doing nothing is not an option**,
- a **cosmetic deal is unacceptable**,
- and we need **real numbers**, not slogans.

I also agree that workers must not be the only ones asked to contribute. That is exactly why I have repeatedly argued for a **shared-sacrifice package**.

But I do **not** accept the way you keep turning “no closures” into an absolute frame without giving management and the owners a credible path to the required savings. If we present only red lines, we risk forcing the company into a confrontation that management will then use to justify harder measures.

Where I differ from you is this:
- I can support **no compulsory redundancies**.
- I can support a **serious no-closure settlement**.
- I cannot support an approach that treats every substantial capacity adjustment as illegitimate by definition.

The union line remains: **no forced layoffs, no plant closures as a bargaining method, protect the collective wage framework**. But that framework must still produce a credible restructuring result, not just a defensive posture.

## To management
You are right about one thing: the company needs **durable structural savings**. I do not dispute that.

What I reject is the implication that closures and layoffs are the default proof of seriousness. They are not. They are one route, and a destructive one if used prematurely.

You still have not answered the bargaining question properly:
- How much savings do you need, exactly?
- How much can come from working time, redeployment, voluntary exits, productivity, and labor-cost moderation?
- What part of the burden will management and owners take?

If you want the union to discuss deeper labor-side concessions, then bring a serious package that respects the workforce and the collective framework.

And let me be clear: **the company will not buy industrial peace by making workers carry all the pain**.

## To the family holding
I hear your concern about value destruction and conflict risk. On that, we agree: a prolonged strike or governance breakdown would damage the company.

But you cannot keep closures as a shadow threat and still expect trust. That is exactly what hardens the workforce and makes an agreement harder.

If you want stability, then back a settlement with:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- real owner-side contribution,
- and binding investment commitments.

If the family wants the workforce to show restraint, then the family must also show restraint and responsibility.

## To the state government
Your stance is closer to ours, and I welcome that. But support must become leverage.

The state should use its blocking position to force a genuine alternative evaluation. Not a ritual one — a real one. A plant-network view, site by site, showing how capacity and fixed costs are reduced without closures.

That is where the serious discussion belongs.

## To institutional investors
You are asking the right question — whether the package is durable. Fine. Then look at the full economics:
- strike risk,
- delay,
- reputational damage,
- supply-chain disruption,
- and long-term bargaining credibility.

Those are real costs. A closure-led approach may look stronger on a spreadsheet, but if it triggers a prolonged conflict, it can destroy more value than it creates.

So your benchmark must not be “closures at any cost.” Your benchmark must be: **what package restores competitiveness with the least destructive total cost?**

## On the moderator’s phased compromise idea
I support the idea of a **phased package** only if it is not a backdoor to closures later.

That means:
- no immediate closures,
- a binding initial no-closure settlement,
- clearly quantified savings targets,
- and a genuine shared-sacrifice structure.

What I will **not** accept is a process where workers concede first, while management keeps plant closure threats in reserve and reopens them later if the first stage is “not enough.”

That is not a settlement. That is a delay mechanism.

## What could move us
A serious union-backed solution would need:
1. **No compulsory redundancies**
2. **No plant closures**
3. **Temporary labor-cost relief, not permanent wage dismantling**
4. **Working-time and flexibility tools**
5. **Voluntary exits and internal redeployment**
6. **Management and owner contributions**
7. **Binding job and site guarantees**
8. **Investment commitments for future products at German sites**

That is a fair bargain. It is also the only path that can preserve industrial peace.

## What I cannot support
I will not support:
- a deal that uses “competitiveness” to force layoffs,
- a package where workers pay while others stay insulated,
- or a bargain that turns the collective wage framework into the sole adjustment variable.
