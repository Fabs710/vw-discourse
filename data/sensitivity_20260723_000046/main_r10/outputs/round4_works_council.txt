I will respond directly, because the situation is too serious for vague language.

## First: what I agree with
I agree with the moderator and several other parties on one fundamental point: **doing nothing is not an option**. The company needs restructuring, and it needs a real industrial answer, not slogans.

I also agree that a **purely cosmetic compromise would be irresponsible**. If the package does not materially improve competitiveness, it only postpones the next confrontation.

And I accept the need for **quantification**. Fine. Let’s quantify it. But do not confuse quantification with a pretext for closures.

## Where I strongly disagree
I disagree with management, investors, and the family holding when they act as if **plant closures remain a normal tool** just because they are convenient for balance-sheet arithmetic.

They are not “normal” to the workforce. They are a breach of trust, a destruction of industrial capability, and a direct attack on the co-determination and job-security framework that made this company stable.

So let me restate the works council position without ambiguity:

- **No plant closures in Germany.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No dismantling of the historic job-security framework.**
- **No weakening of co-determination.**

Those are not bargaining ornaments. They are red lines.

## To management
You say a no-closure package must be “economically equivalent in substance.” Fine — then prove the closure case is actually necessary.

What you have still not shown is that closures are unavoidable. The expert input did **not** prove that. It said a no-closure package can generate meaningful savings and can be credible if it is sufficiently deep and properly designed.

So your position is not simply “we need restructuring.” That part is undisputed.

Your real position is: **we want the option to close plants and cut jobs if management prefers that route.**  
That is what I reject.

If you claim the alternative cannot work, then bring the site-by-site proof:
- current utilization,
- fixed costs,
- indirect labor burden,
- savings from shift cuts,
- savings from redeployment and voluntary exits,
- and the exact gap versus closures.

Not abstract warnings. Numbers.

## To the union
I agree with you on the essentials:
- **no compulsory redundancies**,
- **no plant closures as a bargaining method**,
- **no dismantling of the collective framework**.

That is the minimum line of defense.

But I want to be equally clear: if the union accepts temporary labor contributions, they must remain **temporary, negotiated, and matched by binding guarantees**. The workforce cannot be asked for sacrifice while management keeps all strategic freedom and the owners keep closures in reserve.

So yes: temporary moderation, voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, productivity measures — those can be discussed. But only if the result is:
- every German plant remains open,
- no forced layoffs,
- and the framework of protection is preserved.

## To the state government
Your position is closer to ours, but it must be backed by action. Saying closures are “not preferred” is not enough if management still believes it can pressure the board toward them.

Use the blocking power. That is what it exists for.

The state should not become a spectator while a regional industrial base is dismantled. If capacity has to come out, then the first obligation is to design that reduction **without closures and without forced layoffs**.

## To the controlling family
You say you want stability and value preservation. Then stop treating closures as an acceptable fallback threat.

That shadow threat is exactly what destroys trust and makes a negotiated solution harder. The real value destroyer is not a workforce defending its sites. The real value destroyer is forcing a structural rupture and then expecting the workforce to finance it with jobs.

If you want long-term value, support:
- a binding no-closure settlement,
- real management and owner contributions,
- and an industrial plan that protects German sites.

## To institutional investors
You want “durable savings.” So do we. But you are making a dangerous simplification when you treat closures as the benchmark of seriousness.

The expert assessment was not one-sided:
- closures remove fixed costs faster,
- yes,
- but no-closure packages can still deliver a large share of the savings,
- and closures create major execution risk, strikes, reputational damage, and long-term trust destruction.

Those are not side effects. They are part of the cost.

So if you are serious about value, include:
- strike risk,
- delay,
- supply-chain disruption,
- governance instability,
- and the erosion of co-determination credibility.

## On the moderator’s phased compromise idea
I need to be blunt: a “phased no-closure restructuring with trigger-based review” sounds tidy, but it is dangerous if the trigger becomes a backdoor to closures later.

The workforce will not accept a process where:
1. we concede now,
2. management keeps closures in reserve,
3. and later declares the package insufficient and reopens the same threat.

That is not security. That is a delayed ultimatum.

So unless the first phase comes with **binding no-closure and no-forced-layoff guarantees through 2030**, I do not see this as an acceptable framework.

## What a serious no-closure package must include
If you want a real settlement, then the package must have these elements:

### Non-negotiable
- Every German plant remains open.
- No compulsory redundancies.
- Co-determination remains fully effective.
- Historic job-security guarantees are preserved.

### Open to discussion
- Working-time adjustments.
- Internal redeployment across German sites.
- Qualification and reskilling.
- Temporary moderation of bonuses or special payments.
- Productivity measures.
- Voluntary exit options, not forced ones.

### What must be in return
- Binding site and job guarantees through 2030.
- A written management contribution.
- A real owner-side contribution.
- Investment commitments for future products and technologies at German sites.

That is a fair bargain. And it is the only acceptable one.

## What we are not accepting
We are **not** accepting a package where:
- management keeps all strategic freedom,
- owners keep all options open,
- investors get margin repair,
- and the workforce loses plants, jobs, and guarantees.

That is not restructuring. That is social dumping dressed up as strategy.

## On the need for specialists
Yes, a specialist is still needed — but not a generic mediator. We need a **plant-network restructuring modeler** who can quantify, site by site:
- current utilization,
- fixed costs,
- savings from shift cuts and redeployment,
- savings from voluntary exits and attrition,
- and the real gap versus closure scenarios.

Without that, this is just a contest of pressure.

## Bottom line
My position has not changed:
- **No plant closures.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No dismantling of the job-security framework.**
- **No weakening of co-determination.**

I remain ready to discuss a **real no-closure restructuring** with binding guarantees and shared contributions. But if management, owners, or investors continue to treat closures as an acceptable threat before proving the alternative fails, then they are not negotiating in good faith.

Bring the numbers. Prove the necessity. Until then, the works council will defend the plants and the jobs.
