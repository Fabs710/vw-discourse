## My position

The family holding supports a **real restructuring**, not a cosmetic one. We do **not** support preserving the current cost base and calling that a solution. At the same time, we do **not** support a value-destructive escalation that weakens the enterprise for years through a prolonged strike, reputational damage, or a governance crisis.

So my answer is:

- **Yes** to a restructuring that materially restores competitiveness and margins.
- **No** to a settlement that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.
- **No** to a destructive confrontation that destroys value before any savings are realized.

## Where I agree

### With management
You are right that the company needs **durable** savings, not symbolism. The expert has confirmed the basic point: a no-closure package may reduce pain, but it often does **not** fully remove structural overcapacity unless it is unusually deep and sustained.

That matters. The family holding will not back a solution that simply delays the problem.

### With the works council, union, and state
You are also right that a forced, one-sided approach would be dangerous. A closure-first strategy can trigger exactly the kind of conflict that destroys value, know-how, and trust. We do not want that.

The expert’s assessment is useful here: **non-closure restructuring can be real** if it includes shift reductions, volume transfers, attrition, voluntary exits, productivity measures, and temporary labor-cost relief. So I do not dismiss the no-closure route.

## Where I disagree

### To the works council and union
Your blanket refusal of closures and compulsory redundancies is understandable politically, but I cannot treat it as a governing solution.

If your position means:
- every site stays open,
- every guarantee stays unchanged,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no meaningful labor-cost relief,

then that is **not** a restructuring. That is a refusal to confront the economics.

The expert was clear: a no-closure package can be credible, but only if it is **quantified**, **substantial**, and **durable**. So far, I have heard principles, not numbers.

### To management
I also do not accept a posture that treats closures as the first and only serious answer. The expert has made clear that closures are economically powerful, but they also carry major bargaining and conflict costs. If management pushes too hard, too early, it may destroy value faster than it saves it.

So management must not confuse leverage with strategy.

## What would move the family holding

We would support a package only if it satisfies all three of these conditions:

1. **Real savings**
   - Material reduction in cost and capacity.
   - Not symbolic concessions.
   - Not a short-lived patch.

2. **Stability**
   - No open-ended industrial conflict.
   - A settlement that can actually hold.

3. **Long-term value protection**
   - No permanent weakening of the industrial base.
   - No governance crisis.
   - No damage to ownership control or strategic flexibility.

## What I think should happen next

We now need a **proper side-by-side package comparison** with numbers, not slogans:

- What savings does a closure-based plan produce?
- What savings does a no-closure alternative produce?
- What is the headcount effect in each case?
- What one-time costs are required?
- What investment commitments are needed to make the no-closure route credible?
- What labor-cost contribution is temporary versus structural?

I would support using the expert model as the basis for a quantified negotiation. That is the right next step.

## Bottom line

The family holding is **not** choosing between “closures” and “no change.”  
We are choosing between:

- a **credible restructuring** that restores competitiveness, and
- a **weak compromise** that preserves headline peace but erodes value.

If the employee side can produce a no-closure package that is economically equivalent in real terms, I will take it seriously. If not, then the company will need harder measures. But I will not endorse a course that provokes unnecessary conflict, and I will not endorse a settlement that leaves the company structurally impaired.
