I’ll be very direct.

### To the works council
I agree with your core red lines:
- **no plant closures**,  
- **no compulsory redundancies**,  
- **no dismantling of job-security commitments**.

That is the minimum basis for any settlement that could hold. If management is asking workers to carry the restructuring, then the burden must be shared and the outcome must be secure. I also agree that “capacity optimization” cannot become a euphemism for shutting sites and throwing people out.

Where I want to sharpen the point is this: we need more than a political refusal. We need a **negotiated package** that can actually stabilize employment and costs. That means we should be ready to discuss:
- working-time reductions or adjustments,
- temporary wage or bonus moderation,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and reskilling,
- and a time-limited employee contribution.

But only in exchange for **binding guarantees** on jobs and sites. Without that, there is no deal.

### To management
I reject the way you are framing the choice as if closures and layoffs are the only serious tools left.

That is not acceptable as a bargaining premise.

You are right about one thing: the company needs a durable cost solution. But if you want the workforce to contribute, then you must stop treating labour as the default shock absorber. A credible restructuring cannot mean:
- management keeps strategic discretion,
- owners keep their shield,
- and workers pay through lost jobs, lost guarantees, and lower wages.

That is not “shared sacrifice.” That is one-sided adjustment.

If you want a serious discussion, put concrete numbers on the table:
- what exact savings are required,
- what exact capacity reduction is needed,
- what labour-cost contribution you expect,
- and what management and ownership will contribute themselves.

Until then, “we need deep restructuring” is a slogan, not a bargain.

### To the family holding
I appreciate that you do not want a destructive confrontation. But that only matters if you are willing to use your influence to stop management from pushing a closure-driven approach.

You say closures are not your preferred route unless unavoidable. Fine. Then make that explicit in governance terms. Workers will not accept a shadow threat of closures being held over them while they are asked to make concessions.

If the ownership side wants stability, it should support:
- a no-closure settlement,
- a substantial management contribution,
- and a fair employee contribution tied to employment guarantees.

### To the state government
Your position is closer to ours than management’s, but it has to be backed by action, not just concern. Co-determination and the blocking minority are real powers. If you believe plant closures are the wrong answer, then use those powers to force management back to the table with an alternative.

### To institutional investors
You keep saying “economically equivalent.” I understand the point. But I will be equally clear: **a deal that saves money by destroying trust, breaking the collective wage framework, and provoking industrial conflict is not economically equivalent**. It is a short-term accounting fix with long-term damage.

If you want durability, then support a solution that:
- protects the collective wage framework,
- avoids forced layoffs,
- preserves sites,
- and still delivers real savings through negotiated contribution and internal flexibility.

### Our union position
We remain open to a **balanced, negotiated restructuring**. But the conditions are clear:

**Non-negotiable**
- No compulsory redundancies.
- No plant closures.
- No dismantling of the collective wage framework.

**Open to trade**
- temporary labour-cost relief,
- working-time tools,
- reduced bonuses or special payments,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- and a finite employee contribution.

**What must be added**
- management contribution,
- owner-side commitment,
- and binding employment and site guarantees.

If management cannot accept that framework, then it should be honest: it is not negotiating a settlement, it is demanding surrender. In that case, the workforce has every reason to escalate.
