## Our position
As dispersed capital-market shareholders, we support **the restructuring that most credibly restores competitive margins, reduces the structural cost base, and can actually be implemented**.

We do **not** support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a settlement that preserves an uncompetitive German cost base,
- or a prolonged conflict that destroys value without fixing the economics.

That remains our line.

## Where I agree

### With management
You are right on the core diagnosis: the company needs **durable** savings, not a temporary patch. If the cost base stays structurally above peers, the market will continue to discount the business.

You are also right that a no-closure package must be **quantified and credible**, not just asserted. The burden is on the employee side to show how a no-closure route closes the gap.

### With the family holding
We agree that value destruction through a prolonged strike or governance paralysis would be harmful. Investors will not reward a process that burns cash and delays margin repair.

### With the state government, partly
We agree that avoidable industrial conflict is bad for the region and the company. A negotiated solution is preferable if it is economically real.

### With the union and works council, partly
We accept that industrial peace has value and that a negotiated route can preserve capabilities and reduce execution risk. We also accept that workers should not carry the burden alone.

## Where I disagree

### To the works council
Your insistence on:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and preservation of the historic job-security framework

is understandable politically, but **it is not sufficient economically** unless you can prove the alternative delivers equivalent permanent savings.

Right now, your position reads as:
- preserve every site,
- preserve most protections,
- use voluntary and internal measures,
- and assume competitiveness follows.

That is not a plan; that is a hope.

If the no-closure package can truly remove enough fixed cost and excess capacity, then fine — show it. But if it cannot, then refusing closures up front is simply asking shareholders to live with underperformance.

### To the union
We agree on no cosmetic deal. We do **not** agree that “no compulsory redundancies” automatically implies a viable restructuring.

A serious package must still produce:
- durable labor-cost relief,
- a credible headcount path,
- and real capacity reduction.

Temporary moderation alone will not solve a structural overcapacity problem.

### To management
We also do **not** give management a blank cheque for closures.

If closures are used as a threat without proving the no-closure path fails, that will likely harden resistance, increase strike risk, and delay value creation. The market will punish that too.

So management must answer the same question the employee side is being asked:
- what exact savings are required,
- what portion can be delivered without closures,
- and what is the execution risk of each option?

## Our assessment of the options

### 1) Closure-based restructuring
This is the more direct route to fixed-cost removal. From a shareholder perspective, that matters.

If the company has persistent overcapacity, closing or consolidating sites can be economically rational. It removes real overhead, not just wages.

But closures also bring:
- one-time restructuring costs,
- political backlash,
- industrial action risk,
- implementation delay,
- and reputational damage.

So we would support closures only if:
- the no-closure alternative is genuinely insufficient,
- the savings are material and durable,
- and the total value outcome is superior after taking conflict risk into account.

### 2) No-closure alternative
We are open to it, but only if it is **deep enough to be economically equivalent in substance**.

Based on the expert input already on the table, that would need:
- shift reductions,
- capacity consolidation,
- headcount reduction through attrition, voluntary exits, and redeployment,
- temporary labor-cost relief,
- productivity gains,
- and meaningful network reconfiguration.

If the package merely softens the pain while leaving fixed costs too high, we will not treat that as a serious answer.

### 3) Phased compromise
I am cautious.

A phase-one deal is only useful if it does **not** become a delayed reset button for the same conflict later. If the company gives concessions now and reopens the closure question in six or twelve months, then we have simply bought time, not solved the problem.

So any phased model must include:
- clear savings milestones,
- independent verification,
- and a credible consequence if the targets are missed.

Otherwise, it is not certainty; it is procrastination.
