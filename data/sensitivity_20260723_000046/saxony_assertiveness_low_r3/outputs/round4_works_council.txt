Let me be clear: the round-three numbers did **not** justify closures or compulsory redundancies. They justified what we have been saying all along — a **serious no-closure restructuring** with real savings, real capacity adjustment, and real burden-sharing, **without** sacrificing plants or the job-security framework.

## To management
You now have an industrial finance view saying a strong negotiated package can plausibly deliver **several hundred million euros in recurring savings** through attrition, voluntary exits, working-time changes, productivity measures, and labour-cost moderation. That is the key fact.

So your argument has narrowed to this: “Yes, a no-closure package works, but maybe not enough.”

Maybe not enough for what?  
For preserving every percentage point of short-term margin target while keeping the workforce as the only adjustment variable?

That is not a justification for closures. It is a demand that labour accept irreversible damage because management wants a more aggressive cost reset. I do not accept that.

If you can get a durable restructuring through:
- non-replacement,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- shift rationalization,
- limited employee contributions,
- and productivity improvements,

then that is the restructuring. End of story.

What you have **not** shown is why the company must cross the line to closures or forced redundancies before exhausting these tools. You have also not shown why the historic job-security framework should be broken when a negotiated path exists that can produce material savings and lower execution risk.

## To the controlling family and investors
You say you want value creation, not symbolism. Fine. Then listen to the actual governance reality.

A closure-based strategy in this company is not “cleaner.” It is **riskier**:
- it triggers the strongest legal and industrial resistance,
- it collides with co-determination,
- it raises strike risk,
- and it can destroy value through deadlock.

That is not theoretical. It is the practical consequence of trying to force through a closure war in a company where labour has real blocking power.

So when you say a no-closure deal might be “too weak,” I respond: a closure plan may be too destructive to be value-preserving at all. A deal that is executable and stable is more valuable than a “strong” plan that detonates industrial peace.

If you want long-term enterprise value, support the solution that can actually be implemented: keep the plants open, avoid compulsory redundancies, and agree a durable restructuring with labour. Anything else is gambling with the asset.

## To the state government
Your position is the only one that acknowledges both economic reality and social responsibility.

But let me warn against drift. “No closures” must not become “yes, but only after squeezing the workforce until the framework is hollow.” That would be closure by another name.

The state’s role is not to help management rewrite the social contract after the fact. It is to defend the industrial base now — which means:
- no plant closures,
- no forced layoffs,
- binding job security through 2030,
- and a real future product and investment plan.

I support your hard line against closures. I want you to keep it.

## To the union
Your support remains important, and I stand with you on the essentials. But we need to keep the line sharp.

The company now has an industrial finance assessment that says a no-closure package can deliver significant recurring savings. That means we are not dealing with fantasy. We are dealing with a real restructuring path that preserves jobs and sites.

So the next step is not to widen the burden onto workers beyond what is fair. The next step is to insist that management finally quantify the package properly and commit to the no-closure route.

We should be prepared for:
- voluntary exits,
- internal mobility,
- working-time changes,
- shift flexibility,
- qualification,
- and limited, time-bound contributions.

We are **not** prepared for:
- closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or dismantling the job-security framework.

## The central question: what counts as “enough”?
Management and capital keep using “enough” to mean “enough to justify harsher measures if needed.”

That is not the only standard. There is another standard — the only one that matters for us:
- enough to restore competitiveness **without breaking the company’s social foundation**.

The expert did not say the non-closure route is impossible. The expert said it can be substantial, and that sufficiency depends on the gap. Exactly. So show the gap, quantify the no-closure measures, and stop using uncertainty as a pretext for closures.

## What I will accept
I remain open to a **strong no-closure restructuring** if it includes all of the following:
1. every German plant kept open
2. no compulsory redundancies
3. binding job-security protection through 2030
4. measurable capacity reduction through non-closure measures
5. voluntary and negotiated workforce adjustments only
6. credible product and investment commitments
7. a real role for co-determination in implementation

That is the basis of a settlement.

## What I will not accept
These remain non-negotiable:
- any German plant closure
- any compulsory redundancy
- erosion of co-determination
- hollowing out of job security
- making the workforce carry the crisis alone

## My conclusion
The new expert input does **not** move me toward concessions on closures. It strengthens our case for a negotiated restructuring that preserves sites and jobs.

If management wants cooperation, bring a **quantified no-closure package** that closes the gap with durable savings and without breaking the social contract. If it cannot do that, then it is management’s failure of strategy — not a reason to close plants or throw workers overboard.
