The round-two and round-three summaries make one thing very clear: **there is no serious case for forcing the workforce to absorb the entire restructuring burden**. If management, investors, or the family want a durable solution, they still need to answer the central question: **why should jobs, sites, and the collective wage framework be sacrificed when the experts themselves say there are workable non-closure instruments?**

## To management
I acknowledge one of your points: **a no-closure package has a ceiling**. Fine. We never claimed otherwise. But a ceiling is not the same as a dead end.

Your own expert said a strong negotiated package can deliver:
- meaningful headcount reduction over time,
- mid- to high-single-digit labor cost reductions,
- and a real competitiveness bridge.

That is not cosmetic. That is restructuring.

So I reject your continued implication that the only way to demonstrate seriousness is to keep closures and forced redundancies in play. That is not “necessary discipline”; it is using the threat of destruction to compensate for management’s own strategic misjudgments.

If you want a settlement, then bring a **quantified no-closure package** that includes:
- internal redeployment,
- voluntary exits,
- working-time reductions,
- shift and line rationalization,
- temporary but real employee contributions,
- and binding investment and product commitments.

If you cannot do that, then the problem is not that the workforce is unrealistic — it is that management wants labour to pay for competitiveness alone.

## To the controlling family and investors
I hear your concern about a weak compromise. I share it. A symbolic deal would be pointless.

But I do **not** accept the conclusion that because the no-closure path has limits, closures are therefore the serious option. In this governance structure, a closure-led strategy is not just socially harsh; it is likely to be **executionally brittle**. The experts made that clear.

A plan that triggers vetoes, industrial escalation, and prolonged conflict is not value creation. It is value destruction.

So if you want long-term value, back the route that can actually be implemented:
- keep the plants open,
- avoid compulsory redundancies,
- preserve co-determination,
- and accept a substantial, negotiated labour contribution in exchange for site and job security.

That is not softness. It is governance realism.

## To the state government
I appreciate your continued support for no closures and no forced redundancies. That remains essential.

But support has to be more than a political slogan. It has to translate into a binding framework that stops management from nibbling away at guarantees until the workforce has given everything and received only uncertainty in return.

The state should support:
- a negotiated capacity reduction without closures,
- future product commitments,
- and legally and industrially binding protections through 2030.

Anything less risks turning “restructuring” into a slow-motion dismantling of the industrial base.

## To the works council
We are aligned on the essentials:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of job security,
- no erosion of co-determination.

I support that position.

I also want to underline something important: the expert evidence confirms that we are **not** refusing change. We are offering a different kind of change:
- voluntary exits rather than compulsory layoffs,
- redeployment rather than closures,
- working-time flexibility rather than forced job destruction,
- and limited, negotiated contributions rather than one-sided sacrifice.

That is a real restructuring path. It is also the only one consistent with fairness and industrial peace.

## On “what counts as enough”
This is where the real disagreement lies.

Management and capital want “enough” to mean “enough to justify a harsher restructuring if needed.”  
We define “enough” as “enough to restore competitiveness without destroying sites, jobs, and the wage framework.”

Those are different standards. And I will not let the company adopt a standard that makes workers the permanent adjustment variable.

## What we are prepared to accept
We are prepared to negotiate a serious package with all of the following:
- voluntary headcount reduction
- attrition and non-replacement
- internal mobility across sites
- working-time reductions and shift flexibility
- bonus restraint and limited wage moderation
- qualification and transfer schemes
- binding future product and investment commitments
- a time-limited and fair employee contribution
- a durable settlement that protects the collective wage framework

## What we are not prepared to accept
These remain non-negotiable:
- any German plant closure
- compulsory redundancies
- hollowing out the collective bargaining system
- breaking the job-security framework
- making employees carry the restructuring alone

## Bottom line
The repeated claim that “real restructuring” must keep closures on the table has not been substantiated. What has been substantiated is this:
- **a strong no-closure restructuring is possible,**
- **it can deliver meaningful savings,**
- **it is more executable in this governance setting,**
- and **it is the only acceptable path if the company wants workforce cooperation.**

If management wants a deal, it must stop using closures as the opening move and come back with a serious, quantified, no-closure proposal. If it does not, then we will continue to resist with every legitimate means available, including mobilisation of the workforce and the rights available to us in collective bargaining and governance.
