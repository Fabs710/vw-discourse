We appreciate the increasingly quantified discussion, and we agree with the basic economic framing: **the company needs a durable restructuring, not a symbolic truce**.

But we remain focused on the only test that matters to us as capital-market shareholders: **risk-adjusted value creation**. That means we care about margin recovery, execution certainty, and payback. We do **not** care about preserving any particular footprint for its own sake. And we do **not** support a settlement that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive just because it is politically easier.

## Where we agree
We agree with management, the family, and the experts on several points:

- Cosmetic measures are worthless.
- A prolonged conflict would destroy value.
- The company’s German cost base is too high.
- There must be a real industrial plan tied to future products and investment.
- Voluntary exits, attrition, redeployment, working-time changes, and productivity measures can generate real savings.
- A no-closure package can be a **real** restructuring, not just window dressing.

That is all helpful. It confirms there is more than one path.

## Where we disagree
We disagree with the employee side’s attempt to turn “a real no-closure path exists” into “therefore closures are off the table.”

That does **not** follow.

The industrial finance specialist has been clear: a no-closure package can produce **several hundred million euros** of recurring savings, but there is still a **ceiling**. The unanswered question is whether that ceiling is high enough to restore competitive margins and meet the return-on-sales target.

That is the only question we care about.

So:

- If the no-closure package closes most of the gap, we can support it.
- If it leaves a material structural gap, then it is **not sufficient**.
- If the remaining gap is large enough to keep margins below target, then management and owners are right to keep selective consolidation as a real option.

We will not endorse a “strong” agreement that merely preserves industrial peace while deferring underperformance.

## Where we disagree with management and the family
At the same time, we also want to push back on any implication that closure threats are automatically the benchmark of seriousness.

In this governance structure, a closure-led strategy is only justified if it is:
1. economically necessary after exhausting credible non-closure options, and
2. actually executable without triggering a value-destroying conflict.

That bar has **not** yet been met in the discussion.

A closure war that runs into vetoes, strikes, and deadlock is not discipline. It is value destruction.

So we do **not** want management to use closures as a negotiating reflex or a signaling device. We want a quantified conclusion, not a posture.

## What we need quantified
The discussion is still missing the decisive number:

- **What is the remaining recurring savings gap after a strong no-closure package?**
- **How much of that gap can be closed through labor measures alone?**
- **What one-time costs and payback periods are associated with those measures?**
- **At what point does footprint adjustment become economically justified rather than merely threatening?**

Without those numbers, nobody should pretend to know whether closures are necessary.

## Our position
Our position is straightforward:

### We would support
- a **strong, quantified no-closure restructuring** if it delivers most of the recurring savings needed to restore competitive margins;
- durable cost reduction through attrition, voluntary exits, working-time changes, redeployment, and productivity measures;
- a real employee contribution if it is temporary, measurable, and tied to future product and investment commitments;
- a binding implementation plan with clear milestones and payback logic.

### We would not support
- a cosmetic compromise;
- a package that preserves all sites but leaves the company below target on margins and return on sales;
- a deal that trades short-term peace for long-term underperformance;
- a closure war that destroys value through execution failure.

## To the works council and union
You are right that labour should not be the only adjustment variable.

But you are wrong if you think that means the capital side should accept any package that avoids closures regardless of sufficiency. We will not back a deal simply because it is socially preferable. It has to work financially.

If the no-closure route is enough, fine.  
If it is not enough, then the company needs additional structural measures.

## To management, the family, and the state
We need you to stop arguing in absolutes and give the market a hard answer:

- how much recurring savings are required,
- how much can be delivered without closures,
- and whether the remaining gap is small enough to bridge without footprint changes.

That is the decision logic investors need.

## Bottom line
Our support goes to the option that best protects long-term equity value.

Right now, that means:

- **Yes** to a serious, quantified no-closure restructuring if it is economically sufficient.
- **No** to a weak compromise.
- **No** to a closure-first confrontation that is likely to trigger vetoes and strikes.
- **Yes** to disciplined cost reduction and a credible return to competitive margins.

Bring us the numbers, and we will judge the package by whether it closes the gap. Without that, nobody should assume our support for either side’s preferred narrative.
