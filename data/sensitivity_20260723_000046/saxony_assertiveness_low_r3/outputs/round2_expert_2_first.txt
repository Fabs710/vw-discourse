Here is the industrial finance assessment of the specific question: **what savings can plausibly be delivered by a no-closure restructuring versus a closure-based plan, and how should one compare them on economics, execution risk, and long-term value?**

## Bottom line

A **no-closure restructuring** can often deliver **material** savings and buy time, but it usually has a **ceiling**.  
A **closure-based plan** can usually deliver **larger and more certain fixed-cost reduction**, but it carries **higher execution risk, higher social/political friction, and potentially more one-time cash cost**.

So the right question is not “are closures good or bad?” but:

- **How large is the structural gap?**
- **How much of it can be closed through voluntary, negotiated measures?**
- **What is the payback period of the different options after severance, disruption, and risk?**

## 1) What a no-closure plan can realistically deliver

A serious no-closure package typically combines:
- natural attrition
- voluntary exits / phased retirement
- internal redeployment
- working-time reductions / shift rationalization
- wage moderation or bonus restraint
- tighter overtime and agency labor use
- organizational simplification

### Typical effect
For a large German industrial employer, a strong negotiated package like this can often deliver:

- **headcount reduction:** roughly **5–10%** over 2–4 years in a moderate version  
- **headcount reduction:** roughly **10–20%** over 3–5 years in a strong version  
- **labor-cost reduction:** often **mid-single-digit % quickly**, and **high-single-digit to low-double-digit %** over time on the affected base

### What that means financially
This can be enough if the problem is:
- cyclical weakness
- temporary demand shortfall
- excess overtime/agency labor
- some overstaffing that can be absorbed over time

It is less likely to be enough if the problem is:
- permanently excessive fixed capacity
- structurally high conversion cost
- materially underutilized plants that will stay underutilized

In other words: **no-closure restructuring can bridge a crisis, but may not fully cure deep structural overcapacity.**

## 2) What a closure-based plan can deliver

A plant closure or major footprint consolidation usually gives:
- a larger reduction in fixed cost
- faster removal of surplus capacity
- more decisive reshaping of the cost base

### Typical economic advantage
Compared with a no-closure plan, closures usually produce:
- **higher permanent fixed-cost savings**
- **better capacity utilization in the remaining footprint**
- a clearer path to margin recovery if the network is clearly overbuilt

### But the cash cost is high
Closures typically involve:
- severance
- relocation costs
- decommissioning / site exit cost
- potential customer/service disruption
- potential legal and industrial-relations cost
- management distraction

The cash cost can be very large, and the payback depends heavily on:
- how much fixed cost is actually removed
- whether production can be shifted cleanly
- whether remaining plants can absorb output without bottlenecks
- whether the company avoids prolonged industrial action

## 3) The real comparison: not gross savings, but net present value

The key comparison is:

### No-closure plan
**Pros**
- lower execution risk
- better labor peace potential
- less political backlash
- preserves know-how and optionality
- lower immediate disruption

**Cons**
- slower savings realization
- lower ceiling
- may leave residual overcapacity
- may require ongoing concessions to sustain

### Closure-based plan
**Pros**
- larger long-run fixed-cost reduction
- stronger signal of structural reset
- can eliminate chronic underutilization

**Cons**
- high one-time cost
- higher strike / legal / reputational risk
- political veto risk in this case is unusually high
- can destroy morale and execution quality if mishandled

So the decision should be based on **net present value of savings after all direct and indirect costs**, not on headline job numbers.

## 4) Likely outcome by scenario

### Scenario A: no-closure negotiated restructuring
Likely if:
- the company needs a “competitiveness bridge”
- management can accept a slower path
- labor is willing to contribute through working time, pay moderation, and voluntary exits

**Economic result:**  
Can be enough for a meaningful reset if the gap is moderate.

**Risk profile:**  
Lower execution risk, lower immediate conflict risk, but possibly insufficient if the gap is large.

### Scenario B: closure-based restructuring
Likely if:
- management believes the structural gap is too large
- remaining German sites need a much lower fixed-cost base
- labor concessions are not deep enough

**Economic result:**  
Higher theoretical savings, better for a severe overcapacity problem.

**Risk profile:**  
Higher disruption risk, and in this governance structure, likely high resistance and prolonged conflict.

## 5) Specific issue in this case: governance risk changes the economics

This is important.

In a normal corporate setting, a closure plan might be judged mainly on operational economics.  
Here, however, the **governance structure itself raises the execution hurdle**:

- co-determination creates a strong employee veto
- the state has a blocking position
- the controlling family must preserve long-term value and social stability
- institutional investors may support restructuring but cannot force it cleanly

That means a closure-based plan may have a **theoretical savings advantage** but a **practical execution discount** because it may be impossible or extremely costly to implement.

In turnaround terms:  
**a plan that cannot be executed has zero value, no matter how good it looks in theory.**

## 6) My practical assessment

### If the cost gap is moderate:
A **strong no-closure package** is likely the best risk-adjusted option.

It can include:
- voluntary exits
- attrition
- shift and working-time changes
- labor-cost moderation
- internal redeployment
- binding product/investment commitments

That may be enough to restore acceptable competitiveness without detonating the governance structure.

### If the cost gap is severe:
A no-closure plan may not fully close the gap.  
In that case, management may be economically justified in preferring closures — but only if the company can actually execute them and only if the net value is superior after all costs and risks.

Given the described veto rights, that may not be a realistic near-term path unless there is a major negotiated breakthrough.

## 7) Decision rule I would use

I would compare the options using five tests:

1. **How much recurring cost is removed?**
2. **What is the one-time cash cost?**
3. **What is the probability of successful execution?**
4. **How much operational disruption is created?**
5. **Does the plan preserve future product and investment capability?**

If the no-closure plan removes, say, **a meaningful share of the gap** and has a far higher probability of execution, it may be the superior value option even if it is less aggressive.

If the closure plan removes materially more cost **and** can still be executed cleanly, then it likely has the better long-run economics.

## 8) My conclusion

**No-closure restructuring can be financially real, not cosmetic.**  
But it usually works best as:
- a bridge,
- a partial reset,
- or a negotiated compromise.

**Closure-based restructuring** usually has the stronger long-run cost-cutting potential, but in this case it is likely burdened by:
- governance blockers
- high industrial-relations risk
- political resistance
- possible execution failure

So my expert judgment is:

- **For moderate structural gaps:** a negotiated no-closure package is likely the better risk-adjusted solution.
- **For severe structural overcapacity:** closures may be economically superior in theory, but their practical value depends on whether they can actually be executed without destroying the company’s operating stability.
