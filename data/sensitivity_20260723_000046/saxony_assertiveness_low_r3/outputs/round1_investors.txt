As dispersed capital-market shareholders, we are looking at this through one lens: **risk-adjusted value creation**. We do not have the voting block to dictate the outcome, but we do have a strong interest in preventing a weak, cosmetic settlement that leaves the company with a structurally uncompetitive cost base and permanently impaired margins.

### Where we agree
We agree with management, the family holding, and to a degree the state government on the core diagnosis:

- the current cost base is too high,
- overcapacity is a real problem,
- margins are below what a company of this scale should earn,
- and a credible restructuring is necessary if the group is going to fund future product investment and stay competitive.

We also agree with the employee side on one important point: **a prolonged conflict would be value-destructive**. A long strike or extended uncertainty would erode earnings, delay decisions, and pressure the share price further.

### Where we disagree
Our concern is that the conversation can easily drift into either of two equally bad outcomes:

1. **a closure-first confrontation that creates execution risk, political backlash, and strike damage**, or  
2. **a soft compromise that preserves too much of the legacy cost structure and simply kicks the problem down the road**.

The second is the outcome we are most concerned about.

To be explicit:  
We will **not** support a settlement that preserves symbolic job security but leaves the company with:
- structurally uncompetitive labour costs,
- insufficient capacity rationalisation,
- delayed margin recovery,
- or vague “transformation” commitments without measurable savings.

That would be a cosmetic deal, not a restructuring. It might calm the headlines, but it would not restore equity value.

### On management’s position
Management is right that **temporary measures alone are not enough**. Working-time tweaks, internal redeployment, and limited concessions are useful only if they add up to real structural savings. If they do not, then they just postpone the pain and weaken the business further.

So we support management’s insistence on:
- durable cost reduction,
- meaningful capacity cuts,
- and a clear path back to the ROS target.

But management must also be careful not to overplay its hand. If the restructuring becomes a high-risk closure battle, then even a correct diagnosis can destroy value in execution. We would rather see a deal that is economically hard but operationally deliverable than a theoretically “optimal” plan that cannot be implemented.

### On the Works Council and union
We understand the red lines around forced redundancies and plant closures. We are not here to take a position on social policy; we are here to protect capital. But we do need to be clear:

- **No compulsory redundancies** may be acceptable if the savings are still real and durable.
- **No plant closures ever** is harder to support if it prevents genuine restructuring.
- **No structural sacrifice from labour** is not credible if the company’s competitive position remains broken.

In other words, workers need not bear the burden alone, but they cannot be insulated from it entirely if the business model requires change.

### On the state government
We support the state’s preference for a negotiated solution that preserves industrial capacity. A destructive closure strategy could damage the regional ecosystem and hurt long-term value. But the state should also recognize that blocking meaningful restructuring for political reasons is not value-neutral. If the business keeps underperforming, the cost is borne by shareholders through lower returns and by the company through weaker investment capacity.

### Our bottom line
We are prepared to back a restructuring **if and only if** it credibly delivers:

- **material and durable cost reduction**
- **measurable capacity adjustment**
- **a realistic path to competitive margins**
- **execution certainty**
- **no prolonged strike or open-ended conflict**

What we will not back is a settlement that looks balanced on paper but leaves the cost base fundamentally unaddressed.

### What would move us
A package becomes supportable if it includes:
- concrete, quantified savings,
- a binding timetable,
- clear accountability for management delivery,
- and enough structural change to make the return-on-sales target plausible.

If those conditions are not met, then the company should not expect capital markets to reward a compromise just because it is politically comfortable.

So our message is simple:  
**Deliver a real restructuring, not a diplomatic one.**  
We will support the outcome that restores competitiveness and protects long-term enterprise value — and we will not endorse a deal that merely postpones the reckoning.
