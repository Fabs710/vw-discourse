The expert confirms something management has been saying from the start: **there is a real restructuring path without immediate closures, but it has a ceiling**. And that ceiling matters.

Let me respond directly.

## Where I agree
I agree with the expert, the union, and even parts of the Works Council on one crucial point:

- **Cosmetic measures are useless**
- **Working-time, attrition, voluntary exits, and redeployment can deliver real savings**
- **A long strike would destroy value**
- **A negotiated solution is preferable to open conflict**

I also agree that a no-closure package can create a **competitiveness bridge**. That is not in dispute.

## Where I disagree
What I do **not** accept is the claim that the expert’s model proves the workforce case and weakens management’s position.

It does not.

The expert’s own assessment says:

- no-closure measures may deliver **mid-single-digit labor cost reductions quickly**
- and perhaps **high single-digit to low double-digit reductions over several years**
- but **deep structural overcapacity** may still not be solved

That is exactly the point.  
We are not talking about a temporary bridge alone. We are talking about restoring a **permanently uncompetitive cost base** to a level where the brand can survive, invest, and hit return-on-sales targets through 2030.

A package that preserves every site, rejects any closure logic, and limits change to working-time and voluntary measures may be socially preferable to you — but if it leaves the structural cost problem unresolved, then it is **not good enough** for the company.

## On the Works Council
You keep repeating:
- no plant closures
- no compulsory redundancies
- no erosion of guarantees
- no dismantling of co-determination

I understand the position. I reject the conclusion.

The company cannot be held hostage to a promise that was made under a fundamentally different market reality. The domestic cost base is too high. The European market is weaker. Competition is harsher. If we do not materially reduce fixed cost and capacity, then we will simply manage decline.

So when you say “bring a package without closures,” my answer is:  
**bring a package that actually closes the cost gap.**

If it does that, management will engage seriously. If it does not, then it is not an answer to the crisis.

## On the union
You say the expert proves management cannot say “there is no alternative.” That is true only in the narrow sense that there are alternatives to closures.

But there is a difference between **an alternative** and **an adequate solution**.

A competitiveness bridge is not enough if it simply defers the structural adjustment. I do not need a short-lived truce that preserves the old cost base and kicks the issue into the next cycle. I need a durable reset.

If the union wants no closures and no forced layoffs, then it must accept that the labor side must make a **meaningful, sustained contribution**:
- working-time reduction,
- bonus restraint,
- wage moderation,
- removal or suspension of legacy protections,
- and genuine capacity reduction through internal consolidation

not just symbolic gestures.

And to be clear: if the burden-sharing is not real, then the package is not real.

## On the state government
You are right to emphasize regional stability and co-determination. But the state cannot support an arrangement that protects local sentiment while leaving the enterprise structurally weak.

If the business model stays broken, the long-term damage to the region will be worse, not better.  
So I ask the state side to be honest about the trade-off:

- preserving every existing site in full,
- versus restoring competitiveness enough to secure the rest of the industrial base

You cannot maximize both if the economics do not support it.

## On the controlling family
I agree with you on one thing: a destructive closure war would destroy value. But your position cannot stop at “no soft compromise.” The company needs more than rhetorical discipline.

The family ownership should support a restructuring that is:
- durable,
- measurable,
- and strong enough to restore margins

not just politically convenient. If the restructuring is too weak, then long-term value will erode anyway.

## What management needs now
We are not asking for blind acceptance. We are asking for an economically credible package that produces **material structural savings**.

That means:
- measurable capacity reduction
- sustained labor-cost contribution
- clear headcount reduction over time
- no purely cosmetic “transformation” language
- and a credible path to return-on-sales recovery

## What I am prepared to accept
I remain open to a negotiated solution that avoids immediate closures **if and only if** it contains:
1. **durable capacity reductions**
2. **a substantial employee contribution**
3. **binding implementation milestones**
4. **credible savings at a scale that actually fixes the cost base**
5. **room for future investment in products and technology**

## What I am not prepared to accept
I will not accept:
- a symbolic no-closure deal that leaves overcapacity intact,
- a package that preserves all current guarantees without replacement savings,
- or a settlement that shifts the entire burden into deferred promises and soft language.

## Bottom line
The expert did not remove management’s case. It sharpened it.

Yes, there are non-closure tools.  
No, they do not automatically solve the problem.  
And no, management will not endorse a settlement that looks balanced politically but leaves the company financially uncompetitive.

So my position remains:

- We are prepared to negotiate.
- We are open on the instruments.
- We are **not** open on the outcome being too weak to restore competitiveness.

If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then it must help produce a package that is strong enough to make closures unnecessary. That means real concessions, not just red lines.
