I will be direct.

The expert input has clarified the central issue: **a no-closure restructuring can be real, but it has a ceiling; a closure-based plan can go further in theory, but may fail in execution and destroy value in the process.** That is exactly the trade-off the controlling family holding has to judge. My judgment is that **we should not rush into plant closures as the default answer**. But I also will not endorse a settlement that merely preserves the current structure and leaves the company competitively weak.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the investors, and the experts on the diagnosis:

- the current German cost base is too high,
- cosmetic savings are not enough,
- real structural change is necessary,
- and the company must regain margin discipline to fund future investment.

I also agree with the Works Council, the union, and the state government on one important point:

- **a prolonged conflict would be value-destructive**,
- and a closure-driven confrontation in this governance setting would be extremely risky.

So yes: **we need restructuring, but we need it to be executable.**

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the idea that the only serious restructuring is one built around closures and compulsory redundancies.

The expert has just confirmed that a strong negotiated package without closures can deliver:
- meaningful headcount reduction over time,
- mid- to high-single-digit labor-cost reductions,
- and a real competitiveness bridge.

That means the employee side cannot simply say “no closures, no layoffs, no meaningful sacrifice” and assume that is a complete answer. It is not.

At the same time, I do **not** accept management’s implication that because non-closure measures have a ceiling, closures must therefore be the immediate path. That is not good governance. If a closure strategy cannot be executed cleanly because of co-determination, state blocking rights, labour resistance, and the risk of a prolonged industrial conflict, then it is not a serious solution — it is a value hazard.

## To the Works Council
I hear your red lines:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no erosion of job security,
- no hollowing out of co-determination.

I understand why you hold them, and I do not dismiss them lightly.

But I will be equally clear: **if the workforce treats every site, every line, and every legacy guarantee as untouchable, then the company will not regain competitiveness.** That would protect structure, not value. And over time that would threaten the very jobs you want to defend.

If you want the family holding to support a non-closure path, then it must be more than a slogan. It must include:
- measurable capacity reduction,
- genuine productivity improvement,
- voluntary exits,
- working-time flexibility,
- and a durable employee contribution.

A “no closures” package is only credible if it is **strong enough to make closures unnecessary**. Otherwise it is just delay.

## To the union
You are right that workers should not pay alone. I agree.

But I do **not** support the suggestion that management, owners, or capital can be asked to “contribute” in a vague, open-ended way while the labor side keeps all guarantees intact. That is not burden-sharing; that is deferral.

A fair package must produce **real, quantified, durable savings**, not just temporary relief. If that requires:
- bonus restraint,
- working-time reductions,
- wage moderation,
- suspension of legacy premiums,
- and a stronger reliance on voluntary exits,
then those need to be on the table.

What I will not back is a package where the company gives away future flexibility and still lacks the savings needed to restore margins.

## To management
Management is right about the need for structural savings. But I want to be clear: **a plan that is economically attractive on paper but politically and industrially impossible is not acceptable.**

You have to distinguish between:
- the theoretically optimal restructuring, and
- the governable restructuring.

In this company, those are not the same.

So I want management to stop treating closure options as a pressure tactic and instead bring forward a package that can actually be approved and executed. The right standard is not “maximum theoretical savings.” The right standard is **savings that can be realized without permanent value destruction**.

If management can quantify a strong no-closure package, I will support it through governance. If management cannot, then it must explain specifically why not — not just repeat that the cost base is too high.

## To the state government
I appreciate your recognition that the company needs competitiveness, and I share your view that the state’s industrial base matters. But I would caution against a purely defensive posture.

The state cannot support freezing the company in its current shape if that means the business keeps underperforming. A regional anchor is only useful if it remains viable. So I expect the state to engage seriously with a package that delivers:
- real savings,
- protected sites,
- no forced redundancies,
- and a future investment path.

The state should help secure a viable compromise, not simply block change.

## Where the family holding stands
Our position is straightforward:

- We support **real restructuring**.
- We do **not** support a closure war that risks prolonged conflict and value destruction.
- We do **not** support a soft deal that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.
- We support a **governable, durable, value-restoring settlement**.

That means our preference is for a **strong negotiated no-closure package**, but only if it genuinely closes most of the competitiveness gap.

## What would move us
A package becomes supportable if it includes, at minimum:

1. **quantified, durable cost reduction**
2. **measurable capacity adjustment**
3. **voluntary headcount reduction and attrition**
4. **working-time and shift flexibility**
5. **a fair but limited employee contribution**
6. **binding product and investment commitments**
7. **industrial peace sufficient to execute the plan**
8. **no permanent impairment of governance control or enterprise value**

If those conditions are met, I will support the deal through the supervisory board.

If they are not met, then I will not support a cosmetic compromise, and I will not endorse a rushed closure strategy either.

## Bottom line
I am not moving toward consensus for its own sake.

My position is:

- **No to a closure-first confrontation**
- **No to a weak compromise**
- **Yes to a serious negotiated restructuring that restores competitiveness and preserves long-term value**

If the other stakeholders want my support, they need to bring me a package that is **economically sufficient, operationally executable, and stable enough to last**.
