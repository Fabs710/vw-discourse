I appreciate the clarity from all sides, and I want to be equally clear from the perspective of the regional state government as an anchor shareholder.

### Where I agree
I agree with management on one point: **the company does need a credible path back to competitiveness**. We cannot pretend the market has not changed. If the German cost base is structurally too high, that has to be addressed.

I also agree with the Works Council and the union that **the workforce cannot be asked to carry the burden alone**. This company is a regional economic anchor, not just a line item on a balance sheet. Jobs, skills, supplier networks, and the industrial base in this state matter.

### Where I disagree
What I cannot support is a restructuring that is effectively built around:
- **plant closures in Germany as the first or default solution**
- **mass compulsory redundancies**
- **breaking established job-security and co-determination arrangements**
- **sidestepping the state’s and workforce’s blocking rights by presenting a fait accompli**

That would be economically damaging, politically irresponsible, and short-sighted. It would hollow out the regional industrial base and create long-term costs that are much harder to reverse than any short-term savings.

### On management’s position
Management is right that cosmetic savings are not enough. But I do not accept the implication that the only serious options are closures and deep cuts to employment. There are other restructuring tools that can be substantial if management is prepared to negotiate seriously:
- capacity reduction through consolidation of production lines
- working-time adjustments
- internal redeployment and qualification
- temporary, clearly bounded employee contributions
- product and investment commitments that secure future volumes

That is the path we should be testing first, not closure logic.

### On the workforce side
I understand and broadly support the red lines from the Works Council and the union:
- no compulsory redundancies
- no plant closures
- no unilateral dismantling of collective protections

Those are legitimate positions, and they reflect the social partnership model that has long underpinned this company’s strength. But I would also say this: if we want to preserve sites and jobs, we need a package that is economically credible. A total refusal to discuss any structural change would also carry risk.

### What I can support
I would be prepared to support a negotiated settlement if it includes:
1. **binding protection for German sites**
2. **no forced layoffs**
3. **a fair, limited employee contribution**
4. **real investment and product commitments**
5. **measurable structural savings that do not rely primarily on closures**
6. **preservation of co-determination and the state’s veto role**

### What I cannot support
I cannot support a plan that uses the restructuring crisis to:
- weaken the company’s regional commitments,
- force through a permanent reduction in the state’s influence,
- or make Germany bear the social cost of management failure alone.

### My bottom line
The state is not here to block every change. We are here to prevent a destructive one.

So my message to management is: bring forward a restructuring that proves you can restore competitiveness **without sacrificing the company’s role as a regional anchor**.

And to the employee side: I support your core red lines, but I urge you to engage on a package that secures the future rather than simply freezing the present.

If there is a serious social-partnership solution on the table, we can help make it work. If there is a closure-driven plan, we will resist it.
