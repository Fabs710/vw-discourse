The question in my mandate is the same one I’ve been asked to quantify: **how far can a strong no-closure restructuring go, what does it typically save, and where is the ceiling?**

## Bottom line
For a large German automotive site network, a **serious no-closure package** can often deliver **several hundred million euros per year** in recurring savings if it combines:
- attrition and non-replacement,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment and qualification,
- working-time reductions,
- shift rationalization,
- productivity improvements,
- and a negotiated labor-cost contribution.

A realistic order of magnitude is usually:

- **~€200m–€500m p.a.** for a strong, well-executed package at a large brand/group level  
- **~€500m–€800m+ p.a.** only if labor-cost concessions are unusually deep, headcount reduction is large, and working-time/productivity changes are sustained

But there is a ceiling. In a mature automotive plant network, **non-closure measures usually cannot fully substitute for structural capacity reduction** if the system is materially overbuilt.

---

## How the savings typically break down

### 1) Attrition / non-replacement
This is usually the cheapest recurring saving.

- Typical effect: **1–2% of workforce per year** can sometimes be removed without forced layoffs, depending on age profile and hiring freeze.
- Savings depend on loaded labor cost per employee.
- For a workforce with full cost of **€80k–€110k per head/year**, every 1,000 positions removed is roughly **€80m–€110m p.a.** in run-rate labor cost, before backfill and transition costs.

**Practical order of magnitude:**  
- Large site cluster: **€50m–€150m p.a.**
- Group/brand scale: **€100m–€250m p.a.**

### 2) Voluntary exits / severance programs
This can accelerate adjustment beyond natural attrition.

- Good for reducing overhead and excess indirect labor.
- One-time cost is significant, but payback is often **2–4 years** if structured well.
- The savings are durable if positions are truly removed.

**Typical run-rate savings:**  
- **€100m–€300m p.a.** at scale, depending on numbers accepted

### 3) Redeployment / qualification / role conversion
This does not always create immediate cash savings, but it avoids external hiring, preserves know-how, and enables capacity reduction elsewhere.

- Direct cash effect is usually modest in year 1.
- Longer-term savings come from avoiding replacement of departing workers and consolidating functions.

**Typical run-rate savings:**  
- **€20m–€80m p.a.** direct, sometimes more indirectly

### 4) Working-time reductions, shift changes, and plant-calendar changes
This is often underappreciated.

- Fewer shifts, less weekend/overtime, longer planned downtime, and smoother line loading can reduce variable and semi-fixed labor cost.
- But it only saves materially if management actually removes capacity and does not reintroduce it through overtime.

**Typical run-rate savings:**  
- **€50m–€150m p.a.**
- In a very large footprint, possibly more, but rarely enough alone

### 5) Productivity measures
These include:
- headcount-to-output optimization,
- line balancing,
- scrap reduction,
- logistics simplification,
- indirect function cuts,
- procurement/process improvements linked to operations.

These can be real, but they are often partly captured already in management plans. They are also more execution-sensitive.

**Typical run-rate savings:**  
- **€50m–€200m p.a.**  
- Higher numbers require strong implementation discipline and usually some capex/organization redesign

### 6) Labor-cost contribution
This includes:
- wage moderation,
- temporary pay sacrifice,
- bonus deferral,
- reduction of special payments,
- longer working hours for same base pay,
- suspension of allowances.

This is the area with the most political resistance, but also the most visible impact on the cost base.

**Typical run-rate savings:**  
- **€50m–€200m p.a.** if limited and temporary  
- **€200m+ p.a.** only if concessions are broad and durable

---

## What a “strong” no-closure package can realistically achieve
If all of the above are combined and executed well, a plausible aggregate is:

### Conservative strong package
- **€200m–€300m p.a.**

### Credible ambitious package
- **€300m–€500m p.a.**

### Very aggressive negotiated package
- **€500m–€800m+ p.a.**
  - but this usually requires:
    - substantial headcount decline,
    - deeper labor-cost moderation,
    - sustained working-time changes,
    - and strict implementation

At the upper end, you are no longer in “soft restructuring” territory. You are effectively doing **structural capacity adjustment without formal plant closure**.

---

## The ceiling problem
The employee side is right about one thing: these tools can be substantial.

But management and capital are also right about the limit:

### What non-closure measures usually cannot do
They usually cannot fully solve:
- **hard physical overcapacity**
- **misaligned fixed-cost structure**
- **too many sites with similar functions**
- **permanent underutilization of assets**
- **product/location mismatch**

If the underlying issue is that the company has, say, **10–20% too much domestic manufacturing capacity**, then labor measures can soften the cost problem but often do not fully remove the structural burden.

That is why, in industrial restructuring, non-closure measures can often close **part**, sometimes **most**, but not always **all** of the gap.

---

## What “enough” means financially
A useful rule of thumb:

- If a no-closure package closes **most of the gap** and the residual gap is small enough to be bridged by product mix improvements, purchasing, and normal operating leverage, then a no-closure solution is credible.
- If it leaves a **material recurring gap** that continues to depress return on sales by several percentage points, then it is probably insufficient.

For a large OEM, the difference between:
- **2% margin recovery** and
- **4–6% margin recovery**

is often the difference between a negotiable labor deal and a structural footprint issue.

---

## One-time costs and payback
This matters a lot.

A no-closure restructuring can look attractive because it avoids closure costs, but it still carries significant one-time expense:
- severance and transfer packages,
- qualification and retraining,
- bridge financing for working-time arrangements,
- transaction/consulting/implementation costs.

### Typical one-time cost range
- **€300m–€1.5bn+**, depending on headcount reduction depth and incentives used

### Typical payback
- **2–5 years** if the savings are real and the cuts stick
- longer if labor contributions expire or if the company has to backfill later

So the question is not only annual savings. It is also:
- **how durable are they?**
- **how much cash is needed upfront?**
- **how much implementation risk is there?**

---

## Comparison with closure-based restructuring
A closure-based plan usually has:
- **higher nominal savings potential**
- **higher one-time costs**
- **much higher execution risk** in a co-determined, politically sensitive German setting

### Closure-based savings
Depending on scale, closure-based actions can create:
- **€300m–€700m+ p.a.** recurring savings at brand/group level,
- sometimes more if large fixed-cost blocks are removed.

### But:
- legal resistance,
- strike risk,
- political vetoes,
- transition disruption,
- and delayed realization

can erase a lot of the theoretical benefit.

So closure plans may be stronger on paper, but in this governance environment they are often **less certain in value terms** than they appear.

---

## My estimate of the remaining gap
Without the company’s actual internal baseline, I can only give a range.

If management says German operations are materially above target and needs a durable reset, the **residual gap after a strong no-closure package** is often still in the order of:

- **€100m–€400m p.a.** if the package is ambitious and well executed
- **€400m+ p.a.** if overcapacity is severe and the no-closure package is only moderate

That residual gap is what drives the reopening of closure talk.

---

## Practical conclusion
### Established
- Non-closure restructuring can absolutely generate **real, recurring savings**.
- Those savings are large enough to matter strategically.
- They are often sufficient to support a negotiated restructuring and avoid immediate closure logic.

### Plausible
- A strong package can reach **several hundred million euros annually**.
- In some cases, it may close most of the required gap.

### Depends on site-specific data
- Whether the package is **enough** depends on:
  - exact headcount and age profile,
  - plant utilization,
  - fixed-cost structure,
  - product mix,
  - transferability of roles,
  - union willingness on labor contribution,
  - and the actual return-on-sales target gap.
