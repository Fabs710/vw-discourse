From an industrial restructuring perspective, the key question is this:

**Can the company achieve a materially meaningful cost and capacity reduction in Germany without plant closures or compulsory redundancies?**
  
**Short answer:** yes, **to a point**. But the ceiling is real. If management is targeting a very large and durable step-down in fixed cost, then **workforce-adjustment measures alone can usually cover only part of the gap** unless they are combined with:
- lower working time,
- lower output footprint,
- strong voluntary exit,
- permanent organizational simplification,
- and some form of labor-cost contribution.

## 1) What can be achieved without closures?

There are five main levers:

### A. Natural attrition and non-replacement
This is the cleanest lever when plant security is to be preserved.

**Mechanism:**  
- retirements,
- resignations,
- end of fixed-term contracts,
- non-replacement of vacancies,
- internal redeployment to growth areas.

**Typical savings profile:**  
- modest in year 1,
- stronger over 2–4 years,
- but rarely enough alone if overcapacity is structural.

**Order of magnitude:**  
If a large site has a workforce of several thousand and normal attrition is, say, **2–4% per year**, then a 3–5 year program can eliminate a meaningful number of positions without layoffs.  
But in a high-skill industrial environment, you often cannot capture the full attrition rate because some losses must be backfilled in maintenance, logistics, quality, and critical production roles.

**Plausible cost effect:**  
- often **low hundreds of millions of euros per year** at group scale if carried through systematically,
- but that depends heavily on wage mix and headcount base.

### B. Voluntary exit programs
This is usually the next most powerful lever.

**Mechanism:**  
- severance offers,
- phased retirement,
- “window” programs,
- transfer to external entities,
- subsidized exits for older cohorts.

**Typical savings profile:**  
- fast headcount reduction,
- good political acceptability if truly voluntary,
- upfront cash cost can be substantial.

**Trade-off:**  
It reduces payroll faster than attrition, but it is expensive in the short term. The financial case works when the company needs **permanent structural reduction** and can amortize the exit cost over several years.

**Rule of thumb:**  
For an industrial employer in Germany, voluntary exit packages often cost roughly **0.5 to 1.5 years of total compensation per affected employee**, sometimes more for protected or long-tenured staff.  
Net present value can still be attractive if the role disappears permanently.

### C. Reduced working time and working-time flexibility
This is often underestimated.

**Mechanism:**  
- lower weekly hours,
- fewer shift layers,
- temporary or partial working-time cuts,
- short-time-like arrangements in downturns,
- annualized hours and flexible shifts,
- plant-level shift compression.

**What it can do:**  
If properly used, this can reduce labor cost by **several percentage points** without immediately cutting jobs.

**But there is a limit:**  
Working-time cuts save money only while output demand remains weak or can be rephased. If the company needs the same output with fewer hours, the saving evaporates unless productivity rises.

**Illustrative effect:**  
A **5% working-time reduction** can produce roughly a **5% labor-cost reduction on the relevant employee base**, before offsets.  
A **10% reduction** is already substantial and normally requires deep agreement, because it affects income, shift patterns, and plant utilization.

### D. Redeployment and qualification shifts
This creates savings by moving labor from declining to growing functions.

**Mechanism:**  
- move people from low-value assembly or back-office roles into battery, software support, logistics, maintenance, quality, or service operations,
- reduce external hiring,
- use retraining to replace attrition.

**Value:**  
This is often the best way to preserve sites while still reducing total headcount over time.

**Financial effect:**  
The direct savings are not as visible as closures, but the real value comes from:
- avoiding redundancy payments,
- avoiding hiring costs,
- reducing agency labor,
- retaining experienced staff in critical processes.

### E. Labor-cost contribution
This can come through:
- bonus restraint,
- deferred pay increases,
- temporary wage moderation,
- working-time concessions,
- suspension of allowances,
- reduction in variable compensation.

**Important distinction:**  
A one-off contribution helps liquidity; a durable reduction in wage cost helps competitiveness. Management usually needs the second, not just the first.

**Likely range:**  
A negotiated labor-cost contribution in a large German industrial company can sometimes deliver **2–6% of direct labor cost** if it includes working-time and pay elements.  
A very strong package might go higher, but that usually requires something in return: site security, investment commitments, or employment guarantees.

## 2) What kind of savings are realistic?

Without knowing the exact workforce and wage bill, I would frame this in scenarios.

### Scenario 1: Moderate restructuring without closures
Tools:
- attrition,
- voluntary exits,
- working-time reductions,
- redeployment,
- temporary labor-cost moderation.

**Likely outcome:**  
- headcount reduction: **5–10%** over 2–4 years,
- labor-cost reduction: **3–7%** on the relevant German base,
- more if overtime, agency labor, and shift inefficiencies are also cut.

This is often enough for a **competitive reset**, but not for a severe structural overcapacity problem.

### Scenario 2: Strong negotiated restructuring without closures
Tools:
- stronger voluntary exits,
- deeper working-time changes,
- sustained no-hire policy,
- internal capacity consolidation,
- targeted line or shift eliminations,
- wage moderation,
- use of qualification for redeployment.

**Likely outcome:**  
- headcount reduction: **10–20%** over 3–5 years,
- labor-cost reduction: **6–12%** on the affected workforce base,
- total cost reduction larger if fixed overhead and plant utilization improve.

This is the upper end of what is typically feasible **without plant closure or forced layoffs**, assuming real bargaining power on both sides.

### Scenario 3: Deep structural overcapacity problem
If management believes the required correction is in the range of **15%+ permanent cost base reduction** at German sites, then it becomes much harder to do it **entirely** without:
- closures,
- major footprint consolidation,
- or very large workforce concessions.

In that case, the non-closure package can still contribute materially, but it may not close the full gap.

## 3) The economics of “no closures, no compulsory redundancies”

This is the central point.

A no-closure strategy can work if the company’s problem is mainly:
- cyclical weakness,
- temporary EV transition mismatch,
- excess overtime or agency labor,
- duplicated layers,
- inefficient shift patterns,
- some overstaffing that can be absorbed through attrition.

It is much harder if the issue is:
- permanently too much fixed capacity,
- structurally high conversion cost,
- inferior plant economics,
- underutilized lines that will stay underutilized.

In the latter case, you can still preserve sites, but you usually need one or more of:
- significantly lower working hours,
- permanent wage moderation,
- major product allocation changes,
- very aggressive voluntary exits,
- or partial sub-scale production rationalization.

## 4) Key trade-offs

### Preserving site security
Pros:
- avoids political escalation,
- protects know-how,
- stabilizes operations,
- lowers legal and industrial-relations risk.

Cons:
- slower cost adjustment,
- potentially higher residual fixed cost,
- possible need for concessions elsewhere.

### Avoiding forced layoffs
Pros:
- maintains social peace,
- protects morale and productivity,
- reduces litigation and strike risk.

Cons:
- slower headcount reduction,
- exit packages can be expensive,
- some surplus roles remain longer.

### Using working-time reductions
Pros:
- quick to implement if negotiated,
- preserves jobs,
- can bridge cyclical weakness.

Cons:
- not a permanent cure if demand remains structurally lower,
- can create fatigue and income pressure,
- requires careful shift and capacity management.

### Voluntary exits
Pros:
- permanent reduction,
- politically easier than compulsory measures.

Cons:
- expensive upfront,
- risk of losing the wrong skills unless tightly designed.

---

## 5) My assessment of likely financial effects

If the question is whether a negotiated package without closures can make a meaningful dent in the German cost base, my answer is:

**Yes, it can.**
  
A well-designed package can plausibly deliver:
- **mid-single-digit percent reductions** in labor cost relatively quickly,
- and **high single-digit to low double-digit percent reductions** over several years if voluntary exits and working-time changes are substantial.

But if the target is a **very large structural reset**, then no-closure measures usually need to be paired with:
- product and volume commitments,
- permanent organizational simplification,
- and possibly a broader concession package from labor.

Put simply:

- **For a competitiveness bridge:** non-closure measures can be enough.
- **For a deep structural correction:** they may not be enough on their own.
