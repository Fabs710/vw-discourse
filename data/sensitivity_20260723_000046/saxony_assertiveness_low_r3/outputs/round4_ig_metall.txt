## To the works council
We are aligned on the essentials:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of the collective bargaining framework,
- and no attempt to make the workforce carry the entire crisis.

I agree with your core point: the latest expert view confirms that **a serious restructuring without closures is possible**. That matters. It means management cannot honestly claim there is only one route.

Where I want to sharpen the argument is this: the no-closure path is only acceptable if it is **real** and **durable**. That means:
- voluntary exits at scale,
- attrition and non-replacement,
- working-time flexibility,
- shift and line rationalization,
- qualification and redeployment,
- and a fair, time-limited employee contribution.

I support that.  
What I do **not** support is a “contribution” that becomes a permanent wage haircut while management keeps all strategic freedom and all sites remain under threat.

## To management
You are right about one thing: a no-closure package has a ceiling.  
But you are still using that truth too loosely.

A ceiling does **not** automatically justify closures or forced redundancies. It only means we need to know the actual gap. Until you can show that the gap cannot be closed through negotiated measures, your fallback to closures is premature.

You now have the specialist’s indication that a serious negotiated package can generate **several hundred million euros** in recurring savings. That is not pocket change. So you need to answer a simple question:

**What exact recurring savings gap remains after voluntary exits, working-time changes, productivity measures, and a limited labour contribution?**

Until that number is on the table, closures are not a solution — they are a threat.

And let me be equally clear: if management expects labour to accept sacrifice, then management and owners must also contribute visibly. That means:
- management restraint,
- owner support,
- and a serious future investment commitment.

We are not negotiating a one-sided reset where workers pay for strategic mistakes.

## To the controlling family and investors
I agree with you on one point: a symbolic compromise would be worthless.  
I disagree with you on the implication that “strong restructuring” must mean keeping closures in the drawer as the default discipline tool.

In this governance structure, a closure-first strategy is not the smart benchmark. It is a high-risk escalation that can trigger:
- industrial action,
- vetoes,
- deadlock,
- and ultimately value destruction.

So if you really care about value, then support the option that can be executed:
- keep the plants open,
- avoid compulsory redundancies,
- preserve co-determination,
- and agree a meaningful, negotiated labour contribution.

Do not confuse toughness with wisdom. A hard plan that cannot be implemented is not hard-headed; it is self-defeating.

## To the state government
I welcome your clear line against closures and forced redundancies. That remains important.

But I also want to be precise: “no closures” must not become a vague political slogan while pressure is simply shifted downward onto workers through ever-weaker guarantees. If the company is to remain a strong industrial base, then the settlement must be binding.

That means support for:
- a quantified no-closure restructuring,
- a fair employee contribution,
- binding product and investment commitments,
- and job security that is not quietly hollowed out.

## Where I stand on the numbers
The expert has said the no-closure route can plausibly deliver **several hundred million euros annually**. That is enough to justify a serious settlement discussion. It is **not** enough to accept open-ended concessions from workers.

My position is therefore:

### Acceptable
- voluntary exits
- attrition / non-replacement
- internal redeployment
- qualification and transfer schemes
- working-time reductions
- shift rationalization
- temporary and limited wage-related contributions
- binding product and investment commitments
- industrial peace tied to a real deal

### Not acceptable
- plant closures
- compulsory redundancies
- erosion of the collective wage framework
- permanent wage cuts disguised as “contribution”
- a deal where labour pays, but management and owners do not

## The real trade-off
The real question is not whether the company needs restructuring. It does.  
The real question is whether restructuring must be pursued **through destruction** or **through negotiated adjustment**.

We say:
- negotiate real savings,
- preserve jobs and sites,
- protect collective bargaining,
- and share the burden fairly.

That is the only responsible route.
