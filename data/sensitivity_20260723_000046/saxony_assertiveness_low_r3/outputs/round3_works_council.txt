Let me respond plainly.

The round-two summaries confirm exactly what we have been saying from the beginning: **there is a real restructuring path without closures and without compulsory redundancies.** That is not a slogan; it is the central fact of this discussion. If management now insists otherwise, then it is not because alternatives do not exist — it is because it prefers a harsher route.

## To management
You keep saying the non-closure route has a “ceiling.” Fine. But you have not shown that this ceiling is reached yet, and you certainly have not shown why the first response must be to threaten closures and forced layoffs.

Your own experts said a strong negotiated package can deliver:
- meaningful headcount reduction over time,
- mid- to high-single-digit labor cost reductions,
- and a real competitiveness bridge.

That is not trivial. That is a restructuring.  
So stop pretending that only closures count as seriousness.

And I will be even more direct: if management cannot close the gap without breaking the company’s social contract, then management needs to answer for the quality of its own strategic decisions — not ask the workforce to pay for them by losing plants and job security.

## To the controlling family and investors
You both keep saying the same thing in different language: “We don’t want a closure war, but we also don’t want a weak deal.”

I agree on one part: a weak cosmetic deal is not acceptable.

I disagree with the rest of the implication, which is that the burden must therefore shift onto labour through structural insecurity.

The expert evidence does **not** justify that conclusion. It shows:
- a serious no-closure restructuring can reduce costs materially,
- closures have higher execution risk in this governance structure,
- and a failed closure strategy destroys value.

So if you want value protection, then you should support the path that is **executable**. In this company, that means a negotiated restructuring that preserves sites, avoids compulsory redundancies, and protects co-determination.

If you insist on treating closures as the benchmark of seriousness, you are mistaking aggression for strategy.

## To the state government
I appreciate your continued support for site security and no forced redundancies. But let me be clear: that support must not become a gentle invitation for management to keep chipping away at guarantees until nothing is left.

The workforce side will not accept a deal that preserves the logo while hollowing out the job-security framework. We do not need symbolic promises. We need binding commitments.

If the state wants to defend the industrial base, then it should defend it **now** — not after sites have been weakened, jobs lost, and bargaining power eroded.

## To the union
Your position remains aligned with ours on the essentials, and I support that. But I want to underscore one point from the expert’s numbers: yes, working-time flexibility, voluntary exits, redeployment, and limited contributions can produce real savings.

That means we are not refusing restructuring. We are refusing the **wrong restructuring**.

We are prepared to discuss:
- internal redeployment,
- reduced working-time models,
- voluntary exit programs,
- qualification and transfer schemes,
- limited, time-bound employee contributions,
- and binding product and investment commitments.

We are **not** prepared to discuss:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- hollowing out of the job-security framework,
- or treating the workforce as the only adjustable cost item.

That line remains firm.

## The core issue: what counts as “enough”?
Management wants to define “enough” as whatever justifies closures.  
We define “enough” as whatever restores competitiveness **without destroying sites and jobs**.

Those are not the same test.

The expert has already said the no-closure route can deliver a material part of the required adjustment, especially if the company wants a competitiveness bridge and not an industrial wrecking operation. That is the real choice here.

So I reject the argument that:
- if non-closure measures do not solve every problem,
- then closures become inevitable.

That is lazy reasoning. It ignores governance, industrial relations, and the actual value of stability.

## What would be acceptable
If management wants our cooperation, then it must bring a package with all of the following:
1. **every German plant kept open**
2. **no compulsory redundancies**
3. **binding job-security protection through 2030**
4. **measurable capacity adjustment without closures**
5. **voluntary and negotiated workforce measures only**
6. **credible future product and investment commitments**
7. **a genuine co-determination role in shaping implementation**

That is the minimum basis for a serious settlement.

## What is not acceptable
These remain red lines:
- any German plant closure
- compulsory redundancies
- erosion of co-determination
- breaking the historic job-security framework
- using “competitiveness” as a pretext for unilateral social dismantling

If management cannot accept that, then there is no basis for trust and no basis for a responsible deal.

## Bottom line
I will not move just because the other parties repeat “structural change” more often.

The facts are:
- there are viable non-closure instruments,
- a closure-first strategy is politically and industrially hazardous,
- and the workforce will not surrender jobs and sites to prove a point about efficiency.

We are ready for a real restructuring.  
We are **not** ready to sacrifice the German plants or the company’s job-security framework.

If management wants a deal, it must come back with a **quantified no-closure package** that respects these red lines. If it cannot do that, then we will continue to resist — in governance, in negotiations, and, if necessary, through mobilisation of the workforce.
