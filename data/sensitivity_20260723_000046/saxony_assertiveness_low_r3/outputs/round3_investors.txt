We share the central objective of restoring competitive margins and a credible return on sales. On that point, we are aligned with management, the family holding, and the experts: **cosmetic savings are not enough**.

But we also need to be explicit about the economic test we care about as capital-market shareholders: **risk-adjusted value creation, not just headline toughness**. That means we will not support a package that is politically neat but leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive. At the same time, we will not support a closure-led confrontation that is likely to trigger vetoes, strikes, and execution failure.

## Where we agree
We agree that:
- the current German cost base is too high,
- a durable restructuring is necessary,
- a prolonged conflict would destroy value,
- and a credible product/investment plan must accompany any labor deal.

We also agree with the experts that **there is a real no-closure restructuring path** using:
- attrition,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- working-time changes,
- shift rationalization,
- and labor-cost moderation.

That is important, because it proves management cannot simply say “there is no alternative.”

## Where we disagree with the works council and union
We do **not** accept the claim that because a no-closure package can be real, it is automatically sufficient.

The expert testimony was clear: non-closure measures have a **ceiling**. That ceiling matters to us. If the package does not materially close the gap to competitiveness, then it is not enough — regardless of how defensible it is socially.

So we do not support:
- a symbolic no-closure deal,
- a settlement that preserves all legacy structures unchanged,
- or a package that leaves the company still below target on margin and return on sales.

We also do not support the idea that management, owners, or investors should simply absorb the financial downside while labor keeps the full protection framework intact. That is not burden-sharing; that is deferral.

## Where we disagree with management and the family holding
We also want to push back on any implied logic that says closures should remain the default benchmark of seriousness.

In this governance structure, that is not necessarily value-maximizing. A closure-based plan may look cleaner on paper, but if it runs into vetoes, industrial escalation, and prolonged deadlock, it can destroy more value than it creates.

So we will not support a closure-first strategy unless:
1. the non-closure route is demonstrably insufficient, and
2. the closure plan is actually executable without causing a value-destroying conflict.

That is a high bar, and it should be.

## Our position
We are prepared to support a **strong negotiated restructuring** if it is quantitatively credible and operationally executable.

That means we would look for:
- measurable recurring cost reduction,
- durable headcount and capacity adjustment,
- aggressive use of voluntary exits and attrition,
- working-time and shift flexibility,
- a real employee contribution,
- and binding investment/product commitments.

What we are **not** prepared to support is:
- a weak compromise that protects peace but not value,
- or a closure war that risks the business and the share price.

We need a precise comparison of:
- the **minimum recurring savings** required to restore competitive margins and hit the return-on-sales target,
- how much of that can realistically be achieved through **voluntary exits, working-time changes, redeployment, and productivity measures alone**,
- and where the remaining gap is that would require either deeper labor concessions or footprint adjustment.

That analysis should include:
- recurring savings,
- one-time severance/exit costs,
- execution risk,
- strike risk,
- and likely timing to payback.

## Bottom line
Our support goes to the option that best protects long-term equity value.

So our stance is:
- **Yes** to a serious, quantified no-closure restructuring if it closes most of the gap.
- **No** to a cosmetic compromise.
- **No** to a closure war that is likely to destroy value through conflict and execution failure.
- **Yes** to disciplined cost reduction, durable capacity adjustment, and a credible industrial plan.

If the next proposal can show, in numbers, that the no-closure path gets the company back to competitive margins with acceptable risk, we are open to it. If it cannot, then it is not sufficient.
