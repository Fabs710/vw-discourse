I’ll be equally direct.

The latest round confirms something important: **there is a real no-closure path, but it still has to prove it can close the structural gap**. That is exactly where we are. Not on slogans, not on symbols, but on whether the package is strong enough to restore competitiveness.

## To the works council
I agree with you on one point: **cosmetic measures are not enough**. A serious restructuring can absolutely include attrition, voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time changes, shift rationalization, and productivity gains.

Where I disagree is your conclusion that this automatically means closures are off the table. It does not.

You keep saying “the no-closure path exists, therefore no closures are needed.” That is not logic; it is a presumption. The question is not whether there are tools. The question is whether they are sufficient to:
- reduce structural overcapacity,
- lower the cost base durably,
- and restore the brand’s return-on-sales target.

If a no-closure package does that, fine. If it does not, then preserving every plant becomes a way of preserving the problem.

Also, let me be clear: **I do not accept a solution that keeps all sites open, preserves all guarantees through 2030, and still expects management to sign off on a fully competitive future.** That would be a short-term peace purchase, not a restructuring.

## To the union
I agree that workers should not pay for management mistakes alone. That is fair.

But I do **not** agree that a “limited, time-bound employee contribution” can be treated as sufficient regardless of the gap. If the company needs a durable reset, the contribution must be meaningful in the operating cost base, not just politically acceptable.

You now have an industrial finance view saying several hundred million euros of recurring savings may be achievable without closures. Good. That is a serious basis for negotiation. But it is **not** proof that the remaining gap is small. Until we see the quantified remaining gap, you cannot rule out deeper structural measures.

So here is the real issue:
- If the no-closure package closes most of the gap, we can talk about it.
- If it closes only part of the gap, then I will not pretend that partial relief equals competitiveness.

## To the state government
I appreciate your clear line against closures and forced redundancies. But your position cannot be: “No closures, and therefore management must somehow make the numbers work anyway.”

That would not be governance. It would be wishful thinking.

If you want to defend the industrial base, then you should support a package that is economically sufficient. That means being open to real capacity reduction, labor-cost reduction, and binding implementation. “No closures” is not a business model.

I also reject the suggestion that closures are merely a management threat. In this structure, they are a legitimate fallback if the alternative cannot deliver the required structural correction. I am not using them as theater. I am saying openly that the company cannot carry uncompetitive overcapacity forever.

## To the controlling family
You are right to reject a weak compromise. On that we agree.

But you should also stop treating closure avoidance as the primary objective. It is not. The primary objective is long-term value creation. If we can get that through a no-closure package, excellent. If not, then preserving every site becomes value-destructive sentimentality.

You say a closure war would be risky. I agree. That is why I have not said closures are the first choice. I have said they must remain a real option if the no-closure route does not deliver enough. That is not aggression for its own sake. It is discipline.

## To institutional investors
You are right to insist on risk-adjusted value creation. That is exactly why I am not willing to endorse a cosmetic labor deal.

But I also want to be clear: a package that is socially easier but economically insufficient is not risk-adjusted value creation either. It is deferred underperformance. If the no-closure path leaves the company still structurally above cost targets, the market will not reward the delay.

## What the expert input actually means
The expert’s numbers are useful, but they do **not** settle the question in the way the employee side claims.

What we know now is:
- a no-closure package can deliver real savings,
- those savings may be material,
- but there is still a ceiling,
- and sufficiency depends on the actual baseline and target gap.

That means I am not rejecting the no-closure path. I am rejecting the idea that it is automatically enough.

## What would move management
I remain open to a no-closure restructuring if it delivers all of the following:
1. substantial, recurring cost reduction
2. real capacity reduction
3. meaningful headcount reduction over time
4. lower fixed labor cost per vehicle
5. working-time and shift changes with permanent economic effect
6. voluntary exits at scale
7. binding milestones and enforceable implementation
8. a credible path to restore return-on-sales by 2030
9. a genuine labour contribution that is durable, not symbolic

That is the bar.

## What I will not accept
I will not accept:
- a settlement that keeps all German plants untouched and merely redistributes pain,
- a guarantee through 2030 that freezes the cost base,
- a deal that protects the footprint but leaves the brand uncompetitive,
- or a package that relies on management optimism instead of hard savings.
