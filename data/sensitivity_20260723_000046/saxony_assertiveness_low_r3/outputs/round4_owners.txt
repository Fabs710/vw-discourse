I’ll be direct.

The latest round does **not** yet give me comfort that the company has a solution which is both economically sufficient and executable. What it does show is that there is a **real** no-closure restructuring path. That matters. But “real” is not the same as “enough.”

## Where I agree

### With management
I agree that:
- cosmetic measures are not acceptable,
- the German cost base is too high,
- the company needs durable structural change,
- and future products and investment must be tied to any settlement.

I also agree that a deal that merely buys time is not a solution.

### With the experts
The specialist input is helpful because it cuts through the slogans. It confirms:
- voluntary exits, attrition, redeployment, working-time changes, and productivity measures can produce meaningful savings;
- a strong no-closure package can be substantial;
- but there is a ceiling, and sufficiency depends on the actual gap.

That is the right frame.

### With the state government
I agree that a closure war would be risky and could destroy value. I also agree that any solution must be socially and politically durable.

### With the works council and union
I agree that the workforce should not be treated as the only adjustable cost item, and I agree that industrial peace has value.

## Where I disagree

### With the works council and union
You keep arguing as if the existence of a no-closure path proves that closures are unnecessary. It does not.

What the expert said is more nuanced:
- the no-closure route can be real,
- it can generate several hundred million euros in recurring savings,
- but it may still be insufficient if the structural gap is large.

That means your red lines are **not yet justified as a complete solution**. They are a bargaining position. I respect that, but I do not confuse it with an economically proven endpoint.

I also do **not** accept the idea that binding job security through 2030 can remain untouched regardless of what the numbers say. If the company needs a deeper structural reset, then legacy guarantees cannot be treated as sacrosanct.

### With management
At the same time, I do **not** accept a closure-first posture as the default answer.

A closure strategy in this governance structure is not automatically value-creating. It can be value-destructive if it triggers vetoes, strikes, deadlock, and prolonged conflict. So if management wants to keep closures in play, it must prove two things:
1. the no-closure package does not close the gap,
2. and the closure alternative is actually executable without destroying value.

That bar is high, and it should be.

## My position
As controlling shareholder, my position remains:

- **No destructive closure war**
- **No weak compromise**
- **Yes to a serious negotiated restructuring**
- **Yes to a package that materially restores competitiveness**
- **No to a settlement that leaves the company structurally weak**

I am open to a **strong no-closure package**. But it must be strong enough to do the job.

## What I need to see next
The discussion is now stuck on a single unresolved question: **what is the remaining recurring savings gap after a strong no-closure package?**

Before anyone talks again about inevitability, I want that quantified.

## My criteria for support
I would support a package if it includes:

1. substantial, durable cost reduction  
2. measurable capacity reduction  
3. meaningful headcount reduction over time through attrition and voluntary exits  
4. working-time and shift flexibility with real economic effect  
5. a fair but limited labor contribution  
6. binding product and investment commitments  
7. a credible implementation timeline  
8. industrial peace sufficient to execute the plan  

If those conditions are met, I will support the deal through governance.

If they are not, I will not back a cosmetic settlement just because it is politically easier.

## Bottom line
I am not drifting toward consensus for its own sake.

My duty is to protect and grow the long-term value of the family holding’s core asset. That means:
- no closure war,
- no weak compromise,
- and no illusion that preserving every site automatically preserves value.

Bring me a package that is economically sufficient and executable, and I will back it. Otherwise, I will not pretend that a partial deal is a solution.
