I’ll respond directly.

## Where I agree
I agree with the expert, the investors, and even the state on one core point: **we need a durable restructuring, not a cosmetic deal**.

I also agree that:
- labor peace has value,
- future investment is essential,
- and a settlement that destroys know-how, morale, and delivery performance would be self-defeating.

So yes, I am not looking for conflict for its own sake. I am looking for a **credible reset of the cost base and capacity**.

## Where I disagree — fundamentally
What I do **not** accept is the attempt by labour to turn “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no weakening of guarantees” into a permanent veto on structural change.

That is not a restructuring strategy. That is a demand that management absorb an uncompetitive footprint indefinitely.

The numbers now make the issue clearer:
- the expert says a strong no-closure package might yield roughly **€300m–€700m per year**,
- whereas a mixed rationalization package can plausibly deliver **€400m–€1bn+** annually.

That is not a small difference. If we are serious about returning the brand to sustainable margins, funding future products, and restoring competitiveness by 2030, then **incremental voluntary measures may not be enough**.

## To the Works Council
You say closures are a threat, not a solution. I say closures are a **possible outcome of structural overcapacity**, and management would be irresponsible to rule them out in advance.

You keep asking for a “proof” that closures are unavoidable. Fine — but the burden is not on management to prove the impossible. The burden is on you to show how a no-closure model can close a large structural gap **and** preserve competitiveness over time.

So far, your answer is:
- working-time reduction,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- temporary wage moderation,
- productivity gains,
- investment commitments.

Those are useful tools. They are **not automatically sufficient**.

And I want to be blunt: **binding job security through 2030 without a real footprint reduction is not something I can accept** if the economics do not work. That would simply delay the problem and leave us with a weaker company.

## To the Union
I understand your red lines. I do not accept them as binding on the company’s survival.

You argue that closures destroy trust and that conflict would be value-destructive. That is true — if we had chosen closures lightly. We have not. We are confronting a structural cost problem, a shrinking European market, and severe pressure from lower-cost competitors.

You ask for:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no plant closures,
- no weakening of the collective framework.

That would be convenient, but it does not answer the actual question: **how do we materially lower fixed costs and capacity?**

A temporary labor-cost contribution is fine. A bonus restraint is fine. A working-time adjustment is fine. But if those measures do not produce a durable reset, then they are not enough.

## To the State Government
I respect your concern for regional stability, apprenticeships, and industrial presence. But I must be clear: **the state cannot support competitiveness while blocking the measures needed to achieve it**.

A blanket “no closures” stance may sound protective, but if it locks in overcapacity, it merely postpones the pain and increases the risk that we arrive later with a weaker business, fewer options, and less investment capacity.

If you want to be constructive, then support a serious restructuring package — not one that freezes the current structure.

## To the Controlling Family
You say closures are a last resort. I can live with that phrasing only if everyone understands what “last resort” means: it is still a **real** resort when the numbers justify it.

I agree that closure-first overreach would be damaging. But I will not accept a family-led position that supports “long-term value” in words while refusing the structural change needed to protect that value in reality.

If the owners want long-term value, then they need to back management in removing overcapacity, lowering labor cost, and restoring margins. A sentimental defense of every site is not governance.

## To Investors
You are right to focus on quantified, durable savings. The company does not need a symbolic settlement; it needs an operational reset.

I also take your point that a closure-led path carries execution and conflict risk. I am not ignoring that. But the real risk is also on the other side: **a weak compromise that keeps the footprint too large and the cost base too high**. That would burn time, capital, and credibility.

## The hard truth
Here is the truth we should stop pretending around:

- If a no-closure package truly closes the gap, then fine — show it in numbers.
- If it does not, then we must rationalize sites and reduce the workforce further.

I am not prepared to sign up to a deal that:
- preserves the same domestic footprint,
- preserves nearly the same cost base,
- and asks management to call that a turnaround.

That is not a turnaround. That is delay.

## What could move me
I remain open to alternatives **if** they are genuinely equivalent in structural effect. That means:
- verified savings by site,
- real, durable workforce reduction,
- meaningful labor-cost contribution,
- lower fixed cost,
- lower capacity,
- and a credible path back to margin targets.

If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then it needs to put forward something that actually compensates for them in industrial terms, not just politically.

## My position
Let me restate management’s position clearly:

- We need a **structural, sustainable reduction in cost and capacity**.
- We need a **material labor-cost contribution**.
- We need the ability to pursue **site rationalization, including closures where necessary**.
- We will not accept a cosmetic settlement that leaves the cost base uncompetitive.
- We need to free funds for future products and technology to 2030.

## Bottom line
I hear the red lines. I do not accept them.

If the workforce wants to avoid closures and deeper reductions, then bring a package that is:
- quantified,
- durable,
- and sufficient to restore competitiveness.

Until then, management will continue to insist that **all options remain open**, including plant closures and workforce reductions, because that is what the company’s future requires.
