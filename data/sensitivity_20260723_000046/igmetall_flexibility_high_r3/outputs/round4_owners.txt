I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the expert, and the investors on the central point: **this must be a real restructuring, not a cosmetic compromise**. The company needs a durable cost reset, better utilization, and restored margins. If we leave the structure materially uncompetitive, we destroy value over time.

I also agree with labour and the state that **a prolonged conflict would be value-destructive**. A strike cycle, legal escalation, and reputational damage would hurt the business, the workforce, and the family holding alike. So labour peace is not a side issue; it is part of value creation.

And I agree that this discussion must remain **quantified**. Slogans about “competitiveness” or “protection” are not enough.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the attempt by labour and the state to turn “no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” into a blanket veto regardless of the economics.

That is not responsible stewardship if the company’s cost base and footprint are structurally misaligned with demand.

At the same time, I do **not** support management treating plant closures as a reflex or bargaining club. If closures are used too early, you will provoke exactly the instability we all want to avoid. So I reject both extremes:
- closure as the first instinct
- closure ruled out in principle

## To management
You are right that the business needs a material reset. But you still have not made the case in a way that is strong enough for governance.

The expert’s range is useful:
- a strong no-closure package may deliver roughly **€300m–€700m per year**
- a more aggressive rationalization scenario may deliver materially more

That tells me two things:
1. A no-closure package is **not trivial**.
2. But it may still be **insufficient** if the structural gap is large.

So my position is not “close plants now.”  
My position is: **show the site-by-site bridge first**.

I want management to present:
- current gap to target
- savings from voluntary exits
- savings from working-time and flexibility measures
- savings from productivity and overhead reduction
- savings from product allocation and capex concentration
- residual gap after the strongest no-closure scenario

Until that exists, “all options remain open” is still a negotiating posture, not yet a governance case.

## To the works council and union
I understand your red lines, but I do **not** accept that they can stand forever without testing whether the no-closure route actually works.

You say:
- no plant closures
- no compulsory redundancies
- no weakening of co-determination
- no dismantling of job security

I respect the role behind those statements. But as a shareholder representative, I cannot treat them as binding if they prevent the company from regaining competitiveness.

What I can support is a **genuinely protected test package**:
- no closures for a defined period,
- no compulsory redundancies during that period,
- serious workforce contributions,
- site-by-site transparency,
- binding investment commitments,
- and a hard review against quantified milestones.

But I will not support an open-ended guarantee that freezes the structure and merely postpones the issue.

And I need to be clear: if the no-closure package does **not** close most of the gap on a durable basis, then it is not a complete answer. I will not pretend otherwise.

## To the regional state government
I understand your concern for regional stability, apprenticeships, and the company’s role as an industrial anchor. Those concerns are legitimate.

But I do **not** accept that the state can support competitiveness while simultaneously making meaningful adjustment impossible.

A “no closures” stance is only sustainable if the resulting package is enough to restore margins. If it is not, then the state would be helping preserve an uncompetitive structure, which ultimately harms the very jobs it seeks to protect.

So I support your insistence on social sustainability. I do **not** support using the blocking position to freeze necessary change.

## To the controlling family
You say closures are a last resort. I can work with that **only if “last resort” still means real resort**.

Right now, your position risks becoming too comfortable: you support management’s pressure, but want to preserve the appearance of restraint. That is not enough. Either the company is allowed to take structural measures if the numbers require them, or we are collectively creating a fiction that all sites can be preserved without materially changing the economics.

I agree with you on one thing: closure-first overreach would be damaging. We should not do that. But I will not accept a governance posture that rules out structural change in advance and then calls itself responsible ownership.

## On the expert’s benchmark
The expert’s benchmark is useful: roughly **70–80% of the structural gap** should be closed by a no-closure package for it to be credible.

I can accept that as an analytical test.

What I do **not** accept is using the test as a rhetorical shield to keep everything unchanged. If the test shows the gap remains too large, then the no-closure position is insufficient. If the test shows it closes the gap, then fine — that is the outcome we should support. But the numbers must decide.

## What would move me
I would be prepared to back a protected no-closure restructuring if it meets all of the following:
1. a quantified savings bridge by site,
2. a credible duration for the no-closure period,
3. binding investment and product commitments,
4. meaningful employee contributions,
5. measurable management contributions,
6. and a clear milestone review to determine whether the remaining gap is truly closed.

If that test shows the no-closure package is insufficient, then I will not support freezing the enterprise in an uncompetitive state simply to avoid difficult decisions.

## Bottom line
I am not retreating to the labour side’s red lines.  
I am also not endorsing closure as the default answer.

My position is simple:
**prove the economics, site by site.**
If a no-closure solution closes the gap, I can support it.  
If it does not, then the company must be allowed to take the structural measures required to restore competitiveness.

That is the only responsible governance stance for a long-term owner.
