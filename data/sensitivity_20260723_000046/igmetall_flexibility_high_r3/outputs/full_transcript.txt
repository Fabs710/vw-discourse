[Works Council (Betriebsrat) (Plant-level employee representation under co-determination) — Round 1]
As Works Council, I will be very clear from the outset:

We will not accept plant closures in Germany and we will not accept compulsory redundancies. That is the line.

The workforce has already carried a heavy burden in this transformation, and it is not acceptable to break the historic job-security commitments and then present closures and forced layoffs as if there were no alternative. There are alternatives — but they must be negotiated seriously and fairly.

Our priorities are:

- Keep every German plant open
- Secure all jobs through 2030
- Exclude compulsory layoffs
- Preserve co-determination and meaningful worker influence over any restructuring
- Ensure that any employee contribution is matched by binding investment and future-product commitments
- Avoid one-sided cost-cutting at the expense of the workforce while protecting the company’s long-term viability

We are prepared to discuss a restructuring path that avoids closures and forced redundancies, including capacity adjustments, working-time measures, internal redeployment, voluntary instruments, productivity improvements, and a fair negotiated contribution from management, owners, and workforce alike. But that must be on the basis of binding site guarantees, job guarantees, and respect for the people who built this company.

If management is coming here with a fait accompli — closures, mass job cuts, and the dismantling of guarantees — then you should expect determined resistance. We are not here to rubber-stamp a plan that sacrifices workers to fix a strategic failure at the top.

So my message is simple: no closures, no forced layoffs, no weakening of co-determination. If there is to be a real solution, it must protect jobs and sites first, and only then discuss how to make the company competitive.

## Executive view

# 1) What “closing the gap” usually means in plant economics

For an auto assembler, a simplified annual savings bridge looks like this:

- **Labor and overhead fixed cost**
- **Indirect labor**
- **Maintenance / utilities / plant overhead**
- **Logistics and internal transport**
- **Scrap, downtime, and line inefficiency**
- **Product-specific complexity**
- **Under-absorbed fixed costs from low utilization**

The biggest issue in underutilized European plants is usually not the direct assembly labor alone. It is the **fixed-cost absorption problem**:
- same plant, same tooling, same supervision, same engineering support,
- but fewer units spread over those costs.

That means:
- cutting headcount helps,
- but unless output and site structure also shrink, the **remaining fixed cost per unit stays high**.

# 2) A rough quantitative framework

Because the prompt does not provide actual volumes, I have to use **ranges** and industry-typical assumptions.

## A. Typical annual cost ranges per German assembly plant
For a modern OEM assembly site in Germany:
- **Direct + indirect labor:** roughly €150m–€400m/year depending on size and shift pattern
- **Plant overhead / facilities / utilities / maintenance:** €50m–€150m/year
- **Quality, logistics, engineering support, plant SG&A allocation:** €40m–€120m/year

So a meaningful restructuring of one large site can easily affect:
- **€250m–€600m/year** in total site economics, sometimes more if the plant is large and underutilized.

## B. Savings from workforce measures alone
A few order-of-magnitude examples:
- **1,000 average-cost employees** in German automotive manufacturing can represent roughly **€80k–€120k fully loaded cost per year**, so:
  - **€80m–€120m annual gross cost**
- But if some of that is replaced by overtime, temporary labor, subcontracting, or lost productivity, net savings may be materially lower.

So:
- **2,000 job reductions** might yield **€150m–€220m/year net**
- **5,000 job reductions** might yield **€350m–€550m/year net**

That is substantial, but on a group-scale restructuring it may still be **not enough** if the cost gap is measured in high hundreds of millions to low billions.

## C. Savings from plant closure / footprint exit
A closure or major consolidation can remove:
- direct labor
- indirect labor
- fixed plant overhead
- maintenance
- utilities
- local logistics
- site SG&A
- under-absorbed depreciation implications over time

Net annual savings for a site exit can plausibly range from:
- **€150m/year** for a mid-sized plant
to
- **€400m–€700m+/year** for a large, complex site,
depending on whether volume is relocated efficiently and whether duplicate capacity exists elsewhere.

That is why closures are often the only instrument that can rapidly deliver **step-change fixed-cost reduction**.

# 3) Can a no-closure alternative close the gap?

## Short answer
**Yes, but only under fairly strict conditions.**  
It requires enough of the following:
- major output consolidation,
- shift loss / capacity reduction,
- large voluntary exits,
- reduced labor cost per hour,
- lower complexity,
- and real investment discipline.

If those conditions are not met, then no-closure alternatives usually **reduce pain but not enough cost**.

## When it is feasible
A no-closure package is feasible when:
1. German plants are not all equally underutilized.
2. At least one or two sites can absorb more volume.
3. Product portfolio is being simplified.
4. Workforce can decline materially through attrition/voluntary exits.
5. Labor cost per vehicle can be reset enough to offset residual overcapacity.

## When it is not feasible
It fails when:
1. There are too many plants for the forecast volume.
2. EV transition has reduced platform diversity but not site count.
3. Domestic labor cost is structurally above competitor benchmarks.
4. The company insists on broad job guarantees without a matching output or cost reset.
5. There is not enough capex to reconfigure plants for new model mixes.

# 5) What level of savings is “enough”?

For a major OEM passenger-car brand with weak margins, management often needs something like:

# 6) Capacity utilization: the hidden driver

If plants are below efficient utilization, every plant remains expensive even if headcount falls.

## Rough rules of thumb
- Assembly plants are most efficient at high, stable utilization.
- Once utilization falls materially below a practical threshold, fixed cost per vehicle rises sharply.
- A plant at **50%–60% utilization** can become structurally uneconomic even if it still “looks active.”

If the company has several German plants competing for too little volume, then:
- shift reduction
- model consolidation
- and closure/rationalization
are the classic levers.

A no-closure plan must therefore show:
- which plant loses shifts,
- which plant gains volume,
- and how the network still achieves high enough utilization.

If nobody can show that, the no-closure option is probably an accounting compromise rather than an industrial solution.

# 7) Workforce implications

## Likely headcount actions by instrument

### Voluntary exits / early retirement
- Useful, but slow
- Best for larger, older workforces
- Often limited by demographics and union protections
- Can deliver **5%–12%** workforce reduction over time if well-funded

### Redeployment
- Good for smoothing transition
- Does not by itself reduce total labor cost unless labor is removed from the system
- Often a holding action, not a savings engine

### Working-time reductions
- Can lower payroll quickly
- But may reduce output or require more flexible scheduling
- Savings depend on whether fixed costs fall with hours

### Temporary wage concessions / bonuses
- Fast but often temporary
- Useful as bridge financing, not a structural fix unless converted into permanent base-cost change

### Productivity improvements
- Real, but usually gradual
- Helpful in combination
- Rarely enough alone to close a big structural gap

# 8) Capex needs

A true turnaround is not just a cost-cutting exercise. If sites are kept open, the company usually needs capex for:

- platform and line reconfiguration
- automation
- battery / EV integration
- logistics redesign
- digital manufacturing systems
- quality improvement and scrap reduction

If capex is underinvested, the company gets a cost cut today and a competitive problem tomorrow.

So a “no-closure” solution that is credible usually needs:
- **lower labor cost**
- **fewer sites used more efficiently**
- **and higher productivity capex**

That combination can be expensive up front, even if it saves money later.

## Short answer

# How to think about the savings bridge

A restructuring bridge in automotive plants usually has five components:

1. **Headcount reduction**
   - voluntary exits
   - retirement
   - redeployment
   - attrition

2. **Labor-cost measures**
   - temporary wage moderation
   - bonus reductions
   - removal of certain allowances
   - working-time flexibility
   - reduced paid hours

3. **Capacity utilization improvement**
   - higher line loading
   - shift-pattern changes
   - elimination of loss-making variants
   - internal consolidation of volumes

4. **Factory overhead reduction**
   - plant management
   - maintenance
   - logistics
   - indirect labor
   - utilities and services

5. **Product and investment allocation**
   - assigning future models to the remaining sites
   - reducing changeover complexity
   - avoiding capex duplication

A **no-closure** plan can address all five, but it generally cannot eliminate the full fixed-cost burden of a structurally redundant site the way a closure or conversion can.

## 1) What a “strong no-closure package” can plausibly deliver
For a large German OEM with several plants, the recurring savings from a no-closure package typically come from:

- voluntary exits / early retirement
- working-time reductions or flexibility
- bonus / wage moderation or temporary concessions
- overhead and indirect labor reduction
- redeployment and productivity actions
- procurement / logistics / energy savings
- better utilization and shift optimization

### Plausible recurring savings range
A serious package can often produce roughly:

- **€300m–€700m/year**: credible for a strong but politically constrained package
- **€700m–€1.0bn/year**: only if there is substantial headcount reduction, major flexibility gains, and meaningful site rationalization short of closures
- Above that: usually requires **actual footprint reduction** or very aggressive cost takeout beyond what “no closure” normally yields

So the expert’s “several hundred million euros” is reasonable. It is **material**, but not automatically sufficient.

---

## 2) What determines whether it is enough
The critical variable is the **starting structural gap** to target margin.

If the brand needs, for example:

- **€500m–€700m/year** of recurring improvement to reach an acceptable EBIT margin, then a no-closure package may be enough.
- **€1.0bn–€1.5bn+/year** is needed, then no-closure alone is usually **not enough** unless the company is already close to the target.

In practice, the gap usually comes from four buckets:

1. **Labor cost gap**
2. **Underutilized capacity / fixed-cost absorption**
3. **Product mix and plant assignment**
4. **Indirect overhead and complexity**

A no-closure package can address 1 and part of 4; it only partially addresses 2 and 3.

---

## 3) Why site rationalization often matters
If German plants are structurally underutilized, the economics are harsh because fixed costs remain high even after wage concessions.

Typical manufacturing logic:

- A plant running at **70–80% utilization** can often be fixed through working-time and product allocation.
- At **below ~60–65% utilization**, the plant often becomes a fixed-cost drag that no amount of modest labor concession fully cures.
- If multiple sites are below efficient scale, a **selective downsizing / conversion / consolidation** scenario can yield more than labor concessions alone.

That is why investors are pushing for a site-by-site bridge.

---

## 4) Incremental savings: no-closure vs selective rationalization vs full closure
At a high level, the hierarchy is usually:

### A. No-closure package
- Recurring savings: **€300m–€700m/year**
- One-time costs: moderate
- Execution risk: lower industrial conflict, but savings may be capped

### B. Selective rationalization
Examples: partial downsizing, line transfer, site conversion, merged operations, reduced shifts
- Incremental recurring savings over no-closure: **€200m–€600m/year**
- One-time costs: higher than no-closure
- Execution risk: medium
- Often the best compromise if one or two sites are structurally weak

### C. Full closure of one major site
- Incremental recurring savings over no-closure: often **€300m–€900m+/year**, depending on plant size and product transferability
- One-time costs: very high
- Execution risk: high
- Labor/political conflict risk: very high
- Delivery and knowledge-transfer risk can erode some of the modeled savings

So the market logic is: **closures maximize structural savings, but not always net value** once one-time costs, disruption, and delay are included.

---

## 5) One-time costs matter a lot
Any footprint reduction needs to be judged on **net present value**, not just annual savings.

### Typical one-time costs in Germany
- severance / social plan / transfer company costs
- pensions and bridging support
- relocation and ramp-up costs at receiving plants
- tooling transfer
- supplier requalification
- write-downs / impairment charges
- possible labor disruption costs

For a major site action, one-time costs can easily run into the **high hundreds of millions to several billions** depending on scale and asset base.

That means a move that saves €400m/year may still be unattractive if the one-time cost is extremely large and the savings are delayed by 2–3 years.

The right benchmark is not “no-closure vs closure” in the abstract. It is:

If yes:
- a no-closure deal is economically credible

If no:
- the company likely needs **selective rationalization**
- and if the gap is still large after that, then closure becomes more credible

That 70–80% threshold is not a law. It is a practical test of whether the residual gap is small enough to tolerate without undermining competitiveness.
