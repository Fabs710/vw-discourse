---

# 1) The benchmark scenario

The right benchmark is **not** “can we save something without closures?”  
It is:

> **Can a no-closure package close at least ~70–80% of the structural gap on a recurring basis, with acceptable utilization and no dependence on temporary concessions?**

If yes, no-closure is operationally credible.  
If no, then the remaining overcapacity is likely too large for labor-cost measures alone.

That benchmark is consistent with the earlier range already discussed:
- **Strong no-closure package:** roughly **€300m–€700m/year**
- **Selective rationalization / site reduction:** roughly **€700m–€1.5bn+/year**
- **Full closure-led reset:** potentially **€1bn+/year**, depending on the site footprint

---

# 2) What a no-closure package can realistically deliver

A strong no-closure package typically combines:

- voluntary exits / early retirement
- attrition management
- redeployment
- working-time reductions
- bonus moderation / allowance cuts
- overtime reduction
- productivity improvements
- product/volume concentration within existing sites

## Typical recurring savings by lever
These are rough sector ranges for a large German OEM:

- **Voluntary exits / attrition:**  
  **€80m–€250m/year**  
  Depends on headcount reduction scale, salary mix, and replacement avoidance.

- **Working-time changes / shorter hours / shift flexibility:**  
  **€50m–€150m/year**  
  Stronger if it reduces paid hours and premium pay materially.

- **Wage / bonus moderation / allowances:**  
  **€50m–€200m/year**  
  Often temporary or time-limited unless structurally embedded.

- **Productivity / process simplification / indirect labor reduction:**  
  **€75m–€250m/year**  
  Requires management execution and plant-level discipline.

- **Volume/product concentration without closure:**  
  **€50m–€150m/year**  
  Only works if transfer logistics and complexity are managed.

### Total plausible no-closure recurring savings:
**~€300m–€700m/year**

That is material, but it usually **does not fully eliminate** a structural overcapacity problem if one exists at multiple sites.

---

# 3) Why closures or rationalization usually add more value

A closure or deeper site rationalization does three things that a no-closure deal often cannot do:

1. **Removes fixed site overhead**
   - plant management
   - maintenance
   - utilities
   - site services
   - security
   - indirect labor

2. **Eliminates underutilized capacity**
   - reduces cost per unit
   - improves average utilization at remaining plants

3. **Cuts structural complexity**
   - fewer part flows
   - fewer logistics routes
   - fewer launch interdependencies
   - less duplicated capex

That is why a rationalization package often yields:
- **€700m–€1.5bn+/year recurring savings**
- higher EBIT uplift after the one-time charges
- but also more execution and industrial-relations risk

# 5) What the residual gap means

The key analytical question is:

> After the strongest no-closure package, how much gap remains to the company’s margin / EBIT target?

Example framework:

- **Starting structural gap to target:** say **€900m/year**
- **Strong no-closure package:** say **€500m/year**
- **Residual gap:** **€400m/year**

If the residual gap is only a few hundred million and can be closed through additional productivity and product actions, then a no-closure solution may be sufficient.

If the residual gap remains:
- **€500m+**
- and utilization is still weak
- and a plant is still structurally redundant

then the case for rationalization becomes much stronger.

# 6) EBIT impact by scenario

## Scenario A: Strong no-closure package
- **Recurring savings:** **€300m–€700m/year**
- **One-time cost:** moderate
- **EBIT effect:** meaningful, but possibly partial
- **Risk:** savings may be insufficient if overcapacity is deep

## Scenario B: Selective rationalization
- **Recurring savings:** **€700m–€1.5bn+/year**
- **One-time cost:** higher
- **EBIT effect:** typically stronger and more durable
- **Risk:** labor conflict, transfer disruption, launch risk

## Scenario C: Full closure-led restructuring
- **Recurring savings:** potentially **>€1bn/year**
- **One-time cost:** highest
- **EBIT effect:** strongest long-run reset
- **Risk:** maximum conflict and execution risk

---

# 7) Workforce implications

A no-closure restructuring typically involves:
- **3–5% headcount reduction** as a realistic first pass
- more if voluntary exits are heavily incentivized
- fewer compulsory measures, but also less structural cost reset

A rationalization scenario often implies:
- **larger workforce reduction**
- indirect labor cuts
- redeployment of some headcount to remaining plants
- shutdown of duplicated functions

The important issue is not just gross headcount, but **indirect labor and fixed-site burden**.  
If the company keeps all sites open, indirect cost tends to remain sticky.

---

# 8) Capex implications

A no-closure deal usually requires:
- capex to modernize retained plants
- tooling for future models
- automation / productivity capex
- supply-chain reconfiguration

A rationalization plan may require:
- transfer capex
- tooling relocation
- capacity expansion at winner sites
- write-downs / impairment on exiting sites

So:
- **No-closure** often preserves more continuity but may require **higher ongoing support capex** to keep all sites competitive.
- **Rationalization** can reduce future capex duplication but requires **large one-time restructuring charges**.

---

# 9) My bottom-line expert view

## A credible no-closure package is possible if:
- the company’s true structural gap is modest to moderate
- utilization can be lifted materially
- and a meaningful employee contribution is agreed

## It is probably insufficient if:
- multiple sites are underutilized
- future model allocation is weak
- the fixed-cost base is too high
- and the company needs a very large durable margin reset

So the decision should be made on the **residual gap after the strongest no-closure bridge**, not on rhetoric.

---

# 10) The single best benchmark scenario to use

If you need one benchmark for decision-making, I would use:

> **A site-by-site no-closure bridge that produces at least ~€500m/year recurring savings and lifts utilization enough that residual overcapacity is not structurally impairing EBIT.**

If that cannot be achieved, then the no-closure path is likely **economically exhausted**.
