I’ll be equally direct from the capital markets side.

We agree with management, and partly with the family holding, on the core diagnosis: **the current German cost base and capacity profile are not competitive enough**. If the company cannot restore margins, then the equity story deteriorates, capital allocation weakens, and the market will continue to discount the stock. That is not acceptable for us.

But we do **not** support a “hard restructuring” in name only, and we also do **not** support a package that protects every legacy structure while avoiding the actual savings needed. If the result is cosmetic, the share price will not recover and the business will still be carrying the same structural problem.

### Where we agree
- **Cost base must come down materially.**
- **Capacity has to be adjusted to demand.**
- **The turnaround must be durable, not temporary.**
- **Future investment matters** — but only if it is funded by a credible operating reset.
- **Industrial peace is valuable** if it avoids prolonged value destruction.

### Where we disagree
To the works council and union: a blanket “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no weakening of guarantees” line is not a viable investment case. If that position means the company keeps an uncompetitive structure, then it effectively asks shareholders to finance stagnation. We will not support that.

To management: if your restructuring proposal depends on maximal disruption but lacks a credible implementation path, then you will destroy value through conflict, delay, and execution risk. We are not interested in a headline “reset” that produces no lasting improvement in return on sales. Prolonged strikes and uncertainty are value destructive too.

To the state government: we understand the regional and political stakes, but preserving every site cannot be the governing principle if it leaves the enterprise structurally impaired. A company that is uncompetitive for too long will ultimately damage jobs, investment, and shareholder value more broadly than a disciplined adjustment would.

To the family holding: we agree that long-term value matters, but long-term value is not protected by defending the status quo. If the market sees continued drift, the cost of capital rises and the equity valuation suffers. We need a package that is credible enough to re-rate the stock.

### What we would support
We would support an outcome that delivers:
- **measurable, durable cost reductions**
- **real capacity reduction**
- **a substantial labor-cost contribution**
- **voluntary and socially managed workforce reductions where possible**
- **working-time and flexibility measures**
- **binding but targeted investment commitments**
- **a clear timeline with verifiable milestones**

What we need is not ideological purity on either side; we need **numbers that close the gap**.

### What we will not support
We will not support:
- a purely defensive deal that leaves margins structurally impaired
- endless negotiation without execution
- prolonged strikes that erode earnings and confidence
- investment commitments that are not matched by operating savings
- a solution that simply kicks the overcapacity problem into the future

### Our bottom line
We are prepared to back a tough restructuring, including deep workforce adjustment if necessary, **provided it is genuinely credible and disciplined**. We are not locked into any one instrument — closures, voluntary exits, working-time changes, redeployment — but the package must deliver the savings required to restore competitiveness.

If the parties can produce a settlement that materially improves margins and reduces cost sustainably, we will support it. If not, we will side with whichever path most convincingly restores economic discipline, even if that means backing management over the labor side. But we will not endorse a compromise that is simply a more polished version of the current problem.
