We hear your red lines. I will be equally clear from management’s side:

**We cannot accept a settlement that leaves the German cost base structurally uncompetitive.**  
A deal that simply preserves every site and every job guarantee while asking the business to absorb the same overcapacity and the same cost structure would be cosmetic. That is not an outcome we can take to the supervisory board, to the owners, or to the market.

### Where I agree
I agree on one point: **there must be a negotiated, socially responsible solution where possible.**  
We are not seeking disruption for its own sake. We also do not want prolonged strikes, and we understand that co-determination means the workforce has a real say.

I also agree that **investment in future products and technology matters**. A restructuring that cuts costs today but starves the company of future investment is self-defeating.

### Where I fundamentally disagree
I do **not** accept the idea that:
- all German plants must remain open,
- all jobs must be guaranteed through 2030,
- and no compulsory redundancies or closures can ever be part of the solution,

**while at the same time** the company is expected to hit its margin target and fund the transition.

That combination does not add up.

Management’s assessment is that:
- German factory costs are well above target,
- capacity is too high for the market we now face,
- and the European environment is shrinking while competition is intensifying.

If we do not address capacity and labor cost structurally, we will simply carry the crisis forward and make the eventual adjustment harsher.

### What must be on the table
To be constructive, a serious package has to include **real structural savings**, not symbolic ones. That means:

- **credible capacity reduction**
- **substantial labor-cost contribution**
- **durable productivity and flexibility measures**
- **workforce reduction at a scale that actually changes the cost base**
- and, where necessary, **plant closures or partial site rationalization**

I am not saying closures are the only answer. I am saying they cannot be ruled out in advance.

### On guarantees
I will also say plainly: **the old blanket job-security model is over**.  
If the workforce wants long-term security, it has to be based on a company that can survive competitively, not on promises that ignore economic reality.

So yes, we can discuss:
- voluntary exits,
- working-time adjustments,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- targeted investment commitments,
- and a negotiated employee contribution.

But those instruments must deliver **measurable, durable savings**. If they do not, then we are just postponing the problem.

### My position
Management is prepared to negotiate, but not to fake a solution.

So my message back is:
- **I will not sign up to a purely defensive package that rules out closures and leaves overcapacity untouched.**
- **I am open to alternatives only if they produce equivalent structural savings and a durable cost reset.**
- **If that cannot be achieved, then plant rationalization and deeper workforce reductions remain on the table.**

If the union and works council want to avoid closures, then they need to bring a package that genuinely closes the cost gap. Not slogans, not general assurances — real numbers, real savings, and binding commitments.
