I will respond directly.

## Where I agree
I agree with the Works Council on the central point: **a restructuring that relies on plant closures and compulsory redundancies is not acceptable**.  
I also agree with the state government that a purely short-term financial fix would be irresponsible, and with the general idea that future investment, product allocation, and industrial stability matter.

And yes: the company needs a durable solution, not a temporary patch. We are not denying the need for change.

## Where I disagree
I **do not** accept the repeated framing that closures and forced redundancies must remain “on the table” as a normal bargaining lever.

That is not a neutral technicality. It is a threat, and it shifts the burden of strategic failure onto the workforce.

If management, investors, or the family holding want to argue that the cost gap can only be closed through closures and dismissals, then they need to demonstrate that with hard numbers and a site-by-site case. So far, what I have heard is mostly assertion:
- the cost base is too high,
- capacity is too high,
- margins are too low,
- therefore workers must accept closures or job cuts.

That is not yet a legitimate restructuring plan. It is a demand for employees to absorb the pain first and ask questions later.

## To management
You say blanket job guarantees are incompatible with competitiveness. I say the opposite is also true: **a company that destroys trust, know-how, and labor peace will not become competitive by force alone**.

We are willing to discuss:
- working-time reductions,
- voluntary exit programs,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- flexibility measures,
- a temporary employee contribution,
- and even a negotiated suspension or reduction of bonuses and other wage components,

**but only in a package that protects sites, avoids compulsory redundancies, and preserves the collective bargaining framework**.

If you want real concessions from the workforce, then you need to put real protections on the table:
- binding site security,
- a clear no-compulsory-redundancy commitment,
- investment in future products,
- and a credible medium-term path for the German plants.

Without that, the workforce is being asked to pay for management’s strategic correction with no guarantee of security in return.

## To the works council
I support your line that there must be no plant closures and no forced layoffs. That is the right red line.  
But I would also emphasize: the union needs a settlement that is not just defensive, but durable.

That means we should be prepared to discuss a serious shared-sacrifice package, including:
- a temporary labor-cost contribution,
- capacity adjustments through working-time and output changes,
- voluntary instruments,
- and productivity measures,

provided the burden is shared fairly and the company commits to the future of the sites.

## To the state government
I appreciate your rejection of closure-led restructuring. But support in principle is not enough. If pressure escalates, we will need the state shareholder to use its position to prevent any solution built around plant closures and forced redundancies.

If the state believes the industrial base matters, then it must act accordingly when governance decisions are made.

## To the family holding
You say closures are a last resort. But once you keep them available, you already tilt the negotiation toward coercion. The workforce knows what that means in practice: accept cuts now, or risk losing the plant later.

That is not how you build a durable settlement.  
If you want long-term value, then support an agreement that preserves the industrial base and stabilizes labor relations rather than threatening them.

## To investors
You are right about one thing: a cosmetic solution is useless.  
But you are wrong if you believe that the best way to restore value is to pressure for maximum workforce pain. A conflict-heavy restructuring can destroy more value than it creates.

Industrial peace has economic value. Skills retention has economic value. Trust has economic value. If you force the company into a prolonged confrontation, you may get headline savings and weaker long-term performance.

## Our position
Let me state it clearly:

- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No plant closures**
- **No dismantling of the collective wage framework**
- **No one-sided burden on employees**

We are prepared to negotiate a serious contribution from the workforce, but only as part of a package where:
- management and owners also contribute,
- capacity is adjusted through socially responsible means,
- jobs and sites are protected,
- and future investment is secured.

If that cannot be done, then there is no fair settlement to support.

## What would move us
A credible deal would need to include:
- binding site guarantees,
- job security without compulsory redundancies,
- a time-limited employee contribution,
- measurable management and owner contributions,
- concrete investment commitments,
- and a restructuring path that avoids closures.

That is the minimum for a serious agreement.

If the other side insists that closures and forced layoffs remain necessary, then we will treat that as a rejection of a negotiated solution and respond accordingly.
