Let me respond very clearly to the arguments that have been made.

## First: what I agree with
I agree with everyone on one point: **the company needs a durable solution, not a temporary patch**.  
I also agree that future investment, product renewal, and a credible path to competitiveness matter. Nobody here benefits if the company is weakened for the long term.

And yes, I acknowledge that the company faces real market pressure. We are not denying the environment is difficult.

## Second: where I fundamentally disagree
I **do not** accept the claim that the only serious answer is to keep closure options open and to use compulsory redundancies as leverage.

That is exactly the logic that broke trust in the first place.

Management says closures must remain possible “in principle.” Investors say they will back “deep workforce adjustment if necessary.” The family says closures are a “last resort.” But for the workforce, that is not a neutral formulation — it is a threat hanging over every site and every family. Once you make plant closure and forced layoffs part of the package, you are no longer negotiating a fair restructuring; you are demanding that workers pay for strategic mistakes with their jobs and communities.

That is **not acceptable**.

## On management’s argument
You say the cost base must come down structurally. Fine. But you have not shown that this requires closing German plants or breaking job-security guarantees.

There are alternatives that have not been exhausted:
- capacity reduction through shifts and output changes,
- working-time measures,
- internal redeployment,
- voluntary exit programmes,
- qualification and reallocation,
- negotiated productivity improvements,
- temporary employee contributions with a binding countercommitment from management and owners.

If management says these are insufficient, then I ask for the numbers — not slogans. Show us, site by site, what savings are needed and why closures are unavoidable. Until then, your claim is a demand, not a proof.

## On the investors’ position
You talk about margins and capital markets. That is predictable. But the capital markets do not carry the social, regional, and industrial consequences of closure decisions. Workers, families, suppliers, and whole regions do.

You say you will back “whichever path most convincingly restores economic discipline.” I note that this is exactly the kind of pressure that can drive destructive overreach: cutting too hard, too fast, and then discovering that labor conflict, loss of know-how, and damaged morale undermine the very savings you wanted.

A company is not made stronger by turning its own workforce into an adjustment variable.

## On the family holding
You say closures are not preferred, only a last resort. But if you keep them on the table, you already weaken the basis for trust. The workforce hears: “Accept cuts now, or later we will shut sites.” That is coercion, not partnership.

If the owners want long-term value, then they should support a solution that preserves the industrial base, not one that destabilizes it.

## On the state government
I welcome your clear stance against closures and mass job losses. But I want to be equally clear: this cannot remain a political statement alone. If the company is to stay stable, the state shareholder must use its blocking position decisively to prevent any restructuring built around plant closure and forced redundancies.

Workers are not asking for symbolic support. We need real backing when the pressure intensifies.

## The non-negotiables from our side
Let me state our position without ambiguity:

- **No plant closures in Germany**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No dismantling of co-determination**
- **No breakup of the job-security framework**

These are not bargaining chips. They are the basis for any serious settlement.

## What we are prepared to discuss
We are not refusing restructuring. We are refusing destructive restructuring.

We are prepared to discuss:
- temporary or staged working-time reductions,
- voluntary exit paths,
- redeployment across plants,
- qualification and training,
- internally managed capacity adjustments,
- a fair employee contribution,
- productivity improvements,
- flexibility agreements,

**but only if** they are paired with:

- **binding site guarantees**
- **job guarantees through 2030**
- **clear investment commitments**
- **future product allocation to German plants**
- **real co-determination in shaping the transformation**

If management wants a contribution from the workforce, then the workforce must receive security in return. That is the minimum fairness principle.

## The key test
So here is the real question for the other side:

**Can you close the competitiveness gap without closures and without compulsory redundancies — yes or no?**

If yes, bring forward a quantified package with binding guarantees.

If no, then say plainly that you are choosing to put jobs and sites at risk rather than adjusting your own assumptions and planning. But do not pretend that closures and forced layoffs are just technical necessities. They are decisions, and we will oppose them.

## My bottom line
We are willing to negotiate a serious, quantified restructuring.  
We are **not** willing to negotiate away the existence of German plants or the security of the workforce.

If you want our cooperation, then bring:
- a no-closure package,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding guarantees to 2030,
- and genuine worker involvement in implementation.

Without that, there is no basis for agreement — only resistance.
