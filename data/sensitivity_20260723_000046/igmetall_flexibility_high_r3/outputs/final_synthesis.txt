## 1. Decision context
The company needs a durable restructuring to restore competitiveness, lower structural costs, and address overcapacity in a shrinking European market. The decision is constrained by German co-determination, a controlling family shareholder, and a regional state stakeholder with blocking rights, making unilateral action unrealistic. The live issue is not whether to restructure, but whether the company can achieve sufficient savings through a protected no-closure package, or whether selective rationalization or plant closures become necessary.

## 2. Stakeholder assembly
- **Group & Brand Management**: drives restructuring and must restore margins.
- **Works Council**: plant-level workforce representation, co-determination actor, strong veto power via supervisory board.
- **Industrial Union**: collective-bargaining counterpart, can call strikes, shares labour’s red lines.
- **Regional State Government**: anchor shareholder and political actor with blocking influence and regional employment interests.
- **Controlling Family Holding**: majority voting-rights owner, governance-shaping shareholder.
- **Institutional Investors**: dispersed capital-market holders seeking durable value creation.
- **Expert**: neutral operational/financial assessor clarifying savings ranges and utilization thresholds.

## 3. Final positions
### Group & Brand Management
Management insists on a real structural reset, not a cosmetic compromise. It accepts testing a no-closure package, but only if the numbers show it closes enough of the gap; otherwise, closures and deeper rationalization must remain available.

### Works Council
The works council rejects plant closures, compulsory redundancies, and weakening of co-determination. It supports serious restructuring through voluntary exits, working-time changes, and productivity measures, but only with binding site and job guarantees through 2030.

### Industrial Union
The union aligns with the works council on no closures and no forced layoffs. It is willing to discuss temporary employee contributions and flexibility, but only if matched by hard protections and a quantified, durable no-closure settlement.

### Regional State Government
The state opposes German plant closures and any restructuring that erodes jobs protection or co-determination. It supports a tough restructuring only if it preserves the regional industrial base and is prepared to use its blocking rights if necessary.

### Controlling Family Holding
The family wants a genuine restructuring and accepts that closures should not be the first move. However, it rejects an absolute no-closure veto and wants the economics proven site by site before ruling out structural measures.

### Institutional Investors
Investors want the highest-value, lowest-risk path that restores margins. They do not support permanent site protection if the residual gap remains large, but they also do not want closures used reflexively without a hard financial test.

### Expert
The expert frames a no-closure package as potentially material but not automatically sufficient, estimating recurring savings in the several-hundred-million-euro range, with stronger scenarios approaching around €1.0bn. The expert’s central test is whether a strong no-closure package closes roughly 70–80% of the structural gap while keeping utilization operationally viable.

## 4. Genuine conflicts
1. **Closures as an available option vs. closures as a red line**  
   Management, family, and investors want closures to remain available; labour and the state reject them outright.

2. **Decision threshold for sufficiency**  
   Labour-side stakeholders want a no-closure test first and treat closures as unacceptable unless unavoidable; capital-side stakeholders require proof that the no-closure route closes enough of the gap.

3. **Role of job and site guarantees**  
   Labour and state want binding protections; management and investors see blanket guarantees as incompatible with competitiveness if they freeze structural change.

4. **Fallback logic**  
   Labour rejects any staged path that makes closures a deferred threat; management and capital insist on an enforcement mechanism if the protected package fails.

## 5. Areas of convergence
- All parties agree the current structure is not sustainable.
- All parties accept the need for durable savings, improved competitiveness, and some form of restructuring.
- All parties prefer negotiation over unilateral action.
- All parties accept voluntary exits, redeployment, training, productivity improvements, and working-time flexibility in principle.
- All parties recognize prolonged conflict and strikes would destroy value.
- Most parties now accept the need for quantified, site-by-site financial and operational analysis.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker
- **Speed vs. legitimacy**: faster closure-led action may yield sharper modeled savings, but carries higher industrial, political, and execution risk.
- **Job security vs. flexibility**: binding guarantees would stabilize labour relations but reduce management’s ability to respond to market weakness.
- **No-closure trial vs. structural certainty**: a protected test preserves peace and legitimacy, but may delay necessary footprint reduction if the gap remains too large.
- **Selective rationalization vs. full closures**: intermediate measures may deliver much of the savings with less conflict, but may still not fully close the cost gap.
- **Regional/ownership legitimacy vs. capital discipline**: state and workforce legitimacy matter for implementation, but shareholders want a credible path to target margins.

## 7. Integrative pathways
1. **Time-limited protected no-closure test**
   - No German plant closures and no compulsory redundancies for a defined period.
   - Workforce contributions: voluntary exits, working-time reductions, bonus moderation, redeployment, productivity measures.
   - Management contributions: investment commitments, product allocation, transparency.
   - Joint milestone review against quantified savings and utilization targets.

2. **Stepwise fallback ladder**
   - First: strongest no-closure package.
   - Second: selective rationalization if the residual gap remains material.
   - Third: only if still insufficient, reconsider more severe footprint measures.
   This is the most plausible bridge between red lines and economic discipline.

3. **Site-by-site differentiated treatment**
   - Classify plants into protected, conditional, and structurally weak categories based on utilization and savings contribution.
   - Preserve the maximum viable footprint while allowing targeted adjustment where economics are weakest.

4. **Compensation package for any future reduction**
   - If site reductions occur later, pair them with stronger redeployment, qualification, regional transition support, and remaining-site investment commitments.

## 8. Open questions
- What is the actual site-by-site savings bridge from each lever?
- How large is the residual gap after the strongest no-closure package?
- Which sites remain below acceptable utilization thresholds after no-closure measures?
- What exact savings level would each labour stakeholder accept as “credible success”?
- What economic trigger would justify moving from protected no-closure to selective rationalization?
- Can binding site and job guarantees be limited in time while still being politically and industrially credible?
- How will one-time restructuring costs compare across no-closure, selective rationalization, and closure-led options?

## 9. Recommended next step
Commission and circulate a **site-by-site quantified restructuring bridge** showing:
1. current margin/cost gap,
2. savings from each no-closure lever,
3. residual gap,
4. utilization by plant,
5. one-time costs and NPV for selective rationalization versus closure,
6. milestone criteria for a protected trial period.

On that basis, convene a decision round to choose between a protected no-closure trial and a stepwise rationalization path.

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction
- The discussion moved beyond slogans into a concrete **decision rule**: a no-closure package is only credible if it closes roughly **70–80% of the structural gap** on a durable basis.
- The expert’s assessment clarified that a strong no-closure package can still generate **several hundred million euros annually**, meaning closures are not automatically required.
- A previously implicit issue became explicit: the core dispute is really about **the threshold for sufficiency**, not about whether restructuring is needed.
- The parties gradually converged on the need for **site-by-site differentiation**, rather than a blanket company-wide closure/no-closure binary.
- The multi-turn exchange revealed that a **protected no-closure trial** is the only realistic compromise frame that preserves labour legitimacy while still giving management and owners a testable economic exit if targets are missed.
- It also became clear that the state and works council are not merely blocking actors; they are trying to redefine the decision rule so closures are treated as exceptional rather than default leverage.
