I’ll respond plainly.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on one essential point: **this company needs a durable restructuring, not a cosmetic deal**.  
I also agree that:
- future investment matters,
- labor peace matters,
- and any solution has to be implementable, not just politically convenient.

I am not dismissing the role of co-determination, the state shareholder, or the workforce. But recognition of those facts does not change the economic reality we face.

## Where I fundamentally disagree
I do **not** accept the repeated claim that we can close the competitiveness gap while ruling out:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- and a real reduction in labor cost and capacity.

That position may be understandable from a labor standpoint, but it is **not sufficient** to restore the company’s competitiveness.

The core problem remains:
- German factory costs are too high,
- overcapacity is real,
- the market is shrinking,
- and the brand must return to a sustainable margin path.

If we preserve the full structure and simply shift the burden around, we will still not have solved the problem.

## To the Works Council
You say plant closures and compulsory redundancies are non-negotiable red lines. I understand why you say that. But I need to be equally clear: **if those lines make structural adjustment impossible, then they are incompatible with the company’s survival as a competitive industrial business**.

You ask for a “no-closure package” and “no compulsory redundancies” while also demanding binding job security through 2030. That combination is exactly what I cannot accept unless you can show, in hard numbers, that it delivers equivalent structural savings.

So far, I have heard a lot of principle and very little that closes the cost gap.

You list alternatives:
- working-time changes,
- redeployment,
- voluntary exits,
- productivity measures,
- temporary employee contributions.

Those may be part of a package. But I am saying again: **they must produce materially lower structural costs and a smaller footprint**. If they do not, then they are not a solution.

## To the Union
I agree that labor peace has value. I agree that know-how, skills, and trust matter. But I reject the suggestion that management is using closures only as a threat. No — closures remain on the table because the economics are serious.

You say the workforce should not pay for management’s mistakes. I understand the sentiment. But the business cannot afford a moral accounting exercise. It needs a viable operating model.

You are willing to discuss:
- temporary labor-cost contributions,
- bonus restraint,
- flexibility,
- voluntary exits.

That is constructive, but it is **not enough by itself** unless it produces:
- a durable cost reset,
- real capacity reduction,
- and a materially lower fixed-cost base.

If the union’s position is “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no dismantling of the collective framework,” then what exactly is left that changes the economics at scale? That is the question you have not answered.

## To the State Government
I respect your concern for regional stability, apprenticeship pipelines, and industrial capacity. Those concerns are legitimate. But I must challenge the implication that the state can support competitiveness while simultaneously blocking the kind of restructuring needed to achieve it.

You cannot ask management to restore margins, fund future investment, and carry uncompetitive capacity indefinitely. That is not stewardship; that is delay.

If the state wants the company to remain an industrial anchor, then it must accept that some sites, some capacity, and some employment will have to adjust. Otherwise the burden will simply accumulate until the company is weaker, not stronger.

## To the Controlling Family
You are right to emphasize long-term value and the danger of uncontrolled conflict. I agree that a destructive strike cycle would be bad for the company. But I do not accept the idea that “closures are a last resort” in a way that still leaves everyone thinking they can avoid hard decisions.

A last resort is still a real resort.

If the company needs a credible reset, then the owners must support management in pursuing it — not just support a vaguely “balanced” outcome that sounds better than the status quo but leaves the cost base uncompetitive.

You say you will not support a cosmetic package. Good. Then we should be aligned on the key point: **the restructuring must materially change the economics**. That may mean closures, site rationalization, and deeper workforce reduction.

## To the Institutional Investors
I agree with you on the central financial point: if the operating model does not improve, the equity story does not improve. We cannot ask capital markets to fund stagnation.

I also agree that conflict and execution risk destroy value. That is exactly why I am pressing for a settlement that is decisive enough to avoid permanent drift.

Where I differ from your tone is this: it is not enough to say “we back whichever path restores discipline.” The company needs a path that is not just disciplined, but achievable under our governance reality. That means a package that can actually pass the supervisory board and survive industrial resistance.

So yes, we need:
- measurable cost reduction,
- capacity reduction,
- workforce adjustment,
- and a credible implementation path.

If the labor side refuses to move on the core economics, then management and the owners will have to insist on stronger measures.

## My position
Let me state management’s position without ambiguity:

- We need a **structural**, not temporary, reduction in cost and capacity.
- We need a **material labor-cost contribution**.
- We need **workforce reduction at scale**.
- We need the freedom to include **plant closures or site rationalization** where necessary.
- We will not accept a package that preserves the current footprint and simply re-labels it as a turnaround.

## On what could move us
I remain open to alternatives **if** they genuinely deliver equivalent structural savings. That means:
- verified and quantified capacity reduction,
- a meaningful and durable labor-cost reset,
- binding productivity and flexibility commitments,
- and a credible path to restore return on sales.

But I am not prepared to accept:
- blanket job guarantees through 2030,
- a ban on closures,
- and no compulsory redundancies,
while expecting the same business to become competitive again.

That is not a serious solution.

## Bottom line
I hear the resistance. I do not accept it as sufficient.

If the labor side wants to avoid closures and forced redundancies, then bring forward a package that **actually closes the gap**:
- in numbers,
- in timing,
- and in structural effect.

Until then, management’s position remains:
**all options must remain open, including plant closures and workforce reductions, because the company’s competitiveness comes first.**
