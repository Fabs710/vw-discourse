We will be direct.

## Where we agree
We agree with management, the family, the expert, and to a degree even the state and labour side on one basic point: **this company needs a durable restructuring, not a cosmetic settlement**.

We also agree that:
- prolonged strikes destroy value,
- skills and delivery performance matter,
- future investment matters,
- and the discussion has to be **quantified** rather than ideological.

The expert’s ranges are exactly the kind of discipline we need:
- a strong no-closure package might deliver about **€300m–€700m per year**
- a selective rationalization path could plausibly deliver **€700m–€1.5bn+ per year**
- closure-led restructuring is likely the strongest reset, but also the highest execution-risk route

That is the right frame. It tells us the debate is not “restructure or not,” but whether the no-closure option is enough to restore competitive margins.

## Where we disagree
We do **not** support the labour side’s attempt to turn “no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” into a permanent economic veto.

If the residual structural gap is still large after a serious no-closure package, then insisting on protecting every site regardless of economics is not stewardship — it is value destruction.

At the same time, we do **not** support management treating closures as a reflex or using them as a bargaining club. A closure-first move that triggers a long strike, legal conflict, or operational disruption could destroy some of the value it is meant to create.

So our position is not:
- “close plants at any cost,”
and it is not:
- “protect every site no matter what.”

Our position is:
**restore competitive margins with the lowest-risk path that actually closes the gap.**

## To management
You are right that the company needs a material cost and capacity reset. We agree with that.

But you have not yet given the market the hard evidence we need:
- current gap to target
- savings from voluntary exits
- savings from working-time measures
- savings from productivity and overhead reduction
- savings from product allocation and capex concentration
- residual gap after the strongest no-closure package

That bridge is essential.

If the residual gap remains large, then the case for selective site rationalization strengthens. If the residual gap is modest, then a no-closure solution becomes more credible.

What we will **not** support is an opaque “all options open” posture used as pressure without a hard financial test.

## To the Works Council and Union
We understand why you refuse closures and compulsory redundancies. That is your role.

But your red lines only remain viable if a no-closure package is actually capable of restoring margins. Right now, your position is still more normative than financial.

A serious no-closure solution would need to show:
- recurring annual savings near the upper end of the plausible range,
- durable utilization improvements,
- a credible payback period,
- and a residual gap small enough to be acceptable to capital providers.

If that cannot be shown, then shareholders should not be asked to bankroll underperformance indefinitely.

We do agree with you on one point: if workers are asked to contribute, management and owners must contribute as well. Employee concessions cannot be one-sided. But the burden has to be judged against the real economics, not sentiment.

## To the state government
We respect your concern for regional stability, apprenticeships, and the company’s role as an industrial anchor.

But the state cannot support competitiveness while blocking meaningful adjustment if the numbers require it. A “no closures” stance is only sustainable if the resulting package is financially sufficient.

If it is not sufficient, then the state is effectively helping preserve an uncompetitive structure — which ultimately weakens the region’s jobs and the company’s investment capacity.

## To the controlling family
You are right that closure-first overreach would be dangerous. We agree.

But “closures are a last resort” only works if it remains a real resort when the numbers justify it. We do not accept a governance position that rules out structural change in advance and then calls itself disciplined ownership.

What we need from you, and from management, is not rhetoric but proof:
- site-by-site economics
- actual residual gap
- actual savings bridge
- actual investment implications

## What would move us
We would support a **protected no-closure trial** only if it is genuinely testable and time-limited, with:
- no German plant closures during the trial period,
- no compulsory redundancies during that period,
- voluntary exits, working-time changes, productivity measures, and redeployment,
- binding transparency on site economics,
- and a pre-agreed review against hard financial milestones.

But we will be blunt: this only works if the review is real. If the no-closure package does not close most of the structural gap, then the company must be allowed to move to selective rationalization. We will not support an arrangement that leaves the business structurally impaired just to avoid difficult decisions.

## Bottom line
We are not aligned with the labour side’s absolute red lines.  
We are also not aligned with a closure-at-any-cost approach.

We are aligned with **risk-adjusted value creation**.

So our position is:
1. show the site-by-site savings bridge,
2. test the strongest no-closure package,
3. if it closes the gap, support it,
4. if it does not, move to selective rationalization rather than prolonging value destruction.

That is the disciplined capital-market view.
