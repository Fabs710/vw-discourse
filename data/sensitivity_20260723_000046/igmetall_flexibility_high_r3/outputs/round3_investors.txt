We will be blunt.

## Where we agree
We agree with management, the expert, the family holding, and even the state on the basic diagnosis: **this company needs a durable structural reset**.  
A cosmetic deal that leaves the cost base uncompetitive would destroy value, and we would not support it.

We also agree that:
- **industrial peace has value**
- **skills retention matters**
- **future investment matters**
- **delay is costly**
- **the discussion must be quantified, not ideological**

The expert’s ranges are useful because they anchor the debate in economics:
- a strong no-closure package might deliver roughly **€300m–€700m per year**
- a mixed rationalization package could plausibly deliver **€400m–€1bn+ per year**

That is the right frame. It confirms that the real issue is not whether restructuring is needed, but whether the no-closure camp can actually close the gap.

## Where we disagree
We do **not** accept the labour side’s position that closures and compulsory redundancies can be ruled out in principle regardless of the numbers.

That is not a credible capital-allocation position if the business remains structurally overbuilt. If the savings gap is large, then insisting on no closures and no compulsory redundancies simply asks shareholders to tolerate permanently impaired returns.

At the same time, we do **not** support closure for its own sake. A closure-first approach that triggers prolonged strikes, operational disruption, and brand damage could destroy part of the very value it is meant to create.

So our position is not:
- “close plants at any cost,”
and it is not:
- “protect every site regardless of economics.”

Our position is:
**deliver the strongest credible path to sustainable margins, with the lowest execution risk that still actually closes the gap.**

## To management
You are right to insist that the restructuring must materially change the economics.  
You are also right that the current footprint and cost structure may be unsustainable.

But we need more than insistence. We need the **actual bridge**:
- current gap to target
- savings from voluntary exits
- savings from working-time measures
- savings from productivity improvements
- savings from product concentration and capex
- residual gap after the strongest no-closure package

If that residual gap is still large, then your case for site rationalization strengthens materially. If it is small, then a no-closure solution becomes more credible.

What we will **not** support is an opaque “all options open” stance that is used as leverage without a hard financial test. We need a financeable plan, not just bargaining pressure.

## To the Works Council
We understand why you refuse closures and compulsory redundancies. That is your role.  
But your red lines are only sustainable if a no-closure package truly closes the gap.

Right now, your position is still more normative than financial. Saying “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no weakening of co-determination” does not answer the question of how the company gets back to competitive margins if the structural gap remains too large.

If you want investor support for a protected no-closure path, bring a package with:
- quantified recurring savings,
- a credible duration,
- measurable headcount and cost impact,
- binding productivity steps,
- and a clear residual gap that is demonstrably acceptable.

If that cannot be shown, then the no-closure stance becomes a request for shareholders to finance underperformance.

## To the Union
We agree that labour peace and know-how matter.  
We also agree that the workforce should not be treated as the sole adjustment variable.

But we do **not** support an unconditional ban on closures and deeper workforce reduction if the economics require them. A temporary employee contribution, bonus restraint, and working-time flexibility are useful only if they are part of a package that truly restores margins.

If the gap is large and the no-closure measures do not close it, then the union’s position is not a solution — it is a delay mechanism.

## To the State Government
We appreciate the concern for regional employment and industrial stability. That concern is legitimate.

But we do **not** accept the implication that the state can block meaningful adjustment and still claim to be protecting long-term employment. If the company remains structurally uncompetitive, the result is weaker investment, weaker margins, and ultimately weaker jobs.

A “no blanket closures” stance is fine **only if** the resulting package is financially sufficient. If it is not, then the state is effectively subsidizing decline.

## To the Controlling Family
You are right that closure-first overreach would be dangerous.  
You are also right that long-term value, not short-term optics, should guide the decision.

But the family’s “closures are a last resort” language only helps if it is backed by a real willingness to allow the numbers to decide. If a protected no-closure package works, great. If it does not, then rationalization must be allowed.

We do not need reassurance; we need governance discipline.

## Our position
Let us state it clearly:

- **We support a real restructuring**
- **We do not support a cosmetic settlement**
- **We do not support preserving an uncompetitive cost base**
- **We do not support a value-destructive strike cycle**
- **We do not support either ideological closures or ideological no-closure guarantees**

We will back whichever path:
1. restores competitive margins,
2. produces durable cost reduction,
3. is credible to execute,
4. and does not burn more value than it creates.

## What would move us
A no-closure package would need to show:
- recurring annual savings near the upper end of the plausible range,
- a credible payback,
- sustained utilization improvements,
- and a residual gap small enough to be acceptable.

If it cannot do that, then management and owners should stop pretending closures are off-limits.

A rationalization package would need to show:
- site-by-site savings,
- manageable execution risk,
- and a labor-relations path that does not destroy the entire value case.

That is the benchmark.

## Bottom line
We are not on the labour side’s side just because they speak the language of protection.  
We are not on management’s side just because they speak the language of urgency.

We are on the side of **risk-adjusted value creation**.

If a no-closure solution is truly sufficient, we will support it.  
If it is not sufficient, then the economically honest answer is site rationalization and deeper workforce adjustment.

What we will not support is a deal that leaves the company structurally impaired and calls that a compromise.
