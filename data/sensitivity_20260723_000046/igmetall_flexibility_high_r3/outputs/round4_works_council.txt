## What has changed
What has changed is not my position, but the level of pressure to dress up closures as “structured realism.”

I do **not** accept that framing.

So far, that proof has **not** been provided.

## Where I agree
I agree with the expert and with several others on one point: this cannot be cosmetic.  
The company needs durable savings, not a public-relations exercise.

I also agree that:
- skills retention matters,
- industrial peace matters,
- investment matters,
- and a weak compromise that solves nothing would be irresponsible.

That is exactly why I insist on a **real no-closure restructuring** instead of a threat-led one.

## Where I strongly disagree

### 1) I reject the idea that closures are “just another option”
For the workforce, German plant closures are not a neutral tool. They mean broken promises, destroyed local industrial capacity, and a precedent that endangers every site.

Management and investors keep repeating “all options must remain open.”  
I say: **not if that means treating the workforce’s jobs as a bargaining chip from the outset.**

If you want trust, then stop using closures as leverage before the no-closure alternative has been properly tested.

### 2) I reject the assumption that a protected no-closure package must “fail” unless it reaches some arbitrary threshold
The expert’s ranges are useful, but they are still ranges. A range is not a mandate to shut plants.

I do **not** accept a decision rule that says:
- first force the workforce to contribute,
- then if management still wants more, reopen closures.

That is just a staged path to the same outcome.

### 3) I reject the attempt to separate “competitiveness” from “job security”
A company that solves competitiveness by permanently weakening job security and co-determination is not strengthening its future — it is degrading its industrial settlement.

If management and owners want a sustainable turnaround, then they must accept that **German plants remain open** and **no compulsory redundancies occur**. Anything else is not a serious social compromise; it is a rollback.

I will be direct: I do **not** support any framework that treats closure as an automatic second-stage outcome if milestones are missed.

Why? Because once that door is formally opened, the pressure simply shifts. The workforce is then told:
“Concede now, or lose a site later.”

That is not a genuine protection regime. It is deferred coercion.

If management genuinely wants a test, then the test must be:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding site guarantees,
- transparent numbers,
- and a proper co-determined restructuring effort.

Not a trap door.

- substantial employee contributions that are time-limited,
- management and owner contributions that are real,
- investment commitments,
- future model allocation,
- and full co-determination in implementation.

That is the only framework under which the workforce can responsibly cooperate.

Everything else is a demand for trust without security.
