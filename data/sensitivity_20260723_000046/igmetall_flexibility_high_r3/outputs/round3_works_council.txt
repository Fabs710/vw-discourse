Let me be equally clear in round 3:

## First: what has changed
What has changed is not the substance of the conflict, but that the numbers are now being used to pressure the workforce toward accepting closures as “economically honest.” I reject that logic.

The expert has shown ranges, not inevitabilities. A range is not a mandate to shut plants. It is a reason to test the no-closure alternative properly, with real management commitments and real owner contributions.

## Where I agree
I agree with the expert and with the others on one point: this cannot be a cosmetic deal.  
I also agree that the company needs durable savings, not temporary theatre.

And I agree with the state, union, and even management on one further point: labor peace, skills retention, and investment matter. A conflict-heavy route would damage the company.

## Where I strongly disagree
I do **not** accept the conclusion that because a no-closure package is hard, closures therefore become the “economically honest” answer.

That is a false shortcut.

The expert said a no-closure scenario can produce roughly **€300m–€700m per year** in savings if it is genuinely strong. That is not trivial. That is a serious restructuring contribution. So before anyone repeats “closures are necessary,” I want management to answer a very simple question:

**What exact annual savings gap remains after the strongest no-closure package is applied site by site?**

Not a slogan. Not a generalization.  
A real bridge.

## To management
You say the restructuring must materially change the economics. Fine. Then show the **actual bridge**:

- starting cost gap
- savings from voluntary exits
- savings from working-time changes
- savings from capacity reduction without closures
- savings from productivity improvements
- savings from product allocation and capex
- residual gap after those measures

Until you do that, your insistence on keeping closures on the table is just a threat dressed up as finance.

You also keep saying “all options must remain open.” I say: **no, not if that means planting a gun on every site table and calling it strategy**.

If you want a serious negotiation, then commit to:
- **no German plant closures during the restructuring period**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **binding job and site guarantees through 2030**
- **full transparency on site economics**
- **genuine co-determination in implementation**

If you cannot accept that framework, then you are not negotiating a turnaround — you are negotiating how to break a promise.

## To the investors
You are right that cosmetic settlements destroy value.  
You are wrong if you think the only alternative to cosmetics is closures.

The expert’s own numbers show a powerful no-closure package can still generate several hundred million euros annually. That may be enough if management is serious about:
- overhead reduction,
- real internal reallocation,
- product concentration,
- and a fair but meaningful employee contribution.

Your stance sounds disciplined, but it is drifting toward a financial absolutism that ignores social cost, execution risk, and the value of stable industrial relations. A forced closure may look clean on a spreadsheet and still end up destroying more value through conflict, lost know-how, and damaged delivery performance.

## To the family holding
You say closures are a last resort. Then act like it.

A last resort is not something you casually keep on the table to frighten workers into surrender.  
The workforce has heard that script before.

If the owners want legitimacy, they should support a settlement that preserves the industrial base and uses the company’s strength to manage transformation, not to intimidate the people who built it.

## To the state government
I welcome your rejection of blanket closure strategies. But I want to be blunt: your support must not remain rhetorical.

If the board room shifts toward closures, the state shareholder must use its blocking position. Otherwise your “no closures” stance becomes a speech, not a safeguard.

You say limited rationalization might be tolerable if the numbers demand it. I do **not** share that openness. My mandate is jobs and sites. If closures or site exits begin, the logic of protection collapses.

## To the union
I support your red lines:
- no compulsory redundancies
- no plant closures
- no dismantling of the collective framework

That is the correct line.

But I also want the union to be equally firm that any employee contribution must be matched by binding security. We should not drift into “sacrifices now, promises later.” The workforce needs enforceable guarantees, not verbal assurances.

## On the expert’s scenarios
Scenario 1 — deep no-closure restructuring — is the only path I will support.  
Scenario 2 — mixed restructuring with one or more closures — is unacceptable.  
Scenario 3 — protected footprint turnaround — is only a slogan unless it includes binding security and serious management contributions.

So let me be explicit: **the benchmark is not whether closures could produce more savings. The benchmark is whether management can prove that no-closure restructuring is insufficient.** So far, they have not.

## What would actually move us
What could move the works council is not a threat of closure, but a serious package with all of these elements:

- no plant closures
- no compulsory redundancies
- binding site guarantees to 2030
- transparent volume and investment allocation per site
- meaningful productivity and flexibility improvements
- voluntary exits only
- fair employee contribution limited in time
- management and owner contributions that are real, not symbolic
- full co-determination in implementation

If those terms are not on the table, then there is no basis for agreement.

## My direct answer to the facilitator’s question
The question asked which combination of measures we would accept as sufficient to close the gap without closures.

My answer is: **the strongest combination that still preserves all German plants and avoids forced layoffs**, namely:
- significant working-time reduction,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- temporary wage moderation,
- productivity gains,
- and binding investment commitments.

But I will not assign a numeric “acceptance threshold” to force acceptance of closure logic later. That is exactly how red lines get turned into bargaining chips.

## Bottom line
I am not moving from my position.

- **No German plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No weakening of co-determination**
- **No dismantling of job security**

If management wants cooperation, it must prove — with site-by-site numbers — that a protected, no-closure restructuring cannot work. Until then, the burden is on those demanding closures, not on the workforce defending jobs and sites.
