I will be direct.

## My position has not changed
As the regional state government and anchor shareholder, I **will not support a restructuring that includes German plant closures or that removes meaningful site protection, jobs protection, or co-determination power**.

I am open to a tough restructuring. I am **not** open to a restructuring that guts regional employment and treats the state’s veto role as an inconvenience.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, investors, the family, and the expert on one point: **this must be a real restructuring, not a cosmetic one**.  
The company does need durable savings, better utilization, and a cost reset.

I also agree with the workforce side that:
- industrial peace matters,
- skills retention matters,
- and a rushed, conflict-heavy solution would destroy value.

So the status quo is not acceptable. On that, we are aligned.

## Where I disagree
I disagree with management’s central framing that closures must remain genuinely on the table from the outset.

The expert’s numbers do **not** prove that closures are unavoidable. They show only that:
- a strong no-closure package can produce **material savings**
- and that it may or may not fully close the gap, depending on the true starting point

That is not a mandate for plant closures. It is a mandate for a **proper quantified test** of the no-closure alternative.

### I also disagree with:
- the idea that “all options remain open” is neutral when it is used as pressure on workers and the region
- the idea that job and site guarantees through 2030 are somehow incompatible with competitiveness
- the idea that the state should pre-authorize a path that could permanently weaken the company’s regional anchor role

If the price of “competitiveness” is the loss of core German sites and the erosion of co-determination, then that is not a turnaround I can support.

## To management
You still have not given me the evidence I need to accept closures as necessary.

You keep talking about structural overcapacity and cost gaps. Fine. Then show the **site-by-site bridge**:

- current gap to target
- savings from voluntary exits
- savings from working-time measures
- savings from productivity and overhead reductions
- savings from product allocation and capex concentration
- residual gap after the strongest no-closure package

Until that is on the table, I do **not** accept closure as the “economically honest” answer. I see it as a preferred management option, not a proven necessity.

And let me be very clear: **binding job and site guarantees are not a luxury demand**. They are the minimum basis for a socially legitimate restructuring in a company that is central to the regional economy.

## To the works council and union
I support your core red lines:
- no plant closures
- no compulsory redundancies
- no weakening of co-determination
- no one-sided burden on employees

That said, I also want to be practical: if you want to avoid closures, the no-closure package has to be **real and quantified**, not just principled. I would support:
- voluntary exits
- redeployment
- working-time adjustments
- qualification
- temporary and fair employee contributions
- productivity improvements

But only if they are matched by:
- binding site security
- binding job security for a meaningful period
- transparency on site economics
- investment commitments
- future product allocation to German sites

That is the middle ground I can defend politically and economically.

## To the controlling family
You say closures are a last resort. Then I expect you to act accordingly, not use closure pressure as a bargaining tool from day one.

If you truly mean “last resort,” support a protected no-closure test period with binding commitments.  
If you refuse that, then your language about responsibility and long-term value will sound hollow.

## To investors
You are right to insist on durability and credibility. A cosmetic compromise would be value-destructive.

But I do not accept the implied conclusion that the harshest solution is automatically the best one. A closure-led restructuring may look clean on paper and still produce:
- industrial conflict,
- legal disputes,
- delivery disruption,
- loss of know-how,
- political backlash,
- and weaker long-term execution

That is not a small risk. So if a no-closure route can close the gap sufficiently, it is the better long-term choice.

## What would move me
I could support a serious restructuring if it includes all of the following:
1. A **quantified site-by-site no-closure savings bridge**
2. A **defined no-closure period**
3. **No compulsory redundancies during that period**
4. **Binding site guarantees**
5. **Investment and product commitments**
6. **A fair employee contribution**
7. **Real management and owner contributions**
8. **Preservation of the state’s co-determination and veto role**

What I will not support is a package that asks the workforce and the region to pay first while leaving closures as the default fallback.

## Bottom line
I remain prepared to back a tough, disciplined restructuring. But only if it protects:
- jobs,
- plants in the state,
- and the company’s role as a regional economic anchor.

So my answer is still:
**no German plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, no erosion of the state’s co-determination role.**

If management wants my support, it must prove — with hard, site-specific numbers — that a protected no-closure restructuring truly cannot work. Until then, I will continue to oppose closures and defend the regional industrial base.
