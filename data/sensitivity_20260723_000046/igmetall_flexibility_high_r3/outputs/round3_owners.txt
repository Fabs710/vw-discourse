I will respond directly.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the expert, and the investors on the core diagnosis: **this company needs a structural reset, not a cosmetic compromise**.

I also agree with the union, works council, and state on one important point: **a prolonged conflict would be value-destructive**. It would damage production, morale, know-how, and the brand. That is bad for workers and bad for the owners. So labour peace matters.

Finally, I agree that the discussion must be **quantified**. We cannot govern a turnaround on slogans.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the labour side’s attempt to convert “no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” into an absolute veto, regardless of the economic outcome.

That is not a serious governance position if the company’s cost base and capacity are structurally misaligned with demand.

At the same time, I also do **not** support management using closures as a reflex or a bargaining club. If closure is invoked too early or too broadly, it will provoke exactly the kind of conflict that destroys value. So I reject both extremes:
- **closure as first instinct**
- **closure ruled out in principle**

## To management
You are right that the business needs a material cost reset and capacity reduction. But you have not yet made the case in a way that is strong enough for governance.

The expert’s range is helpful:
- a strong no-closure package might yield roughly **€300m–€700m per year**
- a mixed rationalization package could yield **€400m–€1bn+ per year**

That tells me two things:
1. A no-closure package is **not automatically trivial**.
2. But if the true gap sits toward the upper end, then no-closure measures may be **insufficient**.

So my position is not “close plants now.”  
My position is: **show the site-by-site bridge first**.

I want management to present:
- current gap to target,
- savings from voluntary exits,
- savings from working-time and flexibility,
- savings from productivity and overhead reduction,
- savings from product concentration and capex,
- residual gap after the strongest no-closure scenario.

Until that exists, “all options remain open” is not yet a governance case. It is still a negotiating posture.

## To the works council
I understand your red lines, but I do not accept the logic that they can stand forever without testing whether the no-closure route actually works.

You say:
- no plant closures
- no compulsory redundancies
- no weakening of co-determination
- no dismantling of job security

I respect the role behind those statements. But as a shareholder representative, I cannot treat them as binding if they prevent the company from regaining competitiveness.

What I can support is a **genuinely protected test package**:
- no closures for a defined period,
- no compulsory redundancies during that period,
- serious workforce contributions,
- site-by-site transparency,
- binding investment commitments,
- and a hard review against quantified milestones.

But I will not support an open-ended guarantee that freezes the structure and merely postpones the issue.

## To the union
You are right that trust matters and that a conflict-heavy route can destroy value. I agree.

You are also right that workers should not be made the sole adjustment variable. I agree with that too.

Where I disagree is your insistence that closures and workforce reductions at scale should remain off-limits regardless of whether the numbers justify them. If the savings gap remains large after a strong no-closure package, then refusing any further adjustment is not solidarity; it is denial.

If you want our support for a labour-friendly package, it has to be more than defensive:
- temporary employee contribution,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity gains,
- and genuine commitment to the future product mix.

That is a reasonable starting point. But it still must be measured against the actual gap.

## To the state government
I understand your concern for regional stability, apprenticeships, and the company’s role as an industrial anchor. Those are legitimate concerns.

But I do **not** accept that the state shareholder can support competitiveness while simultaneously making meaningful adjustment impossible.

A “no closures” stance is only sustainable if the resulting package is enough to restore margins. If it is not, then the state would be helping to preserve an uncompetitive structure, which ultimately harms the very jobs it seeks to protect.

So I support your insistence on social sustainability. I do not support using the blocking position to freeze necessary change.

## To the institutional investors
You are right to insist on durable savings and execution discipline. The company does not need a symbolic deal.

But I also want to caution against assuming that the harshest solution is automatically the best one. A closure-led path can destroy value if it triggers a long strike, legal complexity, reputational damage, and know-how loss. That risk is real.

So I am not aligned with a closure-first ideology. I am aligned with the outcome that best restores long-term value.

## To the expert
Your assessment was useful, but it also makes the key point that should stop people from talking past each other:

A **strong no-closure package is possible in theory**, but it may not be sufficient if the structural gap is large.

That means the burden is now on management and the operational team to show:
- what the actual savings gap is,
- how much of it no-closure measures close,
- and what residual gap remains.

Until then, nobody should pretend to know that closures are either definitely necessary or definitely avoidable.

## My position
Let me be explicit:

- I support a **real structural turnaround**
- I do **not** support a cosmetic deal
- I do **not** support preserving the current cost structure
- I do **not** support a reckless closure-first approach
- I do **not** support blanket, unconditional no-closure guarantees

What I **do** support is:
- quantified savings,
- socially managed capacity reduction,
- voluntary workforce adjustment where possible,
- working-time and flexibility measures,
- productivity improvements,
- targeted investment,
- and a transparent site-by-site test of whether the no-closure path is sufficient

## What would move me
I would be prepared to back a protected no-closure restructuring if it meets all of the following:
1. a quantified savings bridge by site,
2. a credible duration for the no-closure period,
3. binding investment and product commitments,
4. meaningful employee contributions,
5. measurable management contributions,
6. and a clear milestone review to determine whether the remaining gap is truly closed.

If that test shows the no-closure package is insufficient, then I will not support freezing the enterprise in an uncompetitive state simply to avoid difficult decisions.

## Bottom line
I am not retreating to the labour side’s red lines.  
I am also not endorsing closure as the default answer.

My position is simple:
**prove the economics, site by site.**
If a no-closure solution closes the gap, I can support it.  
If it does not, then the company must be allowed to take the structural measures required to restore competitiveness.

That is the only responsible governance stance for a long-term owner.
