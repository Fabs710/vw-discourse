I will be direct.

We share the same broad objective as management: **the company must restore competitiveness and margins, or the long-term value of the enterprise will erode**. That is not optional. The family holding will not support a cosmetic package that preserves the old cost structure and merely postpones the adjustment.

At the same time, I do **not** support an outcome that damages the company’s industrial base so severely that the long-term value of the core asset is impaired. That includes avoidable destabilisation, uncontrolled labour conflict, and a settlement that creates lasting reputational and operational damage.

### Where I agree
I agree with management on the core diagnosis:
- the German cost base is too high,
- capacity is misaligned with the market,
- and the company needs a durable structural reset.

I also agree that any solution must be **real**, not symbolic:
- voluntary instruments alone may not be enough,
- productivity and flexibility must improve,
- and labour costs cannot remain untouched.

I further agree that future investment matters. A restructuring that cuts too deeply into the company’s ability to invest in new products would be self-defeating.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the framing that closures and mass forced layoffs are the default answer.

Why? Because this is not just a financial exercise. This is a company with:
- a special governance structure,
- co-determination,
- a state shareholder with blocking rights,
- and a workforce capable of making any “hard” solution much more expensive if mishandled.

If you push a closure-led strategy without a serious negotiated alternative, you risk:
- prolonged industrial conflict,
- loss of trust across the system,
- reputational damage to the brand and the owners,
- and a value destruction that may exceed the apparent savings.

That would be a strategic failure, not a restructuring success.

### On the workforce position
I understand why the works council and union reject compulsory redundancies and plant closures. That is consistent with their role. But I will be equally clear: **a blanket “no closures, no job reductions, no compromises” line is not enough either**.

If the company is to survive competitively, then the workforce side must accept that:
- there will be meaningful capacity reduction,
- employment levels will need to come down over time,
- and there must be a material employee contribution.

That said, I draw a line at solutions that permanently weaken the enterprise through open-ended conflict or an uncontrolled deterioration of labour relations.

### On the state government’s role
I appreciate the state’s emphasis on regional stability and industrial capacity. Those concerns are legitimate.

But I also need to say: **the state’s blocking position cannot be used to freeze necessary adjustment indefinitely**. If every structural measure is treated as politically unacceptable, then the company will simply lose competitiveness more slowly and more painfully.

The public interest is not served by preserving every site at any cost. It is served by preserving a viable industrial base over the long term.

### What I would support
The family holding would look favourably on a settlement that includes:
- a credible and measurable cost reset,
- capacity reduction,
- workforce reduction primarily through voluntary and socially managed instruments where possible,
- working-time and flexibility changes,
- a negotiated employee contribution,
- targeted investment commitments in future products and technologies,
- and a durable peace that avoids a destructive strike cycle.

### What I would not support
I will be explicit about the limits:
- I will not support a purely defensive package that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.
- I will not support an arrangement that protects every existing structure while demanding future capital from the owners.
- I will not support endless delay in the name of consensus.
- And I will not support any solution that permanently impairs the company’s governance control or forces the owners to surrender their stewardship role.

### My position to management
Management should come back with a package that is:
- quantitatively credible,
- structurally meaningful,
- and socially sustainable.

If it relies on closures, it must be because no comparable alternative exists and because the savings are demonstrably necessary. But closures are not a preferred outcome for us; they are a last resort if the parties cannot produce a serious alternative.

### My position to labour and the state
If you want to avoid closures and forced redundancies, then you need to bring:
- real labor-cost relief,
- real productivity improvements,
- real flexibility,
- and a package that closes the competitiveness gap in numbers, not in rhetoric.

I am open to a negotiated solution. But I am **not** open to a settlement that preserves the status quo under a different name.

So the path forward is narrow but clear:
- competitiveness first,
- stability second,
- and no outcome that sacrifices the long-term value of the company for short-term comfort.
