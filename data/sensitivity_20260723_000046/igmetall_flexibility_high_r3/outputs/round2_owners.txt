I will respond directly and carefully.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the investors, and the state on the central diagnosis: **the company needs a durable structural reset**.  
The cost base is too high, capacity is misaligned with demand, and the business cannot restore margins by rhetoric or delay. If we do not fix competitiveness, we will all be defending a weaker company in a few years’ time.

I also agree with labour and the state that **a prolonged conflict would be value-destructive**.  
A strike cycle, reputational damage, loss of know-how, and operational instability would harm the enterprise and the family holding’s long-term value. So labour peace matters.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the position that a serious restructuring must exclude closures and deeper workforce reduction in all circumstances.

That is not a credible governance position if the numbers do not work.  
A package that simply preserves the full footprint, preserves the same labor structure, and asks owners to fund the gap would be a cosmetic settlement. I will not support that.

At the same time, I do **not** accept a closure-first approach either.  
If management uses plant closures as a reflex rather than as a last-resort tool, that risks provoking exactly the kind of conflict that destroys value. So there is a line between “necessary structural adjustment” and “value-destructive overreach.”

## To management
You are right that:
- the current structure is uncompetitive,
- voluntary measures alone may not be enough,
- and any solution must materially reduce fixed cost and overcapacity.

But I need to be clear: **simply insisting that closures must remain on the table is not enough**.  
If you want governance support, bring a package that is quantitatively convincing and implementation-ready. I want to see:
- the required savings,
- the timing of the savings,
- the contribution from labor,
- the investment commitments,
- and the operational effect on each site.

If the case for closures is real, show it. If it is not, then do not use it as a bargaining weapon that destabilizes the talks.

## To the works council and union
I understand your refusal of compulsory redundancies and blanket closures. That is consistent with your role, and I do not dismiss it lightly.

But I must also say: **“no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no weakening of guarantees” is not sufficient by itself** unless you can show a package that truly closes the competitiveness gap.  
A company cannot survive on moral arguments alone.

You have proposed:
- working-time reductions
- voluntary exits
- redeployment
- qualification
- productivity improvements
- temporary employee contributions

Those are legitimate tools, and I am open to them. But they must be real, measurable, and durable. If they do not materially change the economics, then they are not a solution — they are delay.

## To the state government
I appreciate your focus on regional stability, employment, apprenticeships, and the company’s industrial role. Those are legitimate concerns.

But I need to say this plainly: **the state’s blocking position cannot be used to freeze necessary adjustment**.  
If every serious structural measure is blocked on principle, the company will drift into weaker competitiveness, and that will ultimately hurt the very regions and jobs you are trying to protect.

So I support your insistence on social sustainability, but not an outcome that makes meaningful restructuring impossible.

## To investors
I agree with your emphasis on credible, durable savings and measurable milestones.  
You are right that the market will not reward a symbolic deal. But I would caution against any approach that treats maximum labor pain as automatically optimal. It is not. If conflict escalates, value is destroyed too.

## My position
The family holding’s position is this:

- We support a **real structural turnaround**.
- We do **not** support cosmetic restructuring.
- We do **not** support preserving the full cost structure and pretending the problem is solved.
- We also do **not** support reckless or indiscriminate closure-led disruption that damages the company’s long-term value.

## What we would support
We would support a package that combines:
- credible capacity reduction,
- meaningful labor-cost relief,
- voluntary and socially managed workforce adjustment where possible,
- working-time and flexibility measures,
- productivity improvements,
- targeted investment commitments,
- and a clear, enforceable path back to margin recovery.

That is the standard.

## What we will not support
We will not support:
- a purely defensive no-change package,
- an agreement that preserves every site and every structure regardless of economics,
- a settlement that creates permanent value erosion,
- or a prolonged industrial conflict that damages the brand and the enterprise.

## Bottom line
So I am not moving to the labour side’s red lines as stated.  
A blanket no-closure, no-redundancy position is not enough unless it is backed by a package that actually closes the gap. At the same time, management cannot simply declare closures unavoidable without a rigorous, site-specific justification.

If the next round is to be useful, then I want:
1. **quantified savings requirements**
2. **site-by-site impact analysis**
3. **a credible no-closure alternative, if one exists**
4. **a fallback plan if it does not**
5. **clear commitments on investment and future products**
6. **a labour contribution that is durable, not symbolic**

I am prepared to back difficult change. I am **not** prepared to back delay disguised as protection, nor destruction disguised as strategy.
