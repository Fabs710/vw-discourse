## Short answer

A credible **no-closure** restructuring can generate **material savings**, but whether it is enough depends on the **starting gap**, the **number of plants involved**, and the **degree of labor-cost and capacity adjustment actually achieved**.

From the ranges already discussed in the conversation, the strongest no-closure package appears capable of delivering roughly **€300m–€700m per year** in recurring savings. That is meaningful, but it may or may not fully close a structural competitiveness gap for a large German OEM.

By contrast, a **closure-based** scenario typically creates a larger and more certain structural cost reduction, because it removes fixed overhead, depreciated underutilized capacity, and a portion of indirect labor and site costs. The EBIT effect is usually strongest after the one-time restructuring charges are absorbed.

## What I would treat as the benchmark test

The right benchmark is:

> **Can the company achieve at least ~70–80% of the required structural savings through a no-closure package, with sustainable utilization and no hidden reversion to overcapacity?**

If yes, a no-closure solution is operationally plausible.  
If no, then the company likely has a real overcapacity problem that cannot be solved by wages, voluntary exits, and working-time measures alone.

# How to think about the savings bridge

A restructuring bridge in automotive plants usually has five components:

1. **Headcount reduction**
   - voluntary exits
   - retirement
   - redeployment
   - attrition

2. **Labor-cost measures**
   - temporary wage moderation
   - bonus reductions
   - removal of certain allowances
   - working-time flexibility
   - reduced paid hours

3. **Capacity utilization improvement**
   - higher line loading
   - shift-pattern changes
   - elimination of loss-making variants
   - internal consolidation of volumes

4. **Factory overhead reduction**
   - plant management
   - maintenance
   - logistics
   - indirect labor
   - utilities and services

5. **Product and investment allocation**
   - assigning future models to the remaining sites
   - reducing changeover complexity
   - avoiding capex duplication

A **no-closure** plan can address all five, but it generally cannot eliminate the full fixed-cost burden of a structurally redundant site the way a closure or conversion can.

# Plant-level logic: what matters operationally

Without an actual plant list and site cost base, I cannot responsibly assign exact figures to each German plant. But I can say what the plant-by-plant analysis must show.

For each site, the company should quantify:

- **current annual output**
- **installed capacity**
- **utilization rate**
- **direct headcount**
- **indirect headcount**
- **fixed site overhead**
- **unit labor cost**
- **annual EBIT contribution**
- **expected volume after product allocation**
- **break-even volume**

A plant with:
- low utilization,
- high indirect labor,
- high logistics cost,
- and no future model allocation

is a strong candidate for rationalization, conversion, or closure if the goal is durable EBIT improvement.

A plant with:
- good utilization,
- strong product allocation,
- and limited fixed-cost burden

is more likely to be a viable no-closure candidate.

# Indicative EBIT effect by scenario

## Scenario A: Strong no-closure package
Assumptions:
- no plant closures
- no compulsory redundancies
- 3–5% workforce reduction through voluntary exits/attrition
- working-time moderation
- selective wage/bonus restraint
- partial volume consolidation
- productivity gain from process simplification

Indicative recurring savings:
- **€300m–€700m/year**

EBIT impact:
- if the current gap is modest, this may recover a meaningful share of lost margin
- if the current gap is large, this likely improves EBIT but does **not** fully reset competitiveness

Operational risk:
- lower conflict risk
- lower know-how loss
- lower one-time restructuring cost
- but savings may be insufficient if overcapacity is severe

## Scenario B: Mixed restructuring with selective site rationalization
Assumptions:
- one or more sites downsized, converted, or partly exited
- stronger headcount reduction
- sharper indirect-cost cuts
- more aggressive capacity consolidation

Indicative recurring savings:
- **€700m–€1.5bn+/year** depending on scale

EBIT impact:
- usually materially stronger than no-closure
- better chance of restoring sustainable margins
- but higher one-time charges and industrial-relations risk

Operational risk:
- volume transfer complexity
- disruption to launch performance
- potential short-term losses from instability

## Scenario C: Full closure-led restructuring
Assumptions:
- closure of one or more whole plants
- transfer or elimination of full fixed-cost blocks
- larger workforce reduction
- major footprint simplification

Indicative recurring savings:
- often the highest structural saving, potentially **above €1bn/year** if the site footprint is large enough

EBIT impact:
- strongest long-run margin improvement
- highest upfront restructuring charge
- highest execution and conflict risk

# What “no-closure” can and cannot do

## It can do:
- reduce payroll expense
- lower overtime and premium pay
- improve utilization
- simplify scheduling
- reduce voluntary attrition costs
- delay or avoid shutdown-related charges
- preserve asset continuity

## It cannot fully do:
- remove a structurally redundant fixed-cost base
- eliminate underutilized plant overhead if the site remains open
- resolve low volume if product allocation remains weak
- create the same degree of cost reset as a full site exit

So if the problem is mostly **labor cost**, a no-closure path can work.  
If the problem is mostly **structural overcapacity**, no-closure measures are usually only a partial fix.

# My assessment of the works council’s position

From a plant-operations standpoint, the works council is correct that:

- a range of savings outcomes exists,
- a no-closure package is not automatically cosmetic,
- and a serious alternative deserves a quantified test.

That is true.

But the operational question is not whether no-closure savings are “serious.”  
It is whether they are **sufficient and durable relative to the overcapacity burden**.

If a site remains open with materially underused capacity, the fixed-cost drag often returns unless:
- volume is reassigned,
- shifts are reduced,
- or the site’s scope is meaningfully changed.

### And then calculate:

2. **One-time restructuring cost**
3. **Resulting EBIT improvement**
4. **Payback period**
5. **Residual capacity slack**

If the no-closure version:
- closes most of the gap,
- gives a payback of roughly 2–4 years,
- and leaves acceptable utilization,

then it is economically credible.

If it leaves:
- persistent underutilization,
- a large residual gap,
- or savings that depend on temporary labor concessions only,

then it is not durable enough.
