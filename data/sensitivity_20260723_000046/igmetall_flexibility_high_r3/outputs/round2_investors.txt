I’ll be direct.

## Where we agree
We agree with management, and with the expert’s assessment, on the core point: **this company needs a real structural reset, not a cosmetic settlement**.

From a capital-markets perspective, the expert’s numbers are exactly the kind of discipline this discussion has been missing:
- a meaningful no-closure package may produce roughly **€300m–€700m per year** in savings in a best case,
- while a mixed package with closure/rationalization can plausibly deliver **€400m–€1bn+ per year**.

That matters. If the gap to target is toward the upper end, then the labour side’s blanket red lines become economically hard to defend.

We also agree that:
- **industrial peace has value**
- **future investment matters**
- **prolonged strikes destroy value**
- **a purely symbolic deal will not re-rate the stock**

## Where we disagree
We do **not** support either of the following extremes:

1. **A cosmetic no-closure deal** that preserves the full cost structure and just postpones the problem.
2. **A maximalist closure-first strategy** that creates so much conflict, execution risk, and reputational damage that the savings are offset by value destruction.

So our position is not “close plants at any cost.”  
It is: **deliver a credible, quantified margin reset with durable savings.**

## To the Works Council and Union
Your insistence on no closures and no compulsory redundancies is understandable, but it is **not acceptable as an absolute position** if it means the company remains structurally uncompetitive.

The expert just confirmed what management has been saying in substance: if the domestic footprint is materially underutilized, then voluntary exits, working-time measures, and redeployment may not be enough. In that case, refusing closures in principle is not a protective stance; it is a way to lock in underperformance.

What would move us is not a slogan, but a **quantified alternative**:
- How much annual savings?
- What reduction in utilization / shifts?
- What headcount reduction by when?
- What permanent change in fixed cost?
- What capex is needed to keep the remaining sites competitive?

If you can truly close the gap without closures, show it in numbers.  
If you cannot, then the blanket rejection of closures is simply asking shareholders to finance an uncompetitive structure.

## To Management
You are right to insist that the solution must materially change the economics. But I want to be equally clear: **you need to prove the implementation path, not just the diagnosis**.

The market will not reward:
- headline toughness with weak execution,
- an announcement that triggers a long strike,
- or a plan that looks decisive but is operationally unworkable.

If you want investor support, bring a package with:
- site-by-site savings,
- a clear utilization plan,
- timing of cash impact,
- and a credible labour peace strategy.

The expert’s framework is helpful here: if you cannot show how the network is reduced to a genuinely competitive footprint, then “all options remain open” sounds more like bargaining pressure than a financeable strategy.

## To the State Government
We understand the regional and political stakes, but preserving every site cannot be the governing principle.

The expert’s assessment makes the central trade-off clear: if overcapacity is structural, then **capacity has to come out somewhere**. If the state blocks that indefinitely, then the company will likely remain under-margined and under-valued. That ultimately damages jobs, not protects them.

We support a socially managed solution, but not one that freezes the cost problem in place.

## To the Controlling Family
You are right that long-term value matters, and right that conflict can destroy it. But “closures as last resort” only works if the alternative is genuinely credible.

So far, the only credible alternatives on the table are:
- some voluntary exits,
- some working-time flexibility,
- some temporary concessions.

Those may reduce pain, but they may not solve the structural problem. If the expert’s upper-end estimate is the relevant one, then the family should not hide behind ambiguity. Either the no-closure package closes the gap, or it doesn’t.

## Our position
As dispersed institutional shareholders, our standard is simple:

- **We will support a tough restructuring if it is credible, quantified, and durable.**
- **We will not support a deal that preserves structural overcapacity and leaves margins impaired.**
- **We will not support prolonged conflict that burns value without changing the economics.**

That means we are open to:
- plant closures or site rationalization **if necessary**
- workforce reductions at scale **if necessary**
- voluntary exits, redeployment, and working-time changes **where they truly help**
- targeted investment commitments **only if matched by real savings**

## What we need next
The next step should not be more general positioning. It should be a quantified bridge:
1. required annual savings to restore ROS
2. plant-by-plant utilization after restructuring
3. headcount path by site
4. savings from voluntary measures versus closure/rationalization
5. capex needed to keep any remaining footprint competitive
6. timeline to cash and margin impact

If the no-closure camp cannot produce that bridge, then it should stop pretending its position is economically neutral.

## Bottom line
We are **not** aligned with a cosmetic settlement, and we are **not** aligned with an ideology of closure for its own sake.  
We are aligned with **the outcome that most credibly restores competitiveness, margins, and valuation**.

If that requires closures or deeper workforce reduction, then that is the economically honest answer. If it can be done without them, we will support that too — but only if the numbers are real, durable, and sufficient.
