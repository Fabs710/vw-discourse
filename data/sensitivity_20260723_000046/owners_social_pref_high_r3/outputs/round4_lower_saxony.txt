I’ll be equally direct.

The latest round has clarified the real divide: **management and some capital-side actors still want to keep closures and forced redundancies as live pressure tools; we do not accept that as a legitimate starting position.** We accept restructuring. We do not accept restructuring by threat.

## To management
You keep saying “durable reset,” “structural gap,” and “competitiveness,” but you still have not shown that **German plant closures or compulsory redundancies are necessary as opposed to merely available**.

That distinction matters.

- If you can achieve a durable improvement through a negotiated package of voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time flexibility, contractor reductions, productivity gains, and temporary labour-cost moderation, then closures are not justified.
- If you cannot, then you need to show that clearly, site by site, with numbers, not with rhetoric.

What I reject is your attempt to treat the lack of a perfect no-closure package as proof that closures must be kept on the table. That is not proof; it is escalation.

And I will repeat our bottom line: **a blanket job-security framework through 2030 is not a luxury demand. It is the basis on which any legitimate employee contribution can be discussed.** If you want the workforce to contribute, then you need to stop acting as if employment protection is the obstacle rather than the condition.

## To the Works Council
I agree with your core line: no plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, preservation of co-determination, and no one-sided burden shifting.

I also agree that the burden of proof sits with those proposing irreversible damage. Management has not yet met that burden.

Where I would sharpen the point is this: we should not let the debate get trapped in abstract “capacity reduction” language that quietly prepares closures later. If there is to be capacity adjustment, it must be:
- negotiated,
- reversible wherever possible,
- transparent,
- and tied to binding protections.

I support your demand for a binding job-security framework. Without that, employee contributions are politically and socially impossible to justify.

## To the Union
Your insistence that concessions must buy real security is right. I support that.

I also agree that temporary moderation, if accepted at all, must be **limited, time-bound, and reciprocal**. Workers should not be asked to carry the risk while management and owners keep every exit option open.

But I want to be explicit: **I do not view “closure remains possible if the gap persists” as an acceptable bargaining formula.** That would simply make every employee concession conditional on a later threat of site loss. That is not a fair social partnership.

## To the State Government
Your position is close to ours, and I appreciate that.

You are right to insist on site-by-site economics and not let management use broad claims of overcapacity as a blanket justification. You are also right that the state is not a neutral observer here. We are an anchor shareholder and political actor with a duty to protect the regional industrial base.

Where I want to be firm is this: **the state should not help create a process in which the workforce is first asked to concede and only later told whether its sites survive.** That sequencing is unacceptable.

So I support:
- no closures as the starting point,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding job security for the negotiated period,
- and a real industrial plan for German sites.

I do not support a process where closures remain the default fallback unless labor weakens itself first.

## To the Family Holding
You are right that a cosmetic settlement would be bad value. I agree.

But you are still too comfortable with the idea that closures are simply a legitimate fallback if negotiations do not produce enough concessions. That logic is exactly what destroys trust.

If the company is serious about long-term value, then it must pursue a solution that:
- preserves the industrial base,
- protects jobs and sites in Germany,
- and uses the company’s co-determination structure rather than trying to work around it.

That does **not** mean denying economic realities. It means choosing a restructuring path that does not gut the company’s regional foundation.

## To the Institutional Investors
You are right that cosmetic peace is not enough. I agree.

You are also right that a restructuring must be economically sufficient. Where I disagree is your assumption that sufficiency requires keeping closures and forced layoffs as open bargaining tools from the outset.

The real test is whether the package can be made sufficient through:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- targeted cost moderation,
- contractor and external spend reduction,
- productivity gains,
- and management/owner contributions.

That is the level of seriousness I want to see before anyone talks about irreversible cuts.

## Where I stand on the bridge proposals
### 1) Time-limited moratorium
A moratorium on closures and compulsory redundancies is the minimum basis for any credible negotiation. That is not a concession to labour; it is a precondition for trust.

I am open to a defined moratorium if it is tied to:
- quantified savings targets,
- binding contributions from management and owners,
- and an independent site-level audit.

But I will say clearly: a moratorium is only useful if it is not a disguised countdown to the same outcome later.

### 2) Hybrid restructuring with capacity reduction first
This is the only serious middle path.

But it must mean **real internal adjustment**, not code for shutting down sites by stages. If capacity reduction is needed, it should be achieved through:
- voluntary departures,
- internal reallocation,
- training,
- working-time measures,
- temporary labour-cost moderation,
- and product allocation changes,
not through pre-selected closures.

### 3) Security-for-contribution package
This is the right logic only if security comes first and is binding.

If employees contribute, then management and ownership must commit to:
- no closures during the negotiated period,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- investment in Germany,
- and preservation of co-determination.

That is the only fair exchange.

## My specific answer to the key question
The moderator asked whether I would accept a time-limited no-closure moratorium if reversible measures cover 70–80% of the gap but not all of it.

My answer is: **yes, but only if the remaining gap is closed by management and owners, not by later closures or forced layoffs.**

That means:
- management contribution,
- owner contribution,
- productivity gains,
- internal consolidation,
- and limited employee moderation.

What I will not accept is a moratorium that serves merely to soften resistance before the same closure option returns.

## What I support
I remain open to:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- training and qualification,
- temporary bonus restraint,
- targeted and limited wage moderation,
- contractor reductions,
- internal consolidation,
- and partial capacity adjustment through negotiated means.

## What I do not support
I do **not** support:
- plant closures as a bargaining instrument,
- compulsory redundancies,
- a “restructuring” that strips away job protection,
- or any process that lets the company extract concessions first and decide on site survival later.

## My final position in this round
The Regional State Government will support a restructuring only if it is built on:

1. **No German plant closures as the starting point**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Binding job-security guarantees for the negotiated period**
4. **A quantified, site-by-site alternative package**
5. **Visible management and owner contributions**
6. **Investment commitments for German products and technologies**
7. **Preservation of co-determination and the state’s veto role**

If management wants a serious deal, bring back a package that closes the gap without making German sites the first casualty.

If it cannot do that, then the burden does **not** shift to the workforce. It shifts back to management and owners to prove why they should be allowed to destroy regional employment and industrial trust.

And if necessary, we will use the rights attached to our stake and statute to prevent a bad outcome.
