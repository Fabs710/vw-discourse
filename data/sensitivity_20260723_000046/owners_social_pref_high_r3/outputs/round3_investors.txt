We appreciate the economist and modeler, because this debate needed to move from slogans to economics.

## Our position, unchanged
From the capital markets’ perspective, the only acceptable outcome is one that **credibly restores competitive margins, lowers the cost base durably, and gives us confidence the restructuring will stick**. We will not support a cosmetic settlement, a temporary truce, or a package that leaves the company structurally impaired.

That said, we are **not** ideologically committed to closures. We are committed to **economic sufficiency**. If a negotiated package can genuinely close the gap, fine. If it cannot, then closures or equivalent hard capacity reduction must remain on the table.

## To the Works Council
We agree with you on one important point: management has to prove its case with numbers, not just invoke “overcapacity” as a mantra.

But we disagree with your current line in two critical respects:

### 1) “No closures” cannot be the starting assumption
You keep treating closures as inherently illegitimate. They are not. They are **value-destructive only if they are unnecessary or badly executed**. If a site is structurally non-viable, preserving it simply shifts the cost to shareholders and risks dragging margins lower for years.

### 2) Blanket job security through 2030 is too rigid
If the company’s own finance model says the structural gap is large, then binding all sites and all jobs through 2030 is not a credible restructuring frame. It may be attractive for labor, but it freezes the cost problem.

We do agree on some points:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- training,
- contractor reduction,
- targeted labor-cost moderation,
- and negotiated productivity measures  
all belong in the package.

But those measures must be **quantified**. A list of instruments is not a solution. We need to know:
- how much each measure saves,
- how quickly,
- and whether it closes the gap after the first tranche of savings.

If it does not, then your “no closures” line is just a demand to postpone the adjustment.

## To the Trade Union
You are right that concessions must not become a backdoor dismantling of collective standards. We agree with that. We also agree that prolonged strikes destroy value.

Where we disagree is your attempt to make **temporary and limited concessions** sound sufficient in principle. They are only sufficient if they deliver **material, durable savings**.

Your position still needs to answer:
- What annual savings do temporary wage moderation, bonus restraint, and working-time flexibility actually generate?
- What savings remain after voluntary exits and contractor cuts?
- How large is the gap after all reversible measures?

If the answer is “still substantial,” then a no-closure settlement is not credible.

---

## To Management
You are right about one core issue: reversible concessions have a ceiling. The economist’s assessment confirms that. A purely no-closure deal can help, but it is unlikely to be enough if the gap is truly structural.

However, your position still lacks the one thing investors need: **site-by-site proof**.

Saying closures remain on the table is not enough. You need to show:
- savings target,
- savings by measure,
- residual gap,
- and why closure or rationalization at a specific site is economically superior to the alternative.

Without that comparison, closures are still just a threat, not a case.

We also do not accept a vague “durable reset” if it comes without milestones. We want:
- annualized savings targets,
- implementation timing,
- and clear margin impact.

If the package cannot credibly restore the brand’s return on sales, then it is inadequate.

## To the Regional State Government
We share your concern that a destructive closure-first approach would be politically and socially costly. We also agree that site-by-site economics matter.

But we do not support your implication that closures should be politically de-prioritized by default. A blocking stance can easily become a shield for underperformance. The state should not make investors and the market hostage to industrial preservation at any cost.

Your best contribution is not a blanket “no closures.” It is to force:
- a proper quantified comparison,
- full disclosure of site economics,
- and a package that either demonstrates a viable no-closure path or honestly shows where hard cuts are needed.

That said, if the comparison shows a genuine negotiated alternative can close the gap, we would back that. If not, we would expect the state not to obstruct necessary rationalization.

---

## To the Controlling Family Holding
We agree with you on process: the decision must be based on economics, not emotion or politics.

Where we differ is that you still seem to want to preserve a broad “no closures unless proven unavoidable” stance as a governance default. That is too slow and too risky if the cost base is already materially out of line.

Ownership should be asking:
- what combination of measures restores competitive margins fastest,
- what is the NPV of each option,
- and how much fixed cost can be removed durably?

If the answer includes hard capacity reduction, ownership should support it. Not because it is pleasant, but because preserving an uncompetitive structure is a worse outcome.

We also agree that a badly executed restructuring can destroy value. But so can delaying the reset until margins erode further and capital allocation credibility weakens.

## What the economist/modeler has clarified
The key point is this: **a negotiated no-closure package can work only if the required gap is modest.**  
If the gap is structural and large, the economic logic shifts toward:
- partial capacity reduction,
- site rationalization,
- or closure of the weakest site(s).

That is the correct analytical frame.

So the debate is not “closures versus no closures” in the abstract. It is:
1. what is the true savings gap,
2. what do reversible measures buy us,
3. and which sites, if any, are structurally non-viable?

That is where we need the next round to go.

---

## Our bottom line
We can support a negotiated restructuring **only if** it contains all of the following:
1. a quantified savings target,
2. real and durable labor-cost reduction,
3. real capacity reduction,
4. a credible path back to target margins,
5. and enough certainty that the market can believe the problem is actually fixed.

We are open to no-closure solutions **only if they close the gap in full**.

If they do not, then we will not support an outcome that preserves every site, avoids hard decisions, and leaves the company uncompetitive.
