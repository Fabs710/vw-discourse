I will be direct.

I agree with all parties on one point: **the company cannot afford drift**. The current cost base and capacity situation are clearly unsustainable if management’s diagnosis is correct. If the core brand misses its margin target for a prolonged period, value destruction will follow, and that is not acceptable for long-term owners.

But I do **not** agree that the only credible answer is to move immediately to plant closures and compulsory redundancies.

### Where I agree
- **Management is right** that structural competitiveness has to be restored.
- **The workforce and the state are right** that this must be negotiated and that trust matters.
- **The union and works council are right** that one-sided burden shifting would be economically and socially damaging.

### Where I disagree
What I cannot support is a restructuring logic that treats **German plant closures as the default solution** and uses the threat of irreversible cuts as the main bargaining instrument.

That approach is dangerous for three reasons:
1. **It risks destroying value permanently** through disruption, lost know-how, and reputational damage.
2. **It escalates conflict** in a company where the workforce and co-determination system can block implementation.
3. **It weakens the long-term industrial base** of the core asset the family holding is responsible for stewarding.

A plan that cannot command durable labour peace is not a strong plan; it is a fragile one.

### What I need to see
If management wants the owners’ support, it must bring a restructuring that is economically hard-nosed **and** implementable. That means:

- a **credible and quantified** path to restoring margins,
- **real capacity reduction**, but not necessarily via immediate closures,
- **material labour-cost improvement**,
- maximum use of **voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time measures, and targeted flexibility**,
- and a binding commitment that preserves the company’s ability to invest in future products and technology.

### On the red lines
I am not endorsing a blanket refusal of all structural change. That would not be serious.

But I also am not prepared to give management a free hand for closures if alternative instruments have not been exhausted or if the economic case is not proven with enough precision. The burden of proof is on management to show:
- why a given site must close,
- why a negotiated alternative cannot achieve comparable savings,
- and why the long-term value impact justifies the social and industrial cost.

### On employee contributions
I agree that employees cannot be the only ones making concessions. But I also agree with management that the workforce contribution cannot be merely symbolic.

So if the alternative to closures is serious, then it must include **real employee contributions**, for example:
- wage moderation for a defined period,
- flexibility on working time,
- limits on bonus growth,
- and productivity improvements.

In return, those contributions must be matched by:
- **binding job security rules**,
- **site security where economically viable**,
- and a clear investment and product commitment.

### My position
The family holding supports a **durable restructuring**, not a destructive confrontation.

So my message to management is:
- do not assume plant closures will be automatically acceptable,
- do not escalate by making irreversible measures the first move,
- and do not expect ownership to back a plan that damages the enterprise’s long-term value or triggers prolonged instability.

To labour and the state:
- do not mistake our preference for stability for a willingness to preserve every structure unchanged,
- and do not expect the owners to accept a solution that postpones the problem and leaves competitiveness unresolved.

### Bottom line
I am open to a negotiated package, but only if it delivers:
- real competitiveness gains,
- durable labour peace,
- and protection of long-term enterprise value.

A deal that avoids closures only by being financially weak is not acceptable.  
A deal that achieves savings by permanently impairing the company is also not acceptable.

Management should come back with a precise alternative that can prove it can meet the economic objective **without** making irreversible damage the price of action.
