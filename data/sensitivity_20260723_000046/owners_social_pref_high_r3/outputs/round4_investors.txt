We agree with the **core diagnosis**: this company has a **structural cost and capacity problem**, and a cosmetic settlement would be value-destructive. We also agree that **reversible measures alone have a ceiling**. That is exactly why we are not backing a “peace at any price” outcome.

But we are **not** prepared to endorse the labour/state line that **closures and compulsory redundancies should be ruled out in advance**. That is not a credible restructuring framework if the economics do not support it.

## To management
You are right that the company needs a **durable reset**, not a pause. We support the insistence on:
- quantified savings,
- site-by-site economics,
- durable margin improvement,
- and a real reduction in fixed costs.

Where we push back is on rhetoric turning into a blank cheque. Saying “closures remain on the table” is not enough. We need the actual case:
- savings target by year,
- savings by measure,
- residual gap after reversible measures,
- and the economics of each site.

If management cannot show that clearly, then it is asking shareholders to accept disruption without proof. That is not acceptable either.

## To the Works Council
We agree with you that management has not yet made the case in full, and that the burden of proof must be real, not theatrical.

But your position goes too far when you insist on:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and binding job security through 2030,
as if those were compatible with any serious structural reset regardless of the numbers.

They are not automatically compatible. If a site is structurally non-viable, preserving it just shifts the cost to shareholders and risks leaving the company uncompetitive for years. That would damage long-term value more than a hard decision now.

So: yes to negotiated alternatives being tested first. No to an upfront veto that prevents hard decisions even if the facts justify them.

## To the Union
We agree that concessions should be **temporary, limited, and reciprocal**. We also agree that the company should not use this process to impose a permanent low-trust regime.

But temporary concessions only matter if they are **material enough** to move margins and cost structure. If they only buy time, they are not a solution.

We support:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- training,
- bonus restraint,
- contractor cuts,
- and targeted wage moderation.

We do **not** support treating those tools as sufficient in principle if the gap remains large. At that point, hard capacity reduction has to stay available.

## To the Regional State Government
You are right that this cannot be handled with slogans. Site-by-site analysis is necessary.

Where we disagree is that you still sound too close to a default “no closures” stance. That may be politically understandable, but it is not enough for a listed industrial group facing a competitiveness gap. The state should force rigorous analysis, not pre-decide the answer.

A binding job-security framework through 2030 is too rigid if it blocks the very adjustment needed to restore value. We will not support a deal that protects every legacy structure while margins remain impaired.

## To the Controlling Family Holding
You are right that a cosmetic settlement would destroy value over time. We also agree that the debate must be evidence-based.

But you should not present closures as merely the last step after every other possibility is exhausted if the economics already point to overcapacity. The right standard is not ideological caution; it is whether the final package restores competitiveness with acceptable risk.

## Our position
We are prepared to support a negotiated restructuring **only if** it is:

1. **Quantified**
2. **Durable**
3. **Credibly margin-improving**
4. **Focused on structural cost reduction**
5. **Not dependent on symbolic labour concessions**
6. **Open to hard capacity reduction if needed**

## What we will not support
We will not support:
- a cosmetic no-closure settlement that leaves the cost base uncompetitive,
- blanket job guarantees that freeze the footprint through 2030,
- or a process where the company is expected to pay for trust without getting real structural change.

## What is needed next
We need the independent site-by-site comparison already requested, but with a sharper mandate:
- annual savings,
- one-time restructuring costs,
- employment impact,
- utilization impact,
- logistics and transfer costs,
- margin effect,
- and NPV under each scenario.

Until that is in hand, we remain open to a negotiated solution — but only one that **actually restores competitiveness**. If the numbers show that hard capacity reduction is required, then it should not be blocked by political or labour red lines.
