From a restructuring-economics standpoint, the round-4 positions are now sharply defined. The key issue is not whether restructuring is needed; it is **which package can credibly close the cost/capacity gap at the lowest total economic and operational cost**.

## What the debate has clarified
There are now three economically distinct paths:

### 1) No-closure negotiated package
This relies on:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- working-time changes,
- bonus restraint,
- temporary wage moderation,
- contractor and external spend cuts,
- productivity gains.

**Economic strength:** low one-time disruption, lower political and labour-relations cost.  
**Weakness:** usually insufficient if structural overcapacity and fixed-cost burden are large.  
**Risk:** may improve liquidity and margins temporarily, but leave the underlying capacity problem unresolved.

### 2) Partial capacity reduction without immediate closures
This is the middle path the union, works council, and state are trying to defend.

It typically means:
- shifting product allocation,
- internal consolidation,
- capacity rebalancing across sites,
- reducing shifts or lines,
- some voluntary departures,
- and selective underutilization of the weakest assets.

**Economic strength:** can remove some fixed cost while avoiding the highest disruption of shutdowns.  
**Weakness:** savings can be slower, less certain, and operationally complex.  
**Risk:** if sites remain duplicated or structurally oversized, this becomes a delay mechanism rather than a true reset.

### 3) Closure-led hard restructuring
This is management/investor/family’s fallback.

**Economic strength:** can deliver the largest fixed-cost reduction and fastest removal of structural overcapacity.  
**Weakness:** high one-time costs, high execution risk, strike risk, political resistance, supply-chain and transfer-cost issues.  
**Risk:** if poorly targeted, closures can destroy capacity that is still economically useful, especially if future demand recovers or EV platform allocation shifts.

---

## What matters analytically
The correct test is **site-by-site incremental economics**:

For each plant, compare:
- avoidable annual fixed cost,
- variable cost position,
- transfer and logistics costs,
- labor-cost differential,
- capex needs to remain competitive,
- utilization forecast,
- and one-time restructuring cost.

A site should be considered structurally non-viable only if, after realistic demand and product-allocation assumptions:
1. it remains materially underutilized,
2. its avoidable fixed cost cannot be reduced enough through internal measures,
3. its future capex requirement is disproportionate,
4. and its retained contribution margin is weaker than the alternative use of capacity elsewhere.

Without that analysis, “closure” is just a bargaining position.  
But equally, without a quantified package, “no closure” is just a slogan.

---

## My read on the bargaining positions
### Works Council / Union / State
Their position is economically coherent **if** they can produce enough savings from reversible measures and internal consolidation to close most of the gap. But if the structural gap is large, their red lines may force the company into a prolonged low-margin equilibrium. That is not sustainable unless ownership accepts lower returns.

### Management / Investors / Family
Their position is economically coherent **if** the site-by-site model shows that partial measures cannot bring the business back to target margins. But they still need to prove that closures are the least-cost solution after accounting for one-time costs, conflict risk, and lost future flexibility.

---

## Likely conclusion from a neutral restructuring lens
The most plausible economically defensible outcome is usually **not** either extreme:
- not a pure no-closure deal,
- and not a blanket closure program.

It is often a **hybrid package**:
- strong voluntary exits,
- working-time and pay moderation,
- substantial internal consolidation,
- selective capacity reduction,
- and only then closure or shutdown of the weakest site(s) if the economics remain clearly negative.

That approach minimizes irreversible damage while still recognizing that some hard capacity removal may be unavoidable.

---

## Bottom line
At this stage, the decisive question is not “closures yes/no,” but:

**Can the company, with quantified reversible measures plus partial capacity reduction, close the structural cost gap and restore acceptable utilization and margin without destroying value elsewhere?**

If yes, closures may be avoidable.  
If no, then the economics will likely justify one or more closures, but only after a rigorous site-by-site proof.
