We are still in a bargaining dispute, not in a declaration of surrender.

I will respond clearly to what has been said:

## To the Works Council
I agree with your core line: **no plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, and no one-sided burden shifting**. That remains the right starting point.

I also agree that management has not yet delivered the level of quantification needed to justify irreversible cuts. “Overcapacity” is not a magic word. If they want employees to make concessions, they must show:
- the actual savings target,
- what measures are truly necessary,
- and why those savings cannot be achieved through negotiated instruments first.

Where I would sharpen the position is this: **job protection and collective-bargaining protection belong together**. We cannot let management use the restructuring debate to weaken the wage framework, fragment conditions, or create a permanent low-trust regime. If concessions are discussed, they must be **temporary, limited, and collectively agreed** — not a backdoor dismantling of the tariff system.

## To management
Your position is still not acceptable as stated.

You keep saying closures “remain on the table” and that compulsory redundancies may be unavoidable. That is exactly the kind of threat that makes a negotiated solution harder, not easier. It tells the workforce: contribute first, and maybe we will still cut sites and jobs later anyway.

That is not shared sacrifice. That is pressure.

If you want a serious negotiation, then put real numbers on the table:
- how much cost reduction is required,
- how much capacity reduction is required,
- what can be achieved without closures,
- and what you yourself are prepared to give up before asking workers to sacrifice wages and security.

I am prepared to discuss:
- temporary wage moderation,
- suspension or reduction of bonus components,
- working-time flexibility,
- a jointly negotiated contribution mechanism,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- training,
- and capacity adjustment through internal and negotiated means.

But I will not accept:
- forced redundancies,
- plant closures as a bargaining weapon,
- or a restructuring that turns employees into the sole shock absorber.

If management wants labour-cost contributions, then management and ownership must also contribute visibly. That means:
- restraint on management remuneration,
- owner support for investment and transition,
- and binding commitments that protect employment and sites.

## To the state government
Your refusal to accept closures and forced redundancies is welcome. But if you want that position to hold, then you need to support a **real industrial settlement**, not just a political statement.

That means using your influence to push management toward a negotiated package that includes:
- binding site security,
- binding job security,
- investment in future products,
- and a fair sharing of sacrifices.

If the state is serious, it should support a framework in which employees contribute through agreed flexibility and temporary cost moderation, while the company commits to Germany as an industrial base.

## To the family holding
I hear the concern for long-term enterprise value, and I do not dismiss it. A destructive conflict would damage value. But value is also damaged when management tries to “solve” the problem by forcing the workforce to pay for it alone.

A durable solution needs trust, and trust needs reciprocity. If workers are asked to moderate pay or accept flexibility, then owners must accept:
- no closures,
- no forced layoffs,
- and a binding commitment to the German sites and the industrial future of the company.

That is the only basis on which sacrifice from the workforce becomes legitimate.

## To the investors
You say you want a durable and credible solution. Fine. So do we.

But credibility does not require plant closures as a first resort. It requires a package that markets can understand **and** the workforce can live with. A strike, mass conflict, or broken collective framework would be value-destructive. So would a settlement that destroys the social foundation of the company.

If you want a real turnaround, support a package with:
- quantified savings,
- negotiated capacity reduction,
- limited, temporary employee contributions,
- no forced redundancies,
- no closures,
- and a clear investment path.

That is more credible than betting on industrial conflict and hoping the workforce will fold.

## Our position, plainly stated
We are prepared to negotiate a fair contribution from employees, **but only if all of the following are on the table**:

1. **No German plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Protection of the collective wage framework**
4. **Binding job security**
5. **A fair burden-sharing package including management and owners**
6. **Investment and future product commitments**
7. **Capacity reduction through negotiated, reversible, non-destructive instruments**

If management cannot work within those conditions, then there is no balanced solution — only pressure against the workforce.

And let me be equally clear: if the company insists on closures or forced layoffs, the union will not support that path. We have already shown that the peace obligation is over. We are ready to defend the collective wage system and the jobs behind it.

If there is a serious alternative, bring it with numbers and guarantees. If not, conflict will continue.
