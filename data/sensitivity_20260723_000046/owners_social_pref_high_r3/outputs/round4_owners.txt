I will be direct.

The family holding supports a **real restructuring**. We do **not** support a cosmetic settlement that preserves the current cost base and calls it peace. That would destroy value over time and fail our stewardship duty.

At the same time, we do **not** support turning plant closures or compulsory redundancies into the default starting point. That is too blunt, too destabilising, and too likely to trigger value-destructive conflict. The burden is on this group to produce a **credible, quantified, site-by-site case** before any irreversible step is taken.

## Where I agree

### With management
- The company does have a structural cost and capacity problem.
- Labor concessions alone will not solve a large overcapacity issue.
- We need durability, not a short-lived truce.
- The discussion must be based on numbers, not slogans.

### With labour and the state
- A one-sided burden shift would be unacceptable.
- Prolonged conflict would damage the enterprise.
- Voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time flexibility, contractor reductions, training, and temporary moderation are legitimate tools.
- If employees contribute, the company must also contribute.

### With the economist and modeler
- Reversible measures have a ceiling.
- If overcapacity is structural and large, the company may need hard capacity reduction.
- The right economic test is site-by-site, not ideological.

## Where I disagree

### 1) I do not accept a blanket “no closures” rule
To the works council, union, and state: I understand the instinct, but I reject the absolutism.

A blanket no-closure stance is not a governance solution. It may feel protective, but it risks freezing structural inefficiency and pushing the company into a slower decline. That is not responsible stewardship.

Closures should not be the starting assumption. But they also cannot be ruled out in advance.

### 2) I do not accept a blanket 2030 job guarantee if it blocks restructuring
A guarantee that prevents the company from adjusting footprint and cost base can become a straitjacket. A promise that undermines competitiveness is not security; it is postponement.

### 3) I do not accept vague concessions
If the workforce is asked to contribute, the contribution must be material, quantified, and durable enough to matter. Symbolic moderation is not enough if the structural gap remains.

## What this means in practice

The only defensible path is a **structured comparison of options**, with independent verification:

1. **No-closure package**
2. **Partial capacity reduction**
3. **Closure option where site economics justify it**

That comparison must show:
- annual savings,
- one-time costs,
- employment impact,
- utilization impact,
- logistics and transfer cost,
- and margin effect.

Without that, no one should claim certainty.

[SUMMON: independent site-level restructuring auditor to verify management’s site-by-site economics, utilization assumptions, transfer costs, and closure necessity claims before any final commitment.]

## To management
You are right that durability matters. But durability is not the same as maximal pressure. If your case for closures is real, then prove it site by site. If it is not real, then stop using it as leverage.

You still need to answer clearly:
- What exact savings target do you need?
- What do voluntary exits, working-time changes, bonus restraint, contractor cuts, and productivity measures produce?
- What gap remains?
- Why is a specific closure or major reduction economically superior?

That is the standard.

## To the works council and union
I hear your demand for security first. I do not dismiss it. But I do reject the conclusion that the company must accept a blanket no-closure, no-forced-redundancy framework before the numbers are tested.

If reversible measures can close most of the gap, then a temporary moratorium may be reasonable as a testing period. But if a residual structural gap remains, the company cannot be forced to absorb that gap forever.

That is where your position becomes too rigid for the stewardship duty we carry.

## To the state government
Your leverage is real, but it should be used to force a serious, transparent process — not to predetermine the outcome. A veto is a tool for preventing bad decisions, not for protecting every existing structure regardless of economics.

I agree with:
- quantified site-by-site analysis,
- binding employee and management contributions,
- and investment commitments for Germany.

I do not agree with the implication that closures should be politically off-limits before the facts are tested.

## To the institutional investors
You are right that cosmetic peace is unacceptable. You are also right that the market needs confidence that the problem is actually fixed.

Where I push back is any suggestion that credibility requires keeping closures permanently in play as a bargaining weapon from day one. Credibility comes from a package that is:
- economically sufficient,
- operationally executable,
- and stable enough to last.

That may include partial capacity reduction. It may include closures if required. But it must be proven, not assumed.

## The family holding’s position
We will support a package only if it meets all of these conditions:

1. **It materially restores competitiveness and margins**
2. **It avoids value-destructive instability**
3. **It includes a real employee contribution**
4. **It includes visible management and owner contribution**
5. **It is backed by a quantified site-by-site business case**
6. **It does not permanently impair governance control or enterprise value**

What we will not support:
- a cosmetic deal,
- a blanket no-closure veto with no economic proof,
- a settlement that merely postpones the problem,
- or a restructuring that destroys trust without fixing the business.

## Bottom line
I am prepared to support:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- internal consolidation,
- working-time changes,
- training and qualification,
- temporary bonus restraint,
- targeted wage moderation,
- contractor and external spend reduction,
- productivity measures,
- and, if the numbers require it, partial capacity reduction.

I am **not** prepared to endorse:
- an outright blanket no-closure position,
- a guarantee that freezes the footprint through 2030,
- or a solution that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

So the next step is not another round of principle statements. It is the **independent site-by-site verification** of the savings, utilization, and closure necessity claims.

Until that analysis is on the table, I will not back irreversible measures. But I also will not back a blanket commitment that rules them out irrespective of the facts.

That is the family holding’s position.
