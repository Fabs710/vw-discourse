I will be equally direct.

We are now in the phase where everyone has stated their red lines. That is useful, but it does not solve the company’s problem. The company still has a cost and capacity gap. If we do not close it in a durable way, we will not restore competitiveness, we will not hit the return-on-sales target, and we will not free the funds needed for 2030 investment.

## To the Works Council
I hear your insistence on security first. I understand it. But I do **not** accept your conclusion.

You keep saying management has not proved closures or compulsory redundancies are necessary. That is too easy a standard. The burden is not to prove closures in the abstract; the burden is to show whether the company can achieve a **durable structural adjustment** without them. The economist and the modeler have already made clear that reversible measures have a ceiling.

So let me be clear:

- If the package is only temporary,
- if it only moderates costs for a short period,
- if it leaves low utilization, duplicated capacity, and high fixed overhead in place,

then it is **not enough**.

A “no-closure” position is not a restructuring strategy. It is a demand to preserve every legacy structure and hope the market becomes easier. That will not happen.

You also frame partial capacity reduction as if it were merely “closure by stealth.” No. Partial capacity reduction is a legitimate restructuring tool, and I welcome that fact that you are at least acknowledging capacity reduction as an economic issue. But if partial reduction does not materially remove fixed cost and overcapacity, then it is just a delay mechanism.

On job security through 2030: I will not give a blanket guarantee that freezes the cost base. That would be a promise to preserve uncompetitive structures regardless of economics. I am not prepared to do that.

## To the Union
You are right about one thing: the company cannot ask for sacrifices while offering nothing in return. That is why management has consistently said we are prepared to pair change with investment, training, redeployment, and a negotiated framework.

But I reject your claim that closures and compulsory redundancies must be removed from the field before the workforce contributes. That sequencing would make structural adjustment impossible whenever the problem is serious.

You keep saying temporary concessions should only exist inside a framework that protects jobs and sites. That is only acceptable if the framework still allows us to reach a truly competitive cost base. If it doesn’t, then it is not a framework for restructuring; it is a framework for drift.

Let me be blunt:  
If the company’s structural gap is large, then a package limited to voluntary exits, bonus restraint, working-time changes, and contractor cuts will not be enough. And if it is not enough, I will not pretend otherwise just to avoid confrontation.

## To the State Government
You are right to demand a quantified, site-by-site comparison. I support that.

But your position still sounds like: “prove closure is unavoidable, and until then it is off limits.” That creates a default veto on hard decisions. I cannot accept that as a governance principle.

If a site is structurally non-viable, then preserving it because the political preference is for no closures would be irresponsible. The state’s role is not to protect every existing site regardless of economics. It is to help secure the industrial base of the group as a whole.

So yes:
- site-by-site verification,
- independent audit,
- transparent assumptions.

But no:
- pre-commitment that rules out closures in advance,
- and no assumption that any remaining gap can be absorbed through political will alone.

## To the Family Holding
You are closer to management on the core issue, and I appreciate that you understand the danger of a cosmetic settlement.

Where I push back is that you still speak as if closures should only come after everything else has been exhausted. In practice, that can become a formula for endless delay. We cannot afford that.

You correctly say the company needs hard numbers and credible milestones. That is true. But those numbers may well point to hard capacity reduction, including site rationalization. Ownership has to be willing to back that conclusion if the data supports it.

What I will not accept is a structure where the family holding insists on competitiveness, but hesitates when competitiveness requires painful structural change.

## To the Institutional Investors
You are right to insist that the settlement must be economically sufficient and durable. That is exactly management’s position.

Where I disagree with you is the suggestion that a no-closure package is only credible if it fully closes the gap by reversible measures alone. In some cases that is simply unrealistic. If the gap is structural, then credibility requires accepting that some capacity must be removed.

In other words:  
**credibility does not mean avoiding closures at all costs; credibility means doing what the economics require.**

I also note that you now openly acknowledge that partial capacity reduction may be the best compromise. Good. That is at least a serious position. But it must be understood as real structural reduction, not a symbolic rearrangement.

## My position, stated plainly
Management’s position remains unchanged:

1. **We need a durable reduction in cost and capacity**
2. **We need a real labour-cost contribution**
3. **We need a settlement that restores margins**
4. **We need funds for future products and technology to 2030**
5. **We are open on the form, but not on the necessity of structural change**

That means I remain open to:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- working-time changes,
- internal consolidation,
- training and qualification,
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- contractor reductions,
- and partial capacity reduction.

But I am **not** open to:
- a cosmetic no-closure deal that leaves the cost base uncompetitive,
- blanket job guarantees through 2030,
- or a settlement that postpones the structural decision.

## What I need from the others
If you want me to shift, then bring something that actually closes the gap.

That means:
- quantified savings by measure,
- a residual gap that is credibly addressed,
- and a package that materially improves utilization and fixed-cost absorption.

If that cannot be shown, then I will continue to insist that closures and compulsory reductions remain available as necessary tools.

## One specific point on the bridging proposals
A time-limited moratorium on closures and compulsory redundancies may be useful **only** if it is tied to real, measurable, and sufficient structural change. I am not interested in a moratorium that merely freezes the problem for 12–18 months and then reopens the same deadlock.

If we do a moratorium, it must be:
- tied to quantified milestones,
- tied to concrete capacity reduction,
- and tied to a clear consequence if the package does not close the gap.

Otherwise it is just delay.

## Bottom line
I respect that labour and the state want security. I respect that investors want credibility. I respect that ownership wants value preservation.

But I am responsible for competitiveness. And competitiveness will not be restored by protecting every structure that is part of the cost problem.

So my line remains:

- **No cosmetic deal**
- **No structural denial**
- **No guarantee that freezes the footprint**
- **Yes to real negotiation**
- **Yes to a hard, quantified package**
- **Yes to structural change where the numbers require it**

If the others want a solution without closures, they must show — in numbers, not declarations — that it truly closes the gap. If they cannot, then they should stop pretending that “no closures” is a solution.
