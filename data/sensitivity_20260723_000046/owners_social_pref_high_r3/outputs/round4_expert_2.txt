From a restructuring-economics standpoint, the next useful step is a **site-by-site decision framework** that compares:

1. **No-closure negotiated package**
2. **Partial capacity reduction**
3. **Closure / shutdown**

The key is to quantify each site on the same basis.

## Decision framework

For each plant, estimate:

- **Annual fixed cost base**
  - labor, maintenance, utilities, overhead, depreciation/capex support
- **Variable conversion cost**
  - direct labor, energy, scrap, logistics, material handling
- **Current and forecast utilization**
  - output vs. installed capacity
- **Avoidable cost under restructuring**
  - what actually disappears if volume or headcount falls
- **One-time restructuring cost**
  - severance, site exit, asset write-downs, transfer costs, pension or contract termination if applicable
- **Incremental capex required to stay competitive**
  - especially for EV / platform conversion
- **Net annual savings**
  - after accounting for any added logistics or transfer burden
- **NPV**
  - savings over 3–5 years less one-time costs, discounted
- **Operational risk**
  - ramp risk, supply-chain disruption, labor conflict, quality loss

## Practical decision rule

A site becomes a strong closure candidate if all of the following hold:

- **persistent underutilization** after realistic product allocation,
- **high fixed cost that cannot be structurally reduced enough** through internal measures,
- **weak contribution margin** relative to alternative sites,
- **high future capex requirement** to remain competitive,
- **positive NPV of closure** versus keeping it open,
- and no strategic reason to preserve it as a buffer or platform node.

By contrast, a site should usually stay open if:

- it can reach acceptable utilization after consolidation,
- the avoided annual savings from closure are modest once transfer/logistics costs are included,
- closure would destroy useful future capacity,
- or the one-time cost and conflict risk overwhelm the benefit.

## Typical economics of the three options

### 1) No-closure negotiated package
Usually yields:
- moderate annual savings
- low one-time cost
- low immediate disruption

But it often cannot remove enough fixed cost if overcapacity is structural.

### 2) Partial capacity reduction
Usually yields:
- better savings than no-closure
- lower disruption than closure
- but more complex execution

This is often the best compromise if the gap is real but not extreme.

### 3) Closure
Usually yields:
- largest long-term fixed-cost reduction
- highest one-time cost
- highest labor and political risk

It only wins if the site is clearly structurally non-viable.

## What I would recommend analytically
Do not decide closure first. Instead:

1. Build a **site P&L** for each plant.
2. Model **reversible measures first**.
3. Then test **partial capacity reduction**.
4. Only then compare against **full closure**.
5. Use **NPV and annual margin impact**, not just headcount, as the decision metric.

## What would settle the dispute
The decisive comparison is whether the package of:
- voluntary exits,
- working-time changes,
- pay moderation,
- redeployment,
- productivity actions,
- and partial capacity reduction

can close enough of the gap to restore target margins **without** adding a closure-level one-time cost and conflict burden.

If it can, closures are not economically necessary.  
If it cannot, then at least one closure may be justified — but only by the numbers, site by site.
