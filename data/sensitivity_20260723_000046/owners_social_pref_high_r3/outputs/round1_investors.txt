We agree on the diagnosis that the company’s cost base and capacity have become a serious problem. We also agree that a purely cosmetic settlement would be a mistake. From a capital-markets perspective, that matters a lot: we need a credible path back to competitive margins, not just a temporary truce.

Where we differ is on the acceptable shape of that path.

### What we support
We would support a restructuring that is:
- **durable**, not a short-lived accounting fix;
- **quantified**, with clear savings and timing;
- **credible to investors and rating agencies**;
- and capable of restoring the core brand’s return-on-sales to target.

We also see the logic in:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- training and qualification,
- and targeted labour-cost moderation.

If those measures can produce the necessary savings with deal certainty, that is preferable to a prolonged fight.

### What we do not support
We do **not** support an outcome that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive.

So if the employee-side response is effectively:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no meaningful labour-cost contribution,
- and broad guarantees that freeze the current structure,

then that is not a solution. It would simply preserve the very overcapacity and rigidity that are eroding value.

Likewise, we do not welcome a drawn-out strike or brinkmanship that destroys earnings, delays investment, and weakens the company further. Prolonged conflict is value-destructive and ultimately harms all shareholders.

### On management’s position
We understand management’s argument that the current structure is not sustainable. But to be blunt: saying “closures are on the table” is not enough by itself. As owners, we need to see the **economic case** and the **implementation path**.

If closures are truly necessary, management must show:
- why alternative measures are insufficient,
- what savings will be achieved,
- what the timeline is,
- and how disruption risk will be contained.

If management cannot prove that, then it should not assume investor support for irreversible measures.

### On the works council, union, and state
We respect the social and political constraints, but we cannot accept that those constraints become a veto over competitiveness.

- **No compulsory redundancies** can be discussed if there is a credible package of voluntary and flexible measures that gets the job done.
- **No plant closures** is not a financially credible starting point if some sites are structurally non-viable.
- **Site guarantees through 2030** may be too rigid if they prevent necessary adaptation.

The burden must be shared. Employees cannot be the only group making concessions, but they also cannot contribute only symbolically.

### Our position
Our position is straightforward:

- We support **real restructuring**.
- We support **negotiated solutions** if they are economically sufficient.
- We do **not** support a settlement that preserves every site and every legacy protection while margins continue to deteriorate.
- We also do **not** support a reckless, inflammatory approach that triggers unnecessary industrial damage.

### What would move us
A package would need to include:
1. **Clear, durable cost reductions** sufficient to restore competitiveness.
2. **Real capacity adjustment** — whether via closures, consolidation, or equivalent hard reductions.
3. **Meaningful employee contribution**, not a symbolic gesture.
4. **Investment commitments** that support future products and technology.
5. **Enough certainty** that markets can believe the company is actually changing.

If management and labour can agree to that, we would view it positively. If the result is a half-measure that protects everyone except the shareholder, we will not support it.

In short: we are open to a negotiated solution, but only if it restores margins decisively and does not leave the company structurally impaired.
