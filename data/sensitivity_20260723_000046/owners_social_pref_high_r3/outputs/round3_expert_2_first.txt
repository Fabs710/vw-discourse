Below is a neutral restructuring model, not a legal or labor-relations opinion.

## Executive conclusion

Based on the information provided, the economically correct framework is:

- **A no-closure package can produce meaningful savings**, but usually only if it combines several measures at once and if the required gap is not too large.
- **Partial capacity reduction is typically the best economic compromise** when a company has structural overcapacity but wants to avoid immediate closures.
- **Full plant closure is economically superior only when a site has chronically low utilization, high fixed overhead, poor product fit, or requires ongoing subsidy through labor concessions.**

Without company-confidential site data, I cannot say which German plant is non-viable. But I can give a decision-useful comparison framework and a reasonable range-based estimate.

# 1) Assumptions used for the model

Because no actual plant-level P&L was provided, I use a stylized German automotive manufacturing cost structure:

### Workforce and cost assumptions
- Fully loaded annual cost per production employee: **€75k–€100k**
- Fully loaded annual cost per indirect/overhead employee: **€90k–€130k**
- Share of workers eligible for voluntary exits over 2–4 years: **15–30%** depending on site
- Severance / bridge / transition cost per exit: **€60k–€180k one-time**, depending on age profile and tenure

### Plant fixed-cost assumptions
Typical annual fixed plant overhead for a large automotive assembly or drivetrain site:
- **€80m–€200m per site per year** including indirect labor, site services, maintenance, utilities, admin, and depreciation-related site burden

### Savings realization timing
- **Year 1:** lower because restructuring costs are front-loaded
- **Years 2–5:** savings stabilize
- **Full closure:** the savings are structurally larger but come with high one-time cash costs and disruption

# 2) How each option works economically

## Option 1: No-closure labor-concession package
Typical elements:
- voluntary exits
- working-time reductions / short-time style flexibility
- bonus restraint
- temporary wage moderation
- contractor reduction
- productivity measures
- internal redeployment

### Typical annual savings range
For a large German automotive plant network, a serious package might generate:

- **€150m–€350m annual savings** in a moderate case
- **€300m–€600m annual savings** in a strong case if concessions are deep and headcount reduction is substantial

### One-time cost
- **€150m–€600m** depending on severance volume, training, redeployment, and bridge costs

### Employment impact
- Mostly **voluntary** reduction
- Could remove **1,000–4,000 jobs** across a large domestic footprint if executed aggressively
- Lower immediate disruption than closure

### Margin effect
If the core brand has, say, **€30bn–€40bn revenue** and currently struggles to meet a low single-digit ROS target, then:
- €200m savings = roughly **0.5–0.7 percentage points of operating margin**
- €400m savings = roughly **1.0–1.3 percentage points**
- €600m savings = roughly **1.5–2.0 percentage points**

That is meaningful, but not necessarily enough if the structural gap is large.

### Economic limitation
This option **does not remove fixed capacity**. If utilization is low, you are still carrying site overhead and under-absorbed fixed cost.

---

## Option 2: Partial capacity reduction without full closure
Typical elements:
- consolidation of production into fewer lines or fewer plants
- shift reductions
- removal of duplicate product allocations
- transfer of some output to higher-utilization plants
- continued operation of retained sites with lower headcount and lower overhead

### Typical annual savings range
This is usually more powerful than concessions alone:

- **€300m–€700m annual savings** for a meaningful rationalization package
- In a more aggressive case, **€700m–€1.2bn annual savings** if a major chunk of duplicated capacity is eliminated

### One-time cost
- **€400m–€1.5bn** depending on:
  - retooling
  - relocation
  - severance
  - logistics redesign
  - dual-run period
  - write-downs of site-specific assets

### Employment impact
- **2,000–7,000 jobs affected** across the system is plausible in a large restructuring
- Usually a mix of voluntary exits, redeployment, and some forced separation if the gap is large

### Margin effect
This option can improve margin by roughly:
- **0.8–2.5 percentage points** depending on scale and baseline revenue

### Economic advantage
This is often the **best balance** if the company has excess capacity but wants to preserve most German production.

### Economic limitation
Savings depend heavily on:
- whether output can be absorbed elsewhere without bottlenecks
- whether logistics costs rise materially
- whether the receiving plants need capex

---

## Option 3: Plant closure
Typical elements:
- full shutdown of one site
- transfer of product to other plants
- elimination of site overhead and indirect cost
- possible product redesign / logistics reconfiguration

### Typical annual savings range
For a significant automotive plant:
- **€150m–€400m per closed site per year** is a reasonable broad range
- If the site is large and underutilized, savings can be higher

### One-time cost
Very high:
- **€500m–€2.0bn+**
  - severance
  - site remediation
  - asset write-downs
  - retooling at receiving sites
  - logistics transition
  - possible strike/disruption costs

### Employment impact
- Usually **largest permanent headcount reduction**
- Could be **1,000–5,000+ jobs per site**, depending on site size and whether production is fully transferred

### Margin effect
A closure can improve margin materially, often by:
- **0.5–1.5 percentage points per major site closed**, after transition

### Economic advantage
This is the strongest lever if the site is:
- structurally underutilized
- duplicative
- high overhead
- high capex demand
- weak product allocation

### Economic downside
It creates:
- high one-time cash cost
- industrial relations risk
- transition risk
- reputational and political friction
- possible loss of flexibility if demand rebounds

# 3) Site-level economics: what to test

For each site, management should build a simple model with these columns:

1. **Current annual labor cost**
2. **Annual indirect/site overhead**
3. **Annual depreciation / site-specific capital charge**
4. **Current utilization**
5. **Unit conversion cost**
6. **Annual output volume**
7. **Margin contribution of products made there**
8. **Cost to move output elsewhere**
9. **Required capex to stay competitive**
10. **One-time closure cost**
11. **Savings under concession-only scenario**
12. **Savings under partial rationalization**
13. **Savings under closure**

The economic decision should then be based on **net present value**, not just annual savings.

### Risk

# 6) What the numbers imply

## If the savings target is modest
If the company needs, say, **€200m–€400m annual savings**, then a **no-closure package may be sufficient**, especially if combined with:
- voluntary exits
- wage moderation
- bonus restraint
- contractor cuts
- internal consolidation

## If the savings target is medium-large
If the target is **€500m–€900m annually**, then:
- a no-closure package may be **too small**
- a **partial capacity reduction** becomes the economically preferred compromise

## If the savings target is very large
If the company needs **€1bn+ annual structural savings**, then:
- labor concessions alone are unlikely to be enough
- at least one major site rationalization becomes economically plausible
- the final answer depends on site-specific fixed cost and utilization

---

# 7) Decision rule I would recommend

A plant should generally be considered for closure or major downsizing if all or most of the following are true:

- utilization is **below ~65%**
- the plant has **high fixed overhead**
- product allocation is duplicative
- transfer costs are manageable
- there is a lower-cost alternative site
- future capex required to keep the plant competitive is high
- negotiated concessions only postpone the cost problem

Conversely, a plant should generally be retained if:
- utilization is high
- the product is strategic
- logistics are efficient
- the site can absorb future EV or platform investment
- labor concessions can close most of the gap without permanent damage

### My neutral assessment

- **Partial capacity reduction is usually the best economic compromise.**
- **Plant closure is only justified when a site is structurally non-viable and the savings clearly exceed the transition costs.**
