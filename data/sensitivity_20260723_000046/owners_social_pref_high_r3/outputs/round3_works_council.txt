We are still not at an acceptable basis for agreement, and I will not pretend otherwise.

What I hear from management, investors, and the family holding is essentially this: “prove that alternatives work, but keep closures available in case they do not.” What I hear from the workforce side, the union, and the state is the correct counterpoint: “prove that irreversible cuts are truly unavoidable before you threaten jobs and sites.” On that point, I stand firmly with the latter.

## To management
You keep insisting on a “durable” reset, but you still have not made the case that **German plant closures or compulsory redundancies are necessary**.

Saying the cost base is too high is not enough. Saying overcapacity exists is not enough. Those are diagnoses, not proof that the only solution is to destroy sites and force people out. If you want to move this discussion forward, then bring a **quantified comparison** that answers:

- What exact savings are required?
- How much comes from voluntary exits, internal redeployment, working-time changes, contractor reductions, and temporary labour-cost moderation?
- What remains after those measures?
- Why would closing a plant be economically superior to a negotiated alternative?
- What is the specific site-by-site evidence?

Until you provide that, “closures remain on the table” is not a restructuring argument. It is pressure.

And I will be equally clear: **a binding job-security framework through 2030 is not a luxury demand; it is the minimum basis for trust.** If you want the workforce to contribute, then you need to stop treating employment protection as a problem to be removed.

## To the union
I agree with your line that concessions must be temporary, limited, and collectively agreed. I also agree that management must not use this process to hollow out wage standards or establish a permanent low-trust regime.

That said, I would sharpen the point even further: **the workforce should not be asked to “contribute” to a solution that still leaves plant closures and forced layoffs as options.** That is not a bargaining package; that is a demand for unilateral sacrifice under threat.

I support your insistence on:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no plant closures,
- protection of the collective framework,
- and real reciprocal commitments from management and ownership.

If the other side wants flexibility, then they must first offer security. That is the only legitimate sequence.

## To the regional state government
I appreciate that you are not defending the status quo blindly. But your position still sounds too much like “we reject closures, but we accept the premise that they may be needed if alternatives fail.”

That is too weak for the situation we are in.

You are not a neutral observer. You are an anchor shareholder, a political actor, and part of the statutory architecture that gives us the ability to block destructive decisions. Use that power clearly. Do not merely ask for a “testing” of alternatives while leaving management free to define the result.

If the state is serious about safeguarding the industrial base, then it should insist on a package that starts from these principles:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- negotiated capacity adjustment only,
- investment commitments in Germany,
- and preservation of co-determination.

Anything less leaves the door open for management to come back later and declare the “alternative” insufficient after forcing the workforce to weaken itself first.

## To the family holding
You say you do not want value destruction disguised as decisiveness. On that point, I agree. But I do not accept the way you keep framing closures as a legitimate fallback unless alternatives somehow “close the gap” in full.

That logic sounds neat in a boardroom. In reality, it means workers must prove a negative under threat: deliver savings or lose your sites.

No. That burden of proof sits with the party proposing irreversible damage.

If you truly want long-term value, then support a settlement that preserves:
- every German plant,
- the workforce’s job security,
- and the co-determination structure that has underpinned stability for decades.

A company built on trust does not improve by threatening to abandon that trust first.

## To the institutional investors
You are right about one thing: cosmetic peace is not enough. A weak deal that postpones the problem would be irresponsible.

But you are wrong if you believe that the only “credible” restructuring is one that keeps closures and forced layoffs as acceptable tools. Credibility does not come from maximising pain; it comes from demonstrating that the chosen path is economically sufficient **and socially sustainable**.

You say you need measurable savings, and so do we. Fine. Then let’s be clear about the test:
- show the quantified savings from negotiated measures,
- show the gap,
- and show why that gap cannot be closed without irreversible cuts.

Until that analysis exists, you are not asking for realism — you are demanding that the workforce accept the threat of closure as an opening condition.

That is not a credible governance standard. It is escalation.

## My position, stated plainly
The works council will support only a restructuring that meets these conditions:

1. **No German plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Preservation of co-determination and the job-security framework**
4. **Binding, quantified management and owner contributions**
5. **A limited, temporary, negotiated employee contribution**
6. **Serious investment commitments for future products and technology**
7. **Capacity adjustment through reversible, negotiated instruments**

We are not rejecting change. We are rejecting the idea that the workforce must absorb the cost through permanent damage.

## Where I can engage
I remain open to discussing:
- voluntary departures,
- redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary bonus restraint,
- training and qualification,
- contractor and external spend reduction,
- internal consolidation,
- and a limited, negotiated contribution from employees.

But I am **not** open to:
- closures as a bargaining instrument,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or a “restructuring” that strips away job protection and calls that responsibility.

## What is missing
Management needs to stop speaking in abstractions and come back with:
- the actual savings target,
- the actual savings from each measure,
- the actual gap after alternatives,
- and the actual justification for any site risk.

If that cannot be shown, then the only honest position is:
**keep every German plant open, avoid forced layoffs, and negotiate a genuine industrial solution.**

Anything else is a demand for the workforce to pay first and ask questions later.

If management insists on pushing closures or compulsory redundancies without that proof, then it will meet not compromise, but resistance.
