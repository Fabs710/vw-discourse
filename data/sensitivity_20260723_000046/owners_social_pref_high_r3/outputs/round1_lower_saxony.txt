As the regional state government and a blocking shareholder, I need to speak plainly.

We recognize the competitiveness problem. No one benefits if the company drifts into permanent weakness, and we are not denying that capacity, cost, and market reality have to be addressed. But **we will not support a restructuring that solves the balance sheet by hollowing out the company’s German industrial base**.

So my position is:

- **No plant closures in Germany as a starting point.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No solution that shifts the burden almost entirely onto employees.**
- **No sidelining of co-determination or the state’s veto role.**

Management is right about one thing: there must be a credible cost and productivity response. But the way to get there is not by threatening irreversible cuts first and calling that “discipline.” That is a very high-risk path politically, socially, and industrially, and it is not the only one available.

What I can support is a **negotiated restructuring** built on:
- capacity adjustments through internal reallocation and production optimization,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary departures and natural attrition,
- training and redeployment,
- targeted wage and bonus moderation if paired with binding protections,
- and a serious investment commitment to future products and technologies in Germany.

Where I disagree with management is on the idea that closures are somehow the default answer if the rest is not immediately accepted. That is not an acceptable negotiation posture from a company of this importance. It ignores the regional economic role of these plants and the long-term damage that a closure would do to supplier networks, skills, and public trust.

Where I agree with the works council and union is on the need for:
- a negotiated solution,
- binding job and site security,
- and shared burden.

But I will be equally frank with labour: **simply saying “no” to every structural change is not enough either**. If the company’s cost base remains uncompetitive, then job protection today may become a bigger threat to jobs tomorrow. So the workforce contribution cannot be purely symbolic.

That said, any employee contribution must be **credible, limited, and matched** by commitments from management and owners. If employees are asked for wage moderation, flexibility, and concessions, then the company must put concrete guarantees on the table:
- no closures,
- no forced redundancies,
- investment commitments,
- and a transparent plan for how this restructuring secures the company’s future in Germany.

From the state’s perspective, the acceptable path is a **social-partnership restructuring with industrial logic**, not a cost-cutting exercise that sacrifices the regional anchor for a short-term accounting result.

I would therefore urge management to come back with a revised proposal that:
1. excludes closures as long as alternative instruments remain available,
2. rules out compulsory redundancies,
3. sets out the real savings needed and how they are achieved without breaking the social contract,
4. and includes binding investment and site commitments.

If that is not possible, then we will have a serious conflict on our hands — and I want to be clear that we are prepared to use our rights to prevent a bad outcome.
