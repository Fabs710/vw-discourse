We are now at the point where the real divide is unmistakable.

The works council is right to insist that the workforce should not be asked to absorb irreversible damage first and receive “security” later. Management, investors, and the family holding are right about one thing as well: this is not a cosmetic problem, and purely symbolic concessions will not solve it.

But I need to be equally clear: **the argument that closures and compulsory redundancies must remain available as default pressure tools is still not acceptable.** That is not a balanced restructuring logic. It is a way to shift all structural risk onto employees and then call that realism.

## Where I agree

### With the works council
I agree with the core line:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- preservation of the collective framework,
- and no one-sided burden shifting.

I also agree that **temporary concessions only make sense if they buy real security**. Workers should not be asked to fund a restructuring that leaves their own jobs and sites hanging in the balance.

### With the state government
I agree that the company needs a quantified, site-by-site test. That is essential. We should not argue in abstractions.

### With the economist and modeler
I agree that reversible measures have a ceiling and that overcapacity is a real issue. That is exactly why we proposed a negotiated contribution package in the first place. But the modeler also showed something important: **partial capacity reduction is the economically sensible middle path if the company genuinely needs more than reversible measures alone**.

That is the route we should be testing—not a closure-first logic.

## Where I disagree

### With management
You keep saying the company needs a durable reset, and I do not dispute that. What I dispute is your attempt to convert that into a mandate for keeping closures and forced redundancies live until the workforce concedes enough.

You still have not produced the decisive evidence:
- exact savings target,
- savings by measure,
- residual gap,
- and site-by-site proof that a no-closure solution cannot work.

Until that is on the table, closures are not a solution. They are a threat.

And I reject the notion that temporary, limited wage moderation or bonus restraint is only acceptable if it is “material enough” by your definition. If you want a labour contribution, then negotiate a fair one. But do not pretend that the only meaningful contribution is one that weakens job security.

### With the family holding and investors
You both speak in terms of value preservation and credibility. Fine. But credibility is destroyed when one side is forced to surrender job security while the others retain all options.

Your insistence that closures must remain available “in case” the gap is not closed is exactly the problem. It tells the workforce:
- give first,
- trust later,
- and if that is still not enough, we may still cut you.

That is not a fair bargaining framework.

## The union’s position
We remain prepared to support a **real, quantified, and time-limited contribution package** from employees, but only under conditions that make it legitimate and durable.

### We can discuss:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- internal consolidation,
- working-time changes,
- training and qualification,
- bonus restraint,
- targeted and temporary wage moderation,
- contractor and external spend reduction,
- and productivity measures.

### We will not accept:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- dismantling the collective wage framework,
- or a “restructuring” where employees pay first and still remain exposed to later irreversible cuts.

## On the bridge proposals
The moderator’s bridge ideas are useful as process ideas, but the substance still matters.

### 1) Time-limited moratorium
A moratorium on closures and forced redundancies is a minimum requirement for serious negotiation. On that point I support the idea.

But I do **not** accept a moratorium as merely a pause before the same threat returns. If it is only a waiting room for closure, then it is worthless.

### 2) Hybrid restructuring with capacity reduction first
This is the only economically sensible route I can see that is still consistent with our red lines—**if it is genuinely about internal adjustment, not pre-sorting sites for closure**.

So yes to:
- capacity reduction through redeployment,
- voluntary departures,
- working-time flexibility,
- training,
- temporary labour-cost moderation,
- and product allocation changes.

No to:
- “capacity reduction” as a euphemism for closure by stealth,
- or a process that leaves one site after another exposed.

### 3) Security-for-contribution package
This is the right structure, but only if the security is binding and not conditional on management later deciding the contribution was sufficient.

We can contribute only inside a framework that protects:
- jobs,
- sites,
- and the collective bargaining framework.

## Our hard line
Let me state it plainly:

1. **No compulsory redundancies**
2. **No plant closures**
3. **Preservation of the collective wage agreement**
4. **Binding job security through the negotiated period**
5. **Real management and owner contributions**
6. **Limited, temporary employee contributions**
7. **Capacity adjustment through reversible, negotiated means**

That is not maximalism. That is the minimum fair settlement.

## What would move us
What could move the union is not more pressure, but a package with all of the following:
- quantified savings targets,
- binding management contribution,
- binding owner contribution,
- serious investment commitments,
- and a hard guarantee that employees are not paying into a process that can still be converted into closures later.

If management wants a deal, then it must accept that **employees do not bear all the pain**.

## What happens if that is refused
If the company insists on preserving closures and forced redundancies as live options, then it should expect industrial resistance. The peace obligation is over. We are not bargaining under surrender conditions.

## My answer to the key question
Would I support a time-limited no-closure moratorium paired with measurable milestones?

**Yes, if and only if it is a real protection period, not a bridge to later closures.**

Would I support temporary concessions if reversible measures cover most of the gap?

**Yes, if the remaining gap is closed by management, ownership, and productivity gains—not by later cutting plants or forcing layoffs.**

That is the boundary.

## Final position
The union supports a negotiated restructuring only if it delivers:

- no compulsory redundancies,
- no plant closures,
- a protected collective framework,
- binding reciprocal contributions,
- and a durable industrial future for the German sites.

If management and owners want contribution, they will get it. But they will not get it on the basis of open-ended insecurity and irreversible threats.
