I will respond plainly, because the discussion has now reached the point where vagueness is no longer useful.

## To management
I agree with your diagnosis that the company needs a durable competitiveness reset. If German cost levels remain structurally above the market and the brand misses its margin target, value erosion is real. The family holding cannot support drift.

But I do **not** yet see a sufficiently disciplined case for making plant closures the default answer.

You have said the measures must be “meaningful” and “durable,” which is correct. What is still missing is the **site-by-site proof** that:
- the savings needed cannot be achieved through a negotiated package first,
- the disruption cost of a closure is lower than the economic benefit,
- and the remaining industrial footprint remains stronger after the shock.

That proof matters, because a badly executed restructuring can destroy know-how, trigger prolonged conflict, and impair the core asset for years. As owners, we will not endorse value destruction disguised as decisiveness.

So my position is:
- **Yes** to real restructuring.
- **Yes** to material labour-cost improvement.
- **Yes** to capacity reduction.
- **No** to treating closures as the first move before alternatives are properly tested.

If management wants ownership support for harder measures, bring a quantified plan, not just a principle.

## To the Works Council and the Union
I agree with you on one important point: a purely one-sided burden shift would be unacceptable and self-defeating. A long conflict would hurt everyone, including the owners.

I also agree that trust has already been strained and that concessions from employees must be matched by visible commitments from management and ownership.

Where I disagree is with the idea that **“no closures, no compulsory redundancies, job security through 2030”** can be stated as a permanent upfront framework before the economics are tested. That is not a credible governance position, and it would tie the company’s hands too tightly if the numbers do not work.

Let me be equally clear: I am **not** asking you to surrender your interests. I am saying that if you want closures to stay off the table, then the alternative package must be strong enough to close the gap in reality, not just in rhetoric.

So I would see room only for:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- working-time changes,
- temporary and limited wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- productivity improvements,
- and internal consolidation.

But these must produce **real, measurable savings**. If they do not, then we are back to harder options. The burden cannot be shifted entirely onto owners while the operating structure remains unchanged.

## To the Regional State Government
I appreciate your emphasis on industrial base, social stability, and the veto reality under the special statute. That is not just politics; it is part of the company’s constitutional reality.

But I cannot support a position that effectively says “no closures” without a workable alternative that delivers competitiveness. That would simply postpone the problem and increase the eventual damage.

Your call for a quantified, site-by-site case is fair. I support that. Management should be forced to justify any proposed closure with hard numbers and a comparison against negotiated alternatives.

At the same time, I would caution against turning the state’s blocking position into a blanket guarantee of continuity. That would invite strategic complacency. The company must still be able to adapt its footprint if the economics truly require it.

So my position aligns with yours on process:
- test alternatives first,
- demand proof,
- preserve co-determination,
- preserve industrial logic.

But I do **not** accept a political line that rules out structural change regardless of the facts.

## To the Institutional Investors
You are correct that the company needs a credible and investable path back to target margins. You are also correct that cosmetic settlements are value-destructive.

Where I agree with you:
- savings must be real,
- capacity reduction must be real,
- and employee contributions cannot be symbolic.

Where I part company with you is if “real” is equated too quickly with plant closures as the preferred instrument. If alternative measures can deliver the same effect with less destruction, that is superior stewardship.

Investors should expect a package that is:
1. economically sufficient,
2. operationally executable,
3. and stable enough to avoid recurring conflict.

A strike-driven or closure-driven outcome that triggers prolonged operational damage would not be a success. So yes to hard economics, but not to lazy assumptions about how to get there.

## My position as controlling shareholder
The family holding will support a restructuring that restores competitiveness and margins **without permanently impairing the enterprise or our governance position**.

That means:
- **real savings**, not gestures;
- **real capacity adjustment**, not merely reshuffling;
- **real employee contribution**, not one-sided burden shifting;
- **real investment commitments**, so the future is not sacrificed for the present;
- and **no value-destructive instability**.

What I am **not** prepared to support:
- a plan that makes closures the automatic first resort,
- a plan that triggers prolonged industrial conflict,
- a plan that weakens the company’s long-term industrial base for a short-term accounting win,
- or a settlement that damages ownership control through chaos, reputational harm, or governance paralysis.

## Bottom line
I am open to a negotiated package, but only if it is hard enough to work.

That package must answer, with numbers:
- how much savings are needed,
- which measures deliver them,
- what capacity reduction is actually achieved,
- what employee contributions are time-bound and meaningful,
- and what investment/security commitments make the package durable.

If management can demonstrate that a negotiated alternative closes the gap, I will support it.  
If not, then we should not pretend that sentiment can substitute for economics.

I would therefore urge management to return with a **quantified restructuring proposal** and a comparison of:
- negotiated capacity reduction,
- voluntary and flexible measures,
- and, only if unavoidable, any proposed closure.

Until then, I will not endorse irreversible measures on the basis of general assertions alone.
