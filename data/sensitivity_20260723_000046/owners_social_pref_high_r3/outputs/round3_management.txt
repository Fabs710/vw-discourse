I will respond directly.

The economist’s assessment is useful, and in one respect it confirms management’s position: **a purely reversible, no-closure package is unlikely to be enough if the structural gap is large.** That is exactly why I have said from the beginning that this is not a temporary fix problem. We need a durable reset of cost and capacity.

## To the economist
Your analysis supports three points management has made all along:

1. **Labor concessions alone have a ceiling.**  
   Voluntary exits, wage moderation, bonus restraint, and contractor reductions can help, but they do not eliminate fixed costs or chronic overcapacity.

2. **Capacity removal is economically more powerful than tinkering at the margin.**  
   If a site is subscale or duplicative, keeping it open indefinitely simply preserves an uncompetitive cost base.

3. **The real test is site-by-site economics.**  
   I agree. That is why we need a quantified comparison, not slogans about “no closures.”

Where your analysis also matters is this: if the gap is high, then the employee side’s insistence on blanket job security through 2030 is not compatible with the economics. It may be politically attractive, but it does not solve the industrial problem.

## To the Works Council
You keep saying management has not proved closures are necessary. Fair enough — but you have also not produced a package that closes the gap.

What you have offered is a list of instruments:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- working-time changes,
- contractor reductions,
- temporary moderation.

Those are not outcomes. They are inputs. The question is whether they produce enough savings at the scale and duration the business needs.

And I reject your attempt to redefine “proof” as “management must show closure is superior in every case before any hard decision is allowed.” That is not how restructuring works. We compare options. We do not bury the company waiting for a perfect proof standard that always favors delay.

I also reject the idea that **job security through 2030** can be treated as the minimum basis for trust if the company’s cost base remains uncompetitive. If you lock in all sites and all legacy protections, then you have not negotiated a restructuring — you have just deferred the crisis.

## To the Union
You say concessions must be temporary and limited. That is only acceptable if they are **material enough** to restore margins. If they are not, then they are not a restructuring contribution; they are a pause.

I hear your concern about preserving the collective framework. But the collective framework cannot become a shield for structural inefficiency. If the company’s European market is shrinking and German factory costs are above target, then we need real cost relief, not symbolic solidarity.

So let me be blunt:
- I do not accept a no-closure posture as a starting point.
- I do not accept a no-forced-redundancies posture as an absolute if the package does not close the gap.
- I do accept temporary, negotiated measures if — and only if — they deliver durable economic impact.

That is not hostility. That is the minimum required for competitiveness.

## To the State Government
You are right to insist on a quantified, site-by-site case. I support that completely.

What I do **not** support is the way you keep trying to translate “test the alternatives” into “closures should be politically off limits.” The state can ask for proof; it cannot manufacture viability where the numbers do not support it.

If a site is structurally non-viable, then the regional interest is not served by preserving it at any cost. It is served by preserving a stronger industrial base overall, even if that means hard decisions at individual sites.

So I agree on process:
- quantify,
- compare,
- test alternatives first.

But I disagree with your implied conclusion that no German closure can be contemplated. If the facts point there, the company must be allowed to act.

## To the Family Holding
You are right that a badly executed restructuring would destroy value. But you are too cautious if you frame closures as something that should only come after every other option has been tested to exhaustion.

The family holding should be asking a harder question:
- What combination of measures restores the brand’s return on sales,
- protects future investment to 2030,
- and permanently reduces the cost base?

If the answer includes site rationalization, then ownership should support it. Not because it is pleasant, but because value destruction is also caused by protecting uneconomic capacity.

## To the Institutional Investors
You are the most aligned with management on the core issue: **credibility matters, and cosmetic peace is unacceptable.** On that we agree.

Where I push back is your tendency to talk as if “real restructuring” can be achieved only if plant closures stay constantly in play. That is not a fetish for hard choices; it is a recognition that fixed costs and overcapacity are real.

I do agree with your call for:
- quantified savings,
- measurable milestones,
- and a believable path to target margins.

But the market will not believe a settlement that leaves the structural problem untouched. Nor will it believe one that is endlessly delayed by red lines that make meaningful action impossible.

## My position, stated plainly
Management’s objective has not changed, and it will not change:

### Non-negotiable:
- **durable reduction in cost and capacity**
- **material labor-cost contribution**
- **restoration of brand profitability**
- **freed funds for future products and technology to 2030**

### Open on the form:
- closures,
- consolidation,
- voluntary exits,
- working-time changes,
- redeployment,
- temporary wage moderation,
- productivity measures.

### Not acceptable:
- a cosmetic deal that preserves structural overcapacity,
- blanket job guarantees that freeze the cost base,
- symbolic concessions that do not change the economics,
- or a settlement that leaves the company uncompetitive.

## What I take from the economist’s assessment
The right next step is not to repeat principles. It is to produce a **site-by-site quantified comparison** of three scenarios:

1. **No-closure negotiated package**
2. **Partial hard capacity reduction**
3. **Full closure / site rationalization where required**

That comparison must show:
- annual savings,
- one-time restructuring costs,
- headcount effects,
- utilization effects,
- logistics and transfer costs,
- and implementation risk.

I will support a negotiated alternative **if it truly closes the gap**.  
If it does not, then I will not retreat from the conclusion that harder structural measures are necessary.

We are negotiating **how much structural change is enough**.
