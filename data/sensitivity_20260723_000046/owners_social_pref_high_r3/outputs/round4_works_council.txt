We are still not at an acceptable basis for agreement, and I will say that plainly.

The moderator is right about one thing: the debate has now reached the real fault line. It is **not** between “restructuring” and “no restructuring.” It is between:

- a restructuring that starts from **security, negotiated change, and reversible measures**, and
- a restructuring that starts from **threat, irreversible cuts, and then asks the workforce to trust management afterward**.

On that second model, I will not move.

## To management
I agree that the company needs a real restructuring. I do **not** agree that you have shown closures or compulsory redundancies are necessary.

You and the investors keep repeating “durable reset” and “structural gap” as if saying it often enough makes it true. It does not. The expert analysis confirmed what we already knew: reversible measures have a ceiling, but **you still have not quantified the gap in a way that proves closures are needed**.

So let me restate the burden correctly:

- Show the actual savings target.
- Show the savings from voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time measures, contractor cuts, bonus moderation, and productivity.
- Show the residual gap.
- Show why that gap cannot be closed without destroying sites and jobs.

Until you do that, “closures remain on the table” is not analysis. It is pressure.

And I reject your attempt to treat **partial capacity reduction** as a polite version of the same thing. If partial capacity reduction is a euphemism for preparing closures site by site, then say so openly. If it is something else, then show the plan and protect the workforce while doing it.

## To the union
I agree with you on the red lines:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no plant closures,
- no hollowing out of the collective framework,
- no one-sided burden shifting.

That remains the only legitimate starting point.

Where I would go even further is this: temporary concessions are only acceptable if they are paired with **binding no-closure and no-forced-layoff guarantees**. Otherwise the workforce is paying into a process that still leaves its own destruction available later. That is not solidarity; that is extortion by sequence.

I support your position that if the company wants flexibility, it must first provide security.

## To the state government
You are right to insist on site-by-site analysis. But you are still not taking the next necessary step.

You keep saying closures are not the starting point. Fine. But you also keep leaving the door open to them if the “numbers later justify them.” That is exactly the opening management wants: ask the workforce for concessions now, then declare the package insufficient later.

I expect the state, as anchor shareholder and political actor, to use its leverage to stop that game.

That means:
- no German plant closures during the negotiated period,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- a real industrial plan,
- and binding investment commitments for future products in Germany.

If you want the workforce to accept change, then the state must help guarantee that change does not become a license for site destruction.

## To the family holding
You say you do not want a cosmetic settlement. I agree. But you are still too comfortable with the idea that closures can be the “economically necessary” fallback before alternatives are fully secured.

That is not how trust works. You cannot demand loyalty from the workforce and then reserve the right to dismantle its sites as the default pressure point.

If you want long-term value, back a solution that preserves:
- every German plant,
- co-determination,
- and the job-security framework.

If you believe the economics are so tight that some sites are truly non-viable, then prove it in a site-by-site way — not by keeping everyone under threat while the numbers are “worked out.”

## To the institutional investors
You are right about one point: cosmetic peace would be bad. On that I agree.

But you are wrong if you think credibility means keeping closures and forced redundancies permanently available as bargaining weapons. That is not credibility; that is a way to make the workforce pay for management’s strategic mistakes.

You want a package that restores margins and gives the market confidence. Fine. Then support a package that actually does so **without** blowing up the industrial settlement that has sustained the company for decades.

A negotiated no-closure package is not unserious. It becomes serious if:
- it is quantified,
- it is time-bound,
- it has binding contributions from all sides,
- and it is protected by enforceable guarantees.

That is the standard I support.

## On the facilitator’s bridge proposals

### 1) Time-limited moratorium
A moratorium on closures and compulsory redundancies is not a “bridge” to me. It is the **minimum condition** for any serious negotiation. Without it, the workforce is being asked to negotiate under a loaded gun.

A 12–18 month moratorium is better than nothing, but I will not treat it as an acceptable substitute for a real commitment to keep all German plants open. If it is merely a pause before the same threat returns, it is not a solution.

### 2) Hybrid restructuring with “capacity reduction first”
This is where the language starts to become dangerous. “Capacity reduction first” sounds moderate, but in practice it can be used to pre-sort sites for closure later.

I will only entertain capacity adjustment through:
- internal consolidation,
- voluntary departures,
- working-time flexibility,
- training and redeployment,
- temporary cost moderation,
- and product allocation changes

**if and only if** the result is **no German plant closure and no compulsory redundancies**.

If “capacity reduction” means one site dies quietly after another, then no.

### 3) Security-for-contribution package
This is closer to a workable logic, but only if the security is real and binding.

I could support:
- temporary employee contributions,
- if management and owners commit to no closures,
- no forced redundancies,
- investment in Germany,
- and preservation of co-determination.

What I will not support is a package where workers give first and protection remains conditional on management later deciding it was enough.

## Where I stand on the actual test
The moderator asks whether I would accept a time-limited no-closure moratorium if reversible measures cover 70–80% of the gap but not all of it.

My answer is: **not as a route to later closure, no.**

If reversible measures cover most of the gap, then the remaining gap must be closed through:
- management contribution,
- owner contribution,
- productivity gains,
- internal reallocation,
- and temporary employee moderation.

That is what a negotiated solution means.

It does **not** mean: “good news, we only need a little closure now.”

We do not accept a principle in which a company first extracts concessions, then keeps the right to shut sites because the concessions were not quite enough. That is an endless escalation trap and a betrayal of trust.

If management wants a real negotiation, bring back:

But even then, my red lines remain. Proof does not automatically create permission to close plants. It only creates pressure for management, owners, and investors to contribute honestly instead of reaching first for irreversible damage.
