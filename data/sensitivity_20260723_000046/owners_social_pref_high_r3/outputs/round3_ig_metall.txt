We are still at the same fundamental point: **management wants the workforce to bear structural adjustment risk first, while keeping closures and forced redundancies as pressure tools.** That is not a fair bargaining basis, and I will not pretend it is.

## To management
You keep saying the company needs a durable reset. I do **not** dispute that the cost problem is real. What I do dispute is your attempt to turn that into a blank cheque for irreversible cuts.

You have still not answered the central question:

- **What exact savings are needed?**
- **What do voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time changes, contractor reduction, bonus moderation, and productivity measures deliver in hard numbers?**
- **What gap remains after those measures?**
- **Why is a closure economically superior to a negotiated alternative?**
- **Which site is allegedly non-viable, and on what evidence?**

Until you answer that, “closures remain on the table” is not proof. It is leverage.

And I want to be equally clear on the labour side: **a binding job-security framework is not some nice extra.** If employees are asked to make concessions, then those concessions must buy real security, not vague promises that management may still cut sites later.

## To the works council
I support your core red lines:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- preservation of co-determination,
- and no one-sided burden shifting.

That is the right line.

Where I would sharpen it is this: we should not let management divide us by pretending that a “temporary contribution” from workers is somehow compatible with keeping closure threats alive indefinitely. It is not. If employees contribute, then the company must commit to a **credible no-closure and no-forced-layoff framework** for the negotiated period.

So yes to:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- internal flexibility,
- working-time arrangements,
- training,
- targeted and temporary wage moderation,
- and limited bonus restraint,

but only if those measures are tied to **binding guarantees** and a real industrial plan. Not symbolic sacrifice. Not open-ended uncertainty.

## To the state government
I appreciate your insistence on a quantified, site-by-site case. That is correct, and it matters.

But I need to be blunt: your position still sounds too much like “we reject closures as a first step, but we accept them if the numbers later justify them.” That is not enough protection for the workforce or the industrial base.

If the state is serious about using its blocking power and political leverage, then it should insist on:
- a negotiated no-closure solution as the default,
- a full testing of alternatives before any irreversible step,
- and a package in which employees’ contributions are matched by management, owners, and public commitment to future products.

The state should not be helping management preserve the option to cut first and justify later.

## To the controlling family holding
You say you want long-term value, and I agree that value destruction would be disastrous. But I do not accept the idea that workers must “prove” alternative measures can fully close the gap before closures are ruled out.

The burden of proof is on those proposing irreversible damage.

If ownership wants a solution that the workforce can accept, then it must support:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- visible owner contribution,
- management restraint,
- and a future-facing investment plan.

If the family holding backs a settlement built on threat and uncertainty, it should not be surprised when trust collapses and the workforce resists.

## To the institutional investors
You are right that cosmetic peace is not enough. I agree.

But you are wrong if you think “real restructuring” must mean keeping closures and forced layoffs available as bargaining weapons. That is not credibility; that is pressure dressed up as discipline.

If you want a durable and investable outcome, then support a package that is:
- economically sufficient,
- socially sustainable,
- and implementable without a permanent labour conflict.

That means:
- real savings,
- yes;
- real capacity adjustment,
- yes;
- but achieved through negotiated means first, not through plant closure threats.

## Our bargaining position
We remain prepared to discuss a fair employee contribution, but only under conditions that make it legitimate.

### What we can discuss
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- training and qualification,
- internal consolidation,
- reduced contractor and external spend,
- and targeted productivity measures.

### What we will not accept
- German plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- dismantling of the collective wage framework,
- or a restructuring where employees carry the pain while management and owners keep their options fully open.

## The test for any real proposal
If management wants a serious negotiation, bring back a package with:
1. the actual savings target,
2. the savings by measure,
3. the residual gap,
4. the site-by-site rationale for any risk,
5. the management and ownership contribution,
6. and the binding guarantees for jobs, sites, and the collective framework.

Without that, there is no serious basis for agreement.

And let me say this plainly: **if the company insists on preserving closures and forced redundancies as live options, then it should not expect the union to deliver industrial peace or collective concessions.** The peace obligation is over. We are not bargaining under surrender conditions.
