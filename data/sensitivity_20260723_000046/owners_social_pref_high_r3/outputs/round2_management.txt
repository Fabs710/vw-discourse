I will respond directly and clearly.

## To the Works Council

You keep repeating “no closures, no forced redundancies, no changes to the structure.” I understand the political line, but it still does not answer the economic reality.

We are not dealing with a marginal efficiency issue. We are facing a structural cost and capacity problem. If you insist on preserving every German site and every legacy protection, then you are effectively asking management to accept a cost base that is already out of line with the market and to hope the situation improves by itself. It will not.

### Where I agree
- I agree that a cosmetic deal is useless.
- I agree that voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time adjustments, training, and targeted efficiency measures should be used.
- I agree that trust matters, and a settlement must be implementable.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept your claim that closures are somehow off the table before the numbers have been tested. They are on the table because the company needs a real structural reset, not because we enjoy confrontation.

You ask for proof that alternative measures cannot deliver the required savings. Fair enough — but the reverse is also true: show me a package that actually closes the cost and capacity gap. So far, what you are offering is a list of instruments, not a fully credible solution.

And let me be blunt: “binding job security through 2030” for all sites is not compatible with a serious restructuring if the cost base stays where it is. That is not a future-proof plan. That is freezing the problem in place.

## To the Industrial Union

You are right about one thing: concessions must not be open-ended, symbolic, or a backdoor dismantling of collective standards. I do not want a permanent low-trust regime either.

But your position still avoids the central issue: **how do we materially reduce labour cost and capacity?**

You say:
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- training.

Those are all tools, not outcomes. I am prepared to discuss them only if they produce a **durable and substantial** saving. If they do not, then they are not enough.

### What I will not accept
- No closures ever.
- No compulsory redundancies ever.
- No meaningful labour-cost contribution.
- No capacity reduction beyond internal reshuffling.
That combination does not restore competitiveness. It preserves rigidity.

### On burden sharing
Yes, management and owners must also contribute. That is not in dispute. We will not run a restructuring where employees carry everything. But ownership contribution and management restraint do **not** replace the need for a real employee contribution.

If the workforce wants to avoid closures and forced reductions, then the employee package must be meaningful enough to deliver the same economic effect. Otherwise, the choice is not “social partnership versus confrontation”; it is simply postponing an unavoidable adjustment.

## To the Regional State Government

You say you do not want closures, and you emphasize the regional industrial base. I understand that. But your position can become a veto on reality if it is not paired with an alternative that actually works.

I welcome your support for negotiation and industrial logic. But I will not accept a political line that says:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no deep labour-cost adjustment,
- and no reduction in structural overcapacity.

That is not a strategy. That is wishful thinking.

If the state wants to prevent closures, then it must also support a package that delivers:
- real cost relief,
- real productivity gains,
- and real capacity alignment with market demand.

If not, you are asking the company to carry an uneconomic structure indefinitely.

## To the Controlling Family Holding

You are right to insist on long-term value, and you are right that a destructive confrontation would damage the company. But value destruction also happens when we protect an uncompetitive structure because it is politically easier.

You say closures should not be the default and the economic case must be proven. I accept that. But “not the default” does not mean “never.” If a site is structurally non-viable, we cannot pretend otherwise.

What I need from ownership is not a preference for stability. I need support for a restructuring that is hard enough to restore competitiveness. That means:
- material cost reduction,
- material capacity reduction,
- and a settlement the market will believe.

If the only acceptable outcome is one that leaves all sites intact and all legacy protections untouched, then we are not talking about value preservation. We are talking about delay.

## To the Institutional Investors

You are the most honest on the core issue: a solution must be economically sufficient and credible to the market.

I agree with that completely.

Where I differ is that you seem to think closures are simply one option among many and that management should be cautious about them unless proven necessary. That is true in principle. But in practice, if the alternative package does **not** close the gap, then closures are exactly what has to be considered.

You also warn against prolonged strike action. So do I. But strikes become more likely when the employee side assumes management will blink before it makes structural decisions. That will not happen.

### What investors should expect
- real and durable cost reductions,
- real capacity alignment,
- a meaningful labour-cost contribution,
- and a credible path back to target margins.

A half-measure is not investable. A purely defensive settlement is not investable either.

## My position, plainly

Management is prepared to negotiate, but we will not negotiate away the objective.

### Non-negotiable for us:
- the restructuring must deliver a **durable** reduction in cost and capacity,
- the labour cost base must come down materially,
- the company must regain competitiveness and return to target margins,
- and resources must be freed for future products and technology to 2030.

### Open on the form:
- plant closures versus equivalent hard capacity reduction,
- the mix of voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time changes, and labour-cost measures,
- the sequencing and timing of reductions,
- and the precise shape of investment commitments.

### Not acceptable:
- a package that keeps every plant open at any cost,
- blanket job guarantees to 2030 with no structural reset,
- symbolic concessions that do not materially change the economics,
- or a settlement that leaves the company overburdened and uncompetitive.

## Where this leaves us

If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then it must table a package that achieves the same economic effect:
- sizable and durable labour-cost savings,
- real capacity reduction,
- genuine flexibility,
- and enough certainty to restore investor confidence.

If you cannot do that, then closures remain a necessary part of the restructuring discussion.

I am not going to pretend otherwise.
