I will be direct.

## To management
I agree with you on the diagnosis: the current cost base, utilisation, and footprint cannot simply remain unchanged if the company is to restore margins and fund future products. I also agree that a cosmetic compromise would be worse than no deal at all.

Where I **do not** agree is your insistence that closures and compulsory redundancies must remain the default or inevitable answer.

That is too blunt, and it risks destroying more value than it creates.

What matters to the family holding is not whether management can demonstrate toughness. It is whether the outcome is:
- economically real,
- durable,
- governable,
- and not so conflict-heavy that it damages the brand, labour relations, and execution for years.

If you go into this as “closures or nothing,” you may win a theory exercise and lose the enterprise. I will not support that.

At the same time, I will also not support a deal that simply preserves the current structure and calls it compromise. If the package does not materially improve competitiveness and margins, then it is not acceptable.

## To the Works Council and union
I respect the clarity of your red lines, and I agree with you on one point: a value-destroying labour conflict would be harmful and unnecessary.

But I do **not** accept your position that every German plant must remain untouched and that no serious footprint adjustment can ever be discussed.

That is not a credible long-term answer to a structural competitiveness problem.

You are right that workers should not carry the full burden. You are right that management and ownership must contribute visibly. You are right that trust matters. But you are not entitled to treat historic guarantees as untouchable if the economics no longer support them.

If the company can achieve the required savings without closures and without compulsory redundancies, then I am open to that. But it must be **proven**, not merely asserted. If you want to rule out the hard instruments entirely, then your alternative must be genuinely equivalent in scale and durability.

So far, I do not see that from either labour side. I hear resistance, but not yet a fully bankable alternative.

## To the regional state government
I understand and respect your role as anchor shareholder and your concern for regional industrial employment. That concern is legitimate.

But I have to push back on your attempt to make “no closures in Germany” an absolute political premise. If it becomes an absolute, then governance is being used to freeze an uncompetitive structure in place.

That would not protect the company. It would only postpone the problem and likely make the eventual adjustment harsher.

I am willing to back a serious non-closure solution if it is real. I am not willing to treat closure as forbidden regardless of facts. The company needs room to correct capacity and cost. Without that, long-term value erodes.

## To investors
You are right to insist that the numbers must add up. You are also right that a weak or symbolic settlement would be a mistake.

But I would caution against reducing this to a simple “largest possible cut” logic. A package that is financially hard but operationally ungovernable can still destroy value. We need durability, not just headline severity.

So I agree with you on the need for:
- material savings,
- structural change,
- and measurable margin improvement.

Where I differ is that I will not support a route that secures those savings only by triggering prolonged industrial conflict or by damaging the company’s social legitimacy.

## My position
The family holding will support a restructuring only if it meets these conditions:

1. **It restores competitiveness and margins materially.**
2. **It is durable, not temporary theatre.**
3. **It avoids value-destructive instability and prolonged confrontation.**
4. **It preserves the long-term industrial strength of the company.**
5. **It shares the burden fairly, including visible contribution from management and ownership.**

That is our threshold.

## What I can support
- meaningful capacity reduction
- working-time and shift flexibility
- productivity improvements
- voluntary exit schemes
- internal redeployment
- management restraint
- investment commitments for German production and future products
- selective footprint changes if they are truly unavoidable

## What I cannot support
- a status quo deal dressed up as compromise
- one-sided labour sacrifice
- endless delay
- a closure-first ultimatum as the starting point
- any outcome that permanently weakens the enterprise or governance stability

## On the facilitator’s options

### 1) Sequenced restructuring with a closure freeze and quantified trigger mechanism
This is the least bad of the three options, but only if it is designed carefully.

I could support a **time-limited negotiation window** with:
- no closures during the window,
- no compulsory redundancies during the window,
- a jointly accepted independent audit of savings,
- and a clear, quantified benchmark for whether the package is actually sufficient.

But I am **not** willing to accept a mechanism that predetermines a closure outcome if the benchmark is missed. That would just bake the confrontation in from the start.

### 2) Site-by-site package with protected core sites and conditional flexibility elsewhere
This may be workable in principle if it allows the company to protect critical sites while addressing genuine overcapacity elsewhere. I am not opposed to differentiated treatment as such.

What I would not support is a vague two-tier setup that becomes a slow-motion closure process without clarity, fairness, or business justification.

### 3) Shared-sacrifice package with binding burden-sharing rules
This is the right principle, but only if it is real.

Shared sacrifice must include:
- management restraint,
- ownership responsibility,
- employee contribution,
- and credible structural change.

If it becomes a code word for permanent concessions from labour while everyone else preserves all options, then it is not shared and I would not back it.

## Bottom line
I am open to a serious settlement that avoids value-destructive instability, but I will not be pushed into accepting either extreme:
- not a closure-first confrontation,
- and not a no-change defence of the current structure.

If management can bring a package that is hard enough to matter but governable enough to hold, we will consider it. If labour can bring a non-closure alternative that is genuinely equivalent in savings and durability, I will listen.

But I will be clear: **I will not support an outcome that permanently impairs enterprise value or the owners’ governance control.**  
And I will not endorse a deal that merely delays the hard decisions while pretending to solve them.
