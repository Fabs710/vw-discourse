Thank you. I will respond directly and plainly.

## To the Works Council and the union
I understand your red lines. I also need to be equally clear: **if your position is “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no meaningful labour-cost reduction, and no reduction in capacity,” then you are not offering a restructuring — you are defending the status quo that created the crisis.**

That is not acceptable.

### Where I agree
- We all want a future for the German sites.
- We all agree that a cosmetic deal is useless.
- We all agree that labour peace matters.
- We all agree that the company needs investment in future products and technology.

### Where I disagree
#### 1) “No closures” cannot be an absolute if capacity is structurally too high
You keep saying closures are a political choice, not an economic necessity. I disagree.

If German operations have too much capacity for a shrinking European market, then some footprint must change. If you rule out closures in advance, then you are forcing the company to absorb overcapacity indefinitely. That is not industrial policy; that is denial.

I am not saying closure is the only tool. I am saying it cannot be ruled out as a matter of principle if the economics require structural capacity reduction.

#### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” is not compatible with a zero-impact restructuring
You want the company to become materially more competitive, but with no compulsory job reductions. That only works if the alternative measures are large enough to deliver the same economics. So far, I have not heard a package that does that.

Voluntary exits, working-time tweaks, and productivity improvements can help. But they do not automatically solve a large structural cost gap. If you want to exclude layoffs, you need to show how you will close the gap in a way that is actually durable and material.

#### 3) “Fair contribution” must be real, not symbolic
A limited concession from employees is not enough if it leaves the economics basically unchanged. The company needs a genuine labour-cost contribution. That means:
- lower labour costs,
- greater flexibility,
- and structural change in utilisation and footprint.

If your proposal is simply temporary reductions with full restoration later, then it does not solve the problem.

## To the regional state government
You are right that the company needs to remain an industrial anchor. I agree with that. But I cannot accept the implication that German plant closures are automatically illegitimate.

If the state blocks every meaningful footprint adjustment, then it is not protecting the company — it is protecting an uncompetitive structure. That may be politically comfortable, but it is economically irresponsible.

You said alternatives should be exhausted first. Fine. We are exhausting them. But that does **not** mean the outcome can be pre-decided as “no closures, ever.”

## To the family holding
You are right that prolonged conflict destroys value. That is exactly why I am pushing for a settlement that is hard enough to matter. A weak deal that avoids the structural issue will simply bring us back here again, with even less credibility and more pressure.

You also rightly note that one-sided sacrifice is unacceptable. I agree. But that has to apply to the workforce side too. If labour insists on preserving every guarantee while shifting all adjustment away from sites and jobs, that is not shared sacrifice.

## To investors
You are correct that the package must be economically credible and durable. That is precisely my point.

What I do **not** accept is a situation in which everyone says “the numbers must add up,” but only management is expected to confront the structural problem. The numbers do not add up if:
- capacity remains too high,
- labour costs remain too elevated,
- and the company is left with an unchanged footprint.

That is why I am insisting on a package that materially changes economics.

## What I am prepared to discuss
I am not pretending that closures are the only answer. I am saying the final solution must deliver equivalent structural savings. That means we can discuss:
- capacity reductions across the footprint,
- working-time and shift changes,
- productivity gains,
- voluntary exit programs,
- pay and benefit moderation,
- internal redeployment,
- and investment commitments tied to reform.

But I will be explicit: **if the package does not achieve a durable reduction in overcapacity and labour costs, it will not be enough.**

## What I cannot accept
- A deal that preserves every German plant unchanged
- A deal that rules out all compulsory redundancies without offering equivalent savings
- A deal that delivers only temporary, reversible concessions
- A deal that leaves the brand uncompetitive and the return-on-sales gap unresolved
- A deal that postpones the hard decisions into the next cycle

## Bottom line
I am willing to negotiate the **path** — not the necessity — of structural change.

If the workforce side can present a package that genuinely restores competitiveness, I will engage seriously. If the answer remains “no closures, no layoffs, no real cost reset,” then there is no basis for a viable settlement.

The company needs a deep and durable correction, not a comfort deal.
