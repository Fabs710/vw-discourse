I will respond directly, and I will be just as clear as the others.

## To the Works Council
We are aligned on the essentials:
- no plant closures in Germany,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- co-determination must remain intact,
- and the burden cannot fall on workers alone.

I support your refusal to let “closure” become the benchmark solution. Where I want to sharpen the point is this: we must not turn our justified resistance into a passive defense of the status quo. The company does need a real adjustment.

That means we need a **serious non-destructive package**:
- temporary working-time reductions,
- shift and utilization changes,
- voluntary exit programs only,
- internal redeployment,
- productivity improvements,
- and a fair employee contribution.

But I agree with you on the key boundary: any contribution must be **time-limited, reversible, and paired with binding guarantees**. If it becomes permanent erosion, we reject it.

## To management
Your answer still tries to keep the closure threat as the measure of seriousness. That is precisely what I cannot accept.

You keep saying the burden is on labour to prove an alternative is equivalent. No. The burden is on management to **present a credible non-destructive restructuring first**. Plant closures and forced layoffs are not the default proof of seriousness; they are an escalation.

I do agree with one part of your argument: cosmetic measures are not enough. But “material savings” does **not** automatically mean closures. It means the package has to be real. Real can also mean:
- reduced capacity through working-time and utilization,
- voluntary reductions,
- redeployment,
- productivity,
- management restraint,
- and ownership contribution.

What I will not support is a structure where labour gives concessions while closures remain the backstop. That is not a settlement. It is leverage.

## To the regional state government
You are right that the company needs to remain an industrial anchor. You are also right that the restructuring must be real, not symbolic.

But I need to be equally direct: if you truly reject closures and compulsory redundancies, then you must use your governance leverage **now**, not later. Do not hide behind “process” if the process is designed to soften us up for eventual site exits.

I support a structured audit and test phase only if it is used to verify whether a non-closure package works — **not** to prepare a later closure decision. If closures remain a live fallback, then the test is contaminated from the start.

## To the controlling family holding
You say you want durability and value preservation. Fine. Then support a settlement that preserves trust and industrial legitimacy.

Where I disagree with you is your continued openness to “site exits” as a standing option. That is exactly the kind of logic that destroys trust and makes long-term labour peace impossible.

If the family wants stability, it should support:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- visible management restraint,
- and real investment commitments for the German sites.

A family that wants long-term value should not treat plant exits as a routine governance instrument. That is not stewardship; it is short-term pressure.

## To investors
You are right about one thing: a weak compromise would be worse than no deal.

But I reject the idea that only closure-linked restructuring can be “bankable.” A deal is bankable if it is:
- measurable,
- durable,
- enforceable,
- and socially sustainable.

A settlement that creates prolonged conflict and destroys labour relations is not bankable, even if it looks tough on paper.

So yes, we can support a package that delivers real cost reduction. But it must do so **without** closures and **without** compulsory redundancies, and it must include management and ownership contribution as part of the burden-sharing.

## On the facilitator proposals

### Proposal 1: Time-limited non-closure test phase with hard protections
This is the only proposal that comes close to something workable.

I can support a genuine test phase if and only if:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no outsourcing used as a substitute,
- no hidden attrition strategy,
- no built-in closure fallback.

The test must be about proving that a non-destructive restructuring can work. If closure remains the punishment for not hitting a target, then the process is not neutral and I oppose it.

### Proposal 2: Conditional burden-sharing package with a tiered fallback
I reject this.

A tiered fallback is just a slower way of reintroducing pressure for closures after workers have already conceded. That is not shared sacrifice. That is a staged erosion of our position.

### Proposal 3: Site protection for core sites + conditional industrial commitments for the rest
I also reject this as currently framed.

It creates a two-tier map of the German footprint: some sites protected, others exposed. That is how closures become normalized. We are not going to help build that logic.

## What I am prepared to support
I want to be concrete, because this cannot remain abstract.

We can discuss:
- a time-limited working-time reduction,
- temporary moderation in wage components,
- voluntary exit schemes,
- internal transfers and redeployment,
- productivity improvements,
- utilization and shift flexibility,
- and a fair employee contribution.

But only if all of the following are guaranteed:
1. **No plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Protection of co-determination**
4. **Protection of the collective wage framework**
5. **Binding management and ownership contribution**
6. **Binding investment and product commitments for German sites**
7. **Independent verification of the savings**

That is a real package. Anything else is just shifting the burden onto workers.

## What I will not accept
I will not accept:
- closures as a “last resort” hidden in the deal,
- a second-stage process that reopens site exits,
- one-sided labour sacrifice,
- permanent insecurity,
- or a restructuring that weakens the collective framework.

If the company wants our cooperation, it must bring a plan that protects jobs and sites while delivering real savings. If it cannot do that, then it should be honest that it is asking labour to pay for management’s preferred instruments.

## Final word
We are not blocking change. We are blocking **destructive** change.

If management and ownership are serious about a durable future, they should return with:
- a non-closure restructuring,
- no forced layoffs,
- shared burden,
- and real investment.

If they do not, then the workforce will resist — and we have the means to do so.
