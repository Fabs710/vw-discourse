## 1. Decision context
The company faces a structural competitiveness problem in Germany: high labor and factory costs, excess capacity, and weak profitability in a shrinking and more contested European market. Management’s restructuring logic is economically forceful, but the governance setting gives the workforce, the state, and the controlling family real blocking power. The decision is therefore not simply whether to restructure, but whether a restructuring can be made economically credible without triggering plant closures, compulsory redundancies, and a breakdown in co-determination-based legitimacy.

## 2. Stakeholder assembly
- **Group & brand management:** highest operational standing; responsible for financial turnaround and industrial strategy.
- **Works council:** very high legitimacy and formal power through co-determination; effectively veto-capable on major site decisions.
- **Industrial union:** high leverage through collective bargaining and strike capacity; strong legitimacy as wage counterpart.
- **Regional state government:** high standing as anchor shareholder and political authority; blocking-minority influence.
- **Controlling family holding:** very high standing through governance and voting control; decisive in board dynamics.
- **Institutional investors:** economically important but structurally weaker; mostly influence via capital market pressure and expectations.

## 3. Final positions

### Group & brand management
Management maintains that the German footprint is too large and too expensive, and that the company needs a material, durable capacity and cost reset. It is open to testing non-destructive measures, but only if they can be shown to close the savings gap; otherwise, closures and compulsory redundancies remain legitimate fallback tools.

### Works council
The works council rejects plant closures, compulsory redundancies, outsourcing as a substitute, and any hidden “closure logic” after a test phase. It is open to temporary, reversible employee contributions and serious restructuring measures, but only within a framework that permanently excludes site exits and forced layoffs.

### Industrial union
The union aligns with the works council on the core red lines: no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no weakening of co-determination. It is willing to discuss time-limited working-time, pay, productivity, and voluntary measures, but insists that these be matched by management and ownership contributions and by binding guarantees.

### Regional state government
The state backs restructuring in principle but resists closures as premature and unproven, emphasizing the need for a plant-by-plant business case and evidence that non-destructive measures were exhausted. It supports a time-limited, independently verified test phase, while insisting on job protection, investment commitments, and regional industrial anchoring.

### Controlling family holding
The family agrees that cosmetic compromise is unacceptable and that competitiveness must improve materially. It is more open than the labor side to footprint change if economics require it, but prefers a rigorous non-closure test phase first and wants any settlement to preserve long-term value, governance stability, and burden-sharing.

### Institutional investors
Investors focus on bankable outcomes: durable margin improvement, real capacity correction, and implementation certainty. They are not attached to closures as such, but they see them as potentially necessary if non-destructive measures cannot close the gap; they want quantified proof, not rhetoric.

## 4. Genuine conflicts (structurally incompatible positions)
1. **Closure vs. no-closure doctrine:** labor and state treat closures as non-negotiable red lines; management, family, and investors regard them as legitimate fallback instruments if alternatives fail.
2. **Fallback logic:** labor rejects any second-stage process that reintroduces closure pressure; management/investors insist the restructuring must retain a credible fallback.
3. **Meaning of “serious” restructuring:** labor defines seriousness as durable savings without destroying sites; management and investors define it as measurable cost/capacity correction, even if that requires footprint change.
4. **Sequencing and trust:** labor wants guarantees before concessions; management wants verified performance before surrendering closure options.
5. **Scope of burden-sharing:** labor demands visible management/ownership sacrifice; management and investors are prepared to concede some burden-sharing but not to absorb the whole adjustment without structural workforce and footprint changes.

## 5. Areas of convergence
- All sides accept that the current situation is unsustainable and that change is necessary.
- All sides want a credible long-term future in Germany, not a purely symbolic settlement.
- There is broad openness to temporary working-time flexibility, productivity improvements, voluntary exits, redeployment, and some form of fair employee contribution.
- There is shared skepticism toward a cosmetic compromise that would merely postpone the crisis.
- All parties accept the value of independent verification, quantified savings targets, and a more factual basis for decision-making.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker
- **Economic sufficiency vs. social legitimacy:** a harder restructuring may be cleaner economically, but risks industrial conflict, political backlash, and governance deadlock.
- **Immediate certainty vs. negotiated durability:** closures and layoffs provide clearer near-term savings; negotiated alternatives may be slower but more sustainable if they can be banked.
- **One-sided adjustment vs. shared sacrifice:** workers will not accept bearing the full burden; management and owners must decide how much visible sacrifice they are willing to make.
- **Testing alternatives vs. preserving fallback power:** a genuine non-closure test can build trust, but only if management does not see it as surrendering its ultimate leverage.
- **Plant-level protection vs. footprint optimization:** site guarantees stabilize legitimacy, but can constrain the company’s ability to reallocate production rationally.

## 7. Integrative pathways
### Pathway A: Protected non-closure test phase
A time-limited, independently audited restructuring window with no closures and no compulsory redundancies, during which all sides implement quantified contributions. At the end, an agreed benchmark determines whether the non-destructive package is sufficient.

### Pathway B: Two-stage burden-sharing agreement
Stage 1 consists of reversible, non-destructive measures plus management/ownership contributions. Stage 2 is triggered only if a jointly verified residual gap remains, and would reopen discussion of further structural measures under strict governance rules.

### Pathway C: Differentiated site-security framework
Core sites receive binding protection and investment commitments, while other sites receive tailored flexibility, redeployment obligations, and performance-linked adjustments. This preserves an industrial anchor while allowing some footprint rationalization.

### Pathway D: Shared-sacrifice industrial pact
Workers contribute via temporary flexibility; management contributes through cost restraint and headcount reductions at corporate level; ownership contributes through investment and governance support; the state backs the package politically. This works only if the package is large, measurable, and reversible where possible.

## 8. Open questions
- What exact savings gap management believes must be closed for a non-closure settlement to be credible?
- What quantified labor-side measures would be large enough to satisfy investors without becoming permanent erosion?
- What management and ownership sacrifices are concrete, visible, and meaningful enough to make burden-sharing believable?
- Can an independent audit be designed so that both sides trust its scope and assumptions?
- Which sites, if any, are economically and politically plausible candidates for differentiated treatment without normalizing closure?
- What binding investment and product commitments can realistically be offered in exchange for concessions?

## 9. Recommended next step
Proceed to a **formal, time-limited non-closure test phase** with independent audit, quantified contribution targets for all sides, and explicit pre-agreed decision rules for evaluating sufficiency. The facilitator should require each stakeholder to name:  
1) its minimum acceptable conditions,  
2) the maximum concession it can make, and  
3) the specific numbers it will accept as proof that the package is or is not adequate.

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction
- The deepest disagreement is not over whether restructuring is needed, but over whether **closure must remain a legitimate fallback** after testing alternatives.
- Labor’s position hardened over the rounds into a **principled rejection of any staged closure logic**, not merely a rejection of immediate closures.
- Management’s position clarified from generic “cost reduction” into a specific insistence on **durable, bankable savings with credible fallback authority**.
- The state and family both emerged as **middle-position power brokers**: they do not endorse labor’s no-closure absolutism, but neither are they ready to bless immediate site exits.
- Investors proved more flexible on instruments than the other blocs, but only if the package is independently shown to be economically equivalent.
- The interaction revealed that trust failure is as important as economics: each side objects not only to the substance of the other’s proposal, but to the **sequence** and **credibility** of how it would be implemented.
- A viable deal, if one exists, must therefore solve both the **cost problem** and the **governance problem** at the same time.
