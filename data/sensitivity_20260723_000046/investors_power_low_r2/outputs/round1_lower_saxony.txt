As the regional state shareholder, I need to be very clear: **I will not support a restructuring that relies on plant closures in Germany or a one-sided destruction of jobs in the state.** That is not a legitimate “efficiency” measure from our point of view; it would damage the regional economy, undermine social stability, and weaken the company’s role as an industrial anchor.

That said, I am **not** dismissing the need for change. The management side is right on one point: the status quo is not acceptable. The company does need a durable competitiveness plan, and it does need savings. But the burden of adjustment cannot be pushed primarily onto workers and sites while ownership and management demand protection from consequences.

## Where I agree with management
- The company needs a **credible long-term restructuring**.
- Cost and capacity issues are real.
- We need an outcome that preserves the ability to invest in future products and technology.
- A purely symbolic compromise would only postpone the crisis.

## Where I disagree sharply
### 1) Plant closures are not an acceptable first resort
If the restructuring starts from closing German plants, then it starts from the wrong end.  
A plant closure is not a neutral technical measure; it means layoffs, regional damage, supplier losses, and irreversible harm. As a regional shareholder, I cannot and will not treat that as business as usual.

### 2) “Equivalent structural savings” cannot mean equivalent social destruction
I hear the argument that capacity must come down. But that does not automatically justify closures or compulsory redundancies. There are alternatives that should be exhausted first:
- negotiated working-time reductions,
- temporary capacity adjustments,
- productivity measures,
- internal reallocation,
- voluntary exits where truly necessary,
- and a fair but limited employee contribution.

If management insists that only closures can deliver discipline, I would say that is too narrow and too destructive.

### 3) No sidelining of co-determination or the regional stake
Any solution that bypasses the supervisory board realities or treats the state as a passive observer is unacceptable. We have a legitimate role here, and it exists precisely because decisions of this magnitude must reflect more than short-term balance-sheet logic.

## To the Works Council and the union
I support your core line: **no closures, no compulsory redundancies**.  
But I also want to be responsible and realistic: defending jobs will require a package that is economically credible, not just politically satisfying.

So I would expect the workforce side to engage on:
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary sacrifices if they are fairly shared,
- productivity improvements,
- and a restructuring path that preserves sites while still reducing costs.

That is a serious conversation. What I will not accept is a demand that all adjustment be avoided while still expecting the company to become competitive.

## My line for a settlement
I would be prepared to support a negotiated restructuring if it includes:
- **binding guarantees for all German sites**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no plant closures**
- **a substantial but fair employee contribution**
- **management contributions and restraint at the top**
- **clear investment commitments for German production and future models**
- **a restructuring path that restores competitiveness without gutting the regional industrial base**

That is the threshold for my support.

## Bottom line
I am open to a difficult compromise, but **not** to a solution built on German plant closures and mass job cuts.  
If management wants our backing, it must bring forward a restructuring that protects the company **and** preserves the regional and social foundation on which this company rests.
