From the perspective of dispersed institutional investors, the key question is not which side “wins” the negotiation, but whether the outcome restores **risk-adjusted equity value** through a **credible, durable improvement in margins**.

### Where we agree
We agree with management, the family holding, and even parts of the public side on one essential point: **the current cost structure is not acceptable**.  
If German operations remain structurally uncompetitive, the market will continue to discount the company, and investors will keep bearing the cost through weak earnings and lower valuation multiples.

We also agree that a purely cosmetic settlement would be value-destructive.  
A deal that preserves the status quo, avoids hard decisions, and merely postpones the restructuring problem would not be supportable.

### Where we disagree
We do **not** support a restructuring that is defined only by confrontation, plant closure threats, or a conflict that drags on and destroys value in the meantime.

- **Prolonged strike risk is not acceptable.**
- **A settlement that is economically weak is not acceptable.**
- **A plan that leaves the cost base uncompetitive is not acceptable.**

So while we understand management’s argument that the economics must change, we are not persuaded by rhetoric alone. If “deep restructuring” means a long industrial war that damages output, cash flow, and brand strength, that is not disciplined value creation — it is value destruction.

### What would move us
We will support whichever path **credibly and durably** restores competitiveness. That means:

- material, not token, structural savings
- a clear path to improved margins and return on sales
- durable labour-cost reduction
- capacity rationalisation that actually changes the economics
- deal certainty, not open-ended conflict
- protection of investment capacity for future products and technology

We are open to alternatives to closures **if** they deliver equivalent economics. But “equivalent” has to be real, not symbolic. A package of working-time tweaks and temporary concessions that leaves the underlying cost base intact will not be enough.

### Our threshold
If the final package does **not** produce a credible return to competitive margins, we will view it as inadequate.  
If it does, we do not care whether the savings come through closures, capacity cuts, labour concessions, or a combination — but the numbers must add up.

### To management
Bring forward a restructuring that is **hard enough to matter** and **stable enough to be bankable**. We need confidence that the savings will stick and that the company will not be back here again in 12–24 months.

### To labour and the state
We respect the desire to avoid closures and forced layoffs, but that preference is not sufficient on its own. If those outcomes are to be avoided, the substitute must deliver real competitiveness. Otherwise you are just shifting the pain into lower margins, weaker investment, and ultimately a weaker company.

### Bottom line
We are not aligned with any faction by default.  
We will back the outcome that offers the strongest **credible, durable, and financially material** path back to competitiveness. If no such path emerges, we will not support a settlement that merely preserves comfort while impairing shareholder value.
