I will respond directly.

## To the Works Council and the union
You continue to state a position that amounts to: **no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no weakening of guarantees, and no meaningful structural change**.  
I have to say plainly: that is **not** a viable restructuring proposal.

I agree with you on a few points:
- The company needs a future in Germany.
- A cosmetic deal would fail.
- Labour peace matters.
- Investment commitments are necessary.

But I strongly disagree with your core premise that the company can restore competitiveness while treating plant closures and compulsory redundancies as categorically off-limits.

### Why I reject your position
1. **Capacity is structurally too high.**  
   In a shrinking European market with severe price pressure, we cannot preserve the current footprint and still expect the economics to recover.

2. **Voluntary-only tools are insufficient on their own.**  
   Working-time changes, redeployment, and voluntary exits can contribute, but they do not close a large cost gap by themselves.

3. **“Fair contribution” must be material.**  
   A limited, temporary concession that is later reversed does not solve the problem. We need a durable reset in labour cost and utilisation.

4. **Protection of every plant cannot be the starting assumption.**  
   If you rule out footprint change in advance, you are not negotiating restructuring; you are negotiating delay.

I am prepared to discuss non-closure alternatives, but only if they deliver **equivalent, durable savings**. So far, I have not heard a package from labour that proves that.

## To the regional state government
You are right that the company should remain an industrial anchor. I agree with that.  
But I must push back on your attempt to turn “no closures” into the default political line.

If the state’s position is that closures are unacceptable regardless of the economics, then it is effectively asking the company to carry an uncompetitive structure indefinitely. That is not responsible stewardship.

I am not saying closures are the first choice. I am saying they must remain available if the economics require them.  
Blocking any meaningful footprint adjustment would be a mistake.

## To the controlling family holding
I agree with you more than with the labour side on one central point: **the deal must be economically real and durable**.  
A weak compromise would simply postpone the crisis and destroy credibility.

Where I would refine your position is this: we do not need to lock ourselves into a closure-first narrative as the only serious option. But we also cannot accept a settlement that leaves overcapacity and labour costs largely unchanged.

That means:
- real savings,
- real capacity correction,
- and a labour contribution that changes the economics.

## To investors
You are right to insist on durable, bankable savings.  
That is exactly why I am unwilling to accept a deal that merely preserves the current structure under a different name.

Your concern about prolonged conflict is valid, but so is the risk of a deal that looks balanced politically and fails financially. We need an outcome that materially improves margins and restores return on sales, not just one that sounds acceptable in public.

## Where I stand
I remain firm on the following:

### Non-negotiable for management
- a **material and durable** reduction in overcapacity
- a **genuine labour-cost contribution**
- a restructuring that improves competitiveness on a path to 2030
- no cosmetic settlement

### Open on mechanism
I remain open on **how** the savings are achieved:
- closures,
- selective footprint changes,
- working-time adjustments,
- voluntary exits,
- productivity measures,
- internal redeployment,
- investment commitments.

But I will not commit to preserving every site at any cost, and I will not accept a package that leaves the core cost base uncompetitive.

## On the facilitator’s options
### 1) Closure freeze with audited trigger mechanism
This only works if it does **not** become a permanent veto on structural change.  
I can accept a structured negotiation window, but not a device that makes closure impossible regardless of the outcome.

### 2) Site-by-site package with protected core sites and conditional flexibility elsewhere
This is closer to reality than an absolute no-closure stance, because not every site can be treated identically.  
If some sites are structurally weaker, we need a mechanism to address that. I am open to site-specific solutions, but not to a blanket guarantee that freezes the footprint.

### 3) Shared-sacrifice package with binding burden-sharing rules
Yes — if it is truly shared and truly material.  
That means employees contribute, but management and ownership also show restraint.  
What it does **not** mean is that labour receives permanent security with only symbolic concessions in return.

## My answer to the key question
Can the company achieve sufficiently large structural savings without closures and compulsory layoffs?

**Maybe — but only if the alternative package is genuinely equivalent in scale, durability, and certainty.**  
So far, that has not been demonstrated.

Until it is, closures and footprint changes remain legitimate tools, and I will not remove them from the table.

## Bottom line
I am willing to negotiate the path, but not the necessity, of structural change.

If labour wants to avoid closures and compulsory redundancies, then bring a package that truly closes the cost gap — not a political refusal dressed up as industrial policy. If you cannot, then we will have to make the hard decisions required to secure the company’s future.

If needed, I can next set out a concrete management-side threshold package: minimum savings, capacity reduction range, and the labour-cost contribution we would regard as credible.
