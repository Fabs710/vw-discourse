## To the Works Council
You are still asking me to accept a package that says:
- every German plant stays open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no weakening of guarantees,
- no meaningful structural change,
- and then somehow the company becomes competitive.

That is not a restructuring plan. It is a **refusal to confront the economics**.

I agree with you on two points:
1. The company needs a future in Germany.
2. A cosmetic deal would be a mistake.

But I fundamentally disagree with your conclusion that closures and compulsory redundancies must be ruled out in advance. If we do that, we remove the only instruments that can **guarantee** a durable capacity correction at the scale required.

You keep saying non-destructive measures should be tested “seriously.” I am prepared to test them. But testing is not a substitute for outcomes. If the test phase cannot close the gap, then we need the flexibility to act. Otherwise, we are just buying time while the cost base stays broken.

## To the trade union
I appreciate that you are willing to discuss temporary working-time and wage measures. That is the right starting point.

But your position still boils down to:
- temporary concessions,
- no structural loss,
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and expect competitiveness to improve anyway.

That is too weak.

If the company is to fund future products and technology to 2030, we need **durable** savings, not reversible gestures. I can accept time-limited contributions only if they are part of a package that materially resets the cost base and capacity. Otherwise, they are just a pause before the next crisis.

## To the regional state government
You are acting as if “no closures in Germany” is a legitimate governance principle in itself. It is not. It is a political preference that may well collide with the economic reality.

I agree that the company must remain an industrial anchor. But an industrial anchor that is structurally uncompetitive will eventually lose jobs anyway, only more slowly and more painfully.

So I ask you bluntly: are you prepared to support a **real** restructuring, or only one that avoids visible pain? Because if you block all footprint correction, you are not protecting the region in the long run; you are preserving the problem.

## To the controlling family holding
You are right on one crucial point: a weak compromise would simply postpone the crisis and destroy credibility. I agree with that.

Where I push back is your reluctance to back the hard tools. If you agree the package must be economically real, then you must be prepared to support measures that actually produce structural savings. That may include closures, and it may include compulsory redundancies if no alternative delivers the scale needed.

You cannot insist on a durable economic reset while simultaneously narrowing the instruments to those that are least disruptive politically. Those two positions conflict.

## To investors
You are right to demand bankable, durable savings and credible margin improvement. That is exactly what management is trying to deliver.

But I would also caution against assuming that the only credible restructuring is the harshest one on paper. What matters is whether the end state is competitive and executable. That said, if labour offers only temporary and reversible concessions, then you should not confuse that with structural change.

## What I stand by
My position remains unchanged:

- We need a **material and durable reduction in overcapacity**
- We need a **genuine labour-cost contribution**
- We need a restructuring that **improves competitiveness on the path to 2030**
- We cannot accept a cosmetic settlement
- We cannot accept a deal that leaves the core cost base uncompetitive

## What I am open to
I remain open on the mechanism:
- closures
- selective footprint changes
- working-time changes
- voluntary exits
- productivity measures
- internal redeployment
- investment commitments

But openness on mechanism is **not** openness to preserving every plant or ruling out compulsory redundancies regardless of the outcome.

## On the facilitator’s proposals

### Proposal 1: Time-limited non-closure test phase with hard protections
I can support a **time-limited negotiation window** and an **independent audit**. That is sensible.

But I cannot accept a version that makes closures impossible even if the verified savings gap remains. That would turn the test into a permanent veto.

So my condition is:
- no closures during the test phase,
- no compulsory redundancies during the test phase,
- serious quantified measures must be implemented,
- and if the result is insufficient, we return to the table with all structurally necessary options available.

That is not a threat. That is reality.

### Proposal 2: Conditional burden-sharing package with a tiered fallback
This can work only if the fallback is real and the first stage is substantial.

If the first stage is merely symbolic, then the tiered approach becomes delay. If the first stage is strong, then a second stage of deeper structural measures may not be needed. But I will not agree to a process that rules out difficult decisions forever.

### Proposal 3: Site protection for core sites + conditional industrial commitments for the rest
This is closer to the truth than an absolute no-closure stance. Not every site has identical economics.

I am open to differentiated treatment **if it reflects business reality**. What I cannot accept is an abstract promise that every site is equally protected regardless of competitiveness. That is not governance; that is denial.

You all keep repeating:
