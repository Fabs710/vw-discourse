We share the same diagnosis: the current situation is not acceptable, and a cosmetic settlement would destroy value.

But I need to be equally clear: **I am not attached to closures as such, and I am not attached to labour’s red lines as such. I am attached to one thing: a credible, durable return to competitive margins.** If the package does not get the company back to a structurally competitive cost base, then it fails from our perspective — even if it is politically elegant.

## To management
We agree that the status quo is not viable, and we agree that savings must be material and durable.

Where I push back is on the way you frame the choice: **“closures and compulsory redundancies, or an insufficient outcome.”** That may be true, but you have not yet shown it convincingly enough to justify the level of conflict you are inviting.

What I need from you is not rhetoric about structural necessity. I need:
- a **quantified savings target**,
- a **clear path to return-on-sales improvement**,
- and a **credible explanation of why non-closure measures are insufficient**.

If you want capital markets to support a hard restructuring, then the package must be bankable:
- durable labour-cost reduction,
- real capacity correction,
- no open-ended uncertainty,
- and a believable implementation path.

A deal that triggers a prolonged strike cycle is not “hard” in a value-creating sense. It is value-destructive unless the economic gains clearly outweigh the disruption. So far, that balance has not been demonstrated to my satisfaction.

## To the Works Council and the union
I understand why you are resisting closures, compulsory redundancies, and erosion of guarantees. I also agree that the burden should not fall only on workers.

But your current position — **no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no weakening of the framework, and no two-tier footprint logic** — is not enough on its own. From an investor perspective, that can easily become a recipe for preserving overcapacity while postponing the pain.

That is unacceptable to us.

If you want to avoid closures and layoffs, then the substitute has to be **material**, not symbolic. That means:
- real working-time changes,
- real productivity improvements,
- real capacity/utilisation reductions,
- voluntary exits at meaningful scale,
- and a labour-cost contribution that actually moves the economics.

I am open to non-closure solutions, but only if they are genuinely equivalent in scale and durability. If they are not, then “no closures” just means a slower erosion of value.

## To the regional state government
You are right to insist that this company remains an industrial anchor, and you are right that a value-destroying conflict would hurt everyone.

But I do not accept the idea that closures are illegitimate in principle if they are necessary to restore competitiveness. A blocking stance that prevents any meaningful footprint adjustment can just freeze the problem in place.

What I would want from you is not a blanket “no,” but a **credible governance framework** for evaluating alternatives:
- non-closure measures tested seriously,
- quantified savings audited independently,
- and only if the economics still do not add up, then a harder decision set.

That sequencing matters. I am willing to support a non-closure-first process. I am not willing to support a non-closure-forever position regardless of economics.

## To the controlling family holding
You are right about one thing: a weak compromise that preserves the current structure would simply defer the problem. That would be a mistake.

You are also right that prolonged conflict destroys value. We agree on that.

Where I differ is that I do not want the family to frame the discussion as if the only serious alternative to a weak deal is closure-first confrontation. There should be a serious middle ground, but only if it is **measurable, durable, and enforced**.

What matters to us is not whether the package is harsh on paper. It is whether it restores margins without creating a lingering drag from conflict or underperformance. That means:
- real savings,
- real implementation certainty,
- and a deal that the market can believe.

## On the facilitator’s options
### 1) Sequenced restructuring with a closure freeze and quantified trigger mechanism
This is the most promising framework, **if** it is designed to produce a real test rather than a disguised postponement.

I would support:
- a **time-limited closure freeze**,
- a **ban on compulsory redundancies during the window**,
- an **independent audit** of savings,
- and a **predefined review point**.

But the key question is whether the measures under the freeze are strong enough to have a chance of closing the gap. If the labor-side package is too weak, then the process just becomes delay. If it is strong enough, then it may prove that closures are not necessary. That is exactly the kind of evidence I want before the market is asked to accept continued exposure.

### 2) Site-by-site package with protected core sites and conditional flexibility elsewhere
This is more attractive than an absolute all-or-nothing stance, because not every site necessarily has the same economics. But it must be handled carefully.

From a capital-markets perspective, the value test is simple: does the package improve the company’s medium-term cost structure enough to restore competitiveness? If yes, the exact footprint mix matters less. If no, then “protected core sites” becomes just another way to preserve underperformance.

So I would not reject this option outright, but I need it to be driven by economics, not politics.

### 3) Shared-sacrifice package with binding burden-sharing rules
This is the only version of “shared sacrifice” we would support: **one that actually changes the economics**.

That means:
- employees contribute in a meaningful but bounded way,
- management visibly contributes too,
- ownership/governance supports the transition,
- and the outcome is verifiable.

If “shared sacrifice” ends up meaning workers absorb permanent insecurity while others make symbolic gestures, then that is not a settlement we can support.

## Our threshold
We will back **any** outcome that credibly and durably restores competitiveness.

That means:
- competitive margins,
- durable cost reduction,
- no cosmetic compromise,
- and enough deal certainty that the market can believe the savings.

We will **not** back:
- a settlement that leaves structural overcapacity in place,
- a deal that preserves all legacy guarantees but barely improves economics,
- or a compromise that trades short-term peace for long-term underperformance.

## Bottom line
I am not here to defend plant closures for their own sake. I am here to defend shareholder value.

If the parties can produce a package that:
1. materially reduces costs,
2. is durable,
3. is credible to the market,
4. and avoids a prolonged value-destructive conflict,

then we can support it — whether or not it includes closures.

But if the final answer is a politically comfortable deal that leaves the cost base uncompetitive, then I will say clearly: **that is not good enough**.
