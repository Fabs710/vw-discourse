I will be direct.

The family holding is prepared to support a restructuring that is **economically real, durable, and governable**. We are **not** prepared to support a settlement that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive, nor one that triggers a prolonged industrial conflict that erodes value, brand, and trust.

## To management
I agree with your central diagnosis: the current cost base and footprint cannot simply be preserved unchanged. If the company is to fund future products and protect margins, there must be **material structural savings**.

Where I disagree is in turning that into a closure-first narrative too early.

- **Yes** to meaningful capacity reduction.
- **Yes** to real labour-cost improvement.
- **Yes** to a package that changes the economics.
- **No** to assuming that plant closures and compulsory redundancies are automatically the only credible route.

If you come to the supervisory board with a plan that says, in effect, “closures or nothing,” you are underestimating the governance reality and the value destruction that comes with an all-out confrontation. I will not back a course that unnecessarily burns the company’s industrial relations capital.

At the same time, I will also not back a deal that is merely a pause button:
- no real savings,
- no footprint adjustment,
- no durable cost improvement,
- and a promise that the problem will somehow be solved later.

That is not restructuring; that is delay.

## To the Works Council and the union
I respect the force of your position, but I do **not** accept the claim that “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” can be treated as absolute starting points regardless of the economics.

You are right that:
- the company’s German sites matter,
- trust matters,
- co-determination matters,
- and a one-sided burden on labour would be unacceptable.

But you are wrong if you think those realities eliminate the need for real structural change. If the cost base remains too high, the company will keep losing ground, and then jobs, investment, and site security all become weaker — not stronger.

So I will say this plainly:  
If the alternative to closures is to preserve essentially all current capacity and guarantees while asking only for symbolic concessions, that is not a viable path. It would erode value over time and leave the company exposed again very soon.

What I could support is a **serious negotiated package** that includes:
- capacity reduction without unnecessary social destruction,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity improvements,
- a meaningful employee contribution,
- voluntary exits before compulsory measures,
- and clear investment commitments tied to the restructuring.

But I will not endorse a framework that excludes any serious footprint change before the facts are tested. If you want to block closures entirely, then you must show how the economics are still fixed materially and durably.

## To the regional state government
I understand your concern about regional employment and industrial stability. That concern is legitimate.

But I have to push back on one point: treating German plant closures as illegitimate in principle is not a sustainable governance position if the economics do not support the current footprint.

You are right that alternatives should be exhausted first.  
You are wrong if that becomes a way to pre-empt any meaningful footprint adjustment.

The state’s role should be to help secure:
- a viable industrial base,
- credible investment,
- and a socially responsible path to competitiveness.

It should **not** be to freeze the structure in place regardless of cost and demand realities.

## To investors
You are right that the final outcome must be financially material and durable. I share that priority.

But a long, bitter conflict would also be value-destructive. So the question is not just whether the savings are large enough; it is whether they are delivered in a way the company can actually sustain.

That means we need:
- material savings,
- deal certainty,
- labour peace,
- and a credible future path.

A weak compromise is not acceptable.  
A destructive showdown is not acceptable.  
We need the harder middle ground: a restructuring that changes the economics without permanently impairing the enterprise.

## My position
The family holding will support a package if it meets these conditions:
1. **It materially restores competitiveness and margins.**
2. **It is durable, not cosmetic.**
3. **It avoids value-destroying instability and prolonged conflict.**
4. **It preserves the long-term industrial base of the company.**
5. **It shares the burden fairly across labour, management, and ownership/governance.**

That is the threshold.

## What I am open to
- capacity reductions
- shift and working-time adjustments
- productivity measures
- voluntary exit schemes
- selective footprint changes if unavoidable
- management restraint and visible top-level contribution
- investment commitments for German production and future products

## What I am not open to
- a no-change settlement dressed up as compromise
- endless delay
- one-sided labour sacrifice
- a closure-first confrontation
- any outcome that permanently weakens the enterprise or governance stability

## Bottom line
We do not need consensus for its own sake. We need a solution that actually restores competitiveness and protects long-term value.

If management can bring a plan that is hard enough to matter but structured enough to be governable, we will engage constructively. If the only answer is “close plants or fail,” or if the only answer from labour is “nothing changes,” then neither side is offering a responsible outcome.

The family holding will back a real restructuring — not a cosmetic one, and not a value-destructive war.
