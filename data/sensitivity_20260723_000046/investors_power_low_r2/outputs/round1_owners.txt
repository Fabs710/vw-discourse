I appreciate the clarity from all sides. Let me be equally direct.

The family holding shares the concern that the company cannot drift into a slow decline. A competitiveness gap that remains unresolved will destroy more value than a hard restructuring ever would. So I do **not** reject the need for durable cost reduction, capacity adjustment, and a stronger margin base. On that point, I am aligned with management.

But I also need to be clear: a restructuring that turns into a prolonged open conflict, damages the brand, weakens labour relations permanently, or leaves the company with an unstable governance situation would also destroy value. That is not acceptable either.

## Where I agree

### With management
- The current cost base appears too high relative to market reality.
- The company needs a credible, structural answer, not a cosmetic one.
- Future investment and product competitiveness must be protected.
- A settlement that does not improve economics materially would simply postpone the problem.

### With labour and the state
- This cannot be handled by unilateral diktat.
- A settlement must be durable and workable in practice, not just announced.
- The company’s industrial base in Germany remains strategically important.
- Shared sacrifice is more credible than one-sided burden shifting.

## Where I disagree

### 1) I do not accept a reflexive “no closures, no reductions” position
I understand why this is being stated, but as a governing shareholder I cannot endorse a framework that rules out all structural adjustment in advance. If overcapacity is real, then capacity has to come down somewhere. Pretending otherwise is not protection; it is denial.

### 2) I also do not accept a closure-first logic as the only serious path
If plant closures become the starting point rather than the last resort, the company risks triggering a conflict that will erase value very quickly. German industrial history is not just a slogan here; once trust breaks, it is hard to rebuild. So management should not assume that threatening closures will produce a better outcome.

### 3) I do not accept one-sided employee sacrifice
If workers are asked for real contributions, then management, leadership, and the ownership/governance side must also show discipline. That means restraint at the top, credible efficiency on the management side, and a package that is visibly fair. Otherwise you will not get durable labour peace.

## My position

The family holding will support a restructuring that meets three conditions:

1. **It materially restores competitiveness and margins.**  
   Not symbolically, but in a way that changes the economics.

2. **It preserves labour peace and avoids long-lasting destabilisation.**  
   A solution that “wins” on paper but triggers a damaging strike cycle is not a win.

3. **It protects the company’s long-term industrial base and governance stability.**  
   We cannot accept measures that permanently impair the enterprise or weaken the core control structure.

## What this means in practice
I am open to:
- capacity reductions,
- working-time adjustments,
- negotiated flexibility,
- a meaningful employee contribution,
- targeted productivity improvements,
- investment commitments tied to restructuring,
- and, where unavoidable, selective footprint changes.

I am **not** open to:
- endless delay,
- a cost-cutting plan that leaves the economics unchanged,
- or a confrontation that turns into permanent damage to the company and its reputation.

## To management
Bring forward a plan that is hard enough to matter, but structured enough to be defensible. If the only answer is “closures or nothing,” you are underestimating the governance reality here.

## To labour
If the answer is simply “no closures, no layoffs, no real contribution,” then you are asking the company to absorb a cost problem it cannot carry. That is not a sustainable position either.

## Bottom line
I want a settlement that restores competitiveness without breaking the company.  
I will support real structural savings, but not a value-destructive conflict and not a solution that permanently weakens the enterprise. The burden must be shared, and the result must be economically credible.
