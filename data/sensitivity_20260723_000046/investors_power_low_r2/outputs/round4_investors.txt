We agree on the diagnosis: the company needs a real restructuring, not a cosmetic truce. Where I differ from several of you is on the instrument.

From a capital-markets perspective, **our non-negotiable is a credible, durable return to competitive margins**. That means material cost reduction, real capacity correction, and implementation certainty. If the final package does not get the company back to a structurally competitive cost base, it fails — regardless of how politically elegant it is.

## To management
I agree that the current cost base is too high and that voluntary-only measures may not be enough. I also agree that a weak compromise would destroy value.

But you still have **not convincingly shown** why closures or compulsory redundancies must be the default fallback rather than the last resort. Repeating “overcapacity” is not the same as demonstrating that non-closure measures cannot close the gap.

What I need from you is:
- a **quantified savings target**
- a **plant-by-plant business case**
- a clear explanation of **which non-destructive measures have been tested**
- and why those measures are insufficient

If you can show that a non-closure package truly fails to deliver durable margin recovery, then I will not cling to closures as a matter of principle. We are not attached to instruments. We are attached to outcomes.

## To the Works Council and the union
I respect your insistence that workers should not bear the whole burden. I also agree that a value-destroying strike spiral would be harmful.

But your position is still too absolute:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no weakening of guarantees,
- and no two-tier footprint logic.

That may be understandable politically, but it is **not enough economically** unless you can produce a package that is genuinely equivalent in scale and durability. So far, I have not seen that. If you want to avoid closures, then the substitute must be real:
- meaningful working-time changes,
- real productivity gains,
- real capacity/utilisation reductions,
- voluntary exits at sufficient scale,
- and a labour-cost contribution that actually moves the economics.

Temporary concessions are fine only if they are large enough to matter and credible enough to bank. Otherwise they are just delay.

## To the regional state government
I understand your responsibility as an anchor shareholder and your concern for jobs in the region. That is legitimate.

But I do not support turning “no closures” into an absolute principle irrespective of economics. If that becomes the rule, then the company risks preserving an uncompetitive structure and eroding value more slowly, not solving it.

What I can support is:
- a **time-limited non-closure test phase**
- hard protections during that phase
- and an **independent audit** of whether the savings gap is actually closed

What I cannot support is a permanent political veto on footprint correction if the numbers still fail.

## To the controlling family holding
You are right that a cosmetic deal would be worse than no deal. You are also right that prolonged conflict destroys value.

But I would push back on your continued openness to “site exits” as a standing governance tool. From a market-value perspective, the risk is not just the immediate cost of restructuring — it is also the execution drag, trust damage, and brand damage from open-ended confrontation.

So yes, I agree on the need for a real solution. But I will only support one that is:
- economically real,
- durable,
- governable,
- and not so conflict-heavy that it destroys the value it is meant to create.

## To the facilitator’s proposals

### 1) Time-limited non-closure test phase with hard protections
This is the most promising path **if** it is rigorous.

I would support:
- no plant closures during the test period,
- no compulsory redundancies during the test period,
- no outsourcing used as a substitute,
- a quantified labour-side package,
- and an independent audit at the end

But the test must be designed to answer the real question: can a non-closure package close the cost gap sufficiently and durably?

If yes, that is valuable evidence. If no, then the company has a stronger basis for harder decisions. That sequencing is sensible.

### 2) Conditional burden-sharing package with a tiered fallback
This could work only if the first stage is substantial and the fallback is not a disguised pre-commitment to the same conflict. I do **not** want a staged process that simply postpones the closure question while concessions are extracted up front.

### 3) Site protection for core sites plus flexibility elsewhere
This is not my preferred answer, but I would not reject it outright if the economics justify it. The key question is whether the package produces **credible and durable savings**. If it does, the exact footprint mix matters less. If it does not, then “protected core sites” is just another way to preserve underperformance.

## My bottom line
I am **not attached to closures as such**. I am attached to a **credible return to competitive margins**.

That means:
- material savings,
- durable cost reduction,
- implementation certainty,
- and no cosmetic settlement.

I will support **any** package that can be independently shown to restore competitiveness without leaving structural overcapacity and uncompetitive labour costs in place.

But I will **not** support:
- a deal that leaves the cost base broken,
- a settlement that preserves all legacy guarantees while barely changing the economics,
- or an outcome that trades short-term peace for long-term underperformance.

If management wants investors behind a non-closure solution, bring the numbers. If labour wants to avoid closures, bring a package that is genuinely equivalent in scale and durability. Until then, we remain cautious and will judge this by financial results, not by rhetoric.
