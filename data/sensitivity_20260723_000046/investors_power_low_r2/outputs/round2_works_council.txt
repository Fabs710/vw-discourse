Let me respond clearly to each side.

## To management
Your argument is familiar: “cost base too high, capacity too large, therefore closures and job cuts.”  
We reject the conclusion, and we reject the idea that the only serious restructuring is one that breaks the historic protection of the workforce.

You are right about one thing: the company needs a credible future in Germany.  
But you are **not** entitled to define credibility as plant closures and compulsory redundancies.

A plan that destroys trust, triggers mass resistance, and tears up job-security commitments is not a credible restructuring. It is a confrontation dressed up as strategy.

If you want real economic change, then bring forward **specific alternative measures** that avoid closures and forced layoffs:
- binding working-time reductions
- capacity shifts through natural attrition and internal transfers
- voluntary rather than compulsory exits
- productivity improvements
- fair but limited employee contributions
- management restraint and visible top-level sacrifice
- real investment commitments for every German site

What you have **not** shown is why the company must cross the red line of plant closure and forced redundancies. Saying “the numbers are hard” is not enough. The burden of proof is on management, especially after breaking with the historic job-security understanding.

## To the regional state government
I appreciate that you are rejecting closures and one-sided destruction of jobs. On that point, we stand together.  
But I want to be blunt: support is not enough if it remains rhetorical.

If you mean what you say, then use your influence to block any restructuring path that sacrifices German plants and sends workers into insecurity. The state has leverage here, and we expect it to be used to defend industrial employment, not to bless a “balanced” compromise that still ends in closures by another name.

## To the family holding
You speak about value destruction, trust, and governance stability. Fine.  
Then you should understand that **breaking the workforce agreement is also value destruction**. Once management signals that guarantees can be discarded whenever convenient, the company damages its own industrial relations for years.

If the family wants stability, it should support a settlement that preserves plants and jobs rather than forcing a showdown over closures. A durable company is not built by making employees permanently insecure.

## To investors
You keep saying “the numbers must add up.” We agree — but your definition of “adding up” cannot mean that workers carry the full cost while ownership and management preserve all flexibility for themselves.

If the outcome is based on:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- real investment,
- and a fair sharing of burden,

then that is not “comfort.” That is a serious industrial settlement.

What is not acceptable is a financial logic that treats German plants as disposable because they are costlier than some benchmark spreadsheet. Long-term value also depends on stable labour relations, skilled workforces, and industrial legitimacy.

## Our minimum position
Let me state it plainly:

### Non-negotiable
- **No plant closures in Germany**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No dismantling of co-determination**
- **No rollback of the job-security framework**

### Discussable, within limits
- working-time flexibility
- temporary capacity adjustments
- voluntary programmes
- productivity measures
- limited employee contribution, only if shared fairly and matched by management/ownership restraint
- investment and product commitments for all German sites

### Not acceptable
- closure threats as bargaining leverage
- “equivalent savings” defined as equivalent social damage
- open-ended insecurity for the workforce
- using the crisis to weaken collective protections permanently

## On the idea of “structural savings”
I want to challenge this directly: **structural savings do not automatically require structural destruction**.  
There are other ways:
- reduce hours before you reduce jobs
- improve utilisation before you close sites
- renegotiate work organisation before you tear up guarantees
- shift product allocation before you abandon locations
- align investment with a future industrial plan rather than with a closure logic

If management insists that closures are indispensable, then it should present a plant-by-plant, measure-by-measure justification showing why every alternative has supposedly failed. Until then, we will treat closures as a political choice, not an economic necessity.

## What would move us
Only one thing: a binding package that
1. preserves every German plant,
2. rules out compulsory redundancies,
3. protects co-determination and job security,
4. and provides a credible future for production and investment in Germany.

Without that, there is no settlement from our side — only resistance.

## Final point
Do not mistake our willingness to discuss alternatives for weakness.  
We are prepared to negotiate **how** the company adjusts, but not **whether** German plants stay open and workers keep their jobs.

If management wants an agreement, it must come back without closure demands and without forced layoffs. Anything else will meet fierce opposition from the workforce and from this council.
