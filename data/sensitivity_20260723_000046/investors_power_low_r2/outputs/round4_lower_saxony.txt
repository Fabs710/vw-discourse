I will be direct.

## To management
I agree with you on one point: this company needs a real restructuring, not a cosmetic one.

But I do **not** accept your repeated attempt to turn **plant closures and compulsory redundancies** into the default benchmark for seriousness. That is exactly where we differ.

From the state’s perspective, you have still **not made the case** that closures in Germany are unavoidable. You have asserted overcapacity; you have not yet proven that the non-destructive alternatives have been exhausted. That matters.

What I would need to see is:
- a plant-by-plant business case,
- a quantified savings target,
- a clear explanation of what non-closure measures were tested,
- and why those measures would be insufficient **before** any talk of closures.

Until then, closure is not “necessary”; it is only one instrument among several. I will not treat it as inevitable.

## To the Works Council
I am aligned with you on the core red lines:
- no plant closures in Germany,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- co-determination must remain intact,
- and the company must stay a regional industrial anchor.

That said, I need to be candid: a settlement cannot be only a rejection of management’s preferred instruments. There has to be a serious, measurable restructuring path.

So I support your insistence on:
- working-time flexibility,
- shift and utilisation changes,
- voluntary exits,
- internal redeployment,
- productivity gains,
- and fair, limited employee contributions.

But those contributions must be part of a package that also includes:
- visible management restraint,
- ownership contribution,
- and binding investment commitments for German sites.

What I will **not** support is a process where workers give up security while management preserves the right to close sites later.

## To the trade union
I agree that the burden cannot fall on labour alone.  
I also agree that temporary sacrifice must not become permanent erosion.

So yes, I can support a time-limited package with:
- working-time reductions,
- moderated pay elements,
- productivity measures,
- and voluntary programmes,

**if and only if** it is paired with:
- hard protections against closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and binding guarantees that the concessions are reversible once the crisis phase ends.

If that is not on the table, then the contribution is not fair; it is one-sided.

## To the controlling family holding
You are right that the solution must be economically real and durable. I agree with that.

Where I disagree is your continuing openness to site exits as if they were a normal governance tool. This company is not a purely financial asset. It is a major industrial anchor with regional responsibility, and that reality is reflected in its governance.

I am prepared to back difficult measures. I am **not** prepared to back a course that treats German plants as disposable.

If the family wants long-term value, it should support:
- a negotiated restructuring,
- visible management and ownership contribution,
- investment commitments,
- and protection of the industrial base in Germany.

## To investors
You are right to demand durable, credible savings and a real improvement in margins.

But I will say this clearly: a “hard” deal that triggers prolonged conflict, weakens trust, and damages the company’s German base is not automatically a good deal. It may look strong on paper and fail in practice.

What we need is a restructuring that is:
- economically credible,
- socially sustainable,
- and executable without years of open conflict.

That is the only kind of deal I would regard as sound.

## On the facilitator’s proposals

### 1) Time-limited “non-closure test phase” with hard protections
This is the only direction that comes close to something workable.

I can support a genuine test phase if the protections are real:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no outsourcing as a substitute,
- no silent attrition strategy.

But I will be explicit: I do **not** support any design that embeds a closure fallback as a pre-authorised outcome. If the “test” is just a staged ultimatum, then it is not a neutral process.

### 2) Conditional burden-sharing package with a tiered fallback
I am cautious here. A fallback can easily become a slow-motion reopening of the same pressure points we are trying to avoid.

If there is a second stage, it must not be a disguised route to site exits. It must stay within non-destructive instruments and preserve the state’s ability to defend jobs and plants.

### 3) Site protection for core sites + conditional commitments for the rest
I understand the logic, but I remain wary.

A two-tier footprint can quickly turn into a map of expendable sites. That is not a path I can endorse lightly, especially if it undermines regional employment or creates a precedent for later closures.

If differentiation is discussed, it must be tied tightly to:
- investment,
- product allocation,
- and long-term site security.

Not to a quiet prelude to closure.

## My bottom line
I will not support:
- plant closures in Germany as a first or default answer,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or any settlement that weakens co-determination and job security.

I **will** support:
- a serious restructuring,
- measurable employee contributions,
- management and ownership burden-sharing,
- binding investment commitments,
- and a time-limited, independent test of non-destructive alternatives.

So the burden is now on management and ownership to bring forward a package that proves the company can regain competitiveness **without gutting regional employment**. If they can do that, we have a basis. If they cannot, they have still not made the case for the damage they want to impose.

If needed, I can next state the exact minimum conditions under which the state would support a non-closure restructuring package.
