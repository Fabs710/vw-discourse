I will engage directly.

## To management
I agree with your core diagnosis: the current footprint, utilisation, and cost base are not good enough, and a cosmetic settlement would be a mistake. I also agree that we need durability, not a temporary truce.

Where I disagree is the way you keep using closures and compulsory redundancies as the benchmark for seriousness.

That is too blunt for this governance environment and too risky for long-term value. A hard restructuring that triggers prolonged conflict, reputational damage, and execution drag can destroy more value than it creates. As controlling shareholders, we will not back a “tough” plan simply because it looks decisive.

What I need from management is not another assertion that overcapacity exists. I need:
- a quantified savings gap,
- a plant-by-plant business case,
- a clear explanation of what non-destructive measures were tested,
- and why those measures are insufficient.

Until then, closures remain an instrument, not a proven necessity.

## To the Works Council
I respect the clarity of your red lines, and I agree with you on one point: workers should not carry the full burden, and a one-sided restructuring would be unacceptable.

But I do not accept your claim that every German plant must remain untouched under all circumstances. That is not a credible long-term answer if the economics do not work.

You are right to insist on:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- co-determination remaining intact,
- fair burden-sharing,
- and investment commitments.

You are not right to assume that no footprint adjustment can ever be discussed. That would freeze the current cost problem in place and make the eventual correction harsher.

## To the trade union
I agree that any labour contribution must be time-limited, reversible, and fair. I also agree that management and ownership must contribute visibly.

Where I disagree is your refusal to accept that the company may need structural change beyond temporary flexibility. Temporary measures can help, but they do not automatically restore competitiveness.

If the company can close the cost gap without closures or forced redundancies, I am open to that. But the package must be real, not symbolic. I will not support concessions that merely postpone the problem.

## To the regional state government
I understand and respect your role as anchor shareholder and your concern for employment in the region. That concern is legitimate.

But I do not accept a blanket “no closures” position as a governance principle. If it becomes absolute, it risks preserving an uncompetitive structure and ultimately harming the region anyway.

What I can support is a serious non-closure test, with strong protections during the test phase, but not a permanent veto on all footprint correction regardless of economics.

## To investors
You are right to demand durable, bankable savings. We share that priority.

But I would caution against assuming that the only credible restructuring is the harshest one on paper. The real question is whether the outcome is:
- materially better on margins,
- durable,
- and executable without years of destabilising conflict.

A deal that looks strong but creates persistent industrial warfare is not a good deal.

## My position
The family holding will support a restructuring only if it does all of the following:
1. materially improves competitiveness and margins,
2. is durable and not cosmetic,
3. avoids value-destructive instability,
4. shares the burden fairly,
5. includes visible management and ownership contribution,
6. and preserves long-term governance stability.

That is our threshold.

## What I can support
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- working-time and shift flexibility,
- productivity improvements,
- voluntary exit schemes,
- internal redeployment,
- management restraint,
- investment commitments for German production and future products,
- and a time-limited, independently audited non-closure test phase.

## What I cannot support
- a status quo deal dressed up as compromise,
- one-sided labour sacrifice,
- endless delay,
- a permanent no-closure veto regardless of economics,
- or any outcome that permanently weakens enterprise value or governance stability.

## On the facilitator’s framework
### 1) Time-limited non-closure test phase
This is the least bad option, if it is genuinely time-limited and rigorous. I could support:
- no closures during the window,
- no compulsory redundancies during the window,
- an independent audit,
- and a hard quantitative review of whether the savings gap is actually closed.

But I will not accept a design that turns the test into a permanent prohibition on structural change.

### 2) Conditional burden-sharing package with tiered fallback
Potentially workable, but only if the first stage is substantial and the second stage remains economically grounded. If it becomes a way to indefinitely avoid hard decisions, then I would reject it.

### 3) Site protection for core sites plus flexibility elsewhere
This may be workable in principle, but only if it is driven by economics and not by politics. A two-tier setup can easily become a slow-motion closure process, and I would not endorse that lightly.

## Bottom line
I am open to a serious non-closure solution if it is real, material, and independently verified. If labour can produce a package that truly closes the gap without closures or compulsory redundancies, I will listen.

But I will not support a settlement that merely protects every legacy arrangement while leaving the company structurally uncompetitive. And I will not accept prolonged conflict as the price of avoiding hard decisions.

If helpful, I can next specify what minimum savings and capacity correction threshold I would regard as credible from a governance standpoint.
