I will be direct.

## To the Works Council
We are aligned on the essentials: **no plant closures in Germany, no compulsory redundancies, no dismantling of the collective wage framework**. That remains the union’s line as well.

I also agree that “competitiveness” cannot become a euphemism for making workers pay for every strategic error made above them. If the company wants contributions from employees, then they must be **limited, fair, and matched by sacrifices elsewhere**.

Where I would sharpen the point is this: we should not let management turn the discussion into a fake choice between “closures” and “no restructuring.” There are alternatives, but they must be concrete and credible. That means we need measurable proposals on:
- working-time reductions,
- capacity adjustments through reduced shifts and utilisation,
- voluntary rather than compulsory exits,
- internal reallocation,
- productivity measures,
- and a defined employee contribution that does **not** break collective standards.

So yes: we are together on the red lines. But we should keep pressure on management to table a real package, not just slogans.

## To management
Your argument is still essentially: “We need structural savings, therefore closures and job cuts remain justified unless labour accepts a deep enough concession.”

I reject that framing.

### What I agree with
- The status quo is not sustainable.
- The company needs a durable future.
- There must be savings and capacity correction.
- A cosmetic deal would be irresponsible.

### What I do **not** accept
- That plant closures are the default answer.
- That compulsory redundancies are somehow unavoidable.
- That workers should take the dominant share of the pain while management and owners remain largely insulated.
- That the collective wage framework can be treated as a variable to be dismantled because the market is difficult.

If you want a solution that avoids closures and forced layoffs, then show us a package that is **structurally** strong enough without crossing those lines. Don’t just repeat that the economics are difficult; we know they are. The question is whether management is prepared to share the burden and rethink the footprint without sacrificing jobs as the first move.

And let me be clear: if your “material savings” proposal is essentially wage pressure plus latent closure threats, then there is no basis for agreement.

## To the regional state government
Your position is closer to ours than management’s, and I welcome that. But support has to be more than political language.

If you reject closures and mass job destruction, then use your leverage to make that real. The company’s governance structure gives you influence. If that influence is not used to stop a closure-first strategy, then workers will rightly see the public line as empty.

We can work with a settlement that protects industrial employment and still achieves change. But it must be a **real** settlement, not a rearranged version of the same pressure on labour.

## To the family holding
You are right about one thing: a prolonged conflict would destroy value.  
But so would forcing through a restructuring that tears up trust, breaks the job-security framework, and poisons labour relations for years.

So when you say “shared sacrifice,” I agree — but only if that includes visible restraint from ownership and management. That means:
- no one-sided burden shifting,
- no closure ultimatum,
- no attempt to convert the crisis into permanent wage dumping,
- and no expectation that workers will fund competitiveness alone.

If the family wants stability, it should back a deal that protects jobs and sites while delivering genuine, balanced contributions from all sides.

## To investors
You are correct that the final package must be economically credible. We are not arguing for symbolism.

But credibility is not only a matter of quarter-by-quarter margin targets. It also depends on labour peace, industrial capability, and the company’s social licence to operate. A “solution” that triggers strikes, undermines morale, and destabilises production is not value creation.

What would be credible from our side is:
- a negotiated reduction in labour costs,
- temporary flexibility measures,
- a fair employee contribution,
- and productivity/capacity measures that avoid closures and compulsory redundancies.

What is not credible is a demand for the workforce to absorb the full correction while the ownership side keeps everything else unchanged. If the economics truly require sacrifice, then that sacrifice must be distributed fairly.

## Our position, plainly stated

### Non-negotiable
- **No plant closures in Germany**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Protection of the collective wage framework**
- **No one-sided burden shifting to employees**

### Negotiable
- temporary working-time reductions
- shift and capacity adjustments
- voluntary exit schemes
- internal redeployment
- productivity measures
- a fair, limited employee contribution
- investment commitments tied to the future of the German sites

### What would move us
A binding package in which:
1. jobs and sites are secured,
2. the burden is shared fairly,
3. management and ownership contribute visibly,
4. and the company still gains real structural savings.

## Bottom line
We are not going to sign off on a restructuring built around closures and compulsory redundancies.

If management wants labour peace, it has to come back with a **jobs-secure, site-secure, and collectively bargained** alternative that genuinely spreads the burden. If not, the union will defend the workforce with the means available to it. The peace obligation has ended, and we will not let workers carry the full cost of a crisis they did not create.
