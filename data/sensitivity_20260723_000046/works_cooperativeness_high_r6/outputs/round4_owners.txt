I will be direct.

We agree on the diagnosis: the company needs a real restructuring, not a cosmetic one. We also agree that a prolonged strike, reputational damage, or a settlement that simply postpones the problem would destroy value. On that, I am aligned with management, the expert, and the capital markets.

Where I part company with the labour side and, in part, with the state shareholder is on the idea that **closures and compulsory redundancies can be ruled out in advance** and still expect the ownership side to accept that the economics will somehow be solved. That is not a governance standard I can support.

## What I agree with
- **Voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, working-time flexibility, shift changes, and targeted productivity measures** are legitimate and necessary tools.
- A **quantified, enforceable internal restructuring package** deserves serious examination.
- A **hybrid or sequenced approach** can be sensible if it is disciplined and time-bound.
- The company should avoid unnecessary disruption and preserve future industrial capability where that is economically defensible.
- Non-closure measures can capture a substantial share of closure economics.

## What I disagree with
I do **not** accept the works council’s and union’s position that:
- every German plant must remain open unconditionally,
- compulsory redundancies can be excluded in all circumstances,
- and legacy job-security arrangements should remain untouched through 2030.

That is not a restructuring framework; it is an attempt to preserve the current footprint while shifting all adjustment pressure away from the business. The expert has now confirmed that non-closure alternatives may capture **50%–80%** of closure-level savings, not 100% in every case. That is important. It means labour’s position can be part of a solution, but it is not by itself enough.

I also do **not** accept the state shareholder’s position if it hardens into a de facto veto over any meaningful structural adjustment. Regional responsibility matters, but a plant that is protected into uncompetitiveness does not serve the region long term.

## On the expert’s model
The expert’s comparison is exactly the right lens:
- closure gives the largest permanent savings,
- but also the highest one-time cost and execution risk;
- no-closure restructuring can deliver most of the value at lower disruption risk;
- hybrid sequencing is only attractive if milestones are strict and enforcement is real.

That means the right question is not “closures or no closures” in the abstract. The right question is:

**Can the non-closure package deliver enough durable, enforceable savings and capacity reduction to close the economics gap on a risk-adjusted basis?**

If yes, I am open. If no, then structural options must remain available.

## To the works council
You say any review trigger is just a delayed ultimatum. I understand why you say that, but I do not accept your conclusion.

A review mechanism is not a betrayal if it is tied to:
- quantified savings targets,
- measurable capacity reductions,
- a defined timeline,
- and a genuine fallback if performance is missed.

What I cannot support is a blanket, unconditional promise that no site will ever be touched regardless of results. That would remove governance discipline before the restructuring has even begun.

You also say “all German plants open through 2030” is the minimum condition for trust. For me, trust depends on **performance and durability**, not on promises that may freeze the business model in place. If your alternative can truly deliver the economics, then it should stand on its own merits rather than requiring the removal of all management backstops.

## To the union
You are right that employees cannot be the only side to contribute. I agree.

But then the package must be judged by whether it permanently fixes the cost base. Temporary concessions are not enough if the underlying structural problem remains. The company needs:
- durable labour-cost reduction,
- durable capacity reduction,
- and enforceable delivery.

I do not accept the idea that any change to legacy protections is automatically “dismantling” the collective framework. There is a difference between **negotiated, time-limited adjustment** and permanent erosion. We are open to the former, not the latter.

If the union wants the ownership side to support a no-closure route, then bring the numbers:
- what is saved,
- how long it lasts,
- where capacity comes out,
- and how the package is enforced.

## To management
Management is right that the economics must improve materially. But I want to be clear: I will not support a strategy that uses closure as a rhetorical bludgeon without a serious comparison to non-closure alternatives.

The board needs a disciplined decision package:
1. full closure case,
2. strong no-closure case,
3. hybrid case with milestones and backstop,
with each showing:
- annual savings,
- one-time costs,
- timing,
- execution risk,
- and probability-weighted NPV.

If the internal package gets close enough on a risk-adjusted basis, that may well be preferable to closure because it reduces disruption and preserves optionality. But if it does not, then management must not be expected to carry structural overcapacity indefinitely.

## To the state shareholder
I respect the regional role. I do. But I cannot support turning that role into an absolute barrier to economically necessary adjustment.

The state’s best contribution is not to guarantee every site forever. It is to support a solution that:
- preserves the economically defensible core,
- enables investment,
- and restores competitiveness.

If the no-closure package is truly durable and enforceable, I am open to it. If it is not, then the state should not pretend that political comfort is the same as industrial viability.

## My position, plainly
I will support a negotiated restructuring if it meets these conditions:
- **material and durable savings**
- **real capacity reduction**
- **meaningful labour contribution**
- **enforceable milestones**
- **credible investment commitments**
- **no cosmetic deal**

I will **not** support:
- unconditional site guarantees that block competitiveness,
- a settlement that leaves the cost base structurally untouched,
- or a process where management is asked to renounce all structural options before the economics are proven.

## Where I stand on the bridge ideas
### 1) Sequenced restructuring with moratorium
Possible only if the moratorium is genuinely time-bound, savings are front-loaded, and the fallback is real if targets are missed.

### 2) Binding “equivalent savings” package
This is the most promising route, but only if “equivalent” means economically equivalent, not politically comforting. It must be quantified and durable.

### 3) Protected-core / adjustable-periphery
Potentially workable, but only if “adjustable” means real internal restructuring, not a disguised path to closure. If it becomes a two-tier weakening of the German base, I will not support it.

## Bottom line
The family holding will support a serious non-closure alternative **if it is quantified, durable, enforceable, and materially sufficient**. We are not wedded to closure for its own sake.

But we are also not prepared to bless an arrangement that rules out structural action while leaving the company uncompetitive. That would be value-destructive, and it would not protect the long-term interests of the owners, the company, or the workforce.

If the labour side wants to avoid the most severe measures, bring a package that truly matches the economics. If it cannot, then the company must retain its structural options.
