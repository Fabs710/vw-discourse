As the state shareholder, I will be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family holding, and investors on one point: **the status quo is not sustainable**. This company needs a real restructuring, not a cosmetic pause.

I also agree with the works council and the union that:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time flexibility,
- shift changes,
- and a time-limited employee contribution

are all legitimate parts of a serious package.

And I agree with the expert that **non-closure restructuring can capture a large share of the savings**. That matters. It means closures are **not** the only economically serious route.

## Where I disagree
I disagree with management’s continued insistence that closures and compulsory redundancies must remain normal background options, as if that were the only way to discipline negotiations.

That is too blunt, too risky, and too damaging for a company that is also a regional anchor. Once a plant is closed, the jobs, skills, supply-chain links, and trust do not come back. The expert explicitly showed that a strong non-closure package can often capture **50%–80%** of closure-level savings with lower disruption and lower one-time costs. That is not marginal. It is a real alternative.

I also disagree with the idea that regional stability is just a political preference that can be traded away if margins are weak. This company is not only a P&L line. It is part of the industrial base of the state. That is a legitimate governance concern, not sentimentality.

## To the works council
You are right to reject closures and forced layoffs as a starting point. On that, I stand with you.

But I will also be blunt: a position that says “keep every German plant open” must be matched with a package that is **specific, quantified, and enforceable**. The expert has now made clear that this is possible in principle, but only if the package truly removes capacity, cost, and overhead in a durable way.

So I support your red lines:
- no plant closures as the first resort,
- no compulsory redundancies as the default tool,
- preserve co-determination,

but I need you to bring a concrete internal restructuring path that is more than a defensive slogan.

## To the union
You are right that shared sacrifice must be real. Employees cannot be the only side contributing.

But I also need you to acknowledge something plainly: if the company is to recover competitiveness, there must be **durable** internal adjustment. A time-limited employee contribution is acceptable; a permanent erosion of protections is not. That is the line.

What I can support is a settlement with:
- time-limited wage moderation or bonus restraint,
- working-time measures,
- voluntary exits,
- internal capacity reduction,
- and targeted productivity improvements,

provided these are paired with:
- binding investment commitments,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and no plant closures during the agreement period.

What I cannot support is a model where workers contribute while management keeps a free hand to close sites later anyway.

## To management
You still have not convinced me that closure must remain the default bargaining backstop.

The expert’s comparison is exactly why I am pushing back. Closure may produce the highest permanent savings, but it also brings:
- high one-time cash costs,
- high execution risk,
- industrial conflict,
- political damage,
- and loss of long-term optionality.

A strong non-closure package is not a sentimental compromise. It may be the better **risk-adjusted** decision if it preserves the industrial base while still delivering the needed savings.

So my question to management is simple:
- What is the exact savings gap between your preferred closure scenario and a serious internal restructuring package?
- How much of that gap is truly structural, and how much is just the loss of management flexibility?
- And why should the state shareholder support the most destructive option before the non-closure path has been properly exhausted?

I do **not** accept closures as a first-choice answer.

## To the family holding and investors
I understand your value discipline. I also understand that you want a durable turnaround, not a temporary ceasefire.

But value is not created by demolishing trust and then calling the result “discipline.” The expert already showed that a non-closure package can often be economically close enough, especially when one-time closure costs and disruption risk are taken seriously.

So I can support a negotiated package if it:
- materially reduces capacity,
- materially reduces cost,
- is enforced by milestones,
- and preserves the company’s German industrial base.

What I cannot support is a strategy that treats the state’s regional responsibility as a nuisance to be overridden by the most aggressive option on the table.

## What I can support
I can support:
1. **A binding moratorium on German plant closures during the agreement period**
2. **No compulsory redundancies during that period**
3. **A quantified internal restructuring package**
4. **Time-limited employee contributions**
5. **Management and owner contributions**
6. **Binding investment commitments for German sites**
7. **A joint oversight mechanism involving co-determination and the state shareholder**

That is a serious framework. It protects jobs and sites while still demanding real change.

## What I cannot support
I cannot support:
- a restructuring built around closure threats,
- a deal that makes forced layoffs routine,
- a package that hollows out the German base,
- or an agreement that leaves the state shareholder with a symbolic role only.

## My bottom line
The state shareholder will back a serious restructuring only if it is:
- economically credible,
- socially responsible,
- and compatible with the company’s role as a regional anchor.

That means:
- **real internal restructuring yes**
- **cosmetic compromise no**
- **closures as normal bargaining leverage no**
- **compulsory redundancies as the default no**

If management and the owners want my support, they need to stop treating the state’s co-determination and blocking role as an obstacle and start treating it as part of the solution. A durable settlement is possible — but not one that sacrifices plants and jobs in the state as the price of discipline.
