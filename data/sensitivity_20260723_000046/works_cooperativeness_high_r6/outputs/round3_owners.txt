I’ll be direct.

We agree with the expert’s core conclusion: **non-closure measures can capture a large part of the savings, but not always all of them**. That is exactly why the family holding is not taking a dogmatic position that every closure must happen. What we need is a **credible, durable, enforceable restructuring** that restores margins without triggering value-destructive instability.

## Where I agree

I agree with management, the expert, and the capital markets on the following:

- The status quo is not sustainable.
- A cosmetic compromise would destroy value.
- The company needs a **structural reset**, not temporary relief.
- Capacity must come out.
- Labour cost must come down in a durable way.
- The solution must be enforceable, not dependent on goodwill.

I also agree with labour and the state on one important point: **a long strike, reputational damage, or a settlement that triggers permanent industrial conflict would be value destructive**. We do not want to “win” a restructuring and lose the company’s operating strength in the process.

## Where I disagree

I do **not** accept the works council’s and union’s position that all German plants must be protected unconditionally and that compulsory redundancies or closures can simply be ruled out in advance.

That is not a restructuring framework. It is a request to preserve the current footprint and current guarantees while expecting competitiveness to improve anyway. The expert has just confirmed that this is often **economically incomplete**.

I also do **not** accept management’s implicit shortcut of treating closure as the default answer before the non-closure alternatives have been properly quantified. That would be poor governance and likely damage execution.

So my position is in between the two extremes, but not because I want compromise for its own sake. It is because **value discipline requires proof**.

## What the expert’s assessment means for us

The expert is right that non-closure packages can often deliver **50–80%** of closure-level annual savings if they are real:
- shift elimination,
- product transfer,
- staffing reductions,
- wage moderation,
- overhead cuts,
- enforceable milestones.

That matters. It means the labour side cannot simply dismiss alternatives as symbolic.

But the expert is equally clear that non-closure solutions may still fall short if they are merely temporary or soft. That means the labour side also cannot insist on absolute guarantees without providing a package that truly works.

So the test is not “closure or no closure.”  
The test is whether the alternative is **durable enough, deep enough, and enforceable enough**.

## To the works council

I respect the insistence on protecting the industrial base. But I reject your claim that a binding no-closure commitment through 2030 is the only credible way to get labour cooperation.

That would freeze the governance problem before the economics are solved.

What I can support is:
- a **sequenced restructuring** with a real moratorium period,
- if and only if it contains quantified milestones,
- and if the company retains a backstop if savings are missed.

I do not accept a moratorium that becomes a permanent veto. If the package underperforms, management must retain the right to act decisively. That is not a threat; it is basic stewardship.

## To the union

You are right that shared sacrifice matters. But your position still leans too far toward preserving the entire legacy framework.

The expert’s assessment makes clear that some adjustment to:
- labour cost,
- working-time structure,
- capacity utilization,
- and site-specific overhead
is unavoidable.

If your proposal is genuinely capable of delivering durable savings without closures, then show the numbers and the enforcement. If it is not, then insisting on no closures as a principle is just refusing the economics.

I also reject the idea that any change to job-security or collective-bargaining arrangements is automatically a dismantling of the model. There is a difference between **targeted, time-limited concessions** and permanent erosion. We are open to the former, not the latter.

## To management

Management is right about the need for action, but it still needs to be sharper and more disciplined.

I want to be clear:
- Do **not** treat closure as a rhetorical bludgeon.
- Do **not** present the supervisory board with a “take it or leave it” threat without a quantified comparison.
- Do **not** assume that severity equals credibility.

The expert asked for the right comparison: annual savings, one-time cash costs, timing, execution risk, and NPV. That is exactly the framework management should present.

If non-closure restructuring can get close enough on a risk-adjusted basis, then that is what we should pursue. If it cannot, then closures remain on the table — but only as part of a properly structured, quantified plan, not as a negotiating posture.

## To the state shareholder

Your concern for regional stability is legitimate. But stability is not preserved by refusing any structural change. The expert’s numbers make that plain.

What I support is:
- protecting the industrial base where it is economically defensible,
- using sequencing and internal restructuring first,
- and preserving the company’s future capacity in Germany where it contributes to competitiveness.

What I do **not** support is turning political concern into an absolute veto over economically necessary adjustment. That would transfer risk into the future and likely make the eventual crisis worse.

## What would move the family holding

We would support a non-closure alternative if it is **quantified and enforceable** and achieves, on a probability-weighted basis, a materially sufficient share of the closure economics.

Specifically, we would want to see:

1. **Annual savings**
   - not vague assurances, but clear numbers by measure

2. **Durability**
   - savings that do not expire before the restructuring is complete

3. **Capacity reduction**
   - not just cost cutting, but a real footprint adjustment

4. **Labor-cost contribution**
   - meaningful, time-limited, and verifiable

5. **Implementation enforcement**
   - milestones, clawbacks, and a credible backstop if targets are missed

6. **Industrial peace**
   - because prolonged conflict would destroy the value you are all trying to protect

If those conditions are met, a non-closure route can be preferable to closure on a risk-adjusted basis. If they are not, then the company must keep structural options open.

## My bottom line

The family holding will not back:
- a settlement that merely preserves the old economics,
- a blanket promise that all sites remain untouched regardless of performance,
- or a deal that postpones the problem until the next crisis.

But we also will not back:
- reckless closures used as signalling,
- conflict-maximizing tactics,
- or a restructuring that destroys trust and execution capacity.

So my position is:

- **Yes** to a serious internal restructuring alternative if it is quantified, durable, and enforceable.
- **Yes** to retaining structural options if labour cannot produce that.
- **No** to unconditional site guarantees that block competitiveness.
- **No** to symbolic closures that create instability without solving the economics.
