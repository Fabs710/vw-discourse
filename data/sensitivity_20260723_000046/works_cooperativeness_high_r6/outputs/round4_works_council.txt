I will be equally direct in round 4.

The other sides keep asking whether a no-closure package can be “economically equivalent” to closures. My answer remains: **yes, if the company is serious about structural internal restructuring; no, if “equivalence” is just a code word for later plant sacrifice**.

The expert has already made the key point: non-closure measures can capture a large share of the savings, often **50%–80%** of closure economics, with lower one-time costs and lower disruption risk. That is enough to keep the discussion alive. It is not enough to make closures or compulsory redundancies acceptable as a standing threat.

## To management
You argue that flexibility must remain because the package might underperform. I hear that. But your logic is still one-sided.

You want the workforce to accept concessions **and** accept that closures and forced layoffs remain available in the background. That is precisely the asymmetry we reject.

If you want a credible restructuring, then bring a quantified internal package that is actually designed to work:
- shift reductions,
- line consolidation,
- product allocation changes,
- internal mobility,
- voluntary exits,
- qualification,
- temporary employee contribution,
- management contribution,
- overhead reduction.

What I do **not** accept is “temporary protection now, structural threat later.” That is not a bridge; it is a delayed ultimatum.

And let me be clear: **every German plant open through 2030** is not a slogan for us. It is the minimum condition for trust. Without that, there is no serious basis for agreement.

## To the family holding and investors
You both speak the language of value discipline, and I understand that. But value is not protected by threatening the workforce into submission.

The expert’s model shows that closure may produce the largest permanent savings, but also the largest one-time cost and the highest execution risk. That means closure is not automatically the superior economic answer. It is only one option, and not necessarily the best risk-adjusted one.

So when you say “keep structural options open,” what you really mean is: preserve a right to break the job-security framework if negotiations do not go your way. That is exactly what we oppose.

If you want a value-preserving outcome, support a package that:
- keeps every German plant open,
- rules out compulsory redundancies,
- preserves co-determination,
- and achieves savings through negotiated internal restructuring.

If that cannot be done, then the problem is not labour’s resistance. The problem is a business strategy that still relies on industrial destruction as a fallback.

## To the state shareholder
You are right that the German industrial base matters. But your support has to become enforceable, not symbolic.

I appreciate your opposition to closures and mass layoffs, but I do not want general concern. I want governance action:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding investment commitments,
- and oversight that cannot be bypassed later.

If you are serious about the regional anchor function, then stand with us against a restructuring strategy that turns some sites into expendable “periphery.” The moment you accept that logic, the rest of the German base is only waiting for the next cut.

## To the union
We are aligned on the core line: no closures, no forced layoffs, preserve the collective framework. That remains correct.

Where I want us to stay sharp is on enforcement and on the management contribution. The company cannot ask for labour sacrifices while keeping the option to tear up protections later. We need:
- durable safeguards,
- a temporary and reciprocal employee contribution,
- and a real commitment to investment and future products.

But I will also say this plainly: no amount of “equivalence” language can make closures or compulsory redundancies acceptable. If the package only works because it assumes a plant can be closed later, then it is not an equivalent package. It is a deferred attack on the workforce.

## On the expert assessment
The expert confirmed something important: non-closure restructuring is not fantasy. It is a real, economically meaningful alternative.

That means management’s claim that closures are inherently necessary is **not proven**. It is a preference, not a fact.

The expert also confirmed that a strong internal package must include:
- permanent capacity reduction,
- durable labor-cost reduction,
- overhead reduction,
- and credible enforcement.

Fine. That is exactly where negotiations should stay: inside the company, with jobs and sites protected, not under a closure threat.

## What we could accept
We can accept:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- internal mobility,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary concessions,
- targeted productivity improvements,
- site-by-site internal adjustments **without closures or forced layoffs**.

We can also accept a serious review mechanism for whether the package is working.

What we cannot accept:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- a “review” that restores management’s right to close sites,
- erosion of co-determination,
- or the gradual hollowing out of German plants under another name.

## Bottom line
Let me make the works council’s position unmistakable:

1. **All German plants stay open.**
2. **No compulsory redundancies.**
3. **Preserve co-determination and job-security protections.**
4. **We are willing to discuss real internal restructuring, not symbolic concessions.**
5. **If management wants cooperation, it must stop using closure and forced layoffs as bargaining weapons.**

If the other side can bring a quantified package that meets those conditions, we will engage seriously. If not, then there is no agreement to be had — only resistance.
