Below is a neutral financial comparison of the three options, using the expert framework already introduced and the facts given in the scenario.

## Bottom line

On a **3-year probability-weighted NPV** basis, a **deep no-closure restructuring** is often the strongest economic option **if** it can deliver most of the savings quickly and credibly.  
A **full closure** usually produces the **highest steady-state annual savings**, but it also has the **highest one-time cash costs** and execution risk, which often hurts 3-year NPV.  
A **hybrid sequencing / moratorium** solution usually lands in between.

So the key question is not “Which option has the biggest theoretical savings?” but:

**Can the no-closure package remove enough cost, fast enough, at low enough execution risk, to beat closure on risk-adjusted 3-year value?**

## 1) Side-by-side financial comparison

### A. Full closure / shutdown
**Economic profile**
- Highest permanent removal of fixed cost
- Highest one-time restructuring cash cost
- Highest industrial and transition risk
- Savings arrive later because of shutdown timing

**Illustrative economics**
- Annual gross savings: **100% of site’s avoidable fixed-cost base**
- One-time cost: **high**
- Timing: savings delayed, often ramping over 1–3 years
- 3-year NPV: often **negative or weakly positive**, depending on site cost base and closure expense

**Interpretation**
Closure is most attractive only where the site is structurally non-viable and the one-time cost is justified by a very large, durable cost base reduction.

---

### B. Deep no-closure restructuring
This means:
- shift reductions
- line consolidation
- product reallocation
- voluntary exits
- redeployment
- working-time changes
- temporary employee contribution
- management/overhead reduction

**Economic profile**
- Annual savings usually around **50%–80% of closure economics**
- One-time costs are much lower
- Savings can start sooner
- Lower execution and political risk
- Residual fixed cost remains higher than with closure

**Illustrative economics**
- Annual savings: **50%–80% of closure case**
- One-time cost: **low to moderate**
- Timing: faster savings realization
- 3-year NPV: often **better than closure**, especially when closure costs are large

**Interpretation**
This is often the best **risk-adjusted** option if it is truly durable and enforceable. It can outperform closure over a 3-year horizon because it avoids major upfront cash outflows.

---

### C. Hybrid sequencing with moratorium/backstop
This means:
- temporary closure moratorium
- immediate internal measures
- review trigger and fallback if savings underdeliver

**Economic profile**
- Some delay in savings
- Lower immediate conflict and lower near-term disruption
- Better than pure closure on one-time cost
- Usually worse than a clean no-closure package if the delay is substantial

**Illustrative economics**
- Annual savings: **about 60%–70% of closure economics**
- One-time cost: **mid-range**
- Timing: savings delayed relative to deep no-closure
- 3-year NPV: often **slightly positive or modest**, depending on delay and enforcement quality

**Interpretation**
This is a compromise structure. It can work if the moratorium materially improves labor peace and execution certainty. If not, it is often inferior to a direct no-closure deal.

## 2) Illustrative 3-year NPV logic

Using the expert’s illustrative method and a 10% discount rate:

- **Closure**
  - highest annual savings
  - but one-time costs can overwhelm early benefits
  - 3-year NPV often weak

- **Deep no-closure**
  - lower annual savings
  - much lower one-time costs
  - 3-year NPV often strongest

- **Hybrid**
  - intermediate result
  - value depends heavily on whether delay avoids industrial conflict

The expert’s example showed:
- closure: **negative 3-year NPV**

That pattern is economically plausible in large German industrial restructurings when closure costs are large.

## 3) Site-specific execution risk

Since no site file was provided, I can only state the normal risk pattern:

### Closure risk
Highest risk where:
- there is a large local workforce
- the site has political significance
- supply chain re-routing is complex
- asset write-downs and decommissioning are high
- industrial action is likely

### No-closure risk
Highest risk where:
- savings depend on labor concessions that may expire
- product allocation changes are not durable
- internal mobility cannot absorb enough staff
- capacity reductions are too shallow to fix the cost base

### Hybrid risk
Highest risk where:
- the moratorium weakens urgency
- fallback rights are contested
- the package becomes a delay mechanism rather than a restructuring mechanism

## 4) Financial conclusion

From a restructuring finance standpoint:

- **Closure** = best long-run fixed-cost removal, but expensive upfront
- **Deep no-closure restructuring** = often best 3-year value if sufficiently deep and enforceable
- **Hybrid sequencing** = useful only if it meaningfully reduces conflict and still delivers timely savings

---

## 5) Practical decision rule

A no-closure package is financially credible if it can demonstrate, by site:

1. **annual savings close enough to closure economics**
2. **low one-time cash cost**
3. **delivery within 12–24 months**
4. **durable enforcement through 2030 or equivalent horizon**
5. **acceptable residual risk**

If those conditions are not met, then closure or stronger structural action becomes financially more defensible.
