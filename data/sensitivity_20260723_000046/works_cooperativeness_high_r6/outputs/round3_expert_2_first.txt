Below is a neutral financial assessment of the three restructuring paths now being discussed:

1. **Full closure / shutdown of one or more German sites**
2. **No-closure restructuring**
3. **Hybrid sequencing**: temporary moratorium plus backstop closure rights

I will focus on **annual savings, one-time costs, timing, NPV logic, and execution risk**. Because no plant-level cost data have been provided, the figures are **illustrative ranges** for a large German automotive plant or comparable production unit, not verified company-specific numbers.

---

## 1) Executive conclusion

From a restructuring-finance perspective:

- **Closure** usually produces the **highest permanent annual cost reduction**, but also the **highest one-time cash cost** and the **highest execution/industrial disruption risk**.
- **No-closure restructuring** typically captures **most, but not all**, of the closure economics. It usually has **lower one-time cost**, lower disruption risk, and better optionality.
- **Hybrid sequencing** can be the best **risk-adjusted NPV** outcome if it creates immediate cash savings, preserves strike/implementation control, and keeps a credible fallback if targets are missed. But it only works if the milestone design is strict.

The key finance point:  
**The “best” option depends on whether management values maximum permanent savings more than near-term cash preservation and execution certainty.** In German multi-site automotive restructuring, the answer is often not a pure closure, but a **structured non-closure package with strong enforcement**.

---

## 2) Side-by-side financial comparison

### A. Full closure / shutdown

**Typical annual savings**
- Roughly **€100m–€250m+ per year per large site**, depending on size, product mix, and how much overhead is truly site-specific.
- Savings come from eliminating site labor, local overhead, maintenance, utilities, logistics, and some support functions.

**Typical one-time costs**
- Roughly **€150m–€500m+** per site.
- Includes severance, transfer/relocation, contract exits, remediation, write-downs, decommissioning, and possible supply-chain transition costs.

**Timing**
- Cash costs hit early.
- Savings ramp in over 12–36 months depending on legal/process timing and production transfer.
- In German industrial settings, closure timing is often delayed by consultation, industrial action risk, and network reconfiguration.

**NPV**
- Can be attractive if:
  - the site has high fixed cost,
  - alternative capacity exists,
  - closure proceeds without major disruption,
  - and the one-time cost is not excessive.
- Can be worse than expected if:
  - closure triggers lost output,
  - transfer is messy,
  - or the company underestimates remediation and litigation/process costs.

**Execution risk**
- **High**
- Main risks:
  - industrial action,
  - supervisory-board deadlock,
  - delayed implementation,
  - reputational damage,
  - supply disruption,
  - political resistance,
  - loss of future flexibility.

---

### B. No-closure restructuring

This means internal measures only:
- voluntary exits
- redeployment
- qualification
- shift reduction
- capacity rationalization
- wage/bonus moderation
- working-time changes
- support-function consolidation

**Typical annual savings**
- Roughly **50%–80% of closure-level annual savings** if the package is serious and durable.
- For a closure-equivalent site, that might mean **€50m–€200m+ per year** depending on how much capacity and labor cost is actually removed.

**Typical one-time costs**
- Roughly **€50m–€150m+**
- Usually lower than closure because you avoid full decommissioning, remediation, and some transfer costs.

**Timing**
- Usually faster to launch than closure.
- Savings may begin in year 1, but full effect often takes 1–3 years.
- If the agreement is time-limited, the savings may decay unless structural changes are real.

**NPV**
- Often favorable if:
  - industrial conflict is lower,
  - cash preservation matters,
  - the company wants to retain future product optionality,
  - and the site can remain efficient after capacity reduction.
- However, if savings are mostly temporary concessions, NPV can deteriorate materially after the agreement expires.

**Execution risk**
- **Medium**
- Risks:
  - savings erode when concessions roll off,
  - insufficient capacity reduction,
  - hidden overhead remains,
  - management later reopens the debate,
  - labour may resist deep productivity changes.

---

### C. Hybrid sequencing: moratorium phase with backstop

This is the “temporary protection now, structural decision later if targets missed” model.

**Typical annual savings**
- In year 1, lower than closure but potentially material if:
  - voluntary exits,
  - wage restraint,
  - shift trimming,
  - and product/line changes are front-loaded.
- Over 2–3 years, can approach **50%–80% of closure economics** if milestones are achieved.

**Typical one-time costs**
- Lower upfront than closure, because final site-exit costs may be deferred or avoided.
- But there is a risk of **two-stage cost loading**:
  - first-stage restructuring costs,
  - plus later closure costs if the backstop is triggered.

**Timing**
- Useful when the company needs immediate labor peace and cash relief.
- But the economics depend on whether the moratorium is truly time-limited and whether backstop rights are enforceable.

**NPV**
- Can be the **best risk-adjusted option** if:
  - it secures immediate savings,
  - avoids strike-driven output loss,
  - and preserves the option to close later if performance misses.
- Can be the **worst of both worlds** if:
  - it delays decisions,
  - preserves overhead too long,
  - and eventually still requires closure costs.

**Execution risk**
- **Medium to high**, depending on design.
- Main risk is “sequencing without enforcement”: the company gets delay, but not durable economics.

## 3) What matters financially in this case

The decision is not just about headcount. The important question is:

### How much of the site’s cost base is actually removable without closure?

In automotive plants, the hard part is that a meaningful share of cost is:
- fixed or semi-fixed,
- embedded in site-specific infrastructure,
- or linked to legacy guarantees and network logic.

So the practical issue is whether a no-closure package can:
1. remove enough shifts and volume,
2. permanently reduce overhead,
3. lower labor cost enough,
4. and do so with enforceable durability.

If yes, it can be economically close to closure.  
If no, management is correct that the gap remains material.

## 4) Simplified financial illustration

To make the comparison concrete, here is a simple illustrative model for one large German site:

### Assumptions
- Closure annual savings: **€160m**
- Closure one-time cost: **€300m**
- No-closure annual savings: **€115m**
- No-closure one-time cost: **€90m**
- Hybrid annual savings:
  - Year 1: **€70m**
  - Year 2: **€120m**
  - Year 3 onward: depends on outcome
- Discount rate: **10%**
- 3-year horizon for comparison

### Very rough 3-year cash view

#### Closure
- Year 0/1: **-€300m**
- Years 1–3 savings: approximately **€160m/year**
- 3-year gross savings: **€480m**
- Indicative 3-year net before discount: **€180m**

#### No-closure restructuring
- Year 0/1: **-€90m**
- Years 1–3 savings: approximately **€115m/year**
- 3-year gross savings: **€345m**
- 3-year net before discount: **€255m**

#### Hybrid
- Lower early savings, lower early cost
- If it avoids closure altogether, it can outperform closure on NPV
- If it only delays closure, it may underperform both alternatives because it adds delay without fully solving the cost issue

### What this suggests
On a short-to-medium horizon, **no-closure restructuring can outperform closure on cash and NPV** if:
- the one-time closure costs are high,
- the site remains partially useful,
- and disruption from closure would impair output.

On a long horizon, **closure often wins on permanent cost removal** unless the no-closure plan is genuinely structural.

## 5) Site-specific execution risk: what I would flag

Without site-level data, I would classify risk as follows:

### High-risk for closure
- plants with strong union resistance,
- high political visibility,
- co-determination deadlock,
- major supply-chain dependencies,
- or high transfer complexity.

### Moderate-risk for no-closure restructuring
- sites where volume can be reduced by shifts and product mix,
- where voluntary exits can realistically reduce headcount,
- and where support functions can be centralized.

### High-risk for hybrid sequencing
- if the moratorium is too long,
- if the triggers are vague,
- or if the backstop is politically impossible to exercise.

## 6) Financial judgment on the three bridge ideas

### 1. Sequenced restructuring with moratorium
**Financially viable only if**
- the moratorium is short,
- savings are front-loaded,
- and there is a credible fallback.

Otherwise it delays cash benefit and inflates uncertainty.

### 2. Binding “equivalent savings” package
**This is the strongest finance concept** if it is truly measurable.
It converts the dispute from ideology into:
- annual savings,
- one-time costs,
- timing,
- and enforceability.

This is the most likely path to a positive risk-adjusted NPV if the workforce contribution is real and durable.

### 3. Protected-core / adjustable-periphery
Financially acceptable only if “adjustable” means real capacity and cost reduction, not just relabeling.
If it is merely a compromise geography, it will likely leave too much fixed cost in place.

---

## 7) Bottom line

### If management’s objective is:
**maximum permanent cost removal**
- **Closure** is usually strongest.

### If management’s objective is:
**best risk-adjusted net present value with lower execution risk**
- **No-closure restructuring** often has the edge, provided it is deep enough.

### If management’s objective is:
**avoid immediate disruption while preserving a real structural fallback**
- **Hybrid sequencing** is the best negotiation design, but only if milestones are strict and enforceable.

## 8) My neutral recommendation from a restructuring-finance perspective

If I were advising the board purely on economics, I would ask for a **three-way model** with:

1. **Full closure case**
2. **Deep no-closure case**
3. **Hybrid case with milestone triggers**

For each site, the board should compare:
- annual savings
- one-time costs
- timing of cash flows
- probability of execution
- probability-weighted NPV
- impact on network capacity
- disruption risk

My expectation, based on industrial restructuring economics, is:

- **Closure** will show the best structural savings, but not always the best NPV after one-time costs and disruption.
- **A strong no-closure package** can often achieve most of the value at materially lower execution risk.
- **The hybrid package** is only attractive if it truly forces delivery, not if it merely postpones the hard decision.
