I will be direct.

From the family holding’s perspective, the company needs a **structural reset**, not another compromise that preserves the old economics and calls it a turnaround. We support management’s diagnosis that the cost base and capacity are not sustainable. We also support labour peace and stability, because a prolonged conflict would destroy value and weaken the brand. But I will not support any outcome that leaves the company uncompetitive, nor one that turns governance into a mechanism for freezing decline.

## Where I agree

I agree with the following points made by several parties:

- The **status quo is not acceptable**.
- A cosmetic settlement would be value-destructive.
- **Redeployment, qualification, working-time flexibility, and voluntary exits** should be part of the solution.
- The workforce should not carry the burden alone.
- A durable agreement is better than an open-ended conflict.

On those points, we are aligned.

## Where I disagree

I do **not** agree with the attempt by the works council, union, and state shareholder to treat **no closures, no compulsory redundancies, and full preservation of legacy protections** as the default framework.

That is not a restructuring plan. It is a request to keep the economics unchanged while expecting the company to somehow become competitive. It will not work.

I also do **not** support management using plant closures as a rhetorical bludgeon without first showing that the alternative is genuinely insufficient. Closures must not be the first gesture. But they **must remain a real option** if the final package does not deliver the required structural effect.

## The core test

The only test that matters is whether the final package achieves all four of these outcomes:

1. **material capacity reduction**
2. **durable labour-cost improvement**
3. **room to invest in future products and technology**
4. **labour peace and governance stability**

If an alternative to closures can do that, and can do it credibly and durably, then the family holding can support it. If it cannot, then refusing closures in principle is simply refusing reality.

## Response to labour

I hear the insistence on site security and job security. I understand why that is politically and socially attractive. But from an ownership perspective, **permanent guarantees without corresponding economic viability are not stewardship**. They are deferred damage.

What I can support:
- temporary employee contributions,
- voluntary departure programs,
- redeployment and qualification,
- targeted capacity rationalization,
- and a serious future investment commitment.

What I cannot support:
- blanket no-closure commitments through 2030,
- a total ban on compulsory redundancies regardless of circumstance,
- or an agreement that preserves every legacy protection and expects competitiveness to improve by wishful thinking.

If labour wants to avoid the harshest measures, then it has to bring a package that matches the economics. Not slogans. Numbers.

## Response to management

Management is right about the need for a durable reset. But I want to be equally clear: **if you expect governance support, you need to present a coherent restructuring case, not just a severe one**.

That means:
- quantified savings,
- clear sequencing,
- a credible explanation of why specific capacity must come out,
- and a package that is enforceable.

I am not interested in headline-grabbing moves that create conflict without solving the economics. If closures or redundancies are unavoidable, they must be part of a properly structured plan, not a negotiating posture.

## Response to the state shareholder

Your concern for regional stability is legitimate. But it cannot become a veto on economic reality. If the company is forced to protect every site regardless of competitiveness, then it will eventually lose the ability to sustain any of them.

The region is better served by a viable company than by symbolic protection of uncompetitive structures. I would support a negotiated package that preserves industrial capacity **where it is economically defensible**, but not one that makes the company carry permanent excess cost for political comfort.

## What would move us

The family holding could support a settlement if it includes:

- a **credible and durable cost reset**
- **structural capacity reduction**
- a **binding future investment path**
- a **time-limited and fair employee contribution**
- and a **clear mechanism for implementation and enforcement**

If an alternative to closures matches those conditions, we can back it. If not, we will not block necessary structural action just because it is uncomfortable.

## What I reject

I reject:
- a package that protects all sites regardless of economics,
- a settlement that rules out structural options in advance,
- and any arrangement that preserves the company’s decline in the name of social harmony.

That would be a failure of governance and a failure of ownership.

## Bottom line

We are long-term owners. That means we can accept short-term pain if it restores the company’s competitiveness. But we will not accept a “peace” that simply postpones the problem and erodes enterprise value over time.

So my position is this:

- **Yes** to serious restructuring.
- **Yes** to negotiated contributions and future investment.
- **No** to pretending that every German site and every legacy rule can be preserved unchanged.
- **No** to a settlement that does not materially improve margins and competitiveness.

If the next round produces a quantified alternative that truly matches the economic effect of structural measures, I will judge it on its merits. If not, the company must retain the option to act decisively.
