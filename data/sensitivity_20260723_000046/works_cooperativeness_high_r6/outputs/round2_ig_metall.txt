I will be direct.

We agree on the **need for a real restructuring** and on the fact that a cosmetic deal is not enough. We also agree that employees can contribute, and we have already shown that by putting forward a counter-proposal and entering warning strikes once the peace obligation ended.

But I need to correct the way this debate is being framed.

### Where I agree
I agree with:
- the diagnosis that the current cost base and capacity structure are not fit for the competitive environment,
- the need for a durable solution, not a short-term patch,
- redeployment, qualification, working-time flexibility, and voluntary exit options,
- and the principle that **all sides must contribute**.

I also agree that employee contributions can be part of a balanced settlement:
- temporary labour-cost concessions,
- reduced or suspended bonuses,
- working-time measures,
- and possibly a limited, time-bound wage contribution.

That is on the table.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the repeated suggestion that **plant closures and compulsory redundancies remain normal restructuring tools**. For us, they are not. They are the point at which restructuring turns into permanent damage to the workforce and to the company’s industrial substance.

I also do **not** accept a model where employees are asked for “durable” concessions while management and ownership keep their strategic freedom intact and workers carry the main burden. That is not shared sacrifice. That is one-sided adjustment.

And I reject the idea that the collective bargaining framework can be weakened simply because management wants a deeper reset. The wage framework is not a discretionary cost item to be dismantled whenever the balance sheet looks difficult.

### To management
You say closures remain on the table unless we produce an “equivalent” package. Fine — then be precise about what “equivalent” means.

If you want labour to move, then show us:
- the **exact savings target**,
- the **exact capacity reduction target**,
- the time horizon,
- and what management and owners are prepared to give up.

Right now, what I hear is:
- workers should accept pain,
- workers should accept uncertainty,
- workers should accept weakened guarantees,
- and then somehow trust that this will be enough.

That is not a basis for a durable settlement.

If you want the union to help deliver a solution, then the package must include:
1. **No compulsory redundancies**
2. **No plant closures as a bargaining threat**
3. **A binding site-security and investment package**
4. **A fair employee contribution that is temporary and reciprocal**
5. **A management/ownership contribution that is real, not symbolic**

### To the works council
We are aligned on the core line: no forced layoffs, no closures as the default answer, no one-sided dismantling of protections.

Where I want to be careful is this: if we insist on a fully rigid formulation without defining a workable alternative package, we risk being painted as rejecting restructuring altogether. We should not give management that easy argument.

So I support the works council’s red lines, but I want the employee side to keep the door open to:
- targeted capacity adjustments,
- voluntary instruments,
- temporary wage-related contributions,
- and a negotiated industrial plan tied to investment and site security.

### To the state shareholder
Your concern for regional stability is legitimate, and I appreciate that you are not endorsing a blunt closure strategy. But support for the workforce has to be translated into enforceable commitments. “Political concern” is not enough if management can still later impose layoffs through the back door.

If you want labour moderation, then you should support:
- binding employment guarantees,
- limits on involuntary measures,
- and a clear investment commitment for German sites.

### To the family holding and investors
You both keep saying you judge the deal by economics. That is fair. So do we.

But economics cannot just mean “more pressure downward.” A durable solution also requires:
- lower conflict risk,
- labor peace,
- maintained skills,
- and a stable industrial base.

If the final package destroys trust, it may look good on a spreadsheet and fail in implementation. We will not sign up to a settlement that simply shifts the conflict into the future.

### What we can consider
We are prepared to discuss a package with:
- temporary labour-cost contributions,
- reduced bonuses,
- capacity adjustments,
- redeployment and qualification,
- voluntary exits,
- and time-limited working-time solutions,

**if and only if** it is tied to:
- binding no-closure commitments,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- site security,
- and a credible investment plan.

### What we reject
We reject:
- compulsory redundancies,
- plant closures as a negotiating baseline,
- open-ended insecurity,
- and any attempt to make the workforce alone finance the turnaround.

### Bottom line
The union is ready for a serious, fair, and durable settlement.

But the settlement must be based on **shared sacrifice**, not worker sacrifice.  
If management insists on retaining closures and forced layoffs as “real options,” then you should expect the conflict to continue, including further industrial action.

If you want progress, bring a package that proves the burden is being shared by **management, owners, and workforce alike**.
