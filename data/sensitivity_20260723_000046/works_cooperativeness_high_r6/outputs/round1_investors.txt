From the perspective of dispersed institutional investors, the test is straightforward: **does the final package credibly restore competitive margins, or does it merely postpone the problem while locking in a weaker cost base?**

### Where we agree
We agree with management, and partly with the family holding, on the core diagnosis:
- the current cost base is not competitive;
- structural overcapacity has to be addressed;
- a cosmetic settlement would destroy value;
- prolonged uncertainty and strikes would also destroy value.

We also agree that a durable solution should include:
- capacity reduction,
- redeployment and qualification where economically sensible,
- some form of employee contribution,
- and a clear investment plan for future products.

### Where we disagree
We do **not** support a settlement that leaves management without real structural tools. If the outcome simply preserves all German plants, avoids compulsory redundancies in all cases, and protects legacy labour costs almost entirely, then that is not restructuring — it is delay. We would view that as **fundamentally unacceptable** because it leaves the company with an uncompetitive cost base and impaired margins.

At the same time, we do **not** endorse plant closures as a default or performative threat if there is a credible alternative that achieves equivalent savings. Closures should be judged on whether they are economically necessary, not on symbolism or negotiation leverage.

### On the works council and union
Your insistence on no closures and no forced layoffs is understandable from a workforce perspective, but from an investor perspective it is only acceptable **if** you can produce a package that delivers the same financial outcome:
- real and durable savings,
- clear capacity rationalization,
- and measurable improvement in return on sales.

If the proposed alternative is mainly voluntary, temporary, or limited in scope, then it will not be enough. A time-limited contribution that expires before the business is truly fixed does not solve the problem.

### On the regional state
We understand the political and regional concerns, but we cannot support governance being used to protect every site regardless of economics. If political constraints prevent structural action, the result will be continued margin erosion, weaker valuation, and ultimately less capital available for the business.

### On the controlling family
We share the family holding’s long-term value focus. But “long-term” cannot become an excuse for open-ended conflict or a vague promise that future competitiveness will somehow emerge. We need:
- quantified savings,
- timing,
- enforcement,
- and evidence that the measures are durable.

### Our position
We will support **whatever package credibly restores competitiveness**. That means:
- meaningful and durable cost reduction,
- structurally lower capacity if required,
- no purely symbolic compromise,
- and no settlement that leaves returns structurally impaired.

If management and labour can produce an alternative to closures that matches the economics, we can support it. If not, then the company should not rule out hard structural measures simply because they are uncomfortable.

### Bottom line
We are not aligned with any party’s red lines as such. We are aligned with the **financial outcome**:
- competitive margins,
- credible restructuring,
- and a stable path to value creation.

If the eventual deal fails that test, we will treat it as value destructive and will say so publicly through our capital allocation and analyst channels.
