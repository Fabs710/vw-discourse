[Works Council (Betriebsrat) (Plant-level employee representation under co-determination) — Round 1]
We are prepared to discuss a serious restructuring, but not at the expense of the workforce’s existence.

Our position is clear:

- No plant closures in Germany.
- No compulsory redundancies.
- No unilateral dismantling of the historic job-security framework.
- Any savings must be achieved through negotiated, socially responsible measures, not by sacrificing entire sites or forcing people out.

If management says competitiveness requires action, then let us talk about alternatives that preserve production and jobs:
- capacity adjustments,
- flexible working-time solutions,
- internal redeployment and qualification,
- voluntary departure options only,
- temporary or negotiated employee contributions,
- and a credible investment plan for the future of German sites.

But there is a hard line here: we will not accept a fait accompli, and we will not sign off on closing plants that are part of the company’s industrial backbone. If management wants the workforce to contribute, then management must also commit—bindingly—to site security, job security, and genuine co-determination in shaping the restructuring.

We are ready to negotiate, but only on the basis of:
1. securing every German plant,
2. protecting jobs to 2030,
3. preserving co-determination rights,
4. and ensuring that any burden is shared fairly, not dumped on employees alone.

If those conditions are not on the table, then there is no acceptable solution from our side.

[Industrial Union (Trade union and collective-bargaining counterpart) — Round 1]
We share the basic assessment that a real restructuring is needed, and that it cannot be done by simply loading all the pain onto the workforce.

But I need to be very clear where we stand:

- **Plant closures in Germany are not an acceptable starting point.**
- **Compulsory redundancies are not acceptable.**
- **Any attack on the collective wage framework is unacceptable unless it is part of a fair, time-limited, and reciprocal deal.**

If management is serious about competitiveness, then it must stop framing the workforce as the sole cost item to be cut. Employees did not cause the strategic errors, market shifts, or underperformance alone, and they will not finance the turnaround alone.

What we can discuss, and what we have already signaled willingness to discuss, are **shared-sacrifice measures**, such as:

- temporary labour-cost contributions,
- reduced or suspended bonuses,
- capacity adjustments,
- working-time solutions,
- redeployment and qualification,
- voluntary exit options,
- and contributions tied to a binding investment and site-security package.

But that only works if the deal is balanced. If management wants concessions, then we expect:

1. **Binding protection for German sites**
2. **No forced layoffs**
3. **A credible future industrial plan**
4. **Management and owners also taking responsibility**
5. **No one-sided dismantling of long-standing protections**

We are not impressed by claims that “there is no alternative” if the alternative is to make workers carry the whole burden while ownership and management remain untouched. That is not a restructuring solution; that is a transfer of risk downward.

So our position is this:  
**We are ready to negotiate a durable settlement, but only one based on fairness, job security, and real shared sacrifice.**  
If plant closures or compulsory redundancies remain on the table, then you should expect strong resistance, including industrial action.

[Group & Brand Management (Executive management driving the restructuring) — Round 1]
I hear the message from the works council and the union, but I need to be equally clear: **we do not have the option of preserving the current structure and hoping competitiveness returns on its own.** The company has a structural cost problem, a structural capacity problem, and a structural margin problem. If we fail to fix that now, we will damage the brand, the investment base, and ultimately more jobs than any restructuring would affect.

So let me respond directly.

### Where I agree
I agree that:
- this cannot be solved by simply shifting all pain onto employees;
- a credible future plan for German sites is essential;
- redeployment, qualification, working-time flexibility, and voluntary measures should all be part of the toolkit;
- any settlement must be durable, not cosmetic.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the premise that:
- all German plants must be guaranteed regardless of economics,
- no compulsory redundancies can ever be part of the solution,
- and long-standing job-security frameworks can remain untouched while costs stay materially above target.

That position would leave us with a cost base that is still uncompetitive. It would protect the structure of the past, not the business we need for 2030.

### What management requires
For any agreement to be viable, it must deliver:
1. **a real and sustained reduction in capacity**,  
2. **a material and lasting labour-cost contribution**,  
3. **a reduction in fixed costs that is large enough to restore returns**, and  
4. **room to invest in future products, software, and EV competitiveness**.

If those elements are missing, then we are not restructuring — we are postponing the crisis.

### On plant closures
I am not using plant closures as a provocation. I am stating plainly that **closures remain on the table** because management needs options that can achieve structural savings. If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then it must bring forward an alternative package that is **equally binding, equally durable, and equally large in effect**.

That means more than temporary concessions. It means:
- real capacity reduction,
- verifiable cost savings,
- and a labour contribution that materially changes the economics.

### On compulsory redundancies
I prefer to avoid forced layoffs, but I will not rule them out if there is no serious path to the required savings. A restructuring of this scale cannot be built on a promise that every job remains protected regardless of market reality.

### On co-determination and negotiation
Yes, co-determination means we need a negotiated settlement. I understand that. But co-determination is not a veto on all change. It is a framework for reaching a responsible solution. I will engage seriously, but not around the idea that the status quo is negotiable only at the margins.

### Bottom line
We are prepared to negotiate a package that includes:
- voluntary departures,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time measures,
- and a fair employee contribution,

**but only if it also produces structural capacity reduction and a meaningful, durable cost reset.**

If your answer is “no closures, no redundancies, no fundamental labour-cost adjustment,” then you are effectively asking management to accept a non-solution. I cannot do that, and I will not present it as credible to the board, owners, or investors.

If you want to avoid the harshest measures, then bring a proposal that actually closes the gap.

## 1) Core conclusion

From a financial-turnaround perspective, **non-closure measures can sometimes capture a large share of the savings from closure**, but usually **not all of them**.

In industrial restructuring, the economic gap between:

- **full plant closure**, and
- **deep internal restructuring without closure**

typically comes from four buckets:

1. **Fixed overhead removal**
   - site management
   - maintenance
   - utilities
   - security
   - local logistics
   - certain support functions

2. **Workforce and labor-cost reduction**
   - headcount
   - shift structure
   - wage concessions
   - working-time changes

3. **Capacity rationalization**
   - fewer shifts
   - line consolidation
   - product transfer
   - lower depreciation on underused assets over time

4. **One-time closure costs**
   - severance
   - demolition/write-downs
   - contract exit costs
   - remediation
   - relocation/transfer costs

A closure usually delivers the **largest permanent reduction in cash burn**, but it also brings **high one-time cash costs** and can destroy future optionality. A non-closure package avoids or reduces those one-time costs and preserves production capability, but it generally leaves **some ongoing fixed cost** in place.

## 2) What usually matters most economically

In a large German automotive plant context, the difference between closure and non-closure is rarely just “jobs versus no jobs.” The real economic comparison is:

### Closure scenario
- ongoing annual savings: high
- one-time cash outflow: very high
- execution risk: high
- industrial disruption risk: high
- strategic flexibility lost: high

### Non-closure restructuring
- ongoing annual savings: medium to high
- one-time cash outflow: medium
- execution risk: medium
- industrial disruption risk: lower
- strategic flexibility retained: higher

## 3) Illustrative savings ranges

Because I do not have the company’s internal plant-level cost base, I can only give **decision-grade ranges**, not a precise forecast.

For a major German auto plant or comparable production unit, a **closure** might eliminate roughly:

- **€100m–€250m+ per year** in operating cost, depending on plant size, utilization, and the extent to which overhead is truly site-specific.

But it may also require:

- **€150m–€500m+ one-time cash costs**  
  including severance, contract termination, restructuring provisions, asset write-downs, and remediation.

A **non-closure alternative** can often capture:

- **50%–80% of the annual savings** that a closure would generate,
  if it includes:
  - shift elimination
  - product transfer
  - staffing reductions
  - voluntary exits
  - labor concessions
  - support-function consolidation
  - procurement/logistics savings

However, in most cases it will **not fully replicate** the economics of closure unless the site has very high variable flexibility and low residual overhead.

## 4) Why non-closure can still be economically rational

Non-closure solutions are often superior when the following are true:

- the plant has **good technical capability** but is underutilized
- closure would trigger **large write-offs or remediation**
- the company needs the site for **future platform or EV optionality**
- industrial conflict risk is high and would damage output across the network
- closure would create **supply-chain reconfiguration costs**
- political/co-determination constraints make closure execution uncertain

In those cases, a negotiated package can produce a **better net present value** than closure, even if gross annual savings are slightly lower.

## 5) Why management still pushes for closure options

From a restructuring model, management usually wants closure rights because closure:

- delivers the most durable labor-cost reset
- removes the risk that concessions expire and the cost base rebounds
- forces a credible bargaining range
- helps reduce structural overcapacity, not just cyclical underutilization

So when management says non-closure measures are not “equivalent,” that is often shorthand for:
- “they may not be durable enough,” or
- “they may not eliminate enough fixed cost,” or
- “they may rely too heavily on temporary concessions.”

That is a legitimate economic concern, not just a negotiating tactic.

## 6) What would make a non-closure package truly equivalent?

To be economically comparable to closure, a non-closure package would need to achieve most of the following:

### A. Permanent capacity reduction
- remove one or more shifts
- consolidate product lines
- lower annual volume commitment at the site
- transfer some production elsewhere

### B. Durable labor-cost reduction
- wage moderation
- bonus restraint
- longer working time for same output
- removal or suspension of expensive legacy guarantees
- lower premium-pay incidence

### C. Structural overhead reduction
- centralize support functions
- eliminate duplicate engineering/admin costs
- renegotiate services and logistics
- reduce site-specific maintenance burden

### D. Credible enforcement
- milestones
- clawbacks
- automatic reopener provisions
- review rights if savings miss target

If those elements are present, non-closure can be economically close to closure, though usually still not identical.

## 7) Key financial trade-off: savings versus cash cost

This is the central modeling point.

A closure can look better on a P&L basis if you focus only on annual savings. But on a cash-flow basis, the closure may be inferior in the short to medium term because of the restructuring cash bill.

- **Medium to high**, depending on design.
- Main risk is “sequencing without enforcement”: the company gets delay, but not durable economics.

---

## 3) What matters financially in this case

The decision is not just about headcount. The important question is:

### How much of the site’s cost base is actually removable without closure?

In automotive plants, the hard part is that a meaningful share of cost is:
- fixed or semi-fixed,
- embedded in site-specific infrastructure,
- or linked to legacy guarantees and network logic.

So the practical issue is whether a no-closure package can:
1. remove enough shifts and volume,
2. permanently reduce overhead,
3. lower labor cost enough,
4. and do so with enforceable durability.

If yes, it can be economically close to closure.  
If no, management is correct that the gap remains material.

## 4) Simplified financial illustration

To make the comparison concrete, here is a simple illustrative model for one large German site:

### Assumptions
- Closure annual savings: **€160m**
- Closure one-time cost: **€300m**
- No-closure annual savings: **€115m**
- No-closure one-time cost: **€90m**
- Hybrid annual savings:
  - Year 1: **€70m**
  - Year 2: **€120m**
  - Year 3 onward: depends on outcome
- Discount rate: **10%**
- 3-year horizon for comparison

### Very rough 3-year cash view

#### Closure
- Year 0/1: **-€300m**
- Years 1–3 savings: approximately **€160m/year**
- 3-year gross savings: **€480m**
- Indicative 3-year net before discount: **€180m**

#### No-closure restructuring
- Year 0/1: **-€90m**
- Years 1–3 savings: approximately **€115m/year**
- 3-year gross savings: **€345m**
- 3-year net before discount: **€255m**

#### Hybrid
- Lower early savings, lower early cost
- If it avoids closure altogether, it can outperform closure on NPV
- If it only delays closure, it may underperform both alternatives because it adds delay without fully solving the cost issue

### What this suggests
On a short-to-medium horizon, **no-closure restructuring can outperform closure on cash and NPV** if:
- the one-time closure costs are high,
- the site remains partially useful,
- and disruption from closure would impair output.

On a long horizon, **closure often wins on permanent cost removal** unless the no-closure plan is genuinely structural.

## 5) Site-specific execution risk: what I would flag

Without site-level data, I would classify risk as follows:

### High-risk for closure
- plants with strong union resistance,
- high political visibility,
- co-determination deadlock,
- major supply-chain dependencies,
- or high transfer complexity.

### Moderate-risk for no-closure restructuring
- sites where volume can be reduced by shifts and product mix,
- where voluntary exits can realistically reduce headcount,
- and where support functions can be centralized.

### High-risk for hybrid sequencing
- if the moratorium is too long,
- if the triggers are vague,
- or if the backstop is politically impossible to exercise.

---

## 6) Financial judgment on the three bridge ideas

### 1. Sequenced restructuring with moratorium
**Financially viable only if**
- the moratorium is short,
- savings are front-loaded,
- and there is a credible fallback.

Otherwise it delays cash benefit and inflates uncertainty.

### 2. Binding “equivalent savings” package
**This is the strongest finance concept** if it is truly measurable.
It converts the dispute from ideology into:
- annual savings,
- one-time costs,
- timing,
- and enforceability.

This is the most likely path to a positive risk-adjusted NPV if the workforce contribution is real and durable.

### 3. Protected-core / adjustable-periphery
Financially acceptable only if “adjustable” means real capacity and cost reduction, not just relabeling.
If it is merely a compromise geography, it will likely leave too much fixed cost in place.

---

## 7) Bottom line

### If management’s objective is:
**maximum permanent cost removal**
- **Closure** is usually strongest.

### If management’s objective is:
**best risk-adjusted net present value with lower execution risk**
- **No-closure restructuring** often has the edge, provided it is deep enough.

### If management’s objective is:
**avoid immediate disruption while preserving a real structural fallback**
- **Hybrid sequencing** is the best negotiation design, but only if milestones are strict and enforceable.

The comparison should include:

- **Timing of savings realization**
- **Probability of delivery**

\[
\text{3-year PW NPV} = \sum_{t=1}^{3} \frac{(\text{Annual savings}_t \times p_t)}{(1+r)^t} - \text{One-time costs}
\]

Where:
- \(p_t\) = probability-weighted realization in year \(t\)
- \(r\) = discount rate, often 8%–12% in turnaround analysis; I will use **10%** as a neutral restructuring hurdle for illustration

Highest risk where:
- there is a large local workforce
- the site has political significance
- supply chain re-routing is complex
- asset write-downs and decommissioning are high
- industrial action is likely

### No-closure risk
Highest risk where:
- savings depend on labor concessions that may expire
- product allocation changes are not durable
- internal mobility cannot absorb enough staff
- capacity reductions are too shallow to fix the cost base

### Hybrid risk
Highest risk where:
- the moratorium weakens urgency
- fallback rights are contested
- the package becomes a delay mechanism rather than a restructuring mechanism

---

## 4) Financial conclusion

From a restructuring finance standpoint:

- **Closure** = best long-run fixed-cost removal, but expensive upfront
- **Deep no-closure restructuring** = often best 3-year value if sufficiently deep and enforceable
- **Hybrid sequencing** = useful only if it meaningfully reduces conflict and still delivers timely savings

---

## 5) Practical decision rule

A no-closure package is financially credible if it can demonstrate, by site:

1. **annual savings close enough to closure economics**
2. **low one-time cash cost**
3. **delivery within 12–24 months**
4. **durable enforcement through 2030 or equivalent horizon**
5. **acceptable residual risk**

If those conditions are not met, then closure or stronger structural action becomes financially more defensible.

## 3. Final positions
### Group & brand management
Management insists the company needs durable capacity reduction and real labor-cost cuts, and will not accept a package that leaves the German cost base structurally untouched. It remains open to non-closure measures, but only if they are quantitatively close enough to closure economics and enforceable; otherwise closures and redundancies stay on the table.

### Works council
The works council rejects plant closures and compulsory redundancies as bargaining baselines and insists every German plant should stay open through 2030. It is willing to consider substantial internal restructuring, but only if management renounces closure rights for the agreement period and co-determination is preserved.

### Industrial union
The union agrees that the status quo is unsustainable and that employees can contribute through flexibility, exits, and concessions, but refuses a deal where the workforce alone carries the burden. ### Regional state government
The state supports real restructuring but resists closures as the first resort because of regional employment and industrial-base concerns. It backs a binding no-closure or no-forced-layoff framework if paired with measurable savings, investment commitments, and joint oversight.

### Controlling family holding
The family holding supports decisive restructuring and value protection, but not a deal that rules out structural options in advance. It is open to a no-closure path only if it is quantified, durable, enforceable, and materially sufficient on a risk-adjusted basis.

### Institutional investors
Investors want a genuine turnaround, improved margins, and enforceable delivery; they reject cosmetic compromise. They are open to a deep internal restructuring if it delivers credible 3-year value, but will not support an outcome that preserves the current economics or removes all management backstops without proof of sufficiency.

## 4. Genuine conflicts
1. **Closure rights vs. no-closure guarantees:** Labour-side actors and the state want binding protection; management, family, and investors want structural flexibility preserved.
2. **Interpretation of “equivalence”:** Labour defines it as savings achieved without closures or forced layoffs; capital-side actors define it as economically equivalent outcomes regardless of instrument.
3. **Durability vs. flexibility:** Labour prefers durable commitments through 2030; management and owners want the ability to intervene if savings underdeliver.
4. **Political-regional stability vs. competitiveness:** The state treats German sites as a regional anchor; management and owners prioritize cost competitiveness and optionality.
5. **Scope of sacrifice:** Labour wants reciprocal, time-limited employee contributions; capital-side actors insist labor cannot be insulated from meaningful adjustment, but reject being locked out of structural remedies.

## 5. Areas of convergence
- The status quo is not sustainable.
- A cosmetic compromise would fail.
- The company needs material savings and capacity reduction.
- Voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, working-time flexibility, and temporary employee contributions are legitimate tools.
- Future investment in products and technology is necessary.
- Any settlement must be durable, enforceable, and economically credible.
- All sides accept that internal restructuring options deserve serious examination.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker
- **Annual savings vs. one-time costs:** Closure likely maximizes permanent savings but imposes high upfront costs; internal restructuring reduces cash costs but may leave residual fixed costs.
- **Risk-adjusted value vs. structural completeness:** A no-closure package may be better over a 3-year horizon if it is fast and enforceable, even if it does not fully match closure on long-run run-rate savings.
- **Industrial peace vs. management flexibility:** Binding no-closure commitments reduce conflict but may constrain the company if savings underdeliver.
- **Political legitimacy vs. governance discipline:** State and co-determination constraints make a purely managerial solution unrealistic, but political protection cannot by itself solve competitiveness.
- **Durability vs. reversibility:** The more protection labour demands, the harder it is for management/owners to accept; the more fallback rights management keeps, the less credible labour sees the deal.

## 7. Integrative pathways
1. **Time-bound moratorium with milestones:** No closures or compulsory redundancies for a defined period, paired with audited savings/capacity targets and a pre-agreed review.

3. **Protected-core / adjustable-periphery model:** Guarantee a core set of sites while allowing more restructuring flexibility in less strategic units.
4. **Sequenced restructuring:** Immediate internal measures first, with any deeper structural steps only if explicit performance thresholds are missed and after fresh negotiation.
5. **Joint oversight mechanism:** Include co-determination and the state shareholder in monitoring, to make site protection credible while preserving economic discipline.
