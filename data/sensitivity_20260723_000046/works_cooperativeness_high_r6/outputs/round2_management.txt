I will be equally direct.

We are not going to restore this company’s competitiveness by declaring every plant untouchable, every job guaranteed, and every legacy protection permanent while the cost base remains structurally uncompetitive. That is not a turnaround. That is managed decline.

## Where I agree
I do agree on several points raised by labour and the state:

- A **cosmetic deal** would be worthless.
- We need a **durable** solution, not a short-term patch.
- **Redeployment, qualification, working-time flexibility, voluntary exits, and temporary employee contributions** belong on the table.
- The burden must not fall on employees alone; management and ownership also need to carry responsibility for a credible future plan.

On that, we are not in disagreement.

## Where I disagree — and strongly
I do **not** accept the following positions:

- **No plant closures under any circumstances**
- **No compulsory redundancies ever**
- **No fundamental change to the job-security framework**
- **Binding no-closure guarantees through 2030 before any credible cost reset exists**

Those demands would leave the company with a cost structure that is still too high, capacity that is still excessive, and insufficient room to invest in the products and technologies we need for 2030. That is exactly the outcome we cannot accept.

You keep saying we should prove what “equivalent” means. We already have: it means a package that delivers **structural, durable, and material** reductions in cost and capacity. Not temporary comfort. Not accounting tricks. Not concessions that expire before the business is fixed.

## To the works council
You say plant closures and forced layoffs are the point where restructuring becomes destruction. I understand the concern, but I reject the premise that maintaining all German sites is compatible with restoring competitiveness.

If you want to avoid closures, then bring a package that actually achieves the same economics:
- real capacity reduction,
- verifiable fixed-cost reduction,
- a meaningful labour-cost contribution,
- and a structure that lasts beyond the current bargaining cycle.

So far, I have heard red lines, but not an alternative that closes the gap. “Voluntary only” and “temporary contributions” are not enough if the structural problem is permanent.

## To the union
I appreciate that you have acknowledged the need for a real restructuring and that some employee contribution is possible. That is a start.

But I do not accept your attempt to turn “shared sacrifice” into a formula where the workforce contributes only if management gives up all structural options first. That is backwards.

You ask for:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no closures as a bargaining threat,
- site security,
- investment commitments,
- and management/ownership contributions.

We can discuss a package that includes protections and investment, but not if the price is that management gives up the ability to secure the required savings. I will not sign up to a settlement that preserves the framework of the past and leaves the economics unchanged.

## To the state shareholder
I respect the concern for regional stability. But regional stability depends on a viable company, not on freezing the current footprint.

You say closures should not be the default answer. I agree. They are not the default answer. They are a real option if there is no equally effective alternative.

What I cannot accept is the idea that political legitimacy alone can override economic necessity. If the state wants durable jobs, it should support a durable business model — not insist on every site being protected regardless of the economics.

## To the family holding and investors
You are right to focus on long-term value, margins, and investable capacity. That is precisely why I am insisting on structural action now.

But let me be equally clear: I will not pursue a headline-grabbing closure strategy just for signalling. Any closure or capacity decision must be part of a coherent package that materially improves the economics. I am not interested in symbolic cuts; I am interested in durable competitiveness.

## What management is prepared to consider
We are prepared to negotiate a serious package that may include:

- substantial voluntary departures,
- targeted redeployment,
- qualification and transformation measures,
- working-time adjustments,
- temporary employee contributions,
- and investment commitments for future products and technology.

But that package must also include:
- **real capacity reduction**
- **material labour-cost reduction**
- **permanent fixed-cost improvement**
- **a credible path to restore returns**

If it does not deliver that, it is not a solution.

## What we need from the employee side
If you want to avoid the most severe measures, then the alternative must be:

1. **Quantified**
2. **Durable**
3. **Enforceable**
4. **Economically equivalent in effect**

So I am asking plainly: where are the numbers?  
How much capacity comes out?  
How much cost comes out?  
For how long?  
And how is it enforced if the savings do not materialize?

Without that, your position is still a refusal, not a proposal.

## Bottom line
I am not moving off the core requirement:

- the company needs a **structural cost reset**
- the company needs a **structural capacity reduction**
- and the company needs **room to invest in its future**

If the employee side wants to keep every German plant open and rule out forced measures, then it must bring a package that truly matches that requirement. Otherwise, closures and redundancies remain on the table because the alternative is a weak compromise that fails the company.

If helpful, I would next put a concrete management framework on the table — savings targets, capacity targets, and the minimum conditions for avoiding closures — but I will not pretend that the current positions are close. They are not.
