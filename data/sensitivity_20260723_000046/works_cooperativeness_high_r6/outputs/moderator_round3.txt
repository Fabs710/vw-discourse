## Moderator Summary — Round 3

### 1) What has been agreed
There is broad agreement that:
- the current situation is not sustainable;
- a **real restructuring** is necessary;
- a cosmetic compromise would fail;
- voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, working-time flexibility, and temporary employee contributions are legitimate tools;
- future investment in products and technology is required;
- industrial conflict, prolonged uncertainty, and value destruction should be avoided;
- any solution must be **durable, enforceable, and economically credible**.

There is also shared acceptance that:
- the company needs **material savings and capacity adjustment**;
- the discussion should be based on **quantified measures**, not slogans;
- internal restructuring options deserve serious examination.

### 2) Where the core disagreements remain
The main unresolved issues are:

- **Plant closures and compulsory redundancies**
  - Labour representatives and the state shareholder reject them as normal or necessary options.
  - Management, family holding, and investors insist they must remain available if non-closure measures are insufficient.

- **Scope of guarantees**
  - Labour wants binding no-closure / no-forced-redundancy commitments through 2030.
  - Management and capital providers reject unconditional site guarantees.

- **Meaning of “equivalent savings”**
  - Labour defines equivalence as savings achieved without closures or forced layoffs.
  - Management and investors define equivalence by whether the package delivers the required structural economic outcome, even if some sites or jobs are adjusted.

- **Protected-core / adjustable-periphery model**
  - Labour sees risk of selective weakening and hidden erosion.
  - Management and investors see it as a possible way to prioritize protection while still adjusting weaker areas.

- **Role of the state shareholder**
  - Labour and the state want political/regional stability translated into enforceable protection.
  - Management and investors argue regional stability cannot override competitiveness.

### 3) What key trade-offs are still unaddressed
The unresolved trade-offs are:

- **Annual savings vs one-time costs**
  - Closure likely delivers higher permanent savings, but with higher upfront cash costs.
  - Non-closure restructuring likely reduces upfront cost, but may leave residual fixed cost.

- **Durability vs flexibility**
  - Labour prefers durable protections against closure and forced layoffs.
  - Management prefers preserving strategic flexibility if the package underdelivers.

- **Risk reduction vs structural completeness**
  - Non-closure options reduce disruption and conflict risk.
  - Closure may be more structurally complete, but carries higher execution and political risk.

- **Short-term cash relief vs long-term competitiveness**
  - Hybrid or phased solutions may ease immediate pressure.
  - The open question is whether they truly solve the structural cost problem or only defer it.

- **Protection of sites vs selective adjustment**
  - The dispute remains whether all German sites must remain open, or whether some level of differentiated adjustment is acceptable.

### 4) Brief refocus for the next round
The next round should move from principle to **side-by-side quantification**.

A useful next step would be:
- a **site-by-site comparison** of:
  1. full closure,
  2. deep no-closure restructuring,
  3. hybrid sequencing with backstop rights;
- showing for each:
  - annual savings,
  - one-time costs,
  - timing of effects,
  - 3-year NPV impact,
  - and execution risk.

The discussion should then test one narrow question:
**Can the non-closure package achieve a sufficiently durable, enforceable share of closure-level economics to be credible as an alternative?**
