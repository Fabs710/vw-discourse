I will be equally direct.

The employee side keeps repeating “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no plant sacrifices” as if repeating the red lines makes the economic problem disappear. It does not. We all agree the current situation is unsustainable. What we do **not** agree on is whether the company can be reset with only temporary measures and political assurances.

## Where I agree
I agree with the following points raised by the works council, union, state shareholder, and even investors:
- A cosmetic deal would be worthless.
- The status quo is not an option.
- Redeployment, qualification, working-time flexibility, voluntary exits, and temporary contributions are all legitimate tools.
- The solution must be durable and enforceable.
- We need a future investment plan for products and technology.

So no, I am not dismissing labour’s concerns. But agreement on the diagnosis does not mean agreement on the treatment.

## Where I disagree — fundamentally
I do **not** accept that the company can deliver a structural cost reset while:
- keeping every German plant open,
- ruling out compulsory redundancies in all circumstances,
- and preserving legacy guarantees untouched through 2030.

That is not a restructuring package. That is a plea to preserve the old cost structure and hope competitiveness improves anyway.

You all keep saying management has not shown the numbers. I am showing you the logic: if the company’s German footprint remains too large and too expensive, then savings achieved only through temporary concessions will roll off, and the structural problem returns. That is exactly the outcome we cannot afford.

## To the works council
You say plant closures and compulsory redundancies are coercion, not restructuring. I understand the concern, but I reject the conclusion.

The company cannot be expected to carry permanent overcapacity simply because closures are unpleasant. If you want to avoid closures, then bring an alternative that produces **the same durable effect**:
- lower fixed cost,
- lower capacity,
- lower labour cost,
- and a business model that remains viable beyond the current bargaining cycle.

So far, what you have offered is:
- temporary protection,
- voluntary measures,
- and internal shifts.

That may soften the blow, but it does not necessarily solve the economics. And I will not pretend otherwise.

I also reject your claim that a “protected core / adjustable periphery” model is just a hidden closure plan. It is not. It is a way to prioritize what must be secured while allowing necessary adjustment where the business case is weakest. If you refuse any differentiation at all, you are effectively demanding that every site be equally protected regardless of performance. That is not a restructuring strategy.

## To the union
You are right about one thing: shared sacrifice must be real. But then it has to be shared **on all sides**.

What I am not accepting is your demand that management and owners first give up all structural options before labour contributes. That reverses the logic of negotiation.

You also say the collective wage framework must survive intact. I am telling you plainly: it cannot survive intact **if** the company is to regain competitiveness. Some change to labour cost and job-security arrangements is unavoidable. The question is whether it is negotiated and orderly, or forced by crisis later.

You ask for exact savings, exact capacity reduction, exact non-negotiables. Here they are in principle:
- meaningful and durable labour-cost reduction,
- real capacity reduction,
- no purely temporary patch,
- and a package that improves returns enough to fund future investment.

What I will **not** do is promise that no site will ever be touched before we know whether the package works. That would be irresponsible.

## To the state shareholder
Your role as regional stabilizer matters, and I respect that. But regional stability depends on a viable business, not on freezing the current footprint.

If you want to protect the industrial base, then you need to accept that some adjustment may be necessary to save the rest. A refusal to countenance closures or structural reductions in principle may feel protective, but it can also lock in decline.

That said, I do hear your insistence that the company should not simply hollow out its German base. I agree with that in principle. But the way to preserve a strong German base is to make it competitive, not to subsidize inefficiency indefinitely.

## To the family holding and investors
You are right to insist on structural action and value preservation. I agree that a weak compromise would destroy confidence.

But let me also be clear: I will not support a symbolic “hard” strategy that creates confrontation without solving the economics. Any closure or redundancy measure must be part of a coherent package with:
- quantified savings,
- enforceable implementation,
- and a credible path to improved returns.

This is not about headline toughness. It is about durable competitiveness.

## About the bridge packages
### 1) Closure moratorium
A moratorium is only useful if it leads to a credible end-state. If it simply delays the hard decisions while costs keep bleeding, then it is not a solution. I could consider a time-limited moratorium only if it is paired with:
- verifiable savings targets,
- clear capacity reduction,
- and a genuine fallback if the targets are missed.

A moratorium without a credible enforcement mechanism is just a pause before the same problem returns.

### 2) Equivalent savings package
This is the right conversation, but “equivalent” must be measured against the business need, not the comfort level of any one stakeholder.

If the labour side can produce a package that achieves:
- durable savings,
- durable capacity reduction,
- and durable margin improvement,
without closures or forced redundancies, then that is worth evaluating.

But until that package exists in quantified form, closures and redundancies remain legitimate options. Not as a first choice, but as a real one.

### 3) Protected-core / adjustable-periphery
I can work with this concept only if “adjustable periphery” means actual economic adjustment, not cosmetic reshuffling.

If it means:
- line changes,
- shift flexibility,
- product allocation changes,
- redeployment,
- and voluntary instruments,
then yes, that is part of a serious restructuring.

If it means “everything is protected, but management is still expected to meet the same savings target somehow,” then no, that is not credible.

## What would move us
What would move management is not a declaration of opposition. It is a package that meets the conditions we have consistently stated:
- structural and durable cost reduction,
- structural and durable capacity reduction,
- a meaningful labour-cost contribution,
- and room to invest in future products and technology.

If the employee side can bring that without closures, I will engage seriously. If it cannot, then I will not abandon the option of closures and compulsory measures simply because they are unpopular.

## Bottom line
Let me be very clear:

- I agree a cosmetic deal is unacceptable.
- I agree the burden cannot fall on employees alone.
- I agree the company needs future investment.
- I do **not** agree that every German plant can be protected unconditionally.
- I do **not** agree that compulsory redundancies can be ruled out in all circumstances.
- I do **not** agree that legacy protections can remain untouched while costs are reset.

We need a deal that restores competitiveness, not one that delays the reckoning.

If the labour side wants to avoid the most severe measures, then bring a quantified, enforceable package that genuinely matches the economics. Until then, closures and redundancies remain on the table because the company cannot afford a compromise that fails structurally.
