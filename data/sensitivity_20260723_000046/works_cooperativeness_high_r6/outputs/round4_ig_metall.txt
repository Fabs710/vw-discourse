I will be equally direct in round 4.

The latest round confirms two things:
1. **The company needs a real restructuring.**
2. **Management and the owners are still trying to keep closures and compulsory redundancies as a standing bargaining weapon.**

That second point is exactly where I draw the line.

## Where I agree

I agree with the expert and with everyone who says the status quo is not sustainable.  
I also agree that a cosmetic deal would be useless.

And I agree that a serious package can include:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time flexibility,
- shift changes,
- capacity reductions,
- temporary employee contributions,
- and productivity measures.

That is the right terrain for a negotiated solution.

## Where I disagree

I do **not** accept the repeated argument that closures and forced layoffs must remain available in the background in order for restructuring to be “credible.”

That is not credibility. It is pressure.  
And pressure that falls only on employees is not shared sacrifice.

The works council is right on the core point: if labour is being asked to contribute, then the company, owners, and management must also bind themselves. Otherwise the message is simple: employees pay, others keep optionality. That is not fair, and it will not hold.

I also reject the idea that the collective bargaining framework can be treated as a disposable cost block. Employees can contribute. Yes. But the collective wage framework must remain intact as the basis of the settlement. If it is broken open permanently, there is no durable agreement — only a truce before the next conflict.

## On the expert’s assessment

The expert’s numbers matter. They show that non-closure restructuring can often capture **50%–80%** of closure-level savings. That is not trivial. It means management cannot simply claim that closure is the only serious answer.

But the same assessment also says that non-closure restructuring has to be **durable, enforceable, and structural**. I agree.

That means the burden is now on management to show, by site and by measure:
- what savings are needed,
- what savings are delivered,
- over what period,
- and what the management and owners contribute.

Not vague words. Numbers.

## To management

You keep saying you need flexibility because the package might underdeliver. Fine. But your flexibility cannot be unlimited and one-sided.

If you want the union to move, then you need to put on the table:
- exact savings targets,
- exact capacity reduction targets,
- exact duration of concessions,
- and exact management/owner contributions.

What I will **not** accept is a “temporary concession now, closure later if we still feel like it” model. That is not a bridge. It is a trap.

If you want a real bargain, then the company must commit to:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no plant closures,
- binding investment and product commitments,
- and a fair employee contribution that is time-limited and reciprocal.

If you cannot do that, then you are not asking for a negotiated restructuring. You are asking the workforce to finance your freedom of action.

## To the works council

We are aligned on the red lines:
- no closures,
- no forced layoffs,
- no unilateral dismantling of job protections.

I want to sharpen one point. “All German plants stay open” can’t just remain a principle; it has to be paired with a quantified internal restructuring package. That package should specify:
- where capacity comes out,
- how much cost comes out,
- how long concessions last,
- and how the agreement is enforced.

I am fully with you that “review triggers” must not be disguised closure rights. A review can assess performance; it cannot be a backdoor to tearing up protections after workers have already given up ground.

## To the state shareholder

Your role matters only if it becomes enforceable. Political concern is not enough.

If the state wants to protect the industrial base, then it should support:
- binding no-closure commitments,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- clear investment obligations,
- and oversight that cannot be bypassed later.

A regional anchor cannot be defended by vague statements while leaving the workforce exposed to later cuts. If you want to be a stabilizer, then stabilize.

## To the family holding and institutional investors

You both say you care about long-term value. Then support a deal that actually creates it.

Long-term value is not built by destroying trust, weakening the wage framework, and forcing the workforce to absorb all the adjustment. It is also not built by insisting on closure rights as a matter of principle when a serious internal restructuring may achieve most of the economics with lower disruption risk.

The expert was clear: closure has the highest permanent savings, but also high one-time costs and high execution risk. That means closure is not automatically the best answer.

If you want durability and lower conflict risk, support a **quantified, enforceable, no-closure package**. If you refuse that, then you are choosing confrontation over settlement.

## What would move us

What would move the union is not rhetoric about competitiveness. It is a fair package with shared sacrifice and binding protection.

Concretely:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- a time-limited employee contribution,
- management and owner contributions,
- binding investment commitments,
- preservation of the collective wage framework,
- and a real internal restructuring with measurable savings.

That is the only basis on which we can suspend further industrial action and take a settlement seriously.

## Bottom line

We are not refusing restructuring.  
We are refusing a restructuring where the workforce alone pays for the turnaround.

If management and the owners want an agreement, they must stop treating closures and forced layoffs as the default answer and bring a real alternative that protects jobs, sites, and the collective framework.

If they do not, then the union will continue to resist — in bargaining, in the plants, and, if necessary, through industrial action.
