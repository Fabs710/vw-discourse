As the state shareholder, I want to be very clear: I agree that the company needs a serious restructuring. I do **not** agree that the only credible route is plant closures, mass redundancies, and dismantling protections that are part of the company’s stability and regional anchor function.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family holding, and investors that:
- the current cost base is too high,
- the status quo is not sustainable,
- and the company needs a durable turnaround, not a cosmetic fix.

I also agree with labour on a second point:
- the workforce cannot be the only side that contributes.

A negotiated package with:
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- and a time-limited employee contribution

is a legitimate and necessary part of the solution.

## Where I disagree
I strongly disagree with the repeated framing that **closures and compulsory redundancies must remain normal options** unless labour first produces something “equivalent.”

That is too narrow, too aggressive, and too risky. It treats the German industrial base as disposable when, in reality, these sites are part of the company’s long-term value, supply chain resilience, skills base, and regional legitimacy. Once a plant is closed, that capacity and trust are lost.

I also reject the idea that political responsibility can simply be brushed aside because margins are under pressure. The company is not just a financial asset; it is a major regional employer and industrial anchor. That matters for the long term, not just politically, but economically.

## My position on the bridge proposals

### 1) Sequenced restructuring with a closure moratorium phase
A moratorium is better than immediate closure threats, but it is not enough if it is merely a waiting room for later cuts.

If there is to be a sequencing approach, then it must include:
- a real moratorium on plant closures during the agreed period,
- no compulsory redundancies during that period,
- binding investment commitments,
- and a jointly monitored implementation plan.

A “pause” that simply restores closure power at the next review is not a compromise. It is deferred conflict.

### 2) Binding “equivalent savings” package
This is the only direction I am prepared to take seriously, but with an important condition:

“Equivalent” must mean **equivalent savings without resorting to closures or forced layoffs**.

That means management must be prepared to pursue:
- internal capacity reduction,
- product and shift reallocation,
- targeted productivity gains,
- temporary labour-cost contributions,
- and voluntary instruments.

It does **not** mean: “We can call it equivalent if we can still close plants later.” That is not equivalence; that is a disguised closure strategy.

### 3) Protected-core / adjustable-periphery model
I am cautious here.

A “protected core” is only meaningful if it is truly protected. If the “adjustable periphery” means some German sites are quietly made expendable, then I reject the model.

If, however, “adjustable” means:
- shift changes,
- line adjustments,
- redeployment,
- voluntary exits,
- and internal reallocation,

then we are talking about a negotiated restructuring that preserves the company’s base while improving performance. That I can support in principle.

## To management
You say the company needs a structural reset. I accept that. But I do **not** accept that this automatically means closing sites or weakening the job-security framework.

If you want my support, bring me a package that:
- preserves the company’s industrial base in Germany,
- avoids compulsory redundancies,
- protects co-determination,
- and delivers real savings through negotiated internal measures.

Do not ask the state shareholder to bless an outcome that weakens the region and then call it responsible restructuring.

## To the works council and union
I support your refusal to accept closures and forced layoffs as the starting point.

But I also want to be constructive and realistic: “no closures” cannot remain just a slogan. It has to be matched by a package that is specific, quantified, and enforceable.

That means labour should be prepared to discuss:
- temporary concessions,
- internal flexibility,
- and capacity adjustments that do not destroy sites or jobs.

The key point is this: **shared sacrifice must remain shared**. The burden cannot be pushed onto the workforce alone, and it cannot be pushed onto the region through closures that hollow out the company’s home base.

## To the family holding and investors
I understand the long-term value argument. But long-term value is not served by treating the regional base as a variable to be cut away whenever the margin target is missed.

A settlement that destroys trust, triggers industrial conflict, and weakens the company’s roots may look disciplined on paper but be damaging in practice.

## My bottom line
I can support a negotiated restructuring if it includes all of the following:
1. **No plant closures as the first resort**
2. **No compulsory redundancies as the default tool**
3. **A real employee contribution that is temporary and reciprocal**
4. **Binding investment commitments for German sites**
5. **Preservation of co-determination and the state’s blocking role**
6. **A credible industrial plan that protects the company’s regional anchor function**

What I cannot support is a package that normalizes closures, treats forced layoffs as routine, and shifts the company away from its German industrial base.

If management wants the state shareholder’s support, it must put forward a credible alternative that protects jobs and sites while restoring competitiveness. If it cannot, then I will continue to oppose any outcome built on closures and mass layoffs.
