I will be equally clear in round 3.

The union’s position has not changed: **we need a real restructuring, but not one built on plant closures, compulsory redundancies, or one-sided worker sacrifice**. If management and owners want a durable settlement, they have to show that the burden is shared fairly and that jobs, sites, and the collective bargaining framework are not being dismantled as the price of entry.

## Where I agree

I agree with the works council, the state shareholder, and even management on one point: **the status quo is not sustainable**. The current cost base and capacity structure need to change. A cosmetic deal would be a waste of time and would only postpone a bigger conflict.

I also agree that a real solution can include:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary labour-cost contributions,
- and targeted capacity adjustments.

That is exactly why we submitted a counter-proposal and why we have not ruled out employee contributions.

## Where I disagree

I do **not** accept the claim that closures and compulsory redundancies must remain “real options” in the background for negotiations to be credible.

That is not neutrality. It is leverage against the workforce.

If management’s starting point is “accept the threat first, then we talk about alternatives,” then there is no trust, and there is no fair bargaining. The union will not legitimize a process that turns restructuring into a permanent right to discard jobs and sites.

I also do **not** accept any framing that suggests the collective wage framework is simply a cost item to be broken open whenever the company is under pressure. Employees can contribute, yes. But the wage framework itself must survive as a collective standard. If that framework is shredded, there is no durable settlement—only temporary calm before the next round of conflict.

## To management

You keep saying we need a structural cost reset and that closures remain on the table unless we produce something equivalent. Fine. Then be precise.

If you want the union to move, put numbers on the table:
- exact savings required,
- exact capacity reduction required,
- time horizon,
- what management and owners will contribute,
- and which measures are truly non-negotiable.

Right now, what you are offering is a demand for worker concessions combined with preserved management freedom. That is not shared sacrifice.

### What we can consider
We can discuss a package with:
- time-limited wage moderation or bonus restraint,
- working-time measures,
- redeployment and qualification,
- voluntary exit programs,
- and internal capacity reduction.

But only if the package includes:
1. **No compulsory redundancies**
2. **No plant closures**
3. **Binding site and investment commitments**
4. **A fair contribution from management and owners**
5. **Preservation of the collective wage framework**

If those conditions are not met, then the union has no basis to sell concessions to its members.

## To the works council

We are aligned on the core red lines: no closures, no forced layoffs, no unilateral dismantling of protections.

I do want to sharpen one point. It is not enough to say “keep every German plant open” unless the package also explains how the company reaches the needed savings. That is where the union side must stay constructive and specific.

So I support the works council’s line, but I want us to keep pushing for a **quantified internal restructuring package**:
- where capacity comes out,
- how much cost comes out,
- how long concessions last,
- and how the commitments are enforced.

That is the only way to avoid management claiming that labour is simply refusing reality.

## To the state shareholder

Your insistence on regional stability is important. But regional stability must become enforceable, not just political concern. If the state wants to help broker an agreement, it should support:
- binding employment protection,
- binding investment commitments,
- and a governance framework that prevents back-door erosion.

A political promise without enforcement is not enough.

## To the family holding and institutional investors

You both say you care about long-term value. Then you should understand this: long-term value is not created by threatening the workforce until it accepts a bad deal.

A settlement that destroys trust, weakens the wage framework, and triggers repeated industrial conflict may look efficient on paper but will be expensive in implementation and damaging to the company’s industrial base.

If you want durable value, then support a package that:
- avoids industrial confrontation,
- protects skills,
- keeps production anchored,
- and shares sacrifice fairly.

## On the proposed bridge packages

### 1) Sequenced restructuring with a closure moratorium phase
A moratorium is only useful if it is **real protection**, not a waiting room for later cuts.

I could support a time-limited moratorium **only if** it is paired with:
- a binding no-closure commitment for the agreement period,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- a jointly monitored implementation plan,
- and clear investment obligations.

A phase that simply postpones closure threats until the next review is not a solution. It just extends uncertainty.

### 2) Binding “equivalent savings” package
This is the right direction, but only if “equivalent” means **equivalent savings without plant closures or forced layoffs**.

We are willing to discuss:
- temporary employee contributions,
- bonus restraint,
- working-time measures,
- internal capacity reduction,
- voluntary exits,
- and productivity improvements.

What we are not willing to accept is the idea that “equivalent” automatically means some plants must be sacrificed. That is not equivalence; that is a pre-decided outcome.

### 3) Protected-core / adjustable-periphery model
I am cautious here. If “adjustable periphery” means closure logic by another name, we reject it.

If it simply means:
- shift changes,
- line adjustments,
- redeployment,
- internal reallocation,
- and voluntary instruments,
then it is just a restructuring vocabulary we can discuss.

But any model that leaves selected German sites exposed as expendable is unacceptable.

## What would move us

What would move the union is not rhetoric about competitiveness. It is a fair, enforceable package with shared sacrifice.

Concretely, that means:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- time-limited employee contribution,
- management and owner contribution,
- binding investment commitments,
- and protection of the collective wage framework.

If management can table that, then we can work seriously on implementation.

If management cannot, then the union will continue to oppose the restructuring politically and industrially. We have already shown we are prepared to act once the peace obligation has ended.

## Bottom line

We are not refusing restructuring. We are refusing a restructuring where the workforce alone pays for the turnaround.

The company needs a durable solution. So do the employees. But durability requires **fairness, enforceability, and shared sacrifice** — not threats dressed up as economics.

If management wants progress, bring a quantified proposal that protects jobs and sites and shows what management and owners are giving up. Until then, our position remains unchanged.
