I will be equally direct in round 4.

The works council and union continue to talk as if **no closures and no compulsory redundancies** can be the starting point, and as if simply repeating that position makes the economics disappear. It does not. We all agree the company needs a real restructuring. What we do **not** agree on is whether that restructuring can be durable if the German cost base remains structurally too high.

## Where I agree
I agree on several points:
- A cosmetic deal would be worthless.
- The status quo is not sustainable.
- Voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, working-time flexibility, shift changes, and temporary employee contributions are legitimate tools.
- The solution must be durable and enforceable.
- We need future investment in products and technology.

So I am not rejecting all alternatives. I am rejecting **insufficient** alternatives.

## Where I disagree — fundamentally
I do **not** accept that we can restore competitiveness while:
- keeping every German plant open,
- ruling out compulsory redundancies in all circumstances,
- and preserving legacy guarantees essentially untouched through 2030.

That is not a structural reset. That is an attempt to protect the old footprint and hope the cost problem solves itself. It will not.

The expert has already confirmed the basic economics: **non-closure measures can capture a large share of closure savings, but usually not all of them**. That matters. It means the labour side’s position is not enough unless it is backed by a genuinely deep and durable package.

## To the works council
You say closures and forced layoffs are coercion. I say the current cost gap is coercive to the company’s future if it is left unaddressed.

You want a no-closure package. Fine — then make it materially equivalent in economic effect, not just politically preferable. That means:
- real and permanent capacity reduction,
- real labour-cost reduction,
- real overhead reduction,
- and enforceable delivery.

What I do **not** accept is a “no closures” promise with no fallback if the savings underdeliver. That would turn the restructuring into a hope-based exercise. We cannot run a listed industrial group like that.

And I am going to be very clear: a “review” is not a backdoor threat if it is tied to measurable targets and the reality of the business. If the package does not deliver, management must retain the ability to act. That is stewardship, not bad faith.

## To the industrial union
You are right that shared sacrifice must be real. But it must be shared **on all sides**, not just by management and owners giving speeches while the workforce carries the burden.

At the same time, your line still goes too far in treating the collective bargaining framework and job-security arrangements as basically non-touchable. The company’s economics require adjustment there. The question is not whether those arrangements change; it is whether they are changed **negotiated and orderly** or later under much worse conditions.

You keep asking for numbers. Fair enough. The numbers are precisely why I insist on structural measures:
- temporary concessions expire,
- fixed costs remain,
- and the cost base rebounds unless the footprint and the labor model change.

That is the core issue. I will not pretend otherwise.

## To the state shareholder
I respect your regional responsibility. But regional stability depends on a viable business, not on freezing the current footprint.

You argue that plant closures are too risky and too destructive. That may be true in some cases. But it is not a reason to rule them out categorically. Sometimes adjustment is necessary to save the rest of the industrial base.

What I cannot support is a model in which political concern becomes a de facto veto over competitiveness. That would simply postpone the crisis and make the eventual adjustment worse.

## To the family holding and investors
You are right to insist on structural action and long-term value. I agree with that.

But I also reject any idea that “discipline” means using closures as a headline weapon without a quantified comparison. The expert’s framework is the correct one:
- annual savings,
- one-time costs,
- timing,
- execution risk,
- and NPV.

That is exactly how this must be judged. If a no-closure package is genuinely close enough on a risk-adjusted basis, I will consider it. If it is not, then structural options remain necessary.

## What would move management
What would move us is not more rhetoric about no-closure principles. It is a package that is:
- **quantified**
- **durable**
- **enforceable**
- and **sufficient**

Specifically, we need to see by site and by measure:
1. annual savings
2. one-time restructuring costs
3. timing of cash effects
4. how much capacity comes out
5. how much labour cost comes out
6. what happens if savings underperform

If the employee side can produce a package that truly matches the economics without closures or compulsory redundancies, then we will engage seriously. But it has to be real.

## What I reject
I reject:
- unconditional no-closure commitments through 2030,
- unconditional no-compulsory-redundancy commitments,
- legacy protections untouched regardless of economics,
- and “temporary” solutions that simply defer the problem.

I also reject the idea that a delay, a moratorium, or a review cycle is enough if it merely postpones hard decisions while costs remain too high. That is not restructuring. That is delay.

## My bottom line
Let me be unambiguous:

- I agree the company needs a negotiated solution.
- I agree the workforce should not carry the burden alone.
- I agree non-closure alternatives deserve serious evaluation.
- I do **not** agree that all German plants can be protected unconditionally.
- I do **not** agree that compulsory redundancies can be ruled out in all circumstances.
- I do **not** agree that legacy cost and job-security arrangements can remain untouched while we restore competitiveness.

We need a deal that hits the brand’s return-on-sales target, structurally reduces overcapacity and labour costs, and frees funds for future products and technology to 2030. Anything less is not an answer.

If the labour side wants to avoid the more severe structural measures, then bring a quantified, enforceable package that delivers the economics. Otherwise, closures and redundancies remain on the table because I will not sign up to a cosmetic compromise that leaves the company uncompetitive.
