I will be equally direct in round 3.

The core question is still being blurred by management and the owners: they speak about “equivalent economics” as if the only credible route were closures and forced layoffs. That is not a neutral technical claim; it is a choice. And from the works council’s perspective, it is the wrong choice.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on one basic point: **the company needs a real restructuring**. The current situation is not sustainable.

I also agree that a cosmetic compromise would be worthless. If savings, capacity, and future investment are not real, then no one wins — not the company, not the workforce, not the region.

And I agree that there is room for:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time flexibility,
- capacity shifts and line changes,
- and a time-limited employee contribution.

That is the only serious basis for discussion.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the idea that the company must retain plant closures and compulsory redundancies as “real options” in the background in order for negotiations to be credible.

That framing is exactly what destroys trust. It tells the workforce: contribute, but do so under threat. Accept sacrifice, but accept it while management keeps the right to tear up the industrial base. That is not shared sacrifice. It is coercion with better language.

I also do **not** accept the notion that a “protected core / adjustable periphery” model is automatically acceptable. In practice, that can easily become a dressed-up path to selective weakening, followed by a slow erosion of the rest. We have seen enough of these strategies to know that promises of protection can be narrowed later.

## My response to the three bridge packages

### 1) Sequenced restructuring with closure moratorium
A moratorium is better than immediate closure threats, but I am not interested in a temporary pause that merely delays the knife.

If a moratorium is to mean anything, then it must be tied to:
- a **binding no-closure commitment for all German plants through 2030**,
- **no compulsory redundancies through 2030**,
- enforceable investment commitments,
- and a real co-determination role in implementation.

A short moratorium with a later “review trigger” that restores management’s closure power is not a solution. It is an ultimatum in stages.

### 2) Binding “equivalent savings” package
This is the only structure I am prepared to engage with seriously — but only if “equivalent” is defined in a way that does **not** rely on closures or forced layoffs.

If management wants an economically equivalent package, then the test must be:
- quantified savings,
- durable savings,
- and enforceable savings,
achieved through means that preserve jobs and sites.

That means management must be prepared to accept:
- capacity reductions via shift and product allocation changes,
- productivity measures negotiated with the workforce,
- temporary labour-cost contributions,
- and internal reorganization.

What it does **not** mean is: “We will call it equivalent if it gives us the same result through plant sacrifice.” That is not an alternative; that is the same demand in different clothes.

### 3) Protected-core / adjustable-periphery model
I understand why this is being proposed, but I remain skeptical.

For the works council, “protected core” is not enough if it leaves other German sites exposed to closure logic or forced redundancies. We do not bargain by sacrificing some plants so others can be spared. We need a company-wide solution that keeps every German plant open.

So this model is only acceptable if the “adjustable periphery” does **not** mean closures or compulsory redundancies, but rather:
- reallocation,
- job rotation,
- voluntary exits,
- and internal capacity shifts.

If that is all it means, then it is simply a form of internal restructuring — which we are willing to discuss. If it means selected sites become expendable, then we reject it.

## My answer to the moderator’s pointed question
You asked what enforcement mechanism would make a quantified no-closure package credible through 2030.

My answer is clear:

1. **A binding company agreement**
   - no German plant closures,
   - no compulsory redundancies,
   - no unilateral dismantling of job-security protections through 2030.

2. **A binding investment and product commitment**
   - with named volumes, timelines, and site assignments.

3. **A joint oversight mechanism**
   - works council, union, management, and state shareholder involved in monitoring delivery.

4. **Escalation rights if breached**
   - if management breaks the agreement, labour reserves all collective and industrial action rights.

5. **No hidden back doors**
   - no outsourcing, no silent hollowing-out, no transfer of production under another label.

That is what credibility means.

Bring us a package that does **all** of the following:
- keeps every German plant open,
- rules out compulsory redundancies,
- preserves co-determination,
- provides binding investment and product commitments,
- and delivers the required savings through internal, negotiated measures.

If you can do that, then we can discuss how the workforce contributes fairly. But if your package depends on closing sites or threatening jobs, then it is not a restructuring proposal. It is a declaration of war on the workforce.

## To the union
We are aligned on the core line. I support your insistence on no forced layoffs and no closures as the baseline.

Where I would sharpen the message is this: we should not allow management to hide behind “equivalence” unless they first prove they have exhausted non-destructive measures. The burden is not on labour to legitimize closures. The burden is on management to justify why it cannot achieve the needed effect without destroying jobs and sites.

## To the state shareholder
Your role matters if you are prepared to turn regional concern into enforceable protection. We need you to support:
- binding no-closure commitments,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and secure investment for German sites.

Political concern is not enough; we need governance action.

## To the family holding and investors
You keep saying value creation requires structural action. Fine. But value is not created by destroying trust, weakening the workforce, and undermining the company’s home base.

If you want durable value, then support a solution that:
- preserves the industrial base,
- avoids industrial conflict,
- and secures the skills and production capacity needed for the future.

A settlement built on closures and forced layoffs may look hard-nosed. It is actually shortsighted.

## Bottom line
Let me restate the works council’s position plainly:

- **No German plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Preserve co-determination and job-security protections**
- **Willingness to discuss real internal restructuring, temporary employee contribution, redeployment, and qualification**
- **Binding investment and site-security commitments through 2030**

If management and owners can bring a package that meets those conditions, we are ready to work on it. If not, then there is no basis for agreement, and we will continue to resist the restructuring in the supervisory board, in the plants, and publicly.
