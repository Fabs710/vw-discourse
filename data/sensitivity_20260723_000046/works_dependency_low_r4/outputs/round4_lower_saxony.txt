I will respond plainly and from my role as the state’s anchor shareholder.

## First: what I agree with

I agree with everyone on one central point: **the company needs a durable restructuring**.  
Doing nothing is not an option, and cosmetic delay would be irresponsible.

I also agree that:
- numbers matter,
- temporary measures alone are not enough,
- the solution must be executable under co-determination,
- and a prolonged open conflict would damage the company, the workforce, and the region.

So yes: we need a serious, quantified comparison of the alternatives.

## Where I disagree — clearly

### To management
You are right that the status quo is unacceptable.  
But I do **not** accept your repeated attempt to make closures and footprint reduction the default benchmark from the start.

You still have **not demonstrated** that a negotiated non-closure package cannot close the gap in a durable way. Until that proof exists, closures are a **claimed option**, not a justified necessity.

And I want to be explicit: I will not support a process in which the state’s veto role and the workforce’s co-determination are treated as obstacles to be worked around. That is not governance; that is sidelining legitimate stakeholders.

### To the controlling family
You speak of discipline and economic reality, and I do not dismiss that.  
But economic reality also includes:
- conflict costs,
- trust destruction,
- regional damage,
- supplier disruption,
- know-how loss,
- and the long-term cost of weakening the company’s industrial anchor function.

So no: a closure-led solution is **not automatically superior** if it triggers a breakdown in social partnership and regional stability.

### To investors
You are right to focus on value and durability.  
But a solution that looks efficient on paper and blows up politically, industrially, and socially is not risk-adjusted value creation. It is exposed value destruction.

### To the works council and union
I support your rejection of compulsory redundancies and one-sided burden shifting. That remains legitimate and necessary.

But I also need to be honest with you: a flat “no closures, no concessions” line is not enough if nobody can show how the cost gap is closed in a durable way. The company does need structural savings.

That said, I am **not** moving off the core state position:
- no German plant closures as the starting solution,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dilution of co-determination,
- no gutting of regional employment.

Those are not bargaining flourishes. They are the political and economic boundaries I must defend.

---

## On the specialist benchmark

The expert’s benchmark is useful: a non-closure package must be judged on **durable run-rate economics**, not on temporary relief.

I accept that.

I also accept the expert’s practical point that a non-closure package usually needs to be strong to match closure economics. That is precisely why management must now quantify its case properly instead of repeating that closures are “available.”

What I do **not** accept is the leap from:
- “non-closure packages are often weaker”
to
- “therefore closures must remain the pressure instrument.”

That leap is exactly what turns negotiation into coercion.

---

## My position on the three bridge proposals

### 1) Conditional moratorium with independent trigger review
This is the only direction that starts in the right place: **protect jobs and sites first, then test the alternatives honestly**.

But I need to be careful here.

A moratorium is only acceptable if:
- there are **no closures during the moratorium**,
- there are **no compulsory redundancies during the moratorium**,
- and the review is not designed as a hidden countdown to plant rationalization.

If the review is merely a trapdoor back to closures, then it is not a moratorium. It is staged pressure.

### 2) Non-closure first with non-closure contingency measures
This is the more promising route, provided the contingency tools remain **within the non-closure framework**:
- temporary working-time reductions,
- internal redeployment,
- retraining,
- voluntary exits,
- capacity changes through shifts and volumes,
- productivity measures,
- and management/owner contributions.

I can support that direction.

What I will **not** accept is a contingency regime that quietly reintroduces:
- site rationalization,
- partial closures,
- or compulsory redundancies.

That would simply be closure by another name.

### 3) Differentiated site strategy
I understand why this is attractive to management and capital-side stakeholders.

But I have to be clear: I am skeptical if “differentiation” becomes a code word for sorting plants into winners and sacrificial sites. Once you do that, you create a political and industrial logic of expendability. That is destabilizing, and for the state it is unacceptable if it undermines our role as a co-determining anchor shareholder.

I would only consider differentiation if it strengthens the whole domestic footprint and does **not** create a closure roadmap.

---

## What I can support

I can support a serious package built around:
- temporary working-time adjustments,
- internal redeployment,
- retraining and qualification,
- voluntary exit instruments,
- productivity improvements,
- reduced overhead,
- product and volume reallocation,
- temporary or deferred employee contributions,
- management and owner contributions,
- binding investment commitments for German sites.

I can also support a **time-limited, quantified review** if it is genuinely designed to test whether the negotiated package works.

But I will not support:
- a fallback to plant closures,
- a hidden rationalization clause,
- forced layoffs,
- or anything that reduces the state’s blocking role to decoration.

---

## What I need before I can move further

If management wants a serious answer from the state, then bring forward a real comparison, not just assertions:

1. **Non-closure package**
2. **Differentiated-site package**
3. **Partial-closure package**

For each, I want:
- permanent annual savings,
- one-time restructuring costs,
- execution risk under co-determination,
- industrial conflict risk,
- supplier disruption risk,
- and impact on 2030 margin and return on sales.

And I want the comparison to include the **full cost of conflict and trust loss**, not just closure savings.

---

## Bottom line

The state will support a **serious restructuring**.  
We will not support a **dismantling**.

So my position remains:
- no German plant closures as the starting solution,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no sidelining of co-determination,
- no weakening of the state’s veto role,
- and no restructuring that guts regional employment.

If a non-closure package can truly deliver durable competitiveness, that is the path I will support.  
If the only answer is to lean on closures as a threat, then I will oppose that.
