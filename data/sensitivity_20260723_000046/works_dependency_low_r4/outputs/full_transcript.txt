[Works Council (Betriebsrat) (Plant-level employee representation under co-determination) — Round 1]
We will not accept plant closures in Germany and we will not accept compulsory redundancies.

That is the clear position of the Works Council.

We acknowledge that the company is under pressure and that change is necessary. But the burden cannot be placed one-sidedly on the workforce through site closures, broken promises, and forced job cuts. Management has benefited from the skills, loyalty, and flexibility of the workforce for years. Now, when the transition is difficult, the answer cannot be to sacrifice thousands of jobs and destroy industrial capacity in Germany.

Our priorities are clear:

- Every German plant must remain open.
- No operational layoffs.
- Binding job and site guarantees, at least through 2030.
- Any restructuring must be negotiated with co-determination, not imposed.
- Savings must come from alternatives: capacity adjustments, working-time solutions, internal redeployment, training, and a fair employee contribution only if jobs and sites are secured.
- Long-term investment must be committed to German sites, not moved elsewhere.

We are prepared to discuss a sustainable restructuring, but only on the basis of security for the workforce and real co-determination. A plan that starts with closures and forced layoffs is unacceptable to us and will be resisted.

If management wants our support, it must come with credible guarantees, no closures, and no compulsory redundancies.

[Group & Brand Management (Executive management driving the restructuring) — Round 1]
I hear two things clearly from the employee side:

1. You recognize that the company needs a durable restructuring.
2. You reject plant closures and compulsory redundancies as the starting point.

On the first point, we agree. The situation is serious and time is short. The German cost base is too high, our capacity is too large for the market reality, and the brand is not meeting its return target. If we do not fix this structurally now, we will weaken the company’s ability to invest in future products and technology through 2030. That would be irresponsible.

On the second point, I have to be equally clear: **I cannot accept a solution that leaves the cost base and capacity fundamentally unchanged.** A promise of “no closures, no layoffs” sounds stable, but if it only preserves excess capacity and high labour costs, it is not a solution. It postpones the problem and makes the eventual correction more painful.

So let me answer your questions directly.

### 1. Why should employees accept pain while closures remain on the table?
Because the current structure is not sustainable. We are facing a shrinking European market and intense competition, including lower-cost competitors. If German operations remain above target on costs, we will lose room to invest, and that ultimately puts more jobs at risk, not fewer.

I am not saying closures are the first preference. But they are on the table because we need a credible path to a materially lower and durable cost base. If alternatives achieve the same structural result, I am prepared to consider them. What I will not do is sign up to a cosmetic arrangement that protects every site in name while the business continues to drift.

### 2. What is management’s own contribution?
Management is already making the case to investors, owners, and the capital markets that we need to restore competitiveness. That includes taking responsibility for the strategic reset and confronting a failure of the old model. But let me be candid: symbolic management “pain” is not enough.

What matters is whether the package delivers:
- lower structural costs,
- lower structural capacity,
- and a credible path back to margin and investment capability.

That is the real contribution management must make: a plan that actually works and restores the company’s ability to fund its future.

### 3. What binding commitments on future investment and site security are being offered?
I am prepared to discuss binding investment commitments, product allocation, and qualification measures — but only in a package that also secures meaningful savings. Investment without restructuring is not enough. Nor is restructuring without a future industrial base.

If there is a credible alternative that achieves the same savings through working-time flexibility, redeployment, and labor-cost adjustments, then we can discuss site security for the remaining footprint. But site guarantees through 2030 cannot be unconditional if they simply freeze the current structure and prevent necessary change.

### 4. How does this protect the collective bargaining framework?
It protects it by reaching a negotiated settlement that is durable and economically credible. What it does not protect is a framework that becomes disconnected from economic reality. If the workforce contributes, that contribution must be substantial enough to matter. Otherwise we are just buying time.

So here is my position:

- **I am open to alternatives to closures.**
- **I am not open to alternatives that fail to produce real, structural savings.**
- **I am open to a negotiated package.**
- **I am not open to preserving overcapacity and an uncompetitive cost base.**

If the employee side wants to avoid closures and compulsory redundancies, then it has to put something on the table that closes the cost gap in a durable way. That means a genuine labor-cost contribution, capacity reduction, and binding measures that free up funds for the future.

If you want to talk seriously, then bring forward a package that shows:
- how much cost is removed,
- how much capacity is taken out,
- how labor costs are reduced structurally,
- and how quickly the result is delivered.

Without that, we are not solving the crisis. We are just managing it until the next one.

Below is my assessment as an industrial restructuring analyst. I am not taking a stakeholder position; I am comparing restructuring packages on their economic and operational merits.

## Executive view

A **closure-based package** is usually the fastest way to achieve the largest **structural** savings, especially when a company has genuine excess capacity and materially above-target fixed costs. But it carries the highest execution risk in a German automotive context: legal/codetermination constraints, strike risk, talent loss, supplier disruption, and long lead times to realize savings.

A **non-closure package** can sometimes close much of the gap, but only if it combines several measures at scale:
- significant working-time or pay-cost relief,
- major voluntary exits and redeployment,
- meaningful capacity reduction through line rationalization and product reassignment,
- and credible investment/product commitments.

In practice, **non-closure packages usually deliver less and later than closure packages** unless the cost gap is modest or the company can extract unusually strong concessions. If the gap is large and structural, the probability that a pure “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” package fully matches closure economics is low.

## What typically matters in plant-level benchmarking

For a large German carmaker, the relevant question is not “closure vs no closure” in the abstract, but:

1. **How many units of capacity must exit?**
2. **How much fixed-cost reduction is needed at the plant level?**
3. **How much of the labor-cost base can be structurally reset?**
4. **How much value is lost through disruption, downtime, and execution risk?**
5. **How durable are the savings beyond the current bargaining round?**

## Closure-based restructuring: expected effects

### Likely benefits
- **Largest fixed-cost reduction** per site closed.
- **Cleaner capacity reset**: removes overhead, maintenance, and site-specific complexity.
- **Faster medium-term competitiveness improvement** if capacity is truly excess and product allocation can be consolidated.
- **Clearer investor signal** if executed decisively.

### Typical downsides
- **High one-time cash cost**:
  - severance,
  - closure/obligation costs,
  - asset write-downs,
  - remediation and decommissioning,
  - transfer and logistics costs.
- **Long execution timeline** in Germany:
  - works council negotiations,
  - co-determination constraints,
  - possible arbitration/litigation,
  - industrial action,
  - phased ramp-down.
- **Operational disruption**:
  - quality issues during transition,
  - supplier instability,
  - temporary underutilization at remaining plants.
- **Human-capital loss**:
  - departure of experienced operators and engineers,
  - lower morale at retained sites.
- **Regional/political backlash**:
  - pressure on permitting, subsidies, future industrial relations.

### Net assessment
Closure tends to be best when:
- the plant is structurally uncompetitive,
- product pipeline is weak,
- utilization is chronically low,
- and alternative savings are insufficient.

It is less attractive when:
- the site has strategic technology capability,
- closure would create major labor conflict,
- or the company needs multiple sites for resilience and future product optionality.

## Non-closure restructuring: expected effects

### Main instruments
- working-time reductions,
- temporary wage moderation or deferred compensation,
- voluntary exit packages,
- redeployment across plants,
- retraining/reskilling,
- capacity reduction without formal site closure,
- product and shift reallocation,
- procurement and overhead cuts,
- productivity and automation measures.

### Likely benefits
- **Lower immediate social and political cost**.
- **Preserves skills and optionality**.
- **Usually lower one-time cash cost than closure**, though not always lower total cost over time.
- **Less disruption to supply chain and production continuity**.
- **Better labor-relations feasibility** if negotiated with guarantees.

### Main limitations
- **Savings are often partial and slower**.
- **Risk of “hidden overcapacity”** persists if sites remain open with too much labor and overhead.
- **Temporary measures often decay** unless locked in for several years.
- **Can become a deferral mechanism** rather than a genuine reset.
- **May not deliver enough fixed-cost reduction** if the underlying cost gap is large.

### Net assessment
Non-closure restructuring can work when:
- the needed savings are moderate,
- the workforce is willing to trade flexibility for security,
- and management can reallocate volume effectively across the network.

It struggles when:
- multiple plants are underutilized,
- labor cost is materially above benchmark,
- and the company needs permanent capacity removal.

## Comparative benchmark: closure-based vs non-closure packages

### 1) Savings potential
- **Closure-based:** highest probable structural savings.
- **Non-closure:** medium; can be substantial but usually bounded.

Rule of thumb:
- If the company needs a **deep and permanent reduction in fixed cost**, closures or at least site shutdowns/partial shutdowns become increasingly likely.
- If the gap is **single-digit percent of sales or a manageable fraction of labor cost**, a non-closure package may be enough.

### 2) Implementation complexity
- **Closure-based:** very high.
- **Non-closure:** moderate to high, but usually easier than closure.

### 3) Speed to cash
- **Closure-based:** slower cash realization because upfront costs are heavy.
- **Non-closure:** faster to cash if concessions start immediately, but savings may be smaller.

### 4) Durability
- **Closure-based:** more durable once completed.
- **Non-closure:** durability depends on contract length, industrial relations, and whether the company later reverts to old patterns.

### 5) Workforce impact
- **Closure-based:** high job loss concentrated at sites.
- **Non-closure:** lower immediate job loss, but may involve wage moderation, working-time sacrifice, redeployment pressure, and attrition.

### 6) Regional economic impact
- **Closure-based:** materially negative for the affected region.
- **Non-closure:** less severe, but still may reduce local purchasing power if wage or hours concessions are large.

## Practical savings ranges

Without company-specific plant data, only ranges are possible.

### Closure package
A closure package can often achieve:
- **largest permanent fixed-cost reduction**, potentially in the range of a high double-digit share of the affected site’s cost base;
- but with **large one-time charges** that can consume a meaningful part of annual savings in the first 1–3 years.

### Non-closure package
A negotiated alternative without closures can often achieve:
- **material but smaller savings**, commonly through a combination of labor-cost moderation, capacity trimming, and overhead reduction;
- in practice, often enough to cover **part**, sometimes **most**, but not always **all** of the gap versus a closure benchmark.

If management claims a non-closure package is fully equivalent, the burden is to show:
- quantified labor-cost savings,
- permanent capacity exit,
- and low reversion risk.

## Key trade-offs and second-order effects

### Short-term cash vs medium-term competitiveness
- A non-closure deal may improve short-term cash more smoothly, but if it leaves the network bloated, competitiveness remains weak.
- A closure package may hurt near-term cash through restructuring charges, but it can improve medium-term unit economics more decisively.

### Conflict risk
- Closure plans raise strike and veto risk, which can erase expected savings through delay.
- If labor conflict is intense, the “headline” savings can be overstated because implementation drags.

### Hidden costs of “no closure”
- If no plant closes but volumes are still too low, the company can end up with:
  - persistent underabsorption,
  - excess indirect labor,
  - subscale operations,
  - and repeated renegotiation cycles.

### Hidden costs of closure
- If the closed site had process capability or launch expertise, the company may lose operating know-how and flexibility.
- Over-consolidation can create single-point-of-failure risk at remaining plants.

## Regional economic impact

Closure of a German auto plant typically affects:
- direct employment,
- suppliers and logistics firms,
- local consumption,
- municipal tax receipts,
- and apprenticeship pipelines.

A rough multiplier effect in industrial regions can be significant, especially when the site is a major anchor employer. The regional impact is much larger than the direct job count alone because:
- supplier contracts shrink,
- subcontractors absorb volatility,
- and municipal finance weakens.

A non-closure package reduces this shock, but if it relies on wage or hours cuts, the region still feels a contraction—just more gradually and diffusely.

### If the question is:
**“Can a negotiated non-closure package be economically credible?”**
- Yes, **if** the required savings are moderate and the package includes real structural changes.

### If the question is:
**“Can a non-closure package reliably match a closure-based package on savings and durability?”**
- Often **not fully**, unless the company has unusually large room for productivity gains, wage relief, and capacity reallocation.

### If the question is:
**“Which option is lower risk?”**
- In industrial-relations terms, **non-closure** is lower conflict risk.
- In restructuring-completion terms, **closure** is higher certainty once implemented, but only after a risky process.

### Facilitator proposals: possible compromise positions
Below are three concrete bridging options for further negotiation. They are proposals for discussion, not endorsements.

#### 1) Phased restructuring with a time-limited no-closure moratorium
- **For a defined period**: no German plant closures and no compulsory redundancies.
- In exchange, employees accept:
  - temporary working-time reductions,
  - wage moderation or deferral,
  - voluntary exit instruments,
  - redeployment and retraining,
  - capacity reductions through shift and volume changes.
- Management, owners, and state agree to a **joint review milestone** with quantified targets.
- If targets are not met by the review date, the parties reopen discussion on site rationalization.

**Purpose:** gives the employee side immediate protection while giving management a structured path to test whether savings are sufficient without closures.

#### 2) “No closure first” package with a residual fallback mechanism
- Parties jointly design a **non-closure restructuring package** with quantified savings.
- The package is tested against a required savings target.
- If the target is missed, the parties pre-agree that the residual gap will be addressed through:
  - partial site rationalization,
  - line-level consolidation,
  - or other limited footprint adjustments,
  rather than immediate full closures.

**Purpose:** preserves the employee side’s core red line as far as possible, while giving management a fallback if the numbers do not close.

#### 3) Differentiated site strategy
- Not every German site is treated identically.
- Parties classify plants into categories:
  - **core strategic sites**: protected with binding investment and job guarantees;
  - **transition sites**: eligible for capacity reduction, redeployment, or working-time measures;
  - **at-risk sites**: subject to a jointly reviewed rationalization process if economics cannot be repaired.
- In return, the employee side receives stronger guarantees for the core sites, and management receives a path to address the weakest parts of the network.

**Purpose:** breaks the all-or-nothing deadlock by separating strategic protection from economic rationalization.

## Independent restructuring assessment

### Short answer
**Possibly, but only in a narrow set of conditions.**

## 1) Benchmark logic: what “equivalent in substance” means

For a non-closure package to be equivalent to a closure-based package, it must match the closure plan on four dimensions:

1. **Permanent annual run-rate savings**  
   Not just year-1 cash relief. The savings must persist after transition costs.

2. **Structural capacity reduction**  
   If there is genuine overcapacity, you need either:
   - output reallocation to other sites,
   - fewer shifts,
   - lower labor input per vehicle,
   - or some form of permanent footprint shrinkage without closure.

3. **Execution probability under co-determination**  
   A theoretically good plan that cannot pass works council / supervisory-board constraints is not viable.

4. **2030 margin impact**  
   The package must restore competitiveness, not merely defer pain.

A useful practical threshold is this:

- If a non-closure package delivers **at least ~80–90% of the closure plan’s steady-state savings**, with materially lower disruption, it can be considered economically serious.
- If it delivers **well below ~70%**, then it is usually not equivalent in substance unless the company’s gap is actually smaller than management claims.

Those are not legal thresholds; they are restructuring judgment thresholds.

- retraining

### B. Fully non-closure package
Measures may include:
- working-time reductions
- shift cuts
- voluntary exits
- redeployment
- retraining
- wage moderation or deferral
- productivity improvements
- overhead reduction
- product and volume reallocation

**Typical profile**
- **Permanent annual savings:** meaningful, but usually below a closure package
- **One-time costs:** lower than closure-led, but not negligible
- **Execution risk:** lower than closure-led if negotiated well, but risk of underdelivery is real
- **Timing:** faster to start, slower to fully realize if savings depend on behavior change and internal redeployment

**Assessment**
Viable when:
- overcapacity is moderate,
- labor cost is the main issue,
- and the company can obtain durable concessions.

Weak when:
- the gap is primarily fixed-cost / footprint related,
- or management needs a hard structural reset.

---

### C. Differentiated-site package
This is the most common compromise logic in co-determined industrial groups:
- protect core sites,
- concentrate volumes and product assignments,
- reduce shifts or functions at weaker sites,
- avoid immediate explicit closures.

**Typical profile**
- **Permanent annual savings:** better than a pure non-closure package, often materially closer to closure economics
- **One-time costs:** moderate
- **Execution risk:** still significant, but usually more governable than full closures
- **Timing:** can be phased, which helps acceptance and implementation

**Assessment**
In this governance setting, this is often the **most realistic bridge** between labor protection and economic reset.  
It may be the best compromise if full closure is blocked but pure preservation is too weak.

## 3) Job impact

### Fully non-closure package
- lowest immediate involuntary job loss
- more reliance on attrition, voluntary exits, redeployment, and reduced hours
- preserves more know-how and supplier stability
- but may leave excess labor embedded if demand does not recover

### Closure-led package
- highest direct job loss
- most severe regional and supplier disruption
- strongest structural reset
- highest industrial-relations cost

### Differentiated-site package
- job losses are moderated versus closure
- but employment is redistributed rather than fully preserved
- often the most governable compromise in Germany

## 4) Margin and value impact

### Closure-led
Best long-run margin effect if executed cleanly.

### Non-closure
Can look better in the short term because it avoids large one-time charges, but it often underdelivers on fixed-cost reset.

### Differentiated-site
Usually the best balance of:
- durable savings,
- lower conflict risk,
- and practical deliverability.

### Key value point
A weak non-closure package can be worse than a hard closure plan if it:
- preserves overcapacity,
- delays decisions,
- and keeps labor conflict alive.

This is decisive here.

- high strike risk
- high political resistance
- high delay / dilution risk

### Non-closure
- more likely to be approved
- lower immediate conflict risk

### Differentiated-site
- often the best compromise between:
  - economic seriousness,
  - industrial deliverability,
  - and governance feasibility

In Germany, **expected value** matters more than theoretical savings.  
A plan with nominally higher savings but low deliverability may be worse than a slightly weaker plan that can actually be implemented.

## 1. Decision context

## 4. Genuine conflicts (structurally incompatible positions)

1. **All German plants remain open vs. footprint reduction remains available**  
   Employee/state actors treat closures as illegitimate; management, family, and investors treat them as a necessary fallback. These are not merely different preferences; they are mutually exclusive decision rules.

2. **No compulsory redundancies vs. structural cost reset**  
   The workforce side sees forced layoffs as an unacceptable transfer of crisis costs; management and capital-side actors see labor reduction as a possible component of structural reset.

3. **No hidden closure fallback vs. credible economic discipline**  
   Employee/state parties demand that closures be removed from the test period; management and owners believe any test without a closure backstop is economically distorted.

4. **Co-determination as protection vs. co-determination as constraint**  
   Employee/state stakeholders view co-determination as a legitimacy requirement; management and capital-side stakeholders see it as a deliverability risk that any acceptable plan must overcome.

- Most parties accept that a package must be judged on **durable run-rate economics**, not just short-term relief.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker

1. **Economic certainty vs. social and governance feasibility**  
   Closure-led plans may produce stronger structural savings but face severe veto, strike, and implementation risk.

2. **Immediate conflict avoidance vs. long-term competitiveness**  
   A no-closure deal reduces near-term escalation but may underdeliver on structural reset if concessions are too shallow.

3. **All-sites preservation vs. differentiated adjustment**  
   Preserving every site maximizes labor and political acceptance but may leave overcapacity intact; differentiated treatment is more realistic but politically fragile.

4. **Binding guarantees vs. management flexibility**  
   Strong site/job guarantees improve trust but can freeze restructuring options and limit responsiveness if the economics worsen.

5. **Speed vs. legitimacy**  
   Fast decisions favor management’s preferred clarity; legitimate and executable decisions require more bargaining, benchmarking, and independent verification.
