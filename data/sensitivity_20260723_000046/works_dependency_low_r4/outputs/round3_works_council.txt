I will respond directly.

## First: the red lines remain unchanged

Let me be absolutely clear to management, owners, investors, and anyone still hoping this can be blurred out:

- **No German plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No dismantling of co-determination**
- **No tearing up of the job-security framework**
- **No “residual fallback” that normalizes closure as an acceptable endgame**

If the proposal requires any of those, then it is not a compromise. It is a managed defeat of the workforce.

## Where I agree

I agree on three points:

### 1) The company needs a durable restructuring
Yes. Inaction is not an option.

### 2) Cosmetic solutions are useless
Also yes. We do not want a fake deal that just postpones the next crisis.

### 3) Numbers matter
Finally, some truth. We need quantified scenarios, not slogans.

But the conclusion management wants to draw from that is wrong: **numbers do not automatically justify closures or layoffs**. They have to prove that no other route can close the gap while protecting sites and jobs. That proof has not been delivered.

---

## Where I disagree

### To management
You keep saying the structure must change and the company needs a hard reset. Fine. But your answer is always the same: closures, layoffs, wage pressure, and weakening guarantees.

That is not a restructuring plan. That is a transfer of the crisis onto workers.

You claim the numbers may force rationalization. Then show the full numbers:

- site-by-site cost structure,
- plant utilization,
- product allocation,
- overhead by location,
- one-time closure costs,
- conflict and strike costs,
- supplier disruption,
- knowledge loss,
- and the medium-term risk of destroying future capacity.

Do not come here with a one-sided model that treats closure as free and labour as the only variable.

### To the controlling family
You talk about “economic equivalence” and “governance discipline.” But you are not entitled to define equivalence as whatever preserves shareholder flexibility while hollowing out the industrial base.

If you insist on calling closures a fallback, then you are already moving the goalposts. The fallback becomes the threat, and the threat becomes leverage against the workforce.

We reject that logic.

### To investors
You say you only support the best risk-adjusted outcome. Good. Then understand this: a forced closure fight, broken trust, and the destruction of job security is not risk-adjusted value creation. It is value destruction dressed up as discipline.

If you want durable margins, you need a workforce that can plan, invest, and commit. That does not come from fear and coerced exits.

### To the state
You are right that this is not a spreadsheet only. But I want more than words about social partnership. If the state truly rejects a bad restructuring, then it must say plainly that **German plant closures are not the default solution and not the price of political convenience**.

## On the facilitator’s three compromise options

### 1) Time-limited no-closure moratorium
This is the only direction that begins from a defensible basis: protect sites and jobs first, then test alternatives.

But I will be explicit: a moratorium is not a concession from us. It is the minimum serious starting point.

However, I do **not** accept any clause that says if targets are missed, closure discussions automatically reopen. That creates a loaded gun on the table from day one and makes every employee contribution hostage to future closure threats.

If management wants a review, fine. But the review must compare:
- achieved savings,
- management and owner contributions,
- operational improvements,
- and the cost of conflict.

It must **not** become a pretext to reintroduce closures.

### 2) “No closure first” package with a residual fallback mechanism
No. I reject this.

A residual fallback to site rationalization is just closure by another name. It means the employee side pays upfront and management keeps the right to come back for the sites later. That is not acceptable.

We are prepared to discuss a **full non-closure package**, not a trapdoor.

### 3) Differentiated site strategy
This is also problematic if it is used to create “protected” and “at-risk” plants. The moment you label sites as at risk, you undermine confidence everywhere and invite strategic blackmail.

If the company has real capacity issues, then the answer is not to sort plants into sacrifice zones. The answer is to use negotiated adjustments across the network while preserving all German sites.

## What we can discuss

We are prepared to discuss a serious package with the following elements:

- temporary working-time reductions
- internal redeployment
- retraining and qualification
- voluntary exit instruments only
- capacity adjustments through shifts, lines, and product allocation
- deferred or limited labour-cost contributions
- investment commitments for German plants
- productivity improvements
- management and owner contributions

But only under binding conditions:

1. **Every German plant remains open**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Employment security through 2030**
4. **Co-determination is preserved**
5. **No closure trigger, no fallback closure mechanism, no hidden exit clause**

If those conditions are not accepted, then there is no deal.

---

## On “how much savings is enough?”

That is the wrong question if the starting point is closures.

The right question is:

**What is the full gap, and what can be closed through negotiated measures without destroying sites or jobs?**

If management says the gap cannot be closed without closure, then management must prove:
- that every non-closure measure at scale has been exhausted,
- that the social and industrial costs of closure are lower than the costs of negotiated adjustment,
- and that the company’s long-term capability is not damaged by destroying domestic capacity.

I do not believe that case has been made. Not remotely.

---

## What I would require before any discussion could continue

If the other side wants a serious negotiation instead of a staged ultimatum, then bring forward:

- a transparent plant-by-plant benchmark,
- a real non-closure scenario,
- quantified savings from:
  - working-time measures,
  - voluntary exits,
  - productivity gains,
  - product and volume reallocation,
  - overhead reduction,
  - owner contribution,
- and a binding statement that **no German site will be closed**.

Without that, all this “structured compromise” language is just window dressing for closure planning.

---

## Bottom line

We are not here to optimize the terms of plant closure.

We are here to prevent it.

So let me summarize the Works Council’s position in one sentence:

**We will support a durable restructuring only if it keeps every German plant open, rules out compulsory redundancies, preserves co-determination, and secures jobs through 2030.**

Anything else is unacceptable, and if needed we will use every tool available — from co-determination rights to industrial action — to stop it.

If management, owners, or investors want to test whether a non-closure package can work, then they must come back with a fully quantified plan that respects those boundaries. Not a fallback mechanism. Not a disguised closure roadmap. A real alternative.
