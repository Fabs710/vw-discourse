I will be direct.

I agree with one central point from management: **the current structure is not acceptable if it leaves the company permanently uncompetitive.** If German operations remain structurally too expensive and overcapacity is simply preserved, then we are not stewarding the business responsibly. That would erode long-term value for everyone, including the workforce and the regional economy.

I also agree with the state and the union on one important condition: **a settlement that is durable, negotiated, and stable is far better than an open-ended conflict.** Prolonged industrial confrontation would destroy value quickly and would be a failure of governance on all sides.

But I do **not** agree that the only credible route is to put plant closures and large-scale compulsory layoffs at the center of the process.

### To management
You are right to insist on real structural improvement. But you have not yet shown that closures are the necessary first step rather than a leverage point.

If you want the backing of the controlling owner, you need to prove three things:
- that the savings are genuinely durable,
- that the path does not permanently damage the industrial franchise,
- and that the proposal is executable under co-determination and the special governance constraints.

At this stage, I do **not** see enough evidence that “closures first” is the only way to achieve the necessary cost reset. If there are negotiated alternatives that deliver comparable structural savings, then a closure-led approach is not the right one.

### To the Works Council and the union
I understand the refusal of compulsory redundancies and the resistance to closures. That is a legitimate position. But I will also be frank: **“no closures, no layoffs” is not a sufficient economic answer by itself.**

If every plant must remain open unchanged, then the burden simply reappears elsewhere — in margins, investment capacity, and eventually in a weaker company. That is not acceptable either.

So the workforce side needs to engage on measures that are real, not symbolic:
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exit instruments,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- and a genuine labor-cost contribution.

If those measures can produce a credible improvement, then they are preferable to closures. But they must be large enough to matter.

### To the regional state government
I appreciate your insistence that this is not just a financial exercise. That is correct. The company is a major industrial anchor and the region has a legitimate interest in preserving capacity and skilled employment.

But I also need to say this clearly: **the state cannot expect the owners to carry permanent value destruction in the name of social stability.** If the alternatives do not close the gap, then protecting every site as a matter of principle would simply defer the crisis.

I support your call for transparent comparison of options. That comparison should be real, numerical, and binding. If alternatives can deliver the same economics, then the case for closures weakens materially. If they cannot, then the company cannot be forced to remain structurally uncompetitive.

### My position
The controlling family holding will support a settlement that:
- restores competitiveness and margins,
- protects the company’s long-term industrial value,
- preserves labour peace,
- and respects the governance framework.

We will **not** support:
- a deal that freezes overcapacity,
- a deal that permanently impairs enterprise value,
- or a deal that weakens ownership control and governance discipline.

So the test is simple:

Show us a package that delivers:
1. a credible, durable cost reduction,
2. a real capacity adjustment,
3. a meaningful workforce contribution,
4. and investment/security for the remaining footprint.

If those conditions are met through alternatives to closures, then I am open to them. If they are not met, then I will not pretend that symbolism is a strategy.

In short: **I am open to avoiding closures, but only if the alternative is economically equivalent.** Otherwise, we should not kid ourselves about the consequences.
