## To the Works Council and Union
I agree on one point: a cosmetic deal is worthless. If the package merely postpones the problem, it will fail the company and everyone dependent on it.

I also agree that a prolonged conflict would destroy value. That is precisely why I do **not** want a theatrical confrontation. But I do **not** accept your position that the only serious solution is to rule out plant closures and compulsory redundancies in advance.

That is not a restructuring framework. It is a veto on the hard part of restructuring.

You keep saying management must prove closures are necessary. Fair enough. But the burden is not one-sided. You also have to show that a non-closure package can close the cost gap **in full**, permanently, and under the governance constraints we actually have. So far, I have not seen that proof.

## To Management
I support the goal of restoring competitiveness and margins. That part is non-negotiable from my perspective.

But I want to be equally clear: if you want my support, I need more than a general argument that the company is too expensive. I need a **quantified, executable plan** that shows:
- the size of the structural cost gap,
- the permanent savings from each measure,
- the one-time costs,
- the execution risk,
- and the effect on 2030 margins.

If closures are truly required, then I will consider them. If a negotiated alternative can genuinely match the economics, then that is preferable. But I will not support an arrangement that preserves the full footprint while leaving the company structurally weak.

## To the Regional State Government
I respect your role and I agree that the state is not a decorative stakeholder. But I must push back on your framing.

You cannot insist that closures are never acceptable while also asking the company to remain competitive. Those two positions are in tension. If the numbers show that site rationalization is needed, then the state cannot simply block reality.

At the same time, I do agree that a bad restructuring would be worse than no restructuring. A plan that destroys trust, triggers long conflict, and weakens the industrial base would be value-destructive. So yes: social cost matters. But it does not override economic survival.

## On the analyst’s assessment
The expert was useful. The key point I take from it is simple:
- closure-led restructuring is stronger on permanent savings,
- non-closure restructuring is lower-conflict but often weaker on durability and scale.

That matches my concern. A non-closure plan is only acceptable if it is truly equivalent in substance. If it is merely politically comfortable, it is not good enough.

## My position
I will support a solution only if it meets all of the following:
1. **Durable restoration of competitiveness**
2. **Material improvement in margins**
3. **No permanent impairment of the enterprise’s value**
4. **Execution under the actual co-determination and governance constraints**
5. **Industrial peace, or at least no open-ended conflict**

That means I am open to a negotiated alternative **only if it is genuinely economically equivalent**. Otherwise, I will not rule out plant closures or footprint rationalization simply because they are unpopular.
