I will be equally direct.

The state shares the diagnosis that the company needs a durable restructuring. I do **not** dispute that. Nor do I dispute that the current cost structure has to come down. But I do **dispute** the repeated framing that plant closures in Germany are the necessary benchmark for seriousness. They are not. They are the most damaging option, and in a company of this importance they must remain a true last resort, not the starting point.

## Where I agree

- **With management:** the status quo is not acceptable. A cosmetic arrangement that leaves overcapacity and an uncompetitive cost base in place will fail.
- **With the union and works council:** this cannot be a one-sided burden shift onto the workforce.
- **With the controlling family and investors:** a prolonged conflict would destroy value, weaken the company, and damage everyone’s interests.

So yes, we need numbers. We need a package that is economically credible, durable, and executable.

## Where I disagree

### 1) “No closures means no serious restructuring”
That is simply not proven.

Management keeps asserting that closures may be necessary, but I have not yet seen a convincing demonstration that negotiated alternatives cannot close the gap. If there is a real comparison to be made, then make it openly:
- cost savings,
- timing,
- execution risk,
- social costs,
- and long-term industrial consequences.

Until then, closures are an asserted preference, not a demonstrated necessity.

### 2) “Economically equivalent” cannot mean socially destructive
To the investors and the controlling family: I hear your focus on value and competitiveness. That is legitimate. But equivalence is not just a spreadsheet question. A plan that preserves shareholder value by gutting regional employment and destroying industrial trust is not a stable solution. It creates conflict risk, political backlash, and long-term damage to the company’s anchor role.

### 3) “No guarantees” is not acceptable
To management: you are asking the workforce to make material contributions, but you remain vague on what binding security you will provide in return. That is not good enough.

If employees are expected to accept working-time changes, flexibility, wage moderation, or voluntary exits, then the package must include:
- binding site security,
- binding investment commitments,
- protection of co-determination,
- and a credible commitment to avoid compulsory redundancies.

## The state’s position

Our red lines are clear:

- **No German plant closures as the default solution**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No sidelining of the state’s co-determination and veto role**
- **No restructuring that guts regional employment**

That does not mean “do nothing.” It means: restructure through negotiated, reversible, socially responsible measures first.

## What I can support

I am prepared to support a negotiated package that includes:
- temporary or structural working-time reductions,
- internal redeployment,
- retraining and qualification,
- voluntary exit tools,
- capacity reductions,
- and a fair, limited employee contribution,

**if and only if** it is matched by:
- binding investment commitments for German sites,
- real management/owner contributions,
- site and job security,
- and a clear plan to keep the company as an industrial anchor in Germany.

## What I cannot support

I will not support:
- a deal that treats closures as the easy answer,
- a plan that uses the crisis to weaken labour’s governance role,
- or a restructuring that shifts the whole burden onto workers and the region while preserving ownership and management comfort.

## On the numbers

I agree with the moderator: management should put forward two fully quantified scenarios:
1. closure-based restructuring,
2. negotiated alternatives without closures.

But I want the comparison to include not just nominal savings, but also:
- loss of know-how,
- conflict and strike risk,
- regional economic damage,
- transition costs,
- and the impact on future investment capability.

That is the real test of durability.

## My bottom line

The state is **not** blocking restructuring. We are blocking a **bad** restructuring.

If management and ownership want support, they need to show that the company can recover competitiveness **without** sacrificing German plants and without breaking the social partnership model that has sustained this company for decades.

If they can present a serious alternative that achieves the same economic effect while preserving sites, jobs, and co-determination, then we are ready to work with it.

If not, then I will not pretend that closures are inevitable just because they are convenient for some on the capital side.
