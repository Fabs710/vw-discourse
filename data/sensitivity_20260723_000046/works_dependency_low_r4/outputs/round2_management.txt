We are now at the point where slogans have to give way to hard choices.

I agree with one thing the employee side says: a cosmetic deal is worthless. A package that merely delays the problem and leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive is not acceptable. On that point, we are aligned.

Where I **do not** agree is on the claim that “no closures, no layoffs, no guarantees limits” can be the starting and ending point of the discussion. That would not be a restructuring. It would be a freeze. And a freeze is exactly what this company cannot afford.

## To the Works Council
You say every German plant must remain open, no compulsory redundancies, and binding guarantees through 2030.

I understand the political line. I also understand the industrial relations line. But I will say plainly: **I cannot accept a solution that preserves every site regardless of economics**.

Why? Because the core problem is not just temporary pressure. We have a **structural** cost and capacity problem:
- German factory costs are well above target.
- European demand is shrinking.
- Competition is intensifying.
- The brand is missing its return target.

If we leave all sites intact and simply shuffle hours, redeploy people, and promise investment, then we have not solved overcapacity. We have only redistributed it.

You ask us to prove why the same result cannot be achieved without closures. That is fair — and we will do that in numbers. But let me be equally clear: if the numbers show that we do need plant rationalization, then refusing to discuss closures is not a solution. It is a veto on reality.

## To the Union
You ask for exact cost gaps, management contribution, and binding guarantees. Fine.

The exact gap is this: **the business must get to a cost base that supports durable margins and investment capacity to 2030**. That requires more than a temporary wage pause or limited wage moderation. It requires a **material structural contribution** from the workforce and a **real reduction in capacity**.

I am willing to discuss:
- working-time measures,
- voluntary exit tools,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- and wage-related contributions,

but only if they are large enough to change the economics, not just soften them.

What I am **not** willing to accept is a deal where employees contribute a little, management is asked for symbolic pain, and then we are expected to declare victory while the company remains uncompetitive. That would be irresponsible.

## To the Regional State Government
You are right that this is not just a spreadsheet. But it is also not a regional protection program.

I agree that closures should not be treated casually. They are a serious step and not our first preference. But they must remain on the table because the alternative has to be **economically equivalent** — not emotionally preferable.

If you want us to compare options transparently, we will. But transparency cuts both ways:
- show me how the no-closure package closes the cost gap in full,
- show me how it is durable beyond one bargaining round,
- show me how it avoids simply shifting the burden into margins and future underinvestment.

If those answers are not credible, then a no-closure stance is not responsible governance. It is wishful thinking.

## To the Controlling Family
You are right on the central point: a prolonged conflict would destroy value, and the company cannot carry permanent underperformance.

Where I want to be precise is this: **ownership discipline does not mean preserving every existing site at any cost**. It means making sure the company survives as a competitive industrial business.

If the workforce side brings forward a package that truly delivers:
- materially lower labour costs,
- capacity reduction,
- and a durable competitive structure,

then I am open to avoiding closures. But it has to be real. Not aspirational. Not temporary. Not reversible at the first sign of pressure.

## To the Investor Side
You are correct that the market will not reward rhetoric. It will reward execution and margin improvement.

That is exactly why I am pressing for a deep restructuring now. Capital markets are not interested in a settlement that preserves the old structure under a new label. They want:
- a lower fixed-cost base,
- fewer structural overhangs,
- and credible investment funding for the next product cycle.

If the final result does not move those variables enough, then it is not a solution investors can stand behind.

## What management is prepared to discuss
Let me be specific.

We are prepared to discuss a package built around:
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- working-time adjustments,
- internal redeployment where it actually fits,
- retraining and qualification,
- voluntary exit instruments,
- and a genuine labour-cost contribution.

We are also prepared to discuss:
- investment commitments for remaining sites,
- product allocation,
- and a phased implementation path.

But I need to be equally direct about the limits:

- **No unconditional guarantee that every German plant stays open**
- **No acceptance of a structure that leaves overcapacity untouched**
- **No deal that avoids structural labor-cost relief**
- **No settlement that fails to restore competitiveness**

## The test
So here is the test for the next round:

If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then it must present a package that closes the cost gap **in full**, not partially.

That means:
1. quantified capacity reduction,
2. quantified structural labor-cost savings,
3. credible timing,
4. execution certainty,
5. and a footprint that is competitive to 2030.

If that can be done without closures, then I am prepared to consider it seriously.

If it cannot, then we will not pretend that “no closures” is an industrial strategy.

I will not sign off on a settlement that protects every structure except the company’s future.
