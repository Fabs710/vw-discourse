Below is a **domain-only restructuring assessment** of the question raised: **closure-led restructuring versus negotiated non-closure alternatives** for a large German automotive manufacturer under **German co-determination, works-council, and collective-bargaining constraints**.

## Executive assessment

On the facts given, a **closure-led package** is likely to deliver the **highest permanent structural savings** and the cleanest capacity reset, but it also has the **highest execution risk** and the **largest one-time cash cost** in Germany.

A **negotiated non-closure package** can be economically credible only if it includes several measures at scale:
- capacity reduction,
- significant working-time and cost moderation,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment and retraining,
- and binding multi-year labor-cost relief.

If the underlying gap is large and structural, a non-closure package is **unlikely to fully match** closure economics unless the workforce accepts unusually deep concessions and management can reallocate volume very effectively. In many automotive cases, the result is **partial gap closure** rather than full equivalence.

## 1) Comparative economics

### A. Closure-led package

**Permanent savings**
- Typically the **largest** and most durable.
- Removes site overhead, indirect labor, maintenance, and underabsorbed fixed cost.
- Best when a plant is structurally uncompetitive or underutilized.

**One-time costs**
- High.
- Severance, transfer/bridge costs, decommissioning, asset impairments, remediation, logistics, and possible contractual exit costs.
- In a German auto context, one-time charges can be substantial enough to offset a meaningful portion of year-1 to year-3 savings.

**Timing**
- Usually **slower to realize than management wants**, because the process in Germany is not just operational but institutional:
  - co-determination,
  - works-council negotiations,
  - potential arbitration/litigation,
  - strike risk,
  - and phased ramp-down.
- The closure decision may be “permanent” once executed, but the path to execution is often long.

**Execution risk**
- High.
- Risk of industrial action, supervisory-board blockage, political pressure, supplier disruption, and talent loss.
- The biggest risk is not only legal delay; it is **implementation drag** that can erode the expected NPV.

**Value impact**
- Long-term value can improve materially if the company truly has excess capacity and the closed site is structurally weak.
- Near-term value often suffers because restructuring charges and disruption are front-loaded.

### B. Negotiated non-closure package

**Permanent savings**
- Usually **moderate**, not maximal.
- Can be substantial if it combines:
  - pay moderation/working-time changes,
  - voluntary exits,
  - internal redeployment,
  - product allocation,
  - and permanent capacity reduction without formal plant closure.
- But savings are often bounded by the need to keep sites open and employment protected.

**One-time costs**
- Usually lower than closure, though not trivial.
- Costs include voluntary exit incentives, retraining, relocation, temporary productivity loss, and possible wage-top-up or compensation mechanisms.

**Timing**
- Often faster to start generating savings if agreement is reached quickly.
- However, savings may come in **steps** and may be temporary if tied to a bargaining period rather than permanently embedded.

**Execution risk**
- Lower industrial-relations risk than closure if the deal is genuinely negotiated.
- But the risk shifts to **underdelivery**: the package may be too small, too temporary, or too reversible.

**Value impact**
- Better near-term stability.
- Better preservation of know-how and operational continuity.
- But if the package does not fully reset the cost base, the company may need another restructuring later, which reduces value.

## 2) Practical benchmark ranges

Without site-level numbers, only ranges are possible.

### Closure package
A closure-led package can typically generate:
- **high double-digit percentage savings on the affected site’s fixed cost base** over time,
- but with a meaningful up-front cost burden.

In other words, closures are the **strongest tool for structural reset**, not necessarily the cheapest in year 1.

### Non-closure package
A negotiated alternative can often deliver:
- a **material but smaller share** of the closure benchmark,
- especially if the company can extract multi-year wage moderation, hours flexibility, and capacity trimming.

As a practical restructuring rule:
- If the required gap is **moderate**, a non-closure package may close it.
- If the required gap is **large and structural**, a non-closure package often closes **part** of it, but not all.

## 3) German co-determination and bargaining constraints

These constraints matter materially for feasibility:

- **Supervisory board / co-determination** means plant closures are not simply an executive decision.
- **Works councils** can significantly slow or shape implementation.
- **Collective bargaining peace ending** raises strike risk.
- **Blocking positions** held by labor, the state, and controlling shareholders make “clean” closure decisions harder than in a pure shareholder-driven model.

This means the economic “best” solution on paper may not be the one that is executable. Under these constraints:
- closure economics are often **impaired by friction**;
- non-closure economics are often **impaired by insufficiency**.

That is the core trade-off.

## 4) What a credible non-closure package would need to contain

If management wants a non-closure alternative to be credible, it must be quantified in the following way:

1. **Permanent capacity reduction**
   - Not just temporary underutilization.
   - Specific lines, shifts, or volumes must come out.

2. **Structural labor-cost relief**
   - Multi-year, not one-off.
   - Working-time, wage moderation, deferred compensation, or guarantee adjustments.

3. **Voluntary exits at scale**
   - Enough to reduce indirect and direct labor materially.
   - Pure attrition is usually too slow.

4. **Redeployment and retraining**
   - To avoid operational bottlenecks and skill loss.

5. **Investment/product commitments**
   - To keep remaining plants viable and to justify workforce concessions.

6. **Durability**
   - Savings must last beyond the bargaining cycle.

If these elements are not present in sufficient volume, the package is not economically equivalent to closure; it is a delay mechanism.

## 5) Likely outcome under the facts given

Based on the scenario as described:
- Management believes the cost gap is **structural**, not cyclical.
- Employee-side parties insist on **no closures, no compulsory redundancies, and site/job guarantees**.
- Ownership and investors want **economically equivalent savings**.

That combination usually means:

- A **pure no-closure, no-layoff package** is unlikely to deliver the full economic reset management is seeking.
- A **hybrid package** is more plausible:
  - no immediate broad closure,
  - but some combination of selective site rationalization, capacity cuts, deep working-time/labor-cost concessions, and voluntary exits.
- If the business gap is large, the probability rises that some form of **partial closure or deep footprint rationalization** becomes necessary eventually.

## 6) Value comparison: what matters most

From a restructuring value perspective, the key dimensions are:

### Closure-led
- Higher permanent savings
- Higher one-time cost
- Higher conflict risk
- Higher execution certainty only after a difficult process
- Better if the network truly has excess sites

### Non-closure
- Lower one-time cost
- Lower conflict risk
- Faster to negotiate if trust exists
- Lower permanent savings
- Higher risk of underfixing the structural problem

So the value question is not “which is nicer,” but:
**which package closes the cost gap on a durable basis after accounting for delay, friction, and underdelivery risk?**

## 7) My judgment

### If the gap is large
A closure-led or partial-closure package is more likely to deliver the needed reset.

### If the gap is moderate
A negotiated non-closure package may be sufficient, especially if it includes:
- permanent hours reduction,
- meaningful wage moderation,
- high voluntary exit volumes,
- and real footprint simplification.

### If management needs full equivalence
A non-closure package is only credible if it can show, in numbers:
- the same permanent savings,
- the same capacity reduction,
- and similar durability.

That is difficult in practice under the stated constraints.
