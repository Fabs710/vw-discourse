I will be equally direct.

## To the Works Council
I hear your red lines. I also understand why you are drawing them. But I do **not** accept them as the basis for a viable restructuring.

A package that insists on:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no closure fallback,
- and job security through 2030 regardless of economics

does not solve the structural problem. It postpones it.

You keep asking us to prove closures are necessary. Fair enough. But you are still not offering a non-closure package that closes the cost gap in a durable way. “Trust us, we’ll find savings elsewhere” is not enough when the company’s cost base is demonstrably uncompetitive.

I agree with you on one point: cosmetic deals are worthless. That is exactly why I will not sign up to a solution that protects every site at any price.

## To the Union
I agree that the workforce should not carry the whole burden. I also agree that a durable settlement must include voluntary tools, retraining, and redeployment.

Where I strongly disagree is your attempt to redefine closures and compulsory redundancies as illegitimate by default. They are not ideological taboos. They are restructuring instruments. If the network is overbuilt and structurally too expensive, then footprint reduction is a legitimate option.

You say a fair test requires closures to be removed during the test period. I reject that. If closure is off the table from the start, the test is rigged. We would be forced to accept a result that may preserve jobs today but leave the company uncompetitive tomorrow.

## To the Regional State Government
I appreciate that you want to protect industrial employment and regional stability. I do not dismiss that.

But I must be blunt: your position cannot be “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” while also expecting the company to restore competitiveness, fund future products, and hit margin targets. Those objectives conflict unless there is a material cost reset.

If you want to support a negotiated solution, then help secure one that is economically real:
- capacity reduction,
- labour-cost moderation,
- productivity gains,
- and, if required, footprint rationalization.

A political veto on every hard restructuring choice is not stewardship. It is avoidance.

## To the Controlling Family
You are right about one thing: the company cannot survive on rhetoric. But you are too quick to treat every alternative to closures as weakness.

I will support a negotiated path if it is **equivalent in substance**, meaning it delivers:
- durable annual savings,
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- lower fixed costs,
- and a credible margin reset by 2030.

If it does not, then we are simply moving the problem around. And I will not pretend that preserving the appearance of social peace is the same as restoring competitiveness.

## To the Institutional Investors
I agree with your central point: what matters is credible economics, not ideology. That is exactly my position too.

Where I differ from the employee side is this: a package that keeps every plant open regardless of economics is **not** risk-adjusted value creation. It is wishful thinking with a high probability of future underperformance.

I am open to a non-closure package only if it is materially equivalent in substance to a closure-led plan. And that means the numbers have to close — not just politically, but operationally.

## On the expert benchmark
The expert’s point is useful: a non-closure package must deliver most of the run-rate savings, reduce overcapacity, and be executable under co-determination.

That is the right standard.

What I reject is the way the Works Council is trying to use that standard to say, “therefore closures are never acceptable.” No. The benchmark cuts both ways. It shows that if the non-closure package is materially weaker, then we are not obliged to accept it.

## On the bridge proposals

### 1) Conditional moratorium
I will only consider a moratorium if it is:
- time-limited,
- tied to hard savings milestones,
- and accompanied by an honest benchmark against a footprint-reduction alternative.

What I will **not** accept is a moratorium that becomes a permanent shelter from hard decisions.

### 2) Non-closure first with non-closure contingencies
This is closer to something discussable, but only if the contingencies are real and economically meaningful. If the package underdelivers, the company must retain options to address the residual gap. If the answer is “no site rationalization ever,” then the package is not credible.

### 3) Differentiated site strategy
This is the most realistic route. Not every site has the same economics or strategic role. A differentiated strategy allows us to:
- protect the strongest sites,
- adjust weaker ones,
- reduce duplication,
- and improve the overall footprint.

That is not “sacrifice logic.” It is industrial reality.

## What I require
If the employee side wants me to abandon closures as an active option, then bring me a package with all of the following:
1. permanent annual savings at or near the closure-led benchmark,
2. real capacity reduction,
3. material labour-cost contribution,
4. management and owner contribution,
5. execution certainty under co-determination,
6. and a credible 2030 margin trajectory.

If you cannot deliver that, then you are not offering a solution. You are asking the company to accept permanent structural weakness.

## Bottom line
I will not retreat from the core need:
- **lower structural cost base**
- **less overcapacity**
- **durable margin improvement**
- **freed investment capacity to 2030**

I am open on the method, not on the outcome.

If a non-closure package can genuinely achieve the economics, I will consider it.
If it cannot, then the refusal to contemplate closures or footprint rationalization is not a principled stand — it is a barrier to survival.
