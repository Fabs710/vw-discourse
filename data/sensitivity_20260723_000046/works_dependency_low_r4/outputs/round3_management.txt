I will be direct.

## To the Works Council
I agree with you on one point: a cosmetic deal is useless. A package that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive would fail the company and fail everyone. On that, we are aligned.

But I **do not** accept your red lines as stated:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no meaningful restructuring risk,
- and job security through 2030 regardless of economics.

That is not a restructuring. That is a freeze. And a freeze is not compatible with restoring competitiveness.

You keep saying management has not proved closures are necessary. Fair enough — but you have also not shown that a non-closure package can close the gap in full. Until that is demonstrated, “no closures” is not a solution; it is a refusal to confront the scale of the problem.

I am prepared to discuss:
- working-time changes,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- voluntary exits,
- temporary or structural labour-cost relief,
- and capacity reductions.

But I will not sign up to a framework that rules out plant rationalization in advance. If the numbers require footprint reduction, then that has to be on the table.

## To the Union
I agree that the workforce should not carry the burden alone. I also agree that a settlement must be durable and not just a one-off wage cut.

Where I strongly disagree is your claim that closures are merely a bargaining threat and should not be a legitimate fallback. They are a legitimate restructuring tool when the network is overbuilt and the cost base is too high.

You ask for the exact cost gap. The exact point is this: we need a structure that supports durable margins and investment to 2030. That means a **material** reduction in fixed costs, not just temporary flexibility. If the package only softens the problem, it fails.

I am willing to discuss a package that includes:
- labour-cost contributions,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- and productivity measures,

but only if it produces a real reset, not a delay.

## To the Regional State Government
I respect your role and I agree that conflict would be damaging. I also agree that social and regional consequences matter.

But I cannot accept the argument that because closures are painful, they should effectively be ruled out. That would place politics above industrial reality. The state cannot demand competitiveness and at the same time block the instruments needed to achieve it.

A transparent comparison is exactly what we need. But it has to be a real comparison:
- permanent savings,
- timing,
- execution risk,
- and value impact.

If a non-closure package closes the gap in full, I will prefer it. If it does not, then insisting on no closures is not responsible governance.

## To the Controlling Family
I agree with you on discipline: the company cannot survive on rhetoric and permanent underperformance. Where I differ is on the implied idea that we can somehow preserve the whole domestic footprint and still reset the economics.

We need a package that improves margins durably. That may mean:
- reducing capacity,
- consolidating functions,
- and yes, potentially closing plants or parts of plants.

I am open to avoiding closures **if** the alternative is genuinely equivalent in substance. But not if it is simply a way to preserve every site while the company continues to bleed cost.

## To the Institutional Investors
You are right to focus on risk-adjusted value and not ideology. I agree with that.

What I do not accept is the suggestion that the only credible answer is either full closure or an unworkable no-closure promise. There is a middle ground, but it must be economically real:
- substantial labour-cost contribution,
- structural capacity reduction,
- and an executable plan with binding commitments.

However, I will not back a solution that leaves the company with too much capacity, too much fixed cost, and too little room to invest. That is not value creation. That is deferred decline.

## On the compromise options
### 1) Time-limited no-closure moratorium
This can be discussed only if it is **time-limited, conditional, and tied to hard savings**.

I am not prepared to accept an open-ended moratorium that simply postpones the hard decision. If we give time, then the package must deliver measurable reductions in cost and capacity during that period.

### 2) No-closure first with a residual fallback
This is closer to reality than the employee side admits, but I will be frank: if there is no consequence for missing the target, then it is not credible. If the package underdelivers, the company must retain the ability to address the residual gap, including site rationalization. Otherwise we are just gambling with the company’s future.

### 3) Differentiated site strategy
This is the most sensible of the three. Not every site has the same economics, product profile, or strategic role. A differentiated approach is exactly what a serious restructuring requires.

That means:
- some sites protected because they are strategic and competitive,
- some sites adjusted through capacity reduction and redeployment,
- and the weakest parts of the network addressed directly.

## What I need to see
If the employee side wants to argue for no closures, then bring a package that shows, in numbers:
1. permanent annual savings,
2. capacity reduction,
3. labour-cost contribution,
4. execution certainty,
5. and durability through 2030.

If that package is materially equivalent to a closure-led package, I will consider it.

If it is not, then I will not pretend that sentiment can replace economics.

## Bottom line
I am not here to negotiate away the company’s competitiveness.

I am prepared to be flexible on the **method**:
- closures,
- partial closures,
- capacity reductions,
- working-time changes,
- voluntary exits,
- labour-cost contributions.

But I am **not** flexible on the **outcome**:
- the company must have a lower structural cost base,
- less overcapacity,
- and a credible path back to margin and investment strength by 2030.

If the alternative package can genuinely achieve that, then it has a chance.
If it cannot, then the refusal to contemplate closures is not a principled stand — it is an obstacle to survival.
