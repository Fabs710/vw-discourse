## 1. Decision context

The company needs a durable restructuring because its German operations are structurally too expensive and overcapacity is widely acknowledged. The live decision is whether that reset must include plant closures and compulsory redundancies, or whether a negotiated non-closure package can deliver enough permanent savings while preserving the domestic footprint. Governance makes this unusually constrained: co-determination, family control, and a state blocking position all create effective vetoes, so legitimacy and executable consensus matter as much as economics.

## 2. Stakeholder assembly

- **Group & Brand Management**: operational lead for the restructuring; high standing due to agenda-setting power and access to business data.
- **Works Council**: plant-level employee representation and half of the supervisory board; very high standing because of formal veto power and legitimacy.
- **Industrial Union**: wage-bargaining counterpart and strike authority; very high standing due to industrial action capacity and bargaining legitimacy.
- **Regional State Government**: anchor shareholder and political actor; very high standing because of formal stake, board presence, and veto role.
- **Controlling Family Holding**: majority voting control through governance; very high standing as ultimate controlling owner.
- **Institutional Investors**: dispersed capital-market holders with less direct control; moderate standing, mainly through valuation pressure.
- **Independent Restructuring Specialist**: advisory voice only; low formal standing but useful for benchmark setting.

## 3. Final positions

### Group & Brand Management
Management insists the company must achieve a durable structural cost reset and meaningful capacity reduction. It is open to non-closure solutions only if they are economically equivalent in substance to a closure-led plan, and it wants footprint reduction to remain available if alternatives underdeliver.

### Works Council
The Works Council rejects German plant closures, compulsory redundancies, and any hidden fallback to closures. It is willing to discuss working-time measures, redeployment, retraining, voluntary exits, and temporary employee contributions, but only if all German sites remain open and co-determination is preserved.

### Industrial Union
The union shares the Works Council’s rejection of forced layoffs and closure pressure as the default benchmark. It supports serious quantified bargaining, but only within a framework of no compulsory redundancies, no closures, protected collective standards, and durable job security.

### Regional State Government
The state supports restructuring in principle but opposes plant closures as the starting route and rejects any dilution of co-determination or its own blocking role. It is prepared to back negotiated adjustment measures, but only if industrial employment, regional stability, and social partnership are protected.

### Controlling Family Holding
The controlling family agrees that inaction is not acceptable and that real economics must govern the outcome. It will support a negotiated non-closure package only if it proves durable savings and capacity reduction; otherwise it wants footprint adjustment to remain on the table.

### Institutional Investors
Investors want a credible, value-creating restructuring and are skeptical of anything that preserves every site regardless of economics. They will support non-closure only if it delivers durable savings, a real margin reset, and convincing execution; otherwise they favor footprint reduction.

### Independent Restructuring Specialist
The specialist judges that a fully non-closure package can work only in a narrow band of cases where the gap is moderate and employee, management, and owner contributions are all deep. In general, a differentiated-site or partial-closure package is more likely than a pure no-closure deal to match closure economics in this governance setting.

## 4. Genuine conflicts (structurally incompatible positions)

1. **All German plants remain open vs. footprint reduction remains available**  
   Employee/state actors treat closures as illegitimate; management, family, and investors treat them as a necessary fallback. These are not merely different preferences; they are mutually exclusive decision rules.

2. **No compulsory redundancies vs. structural cost reset**  
   The workforce side sees forced layoffs as an unacceptable transfer of crisis costs; management and capital-side actors see labor reduction as a possible component of structural reset.

3. **No hidden closure fallback vs. credible economic discipline**  
   Employee/state parties demand that closures be removed from the test period; management and owners believe any test without a closure backstop is economically distorted.

4. **Co-determination as protection vs. co-determination as constraint**  
   Employee/state stakeholders view co-determination as a legitimacy requirement; management and capital-side stakeholders see it as a deliverability risk that any acceptable plan must overcome.

## 5. Areas of convergence

- All parties accept that **doing nothing is not an option**.
- All parties reject **cosmetic or temporary fixes** as sufficient.
- There is broad support for a **quantified, side-by-side comparison** of restructuring scenarios.
- All parties acknowledge that **industrial conflict, execution risk, and political backlash** matter, not just spreadsheet savings.
- There is shared interest in **voluntary exits, retraining, redeployment, capacity adjustments, and productivity improvements**.
- Most parties accept that a package must be judged on **durable run-rate economics**, not just short-term relief.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker

1. **Economic certainty vs. social and governance feasibility**  
   Closure-led plans may produce stronger structural savings but face severe veto, strike, and implementation risk.

2. **Immediate conflict avoidance vs. long-term competitiveness**  
   A no-closure deal reduces near-term escalation but may underdeliver on structural reset if concessions are too shallow.

3. **All-sites preservation vs. differentiated adjustment**  
   Preserving every site maximizes labor and political acceptance but may leave overcapacity intact; differentiated treatment is more realistic but politically fragile.

4. **Binding guarantees vs. management flexibility**  
   Strong site/job guarantees improve trust but can freeze restructuring options and limit responsiveness if the economics worsen.

5. **Speed vs. legitimacy**  
   Fast decisions favor management’s preferred clarity; legitimate and executable decisions require more bargaining, benchmarking, and independent verification.

## 7. Integrative pathways

### Pathway A: Time-limited non-closure test
A defined period with no closures and no compulsory redundancies, during which a fully quantified non-closure package is implemented and independently reviewed. If targets are met, the package continues; if not, the parties reopen negotiation under a predefined escalation protocol.

### Pathway B: Differentiated-site restructuring
All German sites remain formally open, but sites are categorized by strategic role and receive different volumes, shifts, investment, and productivity measures. This allows real capacity concentration without an explicit closure decision.

### Pathway C: Non-closure first, with non-closure contingencies
The primary package excludes closures and forced layoffs, but includes additional labor flexibility, voluntary exits, redeployment, and owner/management contributions as contingencies if targets are missed. This keeps the solution within the domestic footprint while preserving a response mechanism.

### Pathway D: Independent full-option comparison
An external specialist produces a plant-by-plant comparison of non-closure, differentiated-site, and partial-closure scenarios using the same metrics: permanent savings, one-time cost, execution risk, and 2030 margin impact. This can create a common factual base before final bargaining.

## 8. Open questions

- What is the **actual structural savings gap** that must be closed?
- How much of that gap is **fixed-cost/footprint driven** versus **labor-cost driven**?
- What permanent **labor contribution** is the workforce prepared to make?
- What **management and owner contributions** are credible and material?
- Which sites, if any, are **strategic cores** versus operationally adjustable?
- What level of **execution risk under co-determination** makes a closure-led option inferior in expected value?
- Can a non-closure package reach the specialist’s suggested **80–90% equivalence threshold**?
- What exact conditions would trigger a **second-stage renegotiation** without becoming a de facto closure threat?

## 9. Recommended next step

Commission a fully costed, plant-by-plant scenario pack comparing:
1. non-closure,
2. differentiated-site restructuring,
3. partial-closure / footprint reduction.

Use one shared metric set: permanent annual savings, one-time costs, payback period, job impact, strike/veto risk, and 2030 margin effect. Keep the next round focused on quantified thresholds, not principles, and require each stakeholder to state what minimum package would make them move.

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction

1. **The real dispute is over the legitimacy of closure as a negotiation instrument, not over whether restructuring is needed.**  
   This became clear only after repeated rounds: all sides accept the need for change, but disagree on whether closures may remain live during bargaining.

2. **The sides are trapped in mirrored proof burdens.**  
   Employee/state actors demand proof that closures are necessary; management/capital actors demand proof that non-closure can fully close the gap. The interaction revealed that neither side will move first without a shared benchmark.

3. **“No closure fallback” is not just a red line; it is a trust test.**  
   For employees and the state, any hidden fallback would turn a review into coercion. For management and owners, removing the fallback too early looks like a rigged test. That asymmetry is central.

4. **A pure all-sites-open solution is possible only in a narrow economic band.**  
   The specialist’s assessment introduced a substantive threshold: non-closure works only if the gap is moderate and contributions are durable. This sharply narrowed the plausible compromise space.

5. **Differentiated-site restructuring emerged as the most realistic bridge.**  
   It was not the initial starting point, but repeated discussion showed it may be the only way to preserve co-determination legitimacy while achieving meaningful structural savings.

6. **Execution risk under German governance may matter as much as nominal savings.**  
   The interaction repeatedly surfaced that a theoretically stronger closure plan may be inferior in expected value if it cannot survive vetoes, strikes, or political resistance.

7. **The bargaining moved from moral claims to benchmark governance.**  
   By the end, the decisive issue is not rhetoric about fairness or discipline, but which scenario can survive formal governance constraints while still restoring competitiveness.
