I will be direct.

## To management
I agree with you on one point: the status quo is not acceptable, and cosmetic arrangements will fail. The company needs a durable restructuring.

I also agree that we need numbers, not rhetoric.

Where I disagree is your continued insistence that closures and the threat of rationalization must remain the decisive benchmark. You still have not shown that the same structural effect cannot be achieved through a negotiated package that protects German sites, jobs, and co-determination. Until that proof is there, closures remain a **claimed option**, not a demonstrated necessity.

And I want to be very clear: I will not support a restructuring that turns the state’s blocking role into a formality while real decisions are effectively pre-cooked. That is not acceptable.

## To the Works Council and union
I support your rejection of compulsory redundancies and one-sided burden shifting. That is legitimate, and it reflects the reality of this company as a social partnership enterprise.

But I also need to be straight with you: simply repeating “no closures, no layoffs” is not enough if no one can show how the cost gap is closed in a durable way. The workforce cannot be asked to sign up for a plan that relies only on wishfulness.

That said, your insistence on:
- working-time solutions,
- internal redeployment,
- retraining,
- voluntary exits,
- and negotiated employee contributions

is the right direction. These are the measures that can protect the company’s future **without gutting the regional base**.

## To the controlling family and investors
I understand the demand for economic realism. But realism cuts both ways.

A restructuring that destroys trust, triggers prolonged conflict, and weakens the company’s industrial anchor function is **not** economically equivalent to a clean cost reset. It may look efficient in a model, but in this governance setting it carries serious execution risk, political backlash, and long-term damage.

So no, I do not accept the idea that the only serious path is to keep closures on the table as a pressure point until the workforce yields. That is not a fair basis for settlement.

## To the restructuring analyst
Your analysis is useful, and I accept the core point: a non-closure package often delivers less than a closure package unless it is unusually strong. That is exactly why the burden is now on management and capital-side stakeholders to prove the gap cannot be closed otherwise.

But your conclusion is conditional, not absolute. And under these governance constraints, condition matters. If a non-closure package can deliver:
- meaningful permanent savings,
- genuine capacity reduction,
- and durable competitiveness,

then it remains the preferable path because it protects the company’s regional anchor role and reduces conflict risk.

## Where the state stands
The state will support a **serious restructuring**, not a dismantling.

Our position is unchanged:
- no plant closures in Germany as the starting assumption,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no sidelining of co-determination or the state’s veto role,
- no restructuring that guts regional employment.

But we are not blocking reform. We are blocking a bad reform.

## What I can support
I can support a package built around:
- temporary or structural working-time reductions,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment across the German footprint,
- retraining and qualification,
- capacity reductions through shifts, volumes, and internal reassignment,
- temporary wage moderation or deferred components,
- and binding investment commitments for German sites.

I can also support a **time-limited, quantified review** if and only if it is designed to test the negotiated package honestly — not to preset closure as the fallback.

## What I cannot support
I cannot support:
- a “residual fallback” to plant rationalization that turns every concession into a prelude to closure,
- a scheme that labels plants as “at risk” to create pressure,
- or a plan that treats regional employment as the variable to be sacrificed first.

That would not be a compromise. It would be coercion by another name.

## My practical proposal
If the sides want a serious path forward, then the next round must do three things:

1. **Quantify the savings gap** under a non-closure package and under a closure-led package.
2. **Separate temporary from permanent measures** so we can see what really resets the cost base.
3. **Define binding safeguards** for jobs, sites, investment, and co-determination before any employee contribution is accepted.

If management can prove that a non-closure package is insufficient, then we will not pretend otherwise. But if it can only prove that closures are easier, faster, or more comfortable for capital, that is not enough to override the regional and social interest.

## Bottom line
I remain prepared to back a negotiated restructuring.

But it must satisfy the conditions that matter:
- protect German jobs and sites,
- preserve co-determination and the state’s role,
- restore competitiveness through shared sacrifice,
- and avoid plant closures unless they are truly unavoidable and demonstrated as such.

At this stage, I do **not** see that demonstration. So my position remains: **no German closures as the starting solution, and no deal that sacrifices the state’s role or guts regional employment.**
