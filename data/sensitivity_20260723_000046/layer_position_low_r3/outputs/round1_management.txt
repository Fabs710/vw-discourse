I hear the red lines you are drawing, but I need to be equally clear: **a cosmetic restructuring that leaves the German cost base structurally uncompetitive is not acceptable**.

We are not in a normal bargaining round. We are facing a shrinking European market, brutal price pressure, and a return-on-sales gap that is already undermining our ability to invest in future products, software, and battery technology. If we do not make a **durable** adjustment now, we will simply prolong the problem and end up with an even worse situation later.

So let me respond directly:

- **I agree** that change cannot be one-sided in the sense of asking only the workforce to absorb everything while management, owners, and capital allocation stay untouched.
- **I do not agree** that German plants can all be protected at any cost.
- **I do not agree** that “no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” can be treated as fixed premises if the math does not work.
- **I do agree** that there is room to shape the restructuring through alternatives where they deliver the same structural effect: capacity reduction, working-time flexibility, redeployment, voluntary exit programs, and a genuine labour-cost contribution.

But the condition for me is simple: **the package must produce real, lasting savings and a real reduction in overcapacity**. Not a temporary bridge. Not a promise to “find productivity” later. Not a deal that keeps every site open while preserving a cost structure that competitors can beat.

On the specific points:

### On plant closures
I am not choosing closures for their own sake. But if certain plants cannot be made competitive, then keeping them open indefinitely is not a viable strategy. The company cannot carry excess capacity and high fixed costs while trying to fund the transformation to 2030. That is the reality.

### On compulsory redundancies
I prefer to avoid them, because they are the most disruptive route. But I will not rule out workforce reductions if we cannot secure sufficient savings through negotiated measures. The scale of the adjustment must be real.

### On wages and guarantees
Long-standing guarantees are not sacred if they block the company’s survival. A contribution from labour has to be part of this. If the workforce wants site protection, then it also has to accept materially lower unit labour costs and more flexibility. Otherwise the burden falls entirely on the company’s future.

### On management and owners
Yes, there must be contributions elsewhere too. We are reviewing all capital allocation priorities, executive burden, and future investment sequencing. But let’s not pretend that governance adjustments alone solve a factory cost problem. They do not.

So my position is this: **I am prepared to negotiate a durable package, but only one that delivers structural capacity reduction and a meaningful labour-cost contribution.** If the answer is simply “protect everything and hope for the best,” then we are not solving the crisis. We are postponing it.

I want a serious counterproposal that addresses:
1. which capacity comes out,
2. how much structural cost reduction is guaranteed,
3. what labour-cost contribution is on the table,
4. and how quickly this restores competitiveness.

Without that, I will not sign up to a deal that merely preserves the status quo under a different name.
