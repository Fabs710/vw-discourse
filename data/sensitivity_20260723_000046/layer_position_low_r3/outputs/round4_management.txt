Let me be equally clear in this round.

I agree with everyone on one point: **this must be a real restructuring, not a cosmetic patch.** If we do not restore the cost base, margin, and capacity profile now, we will weaken the company’s ability to invest in the next product cycle and in the technologies we need through 2030.

Where I **do not** agree is the attempt to turn **“no closures”**, **“no compulsory redundancies”**, and **unchanged guarantees through 2030** into the starting and ending point of the negotiation. That is not a restructuring plan. It is a refusal to confront the scale of the problem.

## To the works council and union
You keep saying the burden cannot fall only on employees. On that, we agree.

But you are still not offering a package that proves the required savings can be achieved **without** deeper structural measures. “Voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time flexibility, productivity improvements” — yes, those are part of the answer. They are **not yet the full answer**.

What you are asking management to do is:
- remove overcapacity,
- lower fixed cost,
- improve margins,
- and free investment capacity,

while also:
- protecting every site,
- excluding forced reductions in all circumstances,
- and leaving core guarantees essentially intact.

That is not a credible trade-off unless you can show the numbers. So far, you have not.

## To the state government
I respect the concern for jobs and regional stability. But your blocking position cannot be used to preserve structurally uncompetitive capacity indefinitely. A regional anchor shareholder has a duty to the industrial base as a whole, not just to the status quo of each site.

If the numbers show that a site or major footprint reduction is needed to restore the company’s viability, then the state cannot simply say, “not on my watch,” and expect the enterprise to absorb the long-term damage.

## To the controlling family
You are right about one thing: this cannot be about short-term optics. But “stewardship” also means making hard decisions before the company is forced into a worse position.

I do not accept the implication that a non-closure route is automatically the better long-term solution. It may be — but only if it produces the same structural effect. If it does not, then delaying footprint reduction only postpones the problem and makes the eventual adjustment more painful.

## To the institutional investors
You are right to demand a credible turnaround. But credibility cuts both ways: a plan that sounds socially acceptable and is operationally weak is not credible. Nor is a plan that promises savings later while preserving the exact cost structure that got us here.

I am not proposing closure for its own sake. I am saying that **the economics must govern the outcome**.

## On the expert / audit question
The expert is useful here, but let’s not hide behind process. We already know the key question:

> Can a non-closure package produce a durable, quantified cost reset large enough to restore margins and fund investment through 2030?

If the answer is yes, then good — we have a route.  
If the answer is no, then closures and/or forced reductions remain on the table as legitimate restructuring tools.

I will not pretend otherwise.

## My position, plainly
I am open on **how** the savings are achieved.

I am **not** open on:
- preserving all German capacity by principle,
- ruling out compulsory redundancies in all circumstances,
- or granting site and job guarantees that freeze an uncompetitive structure.

Those positions may be understandable politically. They are not sufficient economically.

## What I can accept
A non-closure package could be acceptable **only if it is truly equivalent in structural effect**. That means:

- real capacity reduction, not just postponement
- durable labour-cost relief, not one-off concessions
- measurable overhead and management cuts
- a timetable that delivers visible savings in 2025–2027
- and enough permanence to restore the brand’s return-on-sales path

If you can bring that, I will consider it seriously.

## What I will not accept
I will not accept:
- a purely symbolic “every site stays open” compromise,
- a package that relies on voluntary tools alone and hopes for the best,
- or a settlement that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive while calling that social responsibility.

That would be a failure to lead.

## On the proposed “no-closure first, fallback later” idea
I do **not** accept a fallback that is designed to be politically impossible in the first place and operationally unavailable later. If we agree to a package, it has to be enforceable and it has to close the gap.

So my answer is:
- yes to a serious non-closure option,
- but only if it is quantified and durable,
- and no to a permanent veto on the restructuring tools we may need if it fails.

## Final word
We are at a point where slogans are no longer enough.

Bring the quantified package:
- cost savings by measure,
- timing,
- one-time costs,
- capacity effect,
- and impact on 2030 return on sales.

If the non-closure route can truly deliver the required structural effect, I will support it. If it cannot, then I will not pretend that closures and deeper workforce reductions are off the table. They are not, and I will say that plainly.
