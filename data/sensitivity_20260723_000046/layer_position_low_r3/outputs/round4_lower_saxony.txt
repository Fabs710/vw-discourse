I will be equally direct.

## My position remains unchanged
I support a **real restructuring**. I do **not** support:
- German plant closures as the starting logic,
- compulsory redundancies as a normal fallback,
- or any outcome that weakens the state’s co-determination and blocking role.

That remains my line.

## Where I agree
### On the need for restructuring
Yes: the company needs a durable reset. Pretending the cost and capacity problem does not exist would be irresponsible. I agree with management, the family, investors, and the expert on that point.

### On burden-sharing
Yes: this cannot be financed by labour alone. Management restraint, overhead cuts, executive discipline, and capital-allocation changes must be real and measurable.

### On the need for numbers
Yes: we need quantified scenarios, not slogans. I support the call for a hard comparison of options.

## Where I disagree
### 1) I do not accept closures as the default “financially legitimate fallback”
A plant closure is not a neutral financial lever. It is the permanent removal of industrial capacity, skilled jobs, supplier ties, and regional stability.

So I reject the framing that says: if the non-closure package is not mathematically perfect, then closures become the natural answer. That is too narrow and too convenient for those who want the sharpest cut.

### 2) I do not accept compulsory redundancies as a standing threat
If the restructuring is built around the permanent possibility of forced layoffs, trust collapses before negotiations even begin. That is not social partnership; it is leverage.

### 3) I do not accept that 2030 guarantees are the obstacle
The workforce is being asked for flexibility, contribution, and restraint. In return, it is entitled to binding protection. If management wants more room to manoeuvre, then it must earn that through a credible, shared solution — not by turning guarantees into collateral damage.

## To management
You still have not shown that all non-closure alternatives have been exhausted or maximized.

If you want anyone to take closure logic seriously, then bring a package that clearly demonstrates:
- what management and owner-side measures contribute,
- what labour-side flexibility contributes,
- what overhead and capacity rationalization contribute,
- and what residual gap remains.

Until then, closures are a preferred instrument, not a proven necessity.

## To the works council and union
I support your insistence that the company should not resolve its crisis by gutting sites and jobs.

But I also want to be clear: a non-closure alternative must be **operational**, not just declarative. It has to show:
- real capacity reduction,
- durable savings,
- measurable management contribution,
- and enough flexibility to restore competitiveness.

If that package is credible, I will back it. If not, we remain stuck.

## To the controlling family and investors
You are right that the company must be made fit for the long term. But long-term value is not protected by permanently threatening the regional footprint.

If you want a durable settlement, then it must respect:
- stable sites,
- skilled labour,
- industrial peace,
- and co-determination.

That is not sentimentality. That is what makes this company a regional anchor rather than a purely financial object.

## On the expert’s scenario logic
The scenario work is useful, but I reject the implied conclusion that closures automatically become acceptable once the non-closure route falls short of the theoretical maximum.

The right question is broader:
- Can the savings gap be closed by a combination of non-closure measures, management contribution, owner contribution, and productivity gains?
- Can that be done durably enough for 2025–2027 and beyond?
- Can it be implemented without tearing apart industrial relations and the regional economic base?

If yes, then we should pursue that route.  
If no, then we still do **not** jump straight to closures as the default political answer. We would need to examine narrower, more limited, and more carefully justified alternatives first.

## What I can support
I can support a package with:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment and qualification,
- working-time flexibility,
- targeted capacity reduction,
- overhead and management cuts,
- executive restraint,
- capital-allocation reprioritization,
- and a negotiated employee contribution,

**provided that** it does not become a disguised path to plant closures or forced layoffs.

## What I cannot support
I cannot support:
- a closure-led restructuring,
- a compulsory redundancy fallback,
- or any deal that erodes the state’s co-determination role.

That would cross the line from restructuring into regional industrial shrinkage.
