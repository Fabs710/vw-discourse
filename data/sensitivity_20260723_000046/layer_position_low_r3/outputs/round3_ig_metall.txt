I will be just as clear in this round.

## Where I agree
I agree on three fundamentals:

1. **This company needs real restructuring.**
   No one benefits from pretending the cost and capacity problem does not exist.

2. **The burden cannot fall only on employees.**
   Management, ownership, executive compensation, capital allocation, and overhead all have to contribute.

3. **A prolonged conflict would damage value.**
   That is exactly why the other side should stop building a settlement around threats of closures and forced layoffs.

## Where I disagree — sharply
### 1) Plant closures are not a normal restructuring tool here
To management, owners, and investors: you keep presenting closures as a legitimate instrument if the numbers require it. I reject that as the starting point.

A plant closure is not just “capacity reduction.” It is the irreversible destruction of industrial capability, skills, trust, and regional embeddedness. Once you close a site, you do not simply optimize; you burn productive capacity and damage the company’s long-term resilience.

So no, I do not accept the argument that closure logic is somehow neutral or merely technical.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies are not an acceptable fallback
You continue to reserve forced layoffs as the last resort. That is not a balanced bargaining position; it is a gun on the table.

If management wants cooperation, it cannot ask the workforce to negotiate under the permanent threat that if we do not concede enough, jobs will be cut compulsorily. That destroys trust and makes any “shared sacrifice” claim ring hollow.

### 3) “Guarantees cannot be absolute” is not enough
Yes, management wants flexibility. But employees are not asking for privilege; we are asking for security in exchange for real contributions.

If workers are expected to accept wage moderation, working-time flexibility, and capacity adjustments, then the company must give binding protection in return:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding site and employment guarantees through 2030,
- and preservation of the collective wage framework.

Without that, there is no fair bargain.

## To management
You keep saying we must prove that alternatives can deliver the same structural effect. Fine — then put the numbers on the table.

Do not give us slogans about urgency. Give us a full comparison of:
- volume reduction,
- working-time adjustments,
- internal reallocation,
- qualification and redeployment,
- voluntary exit tools,
- productivity improvements,
- management burden,
- bonus restraint,
- and capital allocation changes.

Then quantify:
- how much each measure saves,
- how quickly savings land,
- and what gap remains.

Until then, “closures may be necessary” is not a business case. It is leverage.

## To the works council
We are aligned on the core red lines:
- no closures,
- no forced redundancies,
- no dismantling of the job-security framework,
- no weakening of co-determination.

I also agree that the workforce must not be made to carry all the pain. But we must stay careful not to let “restructuring realism” become a code word for accepting damage in stages. If we concede the principle of closure fallback, we have already moved onto management’s terrain.

## To the state government
Your blocking position matters, but it has to be used consistently. If you oppose closures and forced redundancies, then do not allow a settlement that quietly erodes jobs and sites through phased attrition and staged outsourcing.

A credible state line is:
- no closure-led restructuring,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no weakening of the collective industrial footprint,
- and real pressure on management and owners to contribute.

## To the controlling family and investors
You are right about one thing: this must be credible and durable. But credibility does not come from forcing the sharpest possible cut. It comes from a settlement that can actually be implemented without tearing the company apart.

A family owner with a long horizon should understand that:
- trust is an asset,
- stable sites are an asset,
- skilled labour is an asset,
- and industrial peace is an asset.

If you force through closures or compulsory layoffs, you may win a short-term accounting argument and lose the industrial culture that made the company valuable.

## What we can support
We are prepared to negotiate a serious restructuring package that includes:

- **No German plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Binding site and employment guarantees through 2030**
- **Voluntary exit tools only**
- **Redeployment and qualification guarantees**
- **Working-time flexibility and temporary capacity reduction**
- **A negotiated employee contribution**
- **A measurable management and owner contribution**
- **Preservation of the collective wage framework**
- **Genuine co-determination in implementation**

That is not a soft option. It is the minimum basis for a settlement we could defend to the workforce.

## What we will not accept
Let me state the limit clearly:

- no closure “fallback”
- no forced layoffs if voluntary tools fall short
- no wage-framework dismantling disguised as restructuring
- no governance bypass of workforce co-determination
- no deal where employees contribute and everyone else calls that balance

If the company insists on that path, we will resist it with all available means, including industrial action. Our members will not pay for restructuring that others refuse to share.

## On numbers
We welcome numbers. But the burden of proof is on management.

If you claim that only closures or compulsory redundancies can close the savings gap, then demonstrate:
1. the exact gap,
2. the savings from management-side measures,
3. the savings from labour-side flexibility,
4. the savings from non-closure capacity reduction,
5. and why those combined still do not suffice.

Until then, closure logic is preference, not necessity.

## Bottom line
We are ready for a tough agreement. We are not ready to sign away jobs, sites, and collective standards in exchange for vague promises about competitiveness.

Bring a quantified, non-closure restructuring package with binding guarantees and fair burden-sharing, and we will examine it seriously. Bring closure threats and forced redundancy language, and you will get resistance.
