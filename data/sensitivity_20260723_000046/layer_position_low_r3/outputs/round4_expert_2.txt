As the independent scenario-modeling team, our answer is: **a non-closure package can be credible only if it closes most of the structural savings gap on a durable run-rate basis and materially reduces industrial complexity**. Without internal company data, we cannot confirm whether that is achievable here; we can only define the test and model the ranges.

## What can be assessed now
We can compare three archetypes:

1. **Non-closure package**
   - voluntary exits
   - redeployment / qualification
   - working-time changes
   - wage / allowance moderation
   - overhead and management cuts
   - capex and complexity reduction
   - no plant closure, no compulsory redundancies

2. **Hybrid package**
   - all of the above
   - plus targeted line/shift/product rationalization
   - possibly selective capacity exits short of full site closure

3. **Closure / forced-reduction package**
   - plant closure(s) and/or compulsory redundancies
   - larger fixed-cost reset
   - higher one-time costs and higher execution / labor-relations risk

## Defensible financial logic
For a large German carmaker, the key economic variables are:

- **annual run-rate savings**
- **one-time restructuring costs**
- **timing of savings**
- **durability through 2030**
- **implementation risk / delay**
- **impact on operating margin / ROS**

A non-closure package is financially equivalent only if it creates:
- **real capacity reduction**
- **permanent fixed-cost reduction**
- **measurable margin uplift**
- **low risk of slippage or rollback**

Temporary concessions alone are usually not enough.

## Scenario ranges, qualitatively
Without company-specific data, only broad ranges are defensible:

### 1) Non-closure package
Typical strengths:
- lower one-time cash cost
- lower legal / social conflict risk
- faster governance acceptance if negotiated

Typical weaknesses:
- savings often smaller and slower
- risk of partial delivery
- may not remove enough structural overcapacity

### 2) Hybrid package
This is often the most plausible middle ground if closures are politically blocked:
- can capture some structural savings
- can preserve most German footprint
- can be designed to reduce capacity without full site shutdown

### 3) Closure / forced-reduction package
Typical strengths:
- largest and clearest structural cost reset
- strongest capacity reduction
- better chance of hitting ambitious margin targets

Typical weaknesses:
- high one-time closure / redundancy cost
- major labor-relations risk
- possible timing delays from consultation / codetermination / strikes
- risk of hidden costs from disruption, supplier effects, and lost know-how

## What the numbers must show
To judge a non-closure alternative, management would need to provide:

- headcount by site/function
- capacity utilization by plant/line
- labor cost per vehicle and per hour
- overhead allocation by site
- targeted savings by measure
- one-time costs for each measure
- timing curve for 2025–2030
- sensitivity to strike / delay / lower-than-planned voluntary exits

## Key uncertainty
The biggest unknown is whether **the residual savings gap after non-closure measures is small enough** to be acceptable to management, owners, and investors.

If the residual gap is large, then:
- a non-closure deal is socially preferable but economically incomplete, or
- it becomes a hybrid package with some footprint reduction

If the residual gap is modest, a non-closure route may be viable if burden-sharing is real and durable.

## Bottom line
Our neutral assessment is:

- **Non-closure is possible in principle**
- **but only if it delivers durable structural savings close to the closure alternative**
- **otherwise a hybrid or closure-based package will have stronger financial effect**
