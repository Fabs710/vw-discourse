I will engage directly.

### Where I agree
I agree with management, the investors, and to a lesser extent the state on one core point: **this company needs a real structural turnaround, not a symbolic compromise**. If the German cost base remains structurally above peers, then the business will continue to underperform, and that would damage long-term value for everyone, including the workforce and the regions.

I also agree that burden-sharing cannot be one-sided. Management, overhead, capital allocation, and executive burden must all be part of the package. If employees are asked to contribute, the rest of the system must contribute too.

And I agree with the works council, union, and state on one important point as well: **a prolonged conflict would be value-destructive**. Industrial peace matters. A restructuring that triggers open-ended confrontation would weaken the enterprise, not strengthen it.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the labour side’s insistence that all German sites must be protected unconditionally, that compulsory redundancies can be ruled out absolutely, and that existing guarantees are effectively untouchable.

That position is understandable politically, but it is not a credible ownership position if the economics do not work. A controlling shareholder cannot sign off on preserving every structure if that means preserving a cost base the company cannot carry.

I also do **not** accept the state’s framing that closures are somehow merely a political shortcut rather than a legitimate restructuring tool if needed. If a site is structurally uncompetitive, then protecting it forever does not defend the industrial base; it delays the problem and risks a bigger loss later.

### What is unacceptable to me
A few things are clear from the owner’s side:

- **No cosmetic deal**
- **No outcome that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive**
- **No settlement that permanently impairs enterprise value**
- **No solution that creates a prolonged, value-destroying conflict**
- **No arrangement that weakens governance control or makes the company unable to act**

Those are not bargaining flourishes. They are the boundaries of responsible stewardship.

### On the specific arguments

#### To the works council and union
You say closures and redundancies must be off the table, and that alternatives should be exhausted first. That is not unreasonable as a process principle. But it becomes unreasonable if it turns into a permanent veto on structural change.

If alternatives can deliver the same durable effect, I am open to them. But “same durable effect” is the key test. Vague promises about productivity, trust, or redeployment are not enough. The package must actually remove overcapacity and reduce cost in a way that survives beyond the next bargaining round.

So: **yes to negotiated alternatives where they are real; no to a principle that prevents necessary adjustment.**

#### To management
You are right to insist that the package must be structurally significant. But you should not present closures as the only way to establish seriousness. That risks needlessly hardening resistance and may destroy value through conflict before savings are realized.

What I need from management is not a slogan about urgency, but a **specific, credible, quantified plan**:
- which capacity comes out,
- what the savings are,
- how fast they land,
- how employee contribution is structured,
- and what management/owner contribution is concrete.

If the plan can achieve the necessary effect without closures, that is preferable. If it cannot, then you need to say exactly why.

#### To the state
I respect the regional and political responsibility. But blocking all closure logic in principle is not stewardship either. The state should use its position to ensure the restructuring is socially and industrially responsible, not to freeze the industrial map.

#### To the investors
You are correct to demand credibility. But credibility will not come from forcing the sharpest possible headline cuts if they are not implementable. A deal that looks tough but collapses into conflict is not value creating.

### My position on a workable package
I am prepared to support a restructuring only if it contains all of the following:

1. **A real reduction in overcapacity**
   - not just efficiency language, but actual capacity removal or equivalent durable contraction.

2. **A measurable labour contribution**
   - flexibility, working-time changes, moderated cost progression, and where necessary adjustments to legacy protections.

3. **A concrete management and ownership contribution**
   - executive restraint, disciplined capital allocation, and prioritisation of future investment.

4. **A credible path to industrial peace**
   - no open-ended confrontation and no performative brinkmanship.

5. **A governance outcome that preserves the enterprise’s long-term value**
   - not short-term optics.

### What I will not support
I will not support:

- a deal that keeps everything basically as it is and calls it restructuring,
- a deal that relies on endless negotiation without actual implementation,
- or a deal that destroys trust and value through a prolonged labour conflict.

### Final point
So my answer is this: **I am not on the side of “no change.”** I am on the side of a change that is real, durable, and value-preserving.

If management can bring a quantified package that restores competitiveness without unnecessary destruction, I will back it. If the only credible route is deeper structural reduction, then I will not block reality for the sake of comfort.

But I will also be clear: I will not back a settlement that permanently impairs the company’s value or leaves the core business structurally weakened. That is where I draw the line.
