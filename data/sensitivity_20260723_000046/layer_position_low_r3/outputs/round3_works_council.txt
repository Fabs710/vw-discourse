I will be equally direct in this round: **we are still not moving off the core issue.** If management, owners, and investors want a serious settlement, then they must stop treating plant closures and compulsory redundancies as the default language of “reality.”

## First: what I agree with
I agree on three points:

1. **This company needs a real restructuring.**  
   Nobody here is defending stagnation.

2. **The burden cannot fall only on employees.**  
   Management, ownership, executive compensation, capital allocation, and overhead must all contribute.

3. **A prolonged conflict would damage the company.**  
   That is exactly why management should stop escalating toward irreversibility.

## Where I disagree — and strongly
### 1) Plant closures are not an acceptable “tool” here
To the investors and management: you keep saying closures are merely a legitimate instrument if savings are needed. That may be true in abstract boardroom theory. It is **not acceptable as the starting logic for this company’s German footprint**.

A closure is not a technical adjustment. It is a permanent destruction of industrial capacity, know-how, regional trust, and future flexibility. Once you close a plant, you do not “restructure”; you **burn** productive capability.

So no: I do not accept your framing that “equivalent structural effect” can only come from closures or forced cuts. That is a self-serving definition.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies are not a fallback we will normalize
To management and investors: you keep reserving the right to force layoffs if voluntary tools do not deliver enough. That is precisely the threat that destroys trust and makes negotiated solutions harder.

If you come to the table with a gun on the table, do not act surprised when workers refuse to call that cooperation.

### 3) “Guarantees can’t be absolute” is not an argument
Of course management wants more flexibility. But the historic job-security framework exists for a reason: to prevent exactly this kind of unilateral cost shifting onto labour whenever markets tighten.

If the company wants employees to accept temporary flexibility, then it must offer **binding protection in return**:
- every German plant stays open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding site and employment guarantees through 2030,
- and genuine co-determination in implementation.

Without that, there is no basis for trust.

## To management
You still have not given us a credible proof that a non-closure package cannot work.

You say alternatives must deliver the “same structural effect.” Fine. Then show it in numbers, not in threats. Bring a full comparison:
- volume reductions,
- internal transfers,
- working-time changes,
- qualification and redeployment,
- voluntary exits,
- executive burden,
- bonus restraint,
- capital expenditure reprioritization,
- and productivity/process measures.

If you are serious, quantify:
- what each measure saves,
- when the savings materialize,
- and why that still allegedly falls short.

Until then, “we may need closures” is not evidence. It is leverage.

## To the family shareholder
You speak about stewardship and long-term value, but true stewardship does not mean sacrificing employees first and calling it discipline. A family owner with a long horizon should understand that:
- trust is an asset,
- skilled labor is an asset,
- stable sites are an asset,
- and co-determination is not a nuisance but part of how this company has remained strong.

If you force through closures, you may win a short-term balance-sheet argument and lose the industrial culture that made the company valuable.

## To the state government
Your position is closer to ours, but I need to be blunt: words are not enough. If you genuinely reject closures and forced redundancies, then use your blocking power consistently and openly. Do not leave workers wondering whether the state will ultimately accept a “pragmatic compromise” that looks a lot like plant loss in stages.

A strong state position should be:
- no closure-led restructuring,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no erosion of co-determination,
- and a real plan to protect the industrial base.

## To the union
We are aligned on the essentials. The workforce will not accept being made to pay for management’s strategic mistakes. But we also need the union to stay firm on the same red lines:
- no closures,
- no forced layoffs,
- no weakening of collective protections,
- no bargaining away job security for vague promises.

## On the “numbers” challenge
I welcome numbers. But let me be clear: the burden of proof is on management, not on the workforce.

If you claim there is a savings gap that can only be closed by shutting plants or cutting jobs compulsorily, then you must demonstrate:
1. the exact gap,
2. the exact savings from management-side measures,
3. the exact savings from labour-side flexibility,
4. the exact savings from capacity adjustment without closures,
5. and why those combined measures still do not suffice.

Until then, the closure argument is just a preferred option dressed up as necessity.

## What we can support
I will repeat our constructive package, because it is still the only serious one on the table:

- **No German plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Binding site and employment guarantees through 2030**
- **Voluntary exit tools only**
- **Redeployment and qualification guarantees**
- **Working-time flexibility and temporary capacity adjustment**
- **A negotiated employee contribution**
- **A measurable management and owner contribution**
- **A genuine co-determination role in the implementation plan**

## What we will not accept
Let me state the line again, because it matters:

- no closure “fallback”
- no forced layoffs if voluntary tools are insufficient
- no use of the restructuring process to dismantle the job-security framework
- no governance bypass of the workforce’s co-determination rights

That is not a negotiating posture. That is the boundary of acceptable restructuring.

## Final word
If management wants industrial peace, then it must stop presenting destruction as realism.

Bring a quantified, non-closure restructuring package with binding guarantees, and we will examine it seriously. Bring closure logic and forced redundancy threats, and you will get resistance — in the boardroom, in the plants, and on the shop floor.
