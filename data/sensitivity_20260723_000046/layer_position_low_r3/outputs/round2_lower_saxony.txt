I will be very clear.

The state is prepared to support a serious restructuring, but **not** one that treats plant closures in Germany or compulsory redundancies as the first and obvious answer. That is still the wrong logic, and it remains unacceptable to us.

## To management
You are right about one thing: the company needs a **real** restructuring, not a cosmetic one. I do not dispute the need for durable savings, better competitiveness, and more room for future investment.

Where I disagree is your continued insistence that this only becomes “real” if we accept closure-led cuts and leave the workforce to absorb the main shock.

That is not the only path to structural change.

What you have not yet shown is that you have fully exhausted the alternatives that preserve the industrial base:
- internal capacity shifts between plants,
- working-time adjustments,
- temporary volume reductions,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary exit tools,
- and a negotiated employee contribution.

If your position is simply that these measures are insufficient because they are politically harder than closures, then that is not a business case — it is a preference for the easiest irreversible lever.

We can discuss meaningful savings. We cannot support a plan whose starting point is: “close sites first, negotiate later.”

## To the controlling family and investors
I hear the demand for credibility and durability. That is legitimate. But credibility is not created by threatening the regions into submission.

A plan that destroys trust, industrial peace, and local economic stability is not a strong long-term value strategy. It may reduce a spreadsheet line item quickly, but it can create deeper damage:
- loss of skills,
- weaker motivation,
- political backlash,
- and a lasting break in the social partnership that has made this company resilient.

So I reject the idea that “structural change” automatically means closures. Structural change can also mean:
- smarter allocation of production,
- lower cost through negotiated flexibility,
- and a fair burden-sharing settlement that preserves sites and jobs.

If you want our support, bring a package that is quantified and credible — **but not one that assumes German sites are expendable by default**.

## To the works council and union
I agree with you on the core principle: **the burden cannot fall one-sidedly on employees**. I also agree that the company should not use restructuring as a pretext to dismantle co-determination, weaken job security, or hollow out the regions.

But I want to be equally frank: if we insist on absolute “no change” language everywhere, we risk losing the ability to shape the outcome at all.

So I support your red lines on:
- no mass layoffs as a first resort,
- no one-sided attack on the workforce,
- no sidelining of co-determination,
- no reckless closure programme.

Where I would push for more realism is this: we need a package that is tough enough to restore competitiveness while still protecting the regional footprint. That means we should be demanding **better alternatives**, not pretending there is no restructuring problem.

## What the state can support
We can support a deal if it includes:
1. **A credible non-closure pathway as the first option**
   - all alternatives must be tested before any closure is considered.

2. **No compulsory redundancies as the governing principle**
   - workforce adjustment should first come through voluntary, negotiated, and socially responsible means.

3. **Binding site and employment protections for a defined period**
   - because trust matters, and the company needs stability to execute transformation.

4. **Concrete contributions from management and ownership**
   - executive restraint,
   - disciplined capital allocation,
   - and prioritisation of future investment over legacy preservation.

5. **A real employee contribution in a negotiated package**
   - but only if it is matched by contributions elsewhere and does not become a unilateral wage sacrifice.

6. **A clear investment and product commitment**
   - because sites are not preserved by declarations; they are preserved by future products, volume, and technological investment.

## What we will not support
Let me state the limit plainly:

- We will **not** support a package that begins from the assumption that closures are necessary and then asks the workforce to negotiate the size of the damage.
- We will **not** accept compulsory redundancies as the default fallback if management has not seriously pursued alternatives.
- We will **not** accept a restructuring that weakens the state’s co-determination and blocking role or treats it as an inconvenience.
- We will **not** sign off on a solution that guts regional employment and turns the company into a weaker industrial shell.

## On the savings question
Management keeps asking for proof that alternatives can achieve the same structural effect. Fair enough — then put the numbers on the table properly.

But the burden is not only on labour. Management must also quantify:
- how much can be saved through executive and overhead restraint,
- what capital allocation is being redirected,
- how much capacity can be reduced without closures,
- and what combination of work-time, redeployment, and voluntary tools closes the gap.

If you bring a serious model, we will assess it seriously. If you bring only closure arithmetic, we will oppose it.

## Bottom line
The state is not blocking restructuring. We are blocking **bad restructuring**.

We are prepared to support a durable turnaround that:
- protects jobs and sites where possible,
- keeps the company rooted in the region,
- and restores competitiveness through negotiated, socially balanced measures.

But we will not accept an approach that sacrifices the regional economic anchor, sidelines co-determination, or uses closures as the first resort. That line remains firm.
