As the independent financial-operations restructuring advisor, my answer is:

## Short answer
A **credible non-closure package** can be accepted only if it produces a **durable, quantified fixed-cost and capacity reset** that is close enough to the closure-based alternative in economic effect. In practical turnaround terms, I would not require identical savings line-for-line, but I would require:

1. **Sufficient run-rate savings**
2. **Durability through 2030**
3. **Low execution risk**
4. **Clear capacity relief, not just temporary labor concessions**
5. **A believable path to the company’s margin target**

If the non-closure plan cannot meet those tests, then it is not financially equivalent, even if it is socially preferable.

---

## What I would use as a minimum test

Because I do not have the company’s detailed internal model, I cannot name a precise euro threshold with confidence. But structurally, I would use this test:

### 1) Savings coverage
The non-closure package should close **most of the structural gap** to target operating margin on a **run-rate basis**, not just in headline savings. In a major manufacturing turnaround, I would want to see at least:

- **material share of the required savings in fixed cost**
- **permanent or near-permanent savings**, not one-off deferrals
- **capacity reduction translated into lower labor, overhead, logistics, and depreciation burden**

If the package only gives short-term relief, it is not equivalent.

### 2) Durability test
Savings should be durable for **multiple planning cycles**, ideally through **2030**. That means the package should not rely mainly on:
- one-time wage freezes,
- temporary working-time cuts,
- soft redeployment promises without headcount exit,
- or delayed capex.

A good restructuring package converts temporary flexibility into **structural simplification**.

### 3) Execution-risk test
A non-closure package only beats a closure package if it is actually executable. I would assess:
- probability of labor dispute
- timing slippage
- complexity of multi-site coordination
- risk of later backsliding
- risk that voluntary exits undershoot targets

A closure package often has more up-front social conflict but can be cleaner operationally. So the non-closure alternative must compensate with **higher certainty** or **lower implementation risk**.

### 4) Capacity-effect test
There must be a genuine reduction in industrial footprint, even without full site closures. That can come from:
- line consolidation
- shift elimination
- product-family rationalization
- removal of duplicate support functions
- lower direct and indirect headcount
- working-time redesign

If the company keeps all capacity and simply asks people to work differently, the savings are usually too small.

---

## My judgment on the current bargaining logic
From a restructuring standpoint, the labor side is right that **the burden should not fall only on employees**. However, management and capital are also right that a non-closure package must have an **equivalent structural effect**.

That means the real question is not:

> “Can we avoid closures?”

It is:

> “Can we achieve the same fixed-cost reset and capacity discipline without closures?”

Those are not the same thing.

---

## What would count as a credible non-closure package
I would regard the package as credible only if it includes all of the following:

- **Voluntary exits at meaningful scale**
- **Redeployment and qualification with measurable transfer rates**
- **Working-time changes that reduce effective labor cost**
- **Overhead reduction outside the plants**
- **Executive and management-cost restraint**
- **Capex reprioritization that supports cash discipline**
- **A clear permanent reduction in industrial complexity**

And importantly:
- the plan must show the **residual gap after all those measures**
- then show whether that residual gap is still acceptable relative to the turnaround target

---

## Where the burden of proof sits
Financially, the burden of proof is on management to show:
1. the size of the gap,
2. the savings from non-closure levers,
3. the one-time costs,
4. the timing,
5. and the residual shortfall, if any.

Only then can anyone judge whether closures or compulsory redundancies are truly needed.

---

## Bottom line
My threshold is not “no closures at any cost,” and it is not “closures first.” It is:

- **If non-closure measures deliver durable, executable, near-equivalent structural savings, they are financially acceptable.**
- **If they do not, they are not a credible turnaround solution.**
