I will be equally direct.

I agree with one central point from all sides: **the company needs a restructuring that is real, durable, and capable of restoring competitiveness.** A cosmetic package that preserves today’s cost base and simply postpones the problem would destroy value and is not acceptable.

Where I part company with the works council, the union, and the state is on the implication that this can be achieved while treating **no closures, no compulsory redundancies, and full preservation of existing guarantees** as fixed starting points. From a long-term owner’s perspective, that is not a credible basis for an industrial turnaround.

### What I agree with
- **The burden cannot fall only on employees.** Management, governance, and capital allocation all have to contribute.
- **Industrial peace matters.** A prolonged conflict would erode value, damage reputation, and weaken the company’s ability to invest.
- **Alternatives should be used where they genuinely deliver the same structural effect.** Capacity adjustments, working-time tools, voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, and productivity measures should be part of the solution.

### Where I disagree
#### On “no plant closures”
I do not accept “no closures” as a principle. If analysis shows that certain capacities are structurally uncompetitive, then pretending every site must remain open regardless of economics is not stewardship. It is delay.

I am not advocating closures as an ideological goal. But I am saying clearly: **if some German capacity cannot be made competitive, then it cannot be protected indefinitely by governance sentiment alone.**

#### On compulsory redundancies
I share the preference to avoid them. But I will not support a package that rules them out absolutely if negotiated measures do not achieve the required savings. An owner cannot sign off on a restructuring that leaves the company with the same structural problem in a different form.

#### On long-standing guarantees
Those guarantees were created in a different market environment. If they now block competitiveness, they must be adjusted. Not casually, and not recklessly — but they cannot be treated as untouchable.

### What I would consider credible
A package would need to show, in concrete terms:

1. **Which capacity is coming out**
   - not vague promises,
   - not purely theoretical productivity assumptions,
   - but a demonstrable reduction in overcapacity.

2. **What the structural savings are**
   - and that they are durable, not temporary.

3. **What labour contributes**
   - through flexibility, working time, cost discipline, and where necessary a negotiated wage contribution.

4. **What management and owners contribute**
   - lower executive burden,
   - disciplined capital allocation,
   - and prioritisation of investments that improve future returns rather than preserve every legacy structure.

5. **How labour peace is secured**
   - because a restructuring that triggers prolonged strikes may preserve symbolism but destroy value.

### To the works council and union
I hear your insistence on social responsibility. But social responsibility also includes preserving the enterprise’s ability to exist competitively in ten years. If German operations remain above market cost, then refusing structural adjustment is not protection — it is deferred damage.

If you want site protection, then you need to engage seriously with the uncomfortable question of **which sites can still justify their cost position and which cannot**. That cannot be avoided forever.

### To the state
I respect the political and regional implications. But the state’s role cannot be to freeze the industrial structure in place. Blocking necessary adjustment would not protect the region in the long run; it would risk weakening the company until the pressure becomes worse.

### My bottom line
I am **not** prepared to support a “protect everything” outcome. I am **prepared** to support a restructuring that:
- reduces overcapacity,
- lowers the cost base in a durable way,
- avoids unnecessary conflict,
- and includes fair contributions from all sides.

But I need to be clear: **if the alternative to closures is only a softer wording for the same unsolved cost problem, I will not back it.** The company needs structural change, not reassurance.

If management can bring forward a plan that specifies credible capacity exits, a real labour-cost contribution, and protection for the enterprise’s future investment capacity, then I will engage constructively. If not, I will not pretend that the status quo is acceptable.
