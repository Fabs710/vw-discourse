Below is a neutral scenario assessment of **non-closure vs. closure restructuring packages** for a large German carmaker under the governance constraints you described.

## 1) Short answer

A **credible non-closure package is possible only if** it produces:
- a **large enough permanent fixed-cost reduction**,
- **real capacity reduction** through utilization, shifts, and product consolidation,
- **durable labor-cost relief** that survives beyond the next bargaining cycle,
- and **implementation certainty** high enough for capital providers to believe the savings.

If those conditions are not met, a **closure / major footprint-reduction package** will usually deliver:
- faster fixed-cost relief,
- clearer capacity rationalization,
- stronger operating margin improvement,
but with:
- higher one-time costs,
- more execution risk,
- and much higher industrial-political conflict risk under German co-determination and state-blocking constraints.

So the real question is not “closure or no closure” in the abstract. It is whether the **non-closure package closes the quantified savings gap** with enough durability and enforceability.

Still, for a large German OEM, restructuring scenarios can be benchmarked using typical order-of-magnitude logic.

### Main cost levers
1. **Headcount reduction**
   - voluntary exits, redeployment, early retirement, limited compulsory reduction
2. **Working-time / wage measures**
   - temporary reductions, flexibility, moderation of increases, removal of some legacy protections
3. **Capacity reduction**
   - lower shifts, fewer product variants, line consolidation
4. **Overhead and management cost**
   - indirect labor, admin, executive burden, procurement discipline
5. **Footprint actions**
   - plant closure or major line exit, which removes fixed site costs

## 3) Scenario comparison framework

### Scenario A: Non-closure restructuring
Typical measures:
- voluntary exits and early retirement
- redeployment and internal transfers
- working-time flexibility / temporary reductions
- wage moderation or deferred increases
- overhead reduction
- management compensation restraint
- process/productivity improvement
- capacity reduction without closure, e.g. fewer shifts, fewer variants

### Scenario B: Closure / footprint-reduction package
Typical measures:
- at least one plant closure or major site line exit
- direct workforce reductions
- consolidation of production into fewer sites
- fixed overhead removal
- sharper labor-cost reset

## 5) Return-on-sales implication through 2030

Because the brand is missing its ROS target, the relevant question is whether the restructuring can lift operating margin enough to matter by 2030.

### Non-closure package
If the current gap is mainly:
- excess indirect labor,
- some overstaffing,
- inefficiencies,
- and moderate overcapacity,

then a non-closure package can plausibly improve ROS by roughly:

- **+0.5 to +1.5 percentage points** by 2027–2030

This assumes:
- real headcount reduction,
- durable work-rule changes,
- meaningful overhead cuts,
- and no major volume deterioration.

If the structural gap is larger, the improvement may be only:
- **+0.3 to +0.8 pp**

which may be insufficient if the brand needs a stronger reset.

### Closure / footprint-reduction package
A closure package can plausibly improve ROS by roughly:

- **+1.0 to +2.5 percentage points** by 2027–2030

This is more likely when:
- the company has genuine excess domestic capacity,
- fixed site cost is high,
- and the footprint is materially above optimal.

However, the actual realized improvement can be lower if:
- closure execution drags on,
- labor conflict delays implementation,
- or transition costs are larger than planned.

## 6) Governance constraint effect

Under the governance structure you described, the decisive issue is not just economics but **implementability**.

### Constraints that matter
- **Co-determination**: workforce representatives can effectively block or slow major site decisions
- **Supervisory board supermajority**: plant closure likely needs more than a simple majority
- **Controlling family**: can support restructuring, but cannot by itself neutralize all blocking positions
- **State blocking minority / political veto**: materially raises the threshold for closures
- **Union strike leverage**: increases the cost of forced implementation

### Implication
A closure package may be **financially rational but procedurally fragile**.  
A non-closure package may be **financially weaker but far easier to implement**.

That makes the comparison asymmetric:
- **Closure** = higher theoretical savings, higher execution risk
- **Non-closure** = lower theoretical savings, lower governance conflict risk

---

## 7) What would make non-closure credible to management and capital providers

A non-closure package becomes credible only if it includes all of the following:

1. **Quantified permanent savings**
   - not temporary concessions only
2. **Capacity reduction in practice**
   - fewer shifts, fewer variants, lower utilization at selected sites
3. **Binding labor-cost contribution**
   - wage moderation, flexibility, benefit resets, or deferred increases
4. **Significant overhead cuts**
   - not just plant labor savings
5. **Hard timetable**
   - savings visible in 2025–2027, not only by 2030
6. **Enforceable commitments**
   - supervisory-board and collective-bargaining instruments that survive the next cycle

If one or more of these is missing, management will likely view the non-closure option as insufficient.

---

## 8) What would make closure fallback narrowly defensible

From a restructuring-finance perspective, closures become defensible if management can show:
- the non-closure package leaves a **material savings gap**,
- the gap is **too large to close through overhead, working-time, and voluntary exits alone**,
- and the site(s) targeted are structurally underutilized or non-competitive.

But even then, one must model:
- one-time closure costs,
- ramp-down losses,
- supplier disruption,
- and delay due to industrial conflict.

In practice, closure is most credible as a fallback when the company has:
- clear overcapacity,
- duplicated fixed costs,
- and a line/site that can be consolidated without crippling future product strategy.

---

## 9) Neutral judgment

### My assessment
- A **non-closure pathway is not impossible**
- But it is only credible if it achieves **something close to a permanent structural reset**
- If it relies mainly on temporary employee concessions, it will likely be **insufficient**
- A **closure package** likely produces stronger long-run cost reduction, but at materially higher one-time cost and execution risk

### Most likely outcome class
For a large German OEM under these governance constraints, the most realistic settlement is often a **hybrid**:
- no immediate broad closure program,
- but substantial capacity rationalization,
- working-time and labor-cost concessions,
- overhead cuts,
- voluntary exits,
- and a narrowly defined fallback option for underperforming capacity if targets are missed.

That is usually the only structure that can be both:
- financially credible, and
- politically implementable.
