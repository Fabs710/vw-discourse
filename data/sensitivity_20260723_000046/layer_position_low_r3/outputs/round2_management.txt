I will be equally direct.

I agree with one important point from the labour side and the state: **this cannot be a one-sided exercise that leaves management, ownership, and capital allocation untouched while asking employees to carry everything.** That would be neither durable nor credible.

But I do **not** accept the core position that German sites must be protected at any cost, that compulsory redundancies can be ruled out in all circumstances, or that existing guarantees remain untouched if they block competitiveness. If that is the framework, then we are not restructuring — we are preserving the problem.

## To the works council and union
You keep presenting “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no guarantee changes” as if those were natural starting points. They are not. They are outcomes you would like to preserve, but the business reality does not automatically permit that.

I am not interested in a cosmetic solution.

If the package does not:
- reduce overcapacity materially,
- lower the structural cost base in a durable way,
- and improve the brand’s return-on-sales trajectory,

then it will fail. And failing slowly is not better than failing quickly.

You say alternatives must be exhausted first. Fine — but alternatives are only credible if they deliver the same structural effect. That means:
- real capacity exit,
- real cost reduction,
- real flexibility,
- and a real labour-cost contribution.

If your version of “alternative measures” is simply to keep every site open, preserve every guarantee, and hope voluntary tools somehow close the gap, then that is not an alternative. It is a delay tactic.

## To the state government
I hear your concern about regional impact, and I acknowledge your blocking position. But it cannot become a veto over necessary adjustment. Protecting the industrial base long term sometimes means accepting painful changes now.

If the state insists that closures are off-limits in principle, then you are also insisting that the company absorb structurally uncompetitive capacity indefinitely. That is not responsible stewardship of a major industrial asset.

## To the controlling family
You are right on one point: this cannot be a permanent drift toward underperformance. The company needs a reset. But let us not pretend that “structural change” can be achieved by slogans alone. We need actual decisions:
- which capacity is reduced,
- what labour cost comes down,
- and how quickly savings are realized.

I agree that management, owners, and capital allocation must contribute. We are already reviewing executive burden, investment sequencing, and all non-core spending. But those measures do **not** replace industrial restructuring. They complement it.

## To institutional investors
You are right to demand credibility. But credibility does not come from forcing headline cuts without a workable settlement. It comes from a package that can actually be implemented.

A deal that sounds softer but leaves the cost base uncompetitive will not restore value. Neither will an open-ended conflict. What we need is a package that is:
- structurally significant,
- implementable,
- and fast enough to matter for 2030 competitiveness.

## What I am putting on the table
Let me be clear about what management is prepared to pursue:

1. **Meaningful capacity reduction**
   - The company will not preserve all current overcapacity.
   - We need real volume and footprint adjustment, not symbolic trimming.

2. **Structural labour-cost relief**
   - There must be a genuine employee contribution.
   - That can include working-time measures, flexibility, changes to legacy guarantees, and moderated cost progression.
   - If the savings are insufficient, then more direct workforce reduction measures remain on the table.

3. **Management and owner contribution**
   - Executive burden, spending discipline, and capital allocation priorities will be tightened.
   - Future investment must support competitiveness, not legacy preservation.

4. **Protection of future investment capacity**
   - The point of restructuring is to free funds for product, software, and technology investment through 2030.
   - If the current cost structure is protected, that investment capacity disappears.

## What I cannot accept
I cannot accept a settlement that says:
- every German plant remains open regardless of economics,
- no compulsory redundancies can ever occur,
- guarantees are untouchable,
- and management must somehow solve the cost gap without structural change.

That would not restore competitiveness. It would preserve a high-cost structure and call it compromise.

## On your demand for numbers
You want numbers, not slogans. Fair enough.

Then here is the hard truth: if the employee side wants to avoid closures and forced redundancies, it must bring forward a package that produces savings of the same order of magnitude and durability. Not tomorrow. Not in theory. In the implementation path.

That means your proposal must answer:
- how much cost is removed,
- how quickly,
- and with what certainty.

If the answer is “through productivity later” or “through trust in redeployment,” that is not enough.

## My bottom line
I am prepared to negotiate a serious, quantified restructuring. I am **not** prepared to negotiate around the central problem as if it does not exist.

So the choice is not:
- “employees lose, company wins,”

and it is not:
- “everyone keeps everything and the market magically improves.”

The choice is whether we make a real structural adjustment now, or drift toward a deeper crisis later.

If you want to avoid closures, then show me a package that achieves the same structural effect. If you cannot, then do not ask management to pretend the economics have changed.
