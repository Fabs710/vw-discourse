[Works Council (Betriebsrat) (Plant-level employee representation under co-determination) — Round 1]
We will not accept plant closures in Germany and we will not accept compulsory redundancies.

That is the clear position of the works council.

We acknowledge that the company is under pressure and that change is needed. But the burden cannot be placed one-sidedly on the workforce through site closures, forced job cuts, or the destruction of hard-won job-security guarantees. Management did not create trust by suddenly putting historic protections on the table. Trust has already been damaged, and it will not be rebuilt by presenting employees with a fait accompli.

What we will argue for is a restructuring that protects all German sites, preserves jobs, and secures the company’s future through negotiated, reversible measures instead of irreversible cuts. That means:

- no closure of any German plant
- no compulsory redundancies
- binding job and site guarantees through 2030
- a genuine role for co-determination in shaping any restructuring
- fair sharing of sacrifice, including on management side and in the capital allocation decisions, not just among employees

If savings are needed, then they must come from alternatives: capacity adjustments, working-time solutions, voluntary arrangements, redeployment, qualification, productivity improvements, and a negotiated employee contribution — but only within a framework that keeps plants open and protects livelihoods.

We are prepared to negotiate seriously. But if the proposal remains built around closures and forced layoffs, we will resist it with all the means available to us, including industrial action and our rights in co-determination.

[Industrial Union (Trade union and collective-bargaining counterpart) — Round 1]

We recognise the economic pressure, but that does **not** justify turning the workforce into the sole shock absorber.

What we can discuss is a **fair, negotiated contribution** from employees — but only if it is matched by real contributions from management, owners, and capital allocation decisions. That means:

- using capacity reductions, working-time tools, voluntary programmes, qualification, and redeployment instead

If management wants labour to contribute, then management must also contribute: through lower executive burden, reconsideration of dividends and investment priorities, and serious commitments to future products and jobs.

So my position is this: **we are prepared to negotiate a shared-sacrifice package, but only one that protects jobs and sites.** If the plan still depends on closures or forced layoffs, then we will oppose it decisively, including through industrial action.

But the condition for me is simple: **the package must produce real, lasting savings and a real reduction in overcapacity**. Not a temporary bridge. Not a promise to “find productivity” later. Not a deal that keeps every site open while preserving a cost structure that competitors can beat.

### On plant closures
I am not choosing closures for their own sake. But if certain plants cannot be made competitive, then keeping them open indefinitely is not a viable strategy. The company cannot carry excess capacity and high fixed costs while trying to fund the transformation to 2030. That is the reality.

### On compulsory redundancies
I prefer to avoid them, because they are the most disruptive route. But I will not rule out workforce reductions if we cannot secure sufficient savings through negotiated measures. The scale of the adjustment must be real.

### On wages and guarantees

I want a serious counterproposal that addresses:
1. which capacity comes out,
2. how much structural cost reduction is guaranteed,

The central deadlock is **not** over whether restructuring is needed. Nearly all parties accept that it is.

The impasse is over **the acceptable form of restructuring**:

1. **Labour-side stakeholders** insist on:
   - no plant closures
   - no compulsory redundancies
   - binding site and employment guarantees through 2030
   - co-determination protections

2. **Management, investors, and the family holding** insist that:
   - the savings package must be structurally sufficient and durable
   - closures and/or forced reductions must remain available if needed
   - guarantees cannot freeze an uncompetitive structure

3. **The state government** is aligned with labour on avoiding closures and forced layoffs, but also wants a credible, financially defensible package.

So the core impasse is:

> **Whether the company can achieve a durable, quantified cost reset without closures or compulsory redundancies, and if not, what fallback is acceptable.**

A second, related impasse is procedural:

> **Who bears the burden of proof, and what data must be produced before any party concedes on closures or job cuts?**

---

## Facilitator proposals for compromise

### Proposal 1: Phased restructuring with a conditional fallback
A compromise structure could be:

- **Phase 1:** Non-closure measures only, implemented immediately:
  - voluntary exits
  - redeployment
  - working-time flexibility
  - overhead and management burden cuts
  - capex reprioritization
  - productivity/process improvements

- **Phase 2:** A jointly reviewed checkpoint at a fixed date:
  - if savings and capacity reduction targets are met, the no-closure path continues
  - if they are not met, a narrowly defined fallback for selected underperforming capacity becomes available

- **Safeguard:** any fallback would require:
  - fresh quantified evidence
  - renewed consultation
  - and clear limits on scope

This may bridge the gap between labour’s demand for an initial no-closure commitment and management’s demand for a credible backstop.

---

### Proposal 2: Binding employment protection with measurable equivalent savings
A second compromise could be:

- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No immediate plant closures**
- But:
  - binding employee contributions
  - binding management contribution
  - binding owner contribution
  - a jointly agreed savings target
  - and a fixed timetable

If the package fails to deliver the agreed financial result, then the parties would reopen negotiations on footprint reduction, but only after the agreed measures are fully tested.

This may be acceptable where stakeholders want to avoid a closure decision now, but do not want to eliminate the issue permanently.

---

### Proposal 3: Hybrid package with protected sites and limited footprint rationalization
A third compromise could be:

- all major German sites remain open for the duration of the agreement
- but **specific lines, shifts, or product allocations** are consolidated
- voluntary exit tools are expanded
- selective capacity reduction is allowed without full plant closure
- management and ownership make deeper concessions
- the state and works council retain oversight of implementation

This bridges the divide by allowing **structural reduction without immediate full-site closure**, which may be more acceptable to labour and the state while still giving management real cost relief.
