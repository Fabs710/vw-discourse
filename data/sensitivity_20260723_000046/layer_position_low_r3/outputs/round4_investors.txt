We agree with the broad direction of the discussion: **this cannot be a cosmetic restructuring**. If the package does not produce a durable improvement in margins, utilization, and cost base, then it will not protect equity value and we will not support it.

## Where we agree
- **Management is right** that the current structure is not competitive enough and that the company needs a real reset.
- **The family holding is right** that long-term value depends on structural change, not just short-term peace.
- **The works council, union, and state are right** that burden-sharing cannot fall only on employees.
- **The expert is right** that we need a quantified comparison of options, not rhetorical claims.

## Where we disagree
### 1) A blanket “no closures” rule is not acceptable
We do **not** support an absolute ban on German site closures if it prevents a credible turnaround.

A closure is severe, but it is not automatically irrational. If the German footprint is structurally overbuilt and the company remains locked into an uncompetitive cost base, then refusing closure in principle would preserve the problem and destroy shareholder value through continued margin erosion.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be an absolute either
We prefer voluntary tools first. But if voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time flexibility, and other negotiated measures do not deliver enough **durable** savings, management must retain a credible fallback. Without that, the restructuring lacks discipline and may fail.

### 3) 2030 guarantees are only acceptable if they do not freeze an uncompetitive structure
Binding guarantees can support trust, but only if the numbers show the company can still reach competitive margins. If the guarantees block structural correction, they become a value-destroying constraint.

## What would move us
A non-closure package is acceptable **only if it delivers equivalent structural effect**. That means:
- permanent fixed-cost reduction,
- real capacity reduction,
- measurable labor-cost relief,
- visible savings in 2025–2027,
- and a credible path back to competitive return on sales by 2030.

Temporary concessions and one-off measures are not enough.

## What we want to see next
We support the call for a hard scenario comparison, but it needs to be specific. Management, with independent support, should present:

1. **Non-closure package**
2. **Hybrid package**
3. **Closure / footprint-reduction package**

For each:
- annual run-rate savings
- one-time restructuring costs
- timing of savings
- implementation risk
- impact on 2030 ROS
- probability of labor or governance delay

Without that, no one can honestly judge whether the non-closure route is credible.

## To the works council and union
Your insistence that workers should not bear the burden alone is legitimate. But your current red lines are too absolute for us to endorse as-is. If the company can show that some narrowly targeted footprint reduction is the only way to restore competitive margins, then refusing even to consider it would be irresponsible.

## To management
You are right to demand structural change, but you have not yet proven that every non-closure lever has been maximized. Bring the numbers. If the residual gap is material and durable, then deeper cuts remain legitimate.

## To the state
We understand the political responsibility, but the blocking role cannot be used to preserve an uncompetitive industrial structure indefinitely. That would hurt the company, the region, and shareholders.

## Our bottom line
We are **not** defending closures for their own sake. We are defending **risk-adjusted equity value**.

So our position is:
- yes to a serious restructuring,
- yes to burden-sharing,
- yes to non-closure alternatives **if they are financially equivalent**,
- but no to a cosmetic settlement that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.
