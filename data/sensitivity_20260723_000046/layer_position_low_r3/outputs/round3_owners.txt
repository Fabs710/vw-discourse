## My position

The family holding supports a **real restructuring** and will back measures that **durably restore competitiveness, margins, and investment capacity**.

What we will **not** support is a settlement that:
- leaves the company structurally uncompetitive,
- preserves all German capacity by principle,
- turns “no closures” into an absolute veto,
- or creates a prolonged, value-destroying conflict.

That is the line.

---

## Where I agree

### With management
You are right that this cannot be a cosmetic deal. If the cost base remains above target, value erosion continues and future investment is crowded out. We agree that the company needs **real structural change**.

### With labour and the state
You are also right that the burden cannot fall only on employees. Management restraint, overhead reduction, capital allocation, and executive contribution must all be part of the package.

And I agree that a prolonged conflict would destroy value. Industrial peace is not a nice-to-have; it is part of the enterprise value.

### With the expert
The expert is correct that we need a **quantified scenario comparison**, not slogans. We need to know what savings are achieved, when they land, what they cost, and whether they are durable.

---

## Where I disagree strongly

### 1) “No closures” as a principle
I do **not** accept that all German plants must be protected unconditionally.

A controlling shareholder cannot endorse a permanent cost structure that the business cannot carry. If a site is structurally uncompetitive, pretending otherwise does not protect the industrial base; it weakens the whole company over time.

So: **I am open to non-closure solutions, but I will not accept a blanket ban on closures.**

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” as an absolute
I would prefer to avoid forced reductions. But I will not rule them out in all circumstances if negotiated alternatives fail to deliver enough durable savings.

If voluntary tools alone are insufficient, then management must retain a credible fallback. Without that, the restructuring lacks discipline.

### 3) Guarantees through 2030 as untouchable
Binding guarantees can support trust, but they cannot become a straitjacket that blocks adjustment. If they prevent the company from restoring competitiveness, then they are not stewardship; they are delay.

---

## On the core question: non-closure vs. closure

The expert’s scenario work is helpful, and my reading is this:

- A **non-closure package** can be acceptable only if it produces **equivalent structural effect**:
  - real capacity reduction,
  - durable labor-cost relief,
  - meaningful overhead cuts,
  - and savings that are visible in 2025–2027, not just promised for 2030.

- If it does **not** do that, then closure or major footprint reduction remains a legitimate fallback.

I am not choosing closure for its own sake. I am saying that the economics must govern the solution, not slogans about symbolism.

---

## What I would require from any non-closure package

If labour wants us to support a non-closure route, it must be more than a declaration of loyalty to every site. It must be a package with measurable effect:

1. **Permanent savings**
   - not just temporary concessions

2. **Real capacity reduction**
   - lower utilization, fewer shifts, consolidation of product/variant complexity

3. **Workforce contribution**
   - working-time flexibility, moderated cost progression, changes to legacy protections

4. **Management and owner contribution**
   - executive restraint, overhead cuts, disciplined capital allocation

5. **Implementation certainty**
   - milestones, enforcement, and credibility with capital providers

If those conditions are met, I am open. If not, I will not pretend the numbers work.

- what labor-cost relief,
