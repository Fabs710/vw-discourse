I will be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the works council on the essentials:
- this company needs real restructuring,
- the burden cannot fall only on employees,
- and numbers matter.

I also agree with the state, at least in principle, that restructuring must be socially and industrially responsible. And I agree with the expert that we need a quantified comparison, not slogans.

## Where I disagree — sharply
### 1) No plant closures
I do **not** accept closure-led restructuring as the default, the fallback, or the “financially legitimate” answer.

A closure is not just a line item. It is:
- irreversible loss of industrial capacity,
- destruction of skilled jobs,
- damage to supplier networks,
- and a blow to trust that cannot be easily rebuilt.

So when management, investors, and the family say closures remain on the table “if needed,” I hear the real message: employees are expected to carry the risk while capital preserves optionality. That is not burden-sharing.

### 2) No compulsory redundancies
I do **not** accept forced layoffs as a normal fallback either.

If management keeps compulsory redundancies as a permanent threat, then every “negotiated” concession becomes coercion by another name. That is exactly what destroys industrial peace and makes fair settlement impossible.

### 3) The collective framework is not disposable
I also reject the idea that the collective wage and protection framework is the obstacle.

If the company wants labour to contribute, then yes, we can discuss:
- temporary wage restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- qualification and redeployment,
- and targeted capacity adjustments.

But that must come with binding security:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and preservation of the collective framework.

Without that, the company is asking for sacrifice without security.

## On the expert’s scenario logic
The expert is right about one thing: a non-closure path must be proven, not merely asserted.

But I want to be very clear: the burden of proof is on management, not on the workforce.

If management claims there is a savings gap that can only be closed by plants or forced layoffs, then it must demonstrate:
1. the exact gap,
2. the savings from management-side measures,
3. the savings from owner-side and capital-side measures,
4. the savings from labour-side flexibility,
5. and why those combined still do not suffice.

Until then, closure logic is a preference, not a necessity.

## To management
You say you need a real structural reset. Fine. Then show a real restructuring that does **not** start from threatening the workforce.

Bring us a quantified package with:
- capacity reduction through work organization, shifts, product/variant simplification, and internal transfers,
- meaningful overhead and management cuts,
- executive restraint,
- capital allocation discipline,
- voluntary exits and redeployment,
- and durable labour-cost contributions.

Then show the residual gap. Only then can anyone honestly discuss whether the package is sufficient.

What I will not accept is this sequence:
1. declare closures possible,
2. use that threat to pressure labour,
3. call the result “partnership.”

That is not partnership. That is leverage dressed up as realism.

## To the works council
We are aligned on the red lines:
- no closures,
- no forced redundancies,
- no weakening of co-determination,
- no dismantling of the collective framework.

I support your insistence that the company’s industrial base is an asset, not a disposable cost pool. But I also want to be careful: we should not drift into endless abstract rejection. We need to keep pressing for a **credible non-closure package** with measurable contributions from everyone else.

## To the state government
Your blocking position matters only if it is used consistently.

If you oppose closures and forced redundancies, then say so plainly and defend it. Do not leave room for a staged erosion of jobs, sites, and protections under the banner of “pragmatic compromise.”

A credible state stance is:
- no closure-led restructuring,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- real pressure on management and owners,
- and protection of the industrial footprint.

## To the controlling family and investors
You all speak the language of long-term value, but long-term value is not served by hollowing out the workforce and calling that discipline.

If you want credibility, then contribute materially:
- overhead cuts,
- executive restraint,
- capital reprioritization,
- product and investment discipline,
- and acceptance of a settlement that does not sacrifice jobs first.

A non-closure package is not “soft” if it truly removes cost and complexity. But it must be real. I agree with that. What I do **not** accept is using that standard to smuggle closure logic back in as the only “serious” option.

## What we can support
We are prepared to negotiate a serious restructuring package that includes:
- temporary employee contribution,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exit tools,
- redeployment and qualification,
- capacity reduction through internal measures,
- management and owner contribution,
- preservation of the collective wage framework,
- and binding protection of jobs and sites.

That is the basis of a fair deal.

## What we will not support
We will not support:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- a conditional closure fallback,
- or any settlement that makes workers pay for restructuring while others keep all the escape hatches.

If management insists on that path, then it should expect resistance, including industrial action. That is not a threat lightly made; it is the reality of what the workforce will defend.

## [SUMMON: independent industrial-operations and labour-economics audit]
We need a neutral audit that answers one question precisely:
**Can the required savings be achieved without plant closures or compulsory redundancies, if management, owners, and capital all contribute fairly?**

That audit must include:
- savings by measure,
- timing of savings,
- one-time costs,
- impact on 2030 operating margin,
- and a full account of management, owner, and capital-side contributions.

Until that is on the table, I see no reason to move from our line.

## Bottom line
We are open to a tough settlement. We are not open to a settlement where employees are expected to absorb the pain while closures and forced layoffs remain ready in the background.

Bring a quantified non-closure package with real burden-sharing, and we will examine it seriously. Bring closure threats and compulsory redundancy language, and you will get resistance — in bargaining and in the plants.
