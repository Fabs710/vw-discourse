I will be equally clear.

I agree with the expert on one important point: we need to get out of abstractions and into hard numbers. But I do **not** accept the conclusion that closures become the default “financially legitimate fallback” if management cannot prove an alternative with mathematical precision. That may be a boardroom comfort, but it is not an acceptable starting point for a regional anchor shareholder whose responsibility is jobs, industrial stability, and co-determination.

## Where I agree
### To management
Yes: this cannot be a cosmetic deal. I agree that the company needs a real structural reset, and I agree that burden-sharing cannot fall only on employees. Management, overhead, capital allocation, and executive burden must all contribute.

### To the works council and union
Yes: employees should not be asked to carry the entire adjustment. I also agree that social peace and trust are not “soft” issues; they are core assets in this company.

### To the expert
Yes: we need a quantified scenario comparison, not slogans.

## Where I disagree
### On plant closures
I do **not** accept the framing that closures are simply a neutral instrument once the numbers get difficult. A plant closure in Germany is not just a line item. It is the permanent removal of industrial capacity, skilled employment, regional value creation, and future flexibility.

That is precisely why the state’s blocking minority exists. It is there to prevent irreversible decisions being treated as routine cost engineering.

So no: closures are **not** my starting point, and they are not acceptable merely because they are the easier accounting answer.

### On compulsory redundancies
I also do **not** accept compulsory redundancies as a normal fallback. If voluntary tools, redeployment, qualification, working-time flexibility, and management contribution are not enough, then the right response is to redesign the package further — not to jump immediately to forced layoffs.

## My position on the expert’s “financial legitimacy”
The expert is right that a package must be durable and implementable. But the conclusion I draw is different:

- The burden of proof is on management to show that alternatives really have been exhausted.
- The burden is **not** on the state or labour to prove, in advance, that no non-closure solution could possibly work.
- And “financial legitimacy” does not override governance, regional responsibility, and social partnership.

A company can be financially pressured and still choose a path that preserves its industrial base. That is what serious stewardship looks like.

## What I would support
I am prepared to support a restructuring package if it includes all of the following:

1. **A credible non-closure first path**
   - Capacity adjustments, internal reallocation, and working-time tools must be tested seriously before any closure logic is even discussed.

2. **No compulsory redundancies as the governing principle**
   - Workforce adjustment should rely on voluntary and socially responsible measures first.

3. **Binding site and employment protection through 2030**
   - If workers are asked to contribute, they need security in return.

4. **Concrete management and owner contribution**
   - Executive restraint, overhead reduction, and capital allocation changes must be real, not symbolic.

5. **A quantified savings package**
   - I want the numbers: how much comes from each lever, when it lands, and what remains uncovered.

6. **A genuine role for co-determination**
   - Not a finished plan presented for rubber-stamping.

## What I cannot support
I will not support:
- a plan that starts from plant closure,
- a plan that treats forced redundancies as routine,
- a plan that weakens the state’s co-determination role,
- or a plan that gut-punches the regional employment base and then asks for political approval afterward.

## My response to management
If you want our support, bring a package that demonstrates, in numbers, how the company can restore competitiveness **without** assuming closures are the first resort.

If you cannot do that, then I will not help turn the state into a mere witness to industrial shrinkage.

## My response to labour
I support your red line against closure-led restructuring, and I support resisting compulsory redundancies. But I also need you to engage with the hard part: a non-closure package must be more than a declaration. It must be operational, quantified, and credible.

That means labour-side flexibility, productivity improvements, and a serious negotiated contribution are not optional. They are part of the deal if we are to defend jobs and sites.

## My response to the expert
Bring me the scenario matrix. I want:
- baseline cost gap,
- savings by measure,
- one-time restructuring costs,
- timing of savings,
- impact on return on sales through 2030,
- and the industrial-political risk of each scenario.

That is the right next step. But let me be plain: if the choice is framed as “closures or realism,” I reject that framing. Realism also means understanding that destroying the regional anchor may solve a short-term cost equation while creating a much larger long-term loss.
