## Where I agree

### 1) We need a real restructuring
Yes. No one is denying that the company has a cost and capacity problem. But a real restructuring does **not** automatically mean closing plants and threatening jobs. It means fixing the business without destroying the industrial base.

### 2) The burden cannot fall only on employees
I agree with the union, the state, and even parts of management on this point. If the company needs savings, then management, ownership, overhead, and capital allocation all have to contribute materially. Employees are not the sole shock absorber for strategic mistakes.

### 3) Numbers matter
I agree with the expert and the moderator that we need quantified comparisons. But let me be clear: numbers are not a license for blackmail. They are the basis for showing whether management’s preferred route is genuinely necessary, or whether it is simply the most aggressive option.

## Where I strongly disagree

### 1) I reject the framing that closures are the “financially legitimate fallback” by default
This is where I differ sharply from management, the family, investors, and the expert’s cautious framing.

A plant closure is not just a cost lever. It is:
- irreversible destruction of capacity,
- loss of skilled know-how,
- damage to supplier networks,
- and a signal that this company is willing to sacrifice its own industrial footprint rather than do the harder work of restructuring properly.

So no, I do not accept that closures become acceptable merely because management can draw a spreadsheet showing a faster margin effect.

### 2) I reject compulsory redundancies as a fallback
Again: no.

If management reserves the right to force layoffs when it does not get enough voluntary exits, that is not a negotiated restructuring. That is a threat regime. It destroys trust and makes every “partnership” claim hollow.

### 3) I reject the idea that 2030 guarantees are the problem
Several of you keep saying guarantees cannot be absolute if they block competitiveness. That argument is upside down.

The job-security framework is precisely what keeps this company socially stable through difficult transitions. If management wants flexibility, then it must earn it by offering binding protection in return. Otherwise, the message is: workers take the pain, management keeps the options, and everyone else calls that realism.

## On the expert’s scenario logic

I appreciate the effort to model the alternatives. But I reject the implication that if the non-closure path does not match the closure package line-for-line in financial effect, closures become acceptable by default.

That conclusion is too narrow. It assumes the only relevant value is short-term financial delta. It ignores:
- industrial strategy,
- social stability,
- governance legitimacy,
- the value of co-determination,
- and the long-term cost of destroying trust.

So yes, model the scenarios. But do not pretend the spreadsheet alone settles the matter.

### What a proper test must include
If management wants us to evaluate a non-closure package seriously, then the comparison must include:
- annual run-rate savings,
- one-time restructuring costs,
- timing of savings,
- impact on 2030 operating margin,
- and execution risk.

But it must also include:
- industrial conflict costs,
- loss of trust,
- risk to future cooperation,
- impact on recruitment and retention,
- and the damage from weakening the company’s social compact.

If those are not part of the model, then the model is incomplete.
