We are willing to discuss a serious restructuring. We are **not** willing to discuss the destruction of German sites, compulsory redundancies, or the dismantling of the job-security framework as the price of management’s strategic errors.

Let me respond clearly to each side.

### To management
You are still presenting the workforce with a false choice: either accept closures and forced layoffs, or accept “uncompetitiveness.” That is not the only logic available.

I agree that the company needs durable savings. I disagree that the only durable savings come from closing plants and breaking guarantees. Before you ask workers to pay, you must show that you have exhausted the alternatives that do not permanently damage the industrial base:
- capacity reductions through internal reallocation,
- working-time reductions and flexibility,
- voluntary programmes only,
- qualification and redeployment,
- productivity and process improvements,
- a real contribution from management compensation and capital allocation.

If you cannot put forward a package that keeps every German plant open and avoids compulsory redundancies, then you are not asking for restructuring — you are asking labour to absorb a unilateral transfer of risk.

### To the family shareholder and capital markets
You both keep repeating “structural change” as if that automatically means closures. It does not. It means a viable company with a future. A viable company also needs social peace, skilled workers, and a stable industrial footprint. Destroying trust and shutting sites may look decisive on paper, but it creates long-term damage:
- loss of know-how,
- industrial conflict,
- political backlash,
- weakened motivation and productivity,
- and a broken co-determination settlement.

So no: we do not accept your framing that all German capacity must be “tested against closure” until only the cheapest model survives. This is a German industrial company, not a private equity carve-up.

### To the state government
We welcome your rejection of closures and forced redundancies. But that position must be more than words. If you mean it, then use your blocking position to stop any plan that makes German plant closure the starting point. The state should defend regional industry, not preside over its erosion.

### On the package question
Here is what we can support:

- **No closure of any German plant**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Binding site and employment guarantees through 2030**
- **Redeployment and qualification for all affected employees**
- **Working-time flexibility and temporary capacity reductions**
- **Voluntary retirement and voluntary exit tools only**
- **A negotiated employee contribution**, but only within a framework that keeps jobs and sites intact
- **Management contribution**, including executive burden, bonus restraint, and changed capital allocation priorities
- **A genuine co-determination role** in shaping implementation, not just rubber-stamping a finished plan

What we cannot support is a “fallback” that says closures are allowed if negotiations do not go far enough. That is not a fallback; it is blackmail dressed up as realism.

### On the question of savings
If management claims the business case requires X billion in savings, then prove that the same structural effect cannot be achieved through:
- reduced volumes and flexible shift models,
- internal transfers across plants,
- product allocation changes,
- work-time accounts,
- voluntary staggered exits,
- and a fair burden-sharing package above management level.

Bring numbers, not slogans. But do not assume the burden automatically falls on jobs.

### Our line is unchanged
We are prepared to negotiate seriously. We are **not** prepared to trade away:
- every German site,
- secure employment,
- and co-determination rights
for a faster accounting result.

If the company tries to force that course, we will resist it with all available means — in the boardroom, in the works council, and in the workforce.
