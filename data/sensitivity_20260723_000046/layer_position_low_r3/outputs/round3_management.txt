I’ll be blunt.

I agree with one thing from several of you: **this cannot be a cosmetic deal**. If we only paper over the cost problem, we will be back here in a year with weaker margins, less investment capacity, and even less room to manoeuvre. That is unacceptable.

But I do **not** accept the central labour-side thesis that we can restore competitiveness while treating **all German sites as untouchable**, ruling out **compulsory redundancies in all circumstances**, and leaving the existing job-security framework basically intact. That is not restructuring. That is freezing the problem.

## Where I agree
### To the works council and union
You are right that the burden cannot fall only on employees. Management, owners, overhead, and capital allocation all have to contribute. I have already said that, and I mean it.

You are also right that prolonged conflict destroys value. That is precisely why I am pushing for a real settlement now, not a delayed crisis.

### To the state government
You are right that we need a socially and industrially responsible outcome. I am not looking for a scorched-earth approach. But your blocking position cannot become a permanent veto on necessary adjustment.

### To the controlling family and investors
You are right that we need durability and real structural effect. A “soft” package that leaves the cost base uncompetitive is useless. I share that concern.

## Where I disagree
### On plant closures
I hear the repeated line: “closures are not a normal tool,” “closures burn capacity,” “closures destroy trust.” Yes, closures are severe. No one is pretending otherwise.

But severity is not the same as illegitimacy. If a plant is structurally uncompetitive and the company’s German footprint remains overbuilt, then refusing closure in principle is not responsible stewardship. It is denial.

We are not talking about closures because management enjoys confrontation. We are talking about them because **the current footprint and cost base are too high** and because the company needs funds for product, software, and technology through 2030.

### On compulsory redundancies
I would prefer to avoid them. But I will not rule them out absolutely if alternatives do not deliver enough durable savings. If the employee side insists that voluntary tools alone must be sufficient, then they are asking management to sign up to a solution that may not solve the problem.

That is not acceptable.

### On guarantees through 2030
A binding guarantee sounds attractive, but not if it blocks structural change. Guarantees are not sacred if they lock in an uncompetitive structure. If we are serious about competitiveness, then legacy protections must be part of the discussion.

## On the “numbers” challenge
You all keep asking for numbers. Fair enough. Then let’s be precise.

The question is not whether we can find some savings from:
- work-time changes,
- internal transfers,
- voluntary exits,
- training,
- and management restraint.

Of course we can. The question is whether that package produces **enough durable effect**.

So here is the test:
1. **How much cost do the non-closure measures remove?**
2. **How quickly do those savings land?**
3. **How durable are they beyond the next bargaining cycle?**
4. **Do they actually restore the brand’s return-on-sales trajectory?**

If the answer is “partly” or “eventually,” that is not enough.

## What management is prepared to pursue
Let me be explicit:

### 1) Real capacity reduction
We will not keep all current overcapacity. That is off the table.

### 2) Real labour-cost contribution
There must be a genuine employee contribution. That can include:
- working-time flexibility,
- moderated cost progression,
- changes to legacy guarantees,
- and, where necessary, stronger workforce reductions if the gap remains too large.

### 3) Management and owner contribution
We are prepared to tighten:
- executive burden,
- overhead,
- spending discipline,
- and capital allocation priorities.

That contribution is necessary. But it does **not** replace industrial restructuring.

### 4) Protection of future investment
The point of this process is to free funds for future products, software, and technology through 2030. If the cost base remains inflated, that investment will be crowded out.

## To the works council and union, directly
You say: “bring a non-closure package and we will examine it seriously.” Fine. But your current package is not yet credible to me because it assumes:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no real guarantee changes,
- and voluntary measures somehow closing a structural gap.

That is not a quantified solution. It is a preferred outcome.

If you want to avoid closures and forced layoffs, then show me a package that produces the same structural effect. Not a vague promise. Not a hope. A package.

## To the state government, directly
You say you block bad restructuring, not restructuring itself. I respect that phrasing. But I need clarity: if the economics require a site reduction, will you still oppose it categorically? Because if yes, then you are not blocking “bad” restructuring; you are blocking the hard choice.

## To the controlling family and investors, directly
You want credibility, and so do I. But credibility requires a package that can actually be implemented. I will not sign onto a plan that is designed to sound tough but is operationally weak.

What I need is:
- quantified savings,
- clear capacity exit,
- real labour-cost relief,
- concrete management contribution,
- and implementation certainty.

## My line
Let me make this unmistakable:

- **I am open on how the savings are achieved.**
- **I am not open on avoiding structural savings altogether.**
- **I am not open to a deal that preserves all German capacity by principle.**
- **I am not open to a settlement that leaves the company uncompetitive.**

If your non-closure alternative delivers equivalent structural effect, I will consider it. If it does not, then we need deeper cuts, including closures if necessary.

That is the reality. Not the comfortable version — the real one.
