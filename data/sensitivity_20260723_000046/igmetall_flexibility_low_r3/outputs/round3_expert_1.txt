## Independent industrial assessment

Short answer: **yes, a closure-free restructuring can plausibly deliver a large share of the needed savings, but not always the full amount, and usually not as fast or as cleanly as a plant-closure package.** Whether it is sufficient depends on the size of the savings gap and the company’s plant utilization. For a large German OEM with structural overcapacity, the economic test is not ideological; it is arithmetic.

## What is likely true
Based on the scenario, the company appears to need:
- a **durable reduction in fixed costs**,
- **lower labor cost per vehicle**,
- and **better capacity utilization**.

Those savings can come from measures other than closures:
- voluntary exits and attrition,
- shorter shifts / working-time moderation,
- reduced overtime and premium pay,
- indirect labor cuts,
- management overhead reduction,
- product simplification,
- internal relocation of work,
- line consolidation within sites,
- and lower support-function cost.

So the works council’s position is not impossible in industrial terms.

## What is also true
A no-closure package usually has three limits:

1. **Slower savings realization**  
   Voluntary exits and redeployment take time. You do not get the same immediate fixed-cost removal as a closure.

2. **Residual overcapacity risk**  
   If installed capacity is materially above demand, keeping all sites open can leave too much fixed cost in the system.

3. **Execution discipline risk**  
   Site-preserving packages often fail when they are too vague, too dependent on goodwill, or too weak on enforcement.

That means a closure-free route can be credible, but only if the company is prepared to make **large, quantified, multi-party concessions**.

## What would have to be true for closure-free to work
A credible non-closure package would usually need:
- **meaningful management overhead cuts**,
- **owner support through lower distributions or bridge liquidity**,
- **material labor contribution** that is real, recurring, and measurable,
- **capacity reduction without full closures**,
- **portfolio simplification**,
- and **binding investment commitments** to protect future product allocation.

If labor contribution is symbolic and management/owners contribute only marginally, then the package is unlikely to close the economics.

## My judgment on feasibility
### Closure-free is plausible if:
- the savings target is moderate rather than extreme,
- plant utilization is not deeply below efficient levels,
- and the company can accept a longer restructuring horizon.

### Closure-free is doubtful if:
- the company needs a very large immediate reset of the fixed-cost base,
- German plants are significantly underutilized,
- or the current footprint is structurally larger than demand can support.

In those cases, closure-free may preserve industrial peace but fail to fully restore competitiveness.

## On timing
A closure-free path is usually:
- **quicker to negotiate** than a closure package,
- but **slower to deliver savings**.

In practice, that means:
- some savings can start within months,
- but the full effect often takes **1–3 years**, sometimes longer,
- while closures can deliver a more immediate footprint reset once implemented.

## Bottom line
If the question is **“Can savings comparable to a closure-based plan be achieved without plant closures?”** the answer is:

- **Sometimes yes, in principle**
- **often partly yes, in practice**
- **not always fully yes, especially if the overcapacity is structurally large**

The deciding factor is whether the company’s required savings gap can be closed by:
- management cuts,
- owner support,
- labor concessions,
- and network rationalization short of closures.
