We agree on the diagnosis: the current structure is not competitive enough, and a cosmetic deal would destroy value.

But we are **not** prepared to reward a restructuring that protects every site at the expense of margins, nor one that preserves too much fixed cost and then calls that “stability.”

## Where we agree
- **The status quo is unacceptable.**
- **The burden cannot fall on labour alone.**
- **Management, owners, and capital must contribute visibly.**
- **Prolonged conflict is value-destructive.**
- **A durable solution matters more than a short-term patch.**

That is the right baseline.

## Where we disagree

### 1) To the Works Council
We respect the insistence on protecting workers and capability. But your position is still too absolute.

A flat **“no closures, no compulsory redundancies”** line may be emotionally understandable, but it is **not sufficient** as a value-preserving restructuring concept. If the German footprint is structurally too large, then keeping every site open can simply lock in an uncompetitive cost base.

The expert has already said the key thing: a closure-free package can work **in principle**, but usually with:
- slower savings,
- higher execution risk,
- and often less than full equivalence to a hard footprint reset.

That means closure-free is **a possibility**, not a principle we can accept in advance regardless of the numbers.

### 2) To the Union
Your willingness to discuss bounded labour contributions is constructive. But it must be tied to **real, measurable savings**, not a symbolic sacrifice.

We would support:
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- and a restructuring contribution from labour,

but only if these are substantial enough to materially improve margins and are not just a delay tactic.

What we will **not** support is a package where labour gives up income while the company still carries too much excess capacity and the economics remain weak.

### 3) To Management
You are right that the company needs structural change. But you still owe the market a sharper answer on the numbers.

Do not ask us to accept “closures may be necessary” as a substitute for a business case. We need to know:
- the exact savings target,
- what comes from management cuts,
- what comes from owner support,
- what comes from labour,
- what can be achieved without closures,
- and what gap remains if closures are excluded.

Without that, “closures remain on the table” is just a negotiating posture.

### 4) To the State Government
We respect the governance structure and the regional role, but a blocking position cannot become a veto against economically necessary repair.

If a site-preserving package is to be credible, it must be **quantified** and **durable**. If it simply protects employment while leaving margins structurally impaired, then it is not saving value — it is postponing the loss.

### 5) To the Family Holding
You are right to reject managed decline. But the same applies to a socially explosive restructuring that triggers prolonged strikes and destroys execution.

We do not want “hardest possible cuts.” We want the **highest risk-adjusted value outcome**.

That means:
- lower fixed cost,
- real capacity reduction,
- visible management and owner contribution,
- and a path that actually restores return on sales.

If the closure-free route can deliver that, fine. If it cannot, then closure or deep site restructuring may be unavoidable. But that has to be proven, not assumed.

---

## Our financial bottom line
From a capital-markets perspective, we can support **either** of two outcomes:

### Acceptable:
A **closure-free package** that delivers:
- quantified structural savings,
- a meaningful reduction in labour cost,
- tangible overhead and management cuts,
- owner contribution,
- and a credible timetable to competitive margins.

### Also acceptable:
A **selective structural reset**, including closures if truly necessary, **but only** if it is:
- clearly justified by the savings gap,
- executable without becoming a prolonged industrial war,
- and paired with strong management and owner contribution.

### Not acceptable:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a deal that preserves too much excess capacity,
- or a “site-preserving” package that leaves margins structurally impaired.

That last point is the line for us. We will not support a settlement that protects jobs today by weakening the company’s ability to invest, compete, and generate returns tomorrow.

## On the proposed compromise frames
- **Time-bound no-closure test phase:** reasonable only if it has hard savings milestones and a credible enforcement mechanism.
- **Hybrid restructuring:** potentially workable, but only if it produces real, durable savings rather than deferred conflict.
- **Exchange package with labour contribution for binding security:** the most promising frame, but only if the economics are real and the security is not just rhetoric.

## What we need next
Before taking a firmer position, we need a quantified comparison of:
1. closure-based restructuring,
2. no-closure restructuring with strong labour/management/owner contributions,
3. hybrid package,

including:
- annual run-rate savings,
- timing to realization,
- one-time restructuring cost,
- execution risk,
- and expected margin impact.

## Final position
We are open to a negotiated solution.  
We are **not** open to a politically comfortable solution that leaves the company financially weak.  
And we are **not** willing to endorse an unconditional ban on closures before the numbers are proven.

Show us a package that gets the company back to competitive margins with discipline and certainty. Then we can support it.
