I will be equally direct.

The state government does **not** accept a restructuring logic that begins with German plant closures and compulsory redundancies as the default answer. That is not a neutral “business necessity” to us; it is a political and industrial decision with long-term consequences for the region, the workforce, and the company’s legitimacy.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family holding, investors, and the expert on several fundamentals:

- **The status quo is not acceptable.**
- **The company needs durable structural savings, not a cosmetic deal.**
- **The burden cannot fall on labour alone.**
- **Management, owners, and capital must all contribute visibly.**
- **A prolonged conflict would destroy value and damage the company.**

I also accept the expert’s core point: a closure-free package can be possible, but only if it is **quantified, material, and credible**, and only if the company is willing to accept a somewhat longer implementation path.

That is the right standard.

## Where I disagree
### 1) I do not accept closures as the starting point
Management is still speaking as if closures are the most “realistic” lever. That may be convenient from an execution standpoint, but it is not sufficient as a justification.

The expert did **not** say closures are inevitable. The expert said:
- non-closure restructuring can work in principle,
- it may cover a large share of the gap,
- and it depends on the size of the savings target and the quality of the package.

That means closures are **an option**, not a proven necessity. So I reject the attempt to frame them as the baseline.

### 2) I do not accept “voluntary only” as the whole answer, but I also do not accept “closures first”
To the works council and union: I understand the resistance to compulsory redundancies and closures. That resistance is legitimate. But a package also has to be economically real, not simply morally satisfying.

To management and investors: you cannot demand immediate acceptance of the hardest tools while refusing to fully test the alternatives. If you want the state to consider a more flexible position, you need to show a **quantified gap** after all non-closure measures are exhausted.

### 3) I do not accept the idea that co-determination is a problem to be worked around
The state’s role and the supervisory-board structure are not obstacles accidentally inserted into the system. They are part of the governance model and part of the company’s social contract. Anyone who treats them as an inconvenience to be overridden is misunderstanding the institution they are trying to govern.

## What would move the state
I am prepared to support a restructuring package if it contains all of the following:

- **clear quantified savings**
- **meaningful management and overhead cuts**
- **visible owner and capital contribution**
- **material labour contribution that is bounded and negotiated**
- **capacity reduction without a default assumption of plant closure**
- **binding investment commitments for the German sites**
- **a credible product and platform roadmap**
- **no compulsory redundancies as the first instrument**
- **full involvement of co-determination in implementation**

That is the kind of package I can defend politically and economically.

## What I cannot support
I cannot support:
- a plan centered on **German plant closures**,
- **mass job losses**,
- **forced redundancies as the first resort**,
- or a restructuring that weakens the company’s regional anchor role.

And I need to be explicit: I am not prepared to back a “site-preserving” label if, in practice, it means hollowing out the region through attrition, hidden deindustrialization, or a transfer of production away from the state.

## To management
You say the company needs a fast and durable reset. I accept that. But you still have not shown that closures are the only way to get there.

Before you ask for irreversible measures, show us:
- the exact savings target,
- the savings from overhead and management cuts,
- the savings from portfolio simplification,
- the savings from working-time and voluntary instruments,
- and the remaining gap.

If that gap remains large, then we can talk honestly about what additional tools are needed. But not before.

## To the works council
Your red lines are understandable, but I want to be equally direct: saying “no closures, no redundancies” is not enough unless you can help produce a package with **hard numbers**. The state will stand with you in resisting unjustified destruction, but we will not ignore economics.

The strongest position is not simply rejection; it is a credible alternative. If you want our full support, bring one.

## To the union
Your willingness to discuss bounded contributions is constructive. That is the right direction. But labour contributions must be matched by genuine management and ownership contributions. If the workforce gives and everyone else only speaks, that will not be accepted.

## To the family holding
You are right that managed decline is unacceptable. But stewardship also means not forcing a socially explosive solution that destroys the company’s regional base. A strong company does not build its future by burning trust in its core industrial location.

## To investors
You are right that cosmetic compromise is worthless. But you are wrong if you think only closures can demonstrate seriousness. A negotiated, quantified, site-preserving package can be credible if it is hard enough, fast enough, and enforceable enough.

## My bottom line
The state will support a **site-preserving restructuring** that is:
- economically credible,
- socially negotiated,
- and consistent with the company’s long-term role as a regional economic anchor.

We will **not** support a plan whose logic is:
1. closures first,
2. layoffs next,
3. and then hope the region absorbs the damage.

If management believes closures are unavoidable, then prove it after the non-closure alternatives have been fully quantified and tested — not before. Until then, the burden of proof remains on those proposing irreversible damage.
