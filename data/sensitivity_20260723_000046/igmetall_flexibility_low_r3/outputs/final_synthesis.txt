## 1. Decision context

The company faces a structural competitiveness problem: high German cost levels, overcapacity, weak margins, and a shrinking European market. The restructuring question is not whether change is needed, but whether the required savings can be achieved through a closure-free package, a hybrid package, or a deeper footprint reset including plant closures and compulsory redundancies.

Governance makes this unusually hard. Co-determination, the controlling family holding, and the regional state’s blocking position all create effective veto points. The discussion therefore centers on what kind of restructuring is economically sufficient, politically survivable, and institutionally enforceable.

---

## 2. Stakeholder assembly

- **Group & brand management**: drives restructuring and seeks durable cost and capacity reduction.
- **Works council**: plant-level workforce representation; strong co-determination role; rejects closures and compulsory redundancies.
- **Industrial union**: negotiates wages, can call strikes; aligned with no compulsory redundancies and no closures as a starting point.
- **Regional state government**: anchor shareholder and political actor; wants site-preserving restructuring, but not an empty one.
- **Controlling family holding**: majority voting-rights holder through governance; prioritizes competitiveness and value preservation.
- **Institutional investors**: dispersed capital-market shareholders; focused on risk-adjusted value and credible restructuring.
- **Independent expert**: assessed industrial feasibility of closure-free vs closure-based options.

Standing-weighted reading: management, family holding, state government, and the union/works council bloc all have strong effective blocking or agenda-setting power. The expert has no formal power but materially shapes the feasible option set.

---

## 3. Final positions

### Group & brand management
Management holds that the German footprint is structurally too large and the cost base too high to leave unchanged. It is open to negotiation, but insists that plant closures must remain available if non-closure measures do not close the savings gap fast enough. Management rejects an unconditional no-closure rule because it sees that as risking an economically insufficient settlement.

### Works council
The works council maintains an absolute red line: no German plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, no dismantling of job security, and no weakening of co-determination. It accepts the need for restructuring but insists that management has not yet proven that non-closure measures are insufficient. Its view is that the burden of proof lies with those proposing irreversible damage.

### Industrial union
The union is aligned with the works council on core red lines and is willing to consider bounded, temporary labour contributions only in exchange for real security. It rejects closures as the default bargaining threat and emphasizes that any workforce contribution must be matched by management restraint, owner support, and enforceable guarantees. It remains open to a serious deal if the contribution-security exchange is genuine and measurable.

### Regional state government
The state supports restructuring but rejects a plan that starts from closures and compulsory redundancies as the default answer. It wants a site-preserving solution that is economically credible and politically defensible, and is prepared to use its blocking position to prevent premature irreversible action. It also asks for quantified comparison before endorsing any path.

### Controlling family holding
The family holding agrees that the company needs a durable cost and capacity reset and that cosmetic compromise would destroy value. It does not accept an absolute no-closure principle, but also does not endorse closures as a slogan or first move. Its position is conditional: it would support a site-preserving deal if the numbers show it is hard enough to restore competitiveness.

### Institutional investors
Investors want durable restructuring, shared sacrifice, and value preservation, but not a weak settlement that merely delays the problem. They are not ideologically committed to closures, yet they insist on quantified proof that a closure-free or hybrid package actually restores margins and execution discipline. Their threshold is financial credibility, not political convenience.

### Independent expert
The expert concludes that a closure-free restructuring is possible in principle, but usually slower, less comprehensive, and riskier to execute than a closure-based reset. It estimates that strong no-closure packages may achieve only part of the savings of a hard footprint reset, while hybrid packages often offer the best balance. The expert’s crucial contribution is that the dispute is not feasibility in the abstract, but whether savings are large enough, fast enough, and durable enough.

---

## 4. Genuine conflicts

1. **Closure veto vs closure availability**  
   Labour and the state want closures ruled out as a starting point; management, family holding, and investors insist closures must remain available if needed.

2. **Security first vs economics first**  
   Labour wants binding site/job security before making concessions; management wants credible quantified savings before committing to strong guarantees.

3. **Absolute job protection vs structural capacity reduction**  
   Labour wants no compulsory redundancies and no closures; management argues that material workforce and footprint reduction may be required.

4. **Political legitimacy vs financial sufficiency**  
   The state and labour judge plans by social acceptability and industrial peace; capital judges them by return on sales, speed, and durability.

These are not merely rhetorical differences; they are structurally incompatible unless the savings gap can be closed through non-closure instruments.

---

## 5. Areas of convergence

- All parties accept that **restructuring is necessary**.
- All agree the burden **cannot fall on labour alone**.
- All accept that **management, owners, and capital must contribute**.
- All want to avoid a purely cosmetic deal.
- All recognize that **industrial conflict would be value-destroying**.
- All now accept the need for **quantified comparison** rather than principle-only bargaining.
- There is broad willingness to consider a **negotiated, hybrid, or time-bound test approach**.

---

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker

1. **Speed vs social stability**  
   Closure-based action likely yields faster fixed-cost relief, but raises conflict and implementation risk.

2. **Depth of savings vs preservation of sites**  
   No-closure packages preserve legitimacy and industrial peace, but may not fully close the structural savings gap.

3. **Irreversibility vs flexibility**  
   Closures are irreversible and politically costly; no-closure paths preserve optionality but can drift into under-delivery.

4. **Trust vs enforceability**  
   Labour wants trust-based guarantees; capital wants hard enforceable milestones. The settlement must convert trust into verification.

5. **Governance constraints vs strategic clarity**  
   Co-determination and blocking stakes make decisive restructuring harder, but also raise the cost of any plan that is not socially and politically durable.

---

## 7. Integrative pathways

### A. Time-bound no-closure test phase
A negotiated period with no plant closures and no compulsory redundancies, paired with quarterly savings milestones, management/owner contributions, and a hard review gate. This is the strongest bridge between labour’s security demand and capital’s need for proof.

### B. Hybrid restructuring
Prioritize voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time moderation, overhead cuts, and portfolio simplification; reserve targeted footprint action only if quantified thresholds remain unmet. This is the most plausible compromise if non-closure measures are partially but not fully sufficient.

### C. Security-for-contribution exchange
Cap labour contribution, but only in exchange for binding site/job guarantees, visible management restraint, owner participation, and investment commitments. This works best if the guarantees are enforceable and the contribution is temporary and measurable.

---

## 8. Open questions

- What is the **exact savings target** by year?
- How much can management cut through overhead, leadership, and operational simplification?
- What can the family holding and investors contribute through capital restraint or support?
- What is the realistic labour contribution in wages, flexibility, and working time?
- How large is the **residual gap** after all non-closure measures?
- What are the site-by-site utilization and fixed-cost realities?
- Can voluntary exits and redeployment absorb enough surplus labor?
- What execution risk and conflict cost should be assigned to each option?

## 9. Recommended next step

Hold a **numbers round** with a standardized comparison of three scenarios:

1. **Closure-based restructuring**  
2. **Closure-free restructuring** with quantified management/owner/labour contributions  
3. **Hybrid restructuring**

Each scenario should be compared on:
- annual savings through 2030,
- one-time restructuring cost,
- timing of savings,
- execution/conflict risk,
- and return-on-sales impact.

This is the only way to determine whether the no-closure option is a genuine solution or merely a slower path to the same unresolved problem.

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction

1. **The real dispute is not “whether change,” but “who bears irreversible risk.”**  
   That became clear only after multiple rounds: labour wants to avoid irreversible plant closures; management wants not to lock in a weak solution.

2. **No-closure is not the same as no restructuring.**  
   The expert made clear that a closure-free path can still be materially hard if it includes quantified labor, management, and owner contributions.

3. **The closure question is partly a sequencing problem.**  
   Labour wants security first; management wants proof first. The compromise space lies in time-bound tests and audited milestones.

4. **Conflict costs became a serious valuation factor.**  
   Initially the debate looked like a simple savings-versus-protection trade-off. The interaction showed that strikes, trust erosion, and implementation failure may erase the apparent benefit of hard closures.

5. **A hybrid package emerged as the most credible middle ground.**  
   Neither side accepted an unconditional extreme. The iterative discussion narrowed the realistic options toward staged, quantified, enforceable compromise.

6. **Governance structure matters as much as economics.**  
   The supervisory-board veto structure, family control, and state blocking position mean the decision is not just about optimal industrial design, but about what can actually be ratified and implemented.
