I will be equally direct.

I understand the red lines from the workforce, the union, and the state government: no one wants closures, forced redundancies, or a collapse of trust. And I agree on one essential point: **a restructuring that simply dumps the burden on labour would be unacceptable and ultimately value-destructive**.

But I also need to be clear where I disagree.

## Where I agree
- **The burden cannot fall on employees alone.**
  That would be short-sighted and would likely trigger prolonged conflict, reputational damage, and loss of know-how.
- **This must be a durable solution, not a cosmetic one.**
  A temporary patch that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive is not stewardship.
- **Germany remains strategically important.**
  The family holding has no interest in hollowing out the core asset or undermining its long-term industrial base.

## Where I disagree
- **A blanket “no plant closures, no compulsory redundancies” line cannot be the starting point if it makes the company structurally unviable.**
  I cannot support a position that rules out the very tools that may be necessary to restore competitiveness.
- **Site guarantees through 2030 cannot be unconditional if they prevent genuine restructuring.**
  A promise that blocks necessary change merely postpones the crisis and may worsen it.
- **The notion that all sites must be preserved regardless of economics is not credible.**
  If some capacity is permanently surplus, then that reality has to be addressed somehow.

## What I would consider acceptable
I am open to a negotiated solution if it meets three tests:

1. **Real structural improvement**
   - durable cost reduction,
   - lower complexity,
   - a footprint that matches market reality,
   - and clear productivity gains.

2. **Shared contribution**
   - labour contribution through working-time, flexibility, or moderated compensation,
   - management restraint,
   - and a visible contribution from the broader ownership and capital base.

3. **Stability and execution**
   - no endless conflict,
   - no managed decline,
   - and a credible path to investment in future products and technologies.

## On closures and job cuts
I do not say closures must happen. But I do say this plainly: **if comparable savings cannot be achieved through other measures with the same reliability and speed, then selective closures or deep site restructuring cannot be ruled out**.

What I would not support is:
- preserving all structures,
- refusing hard decisions,
- and asking owners to tolerate permanent margin erosion.

That would be a failure of governance.

## On the current positions
- To the **Works Council**: I hear the insistence on protection, but protection without competitiveness is not sustainable. If you want long-term security, the company has to survive and earn its cost of capital.
- To the **Union**: shared sacrifice is reasonable only if it is genuinely shared and results in a competitive company. If concessions merely delay deeper problems, then they are not enough.
- To the **State Government**: I respect the regional responsibility, but public and political concerns cannot override economic reality forever. A blocking position is not a substitute for a viable industrial plan.
- To **Management**: you are right to demand structural change, but you do not get a blank cheque for a socially blind approach. If you want support, you need a plan that is economically sound and institutionally stable.

## Bottom line
My position is not “close plants at any cost,” but it is also not “no closures under any circumstances.”  
I support **a credible restructuring that restores margins and competitiveness**, and I will back the measures necessary to achieve that **if** they are part of a durable settlement that preserves labour peace and the long-term value of the company.

If the alternatives do not deliver real structural savings, then I will not support a package that simply protects the status quo.
