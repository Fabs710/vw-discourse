I’ll be equally direct.

What I hear from the works council and union is a clear demand for security first, and from the state and owners a demand for competitiveness first. Management’s job is to make sure the company does not lose both. Right now, the German cost base is too high, the market is shrinking, and we do not have the luxury of pretending that symbolic concessions will fix a structural problem.

## Where I agree
I agree on three points:

1. **The burden cannot fall on labour alone.**  
   Management, the group, and owners must contribute. We will have to cut overhead, simplify structures, and put real investment behind the sites that remain.

2. **We need a durable solution, not a cosmetic one.**  
   A short-term peace deal that leaves capacity and costs basically unchanged would simply guarantee a worse crisis later.

3. **Germany remains strategically important.**  
   This is not about abandoning the country. It is about creating a German footprint that is actually viable in 2030.

## Where I disagree
### 1) “No closures, no redundancies” is not a credible starting point
I do not accept the claim that German plant closures are off the table from the outset. If we start from that premise, we are no longer restructuring — we are preserving overcapacity.

You have all said “the burden cannot fall on workers alone.” Fine. But if you rule out the measures that actually change the footprint, then you are asking management to solve a structural cost problem with temporary measures only. That will not work.

### 2) Site guarantees through 2030 cannot be unconditional
I will not agree to unconditional guarantees that prevent real restructuring. If a site guarantee blocks the savings needed to restore margins, then it becomes a promise to keep the company uncompetitive.

### 3) Voluntary instruments alone are not enough
Redeployment, working-time shifts, voluntary exits, retraining — yes, those are part of the package. But they are not a substitute for real capacity reduction if we still carry too much overhead and too much production footprint. We need structural savings, not just softer language.

## What I am prepared to put on the table
To be specific, management is prepared to consider a package with:

- **selective plant restructuring or closure where economically necessary**,
- **capacity reductions across the German footprint**,
- **a substantial labour-cost contribution**,
- **working-time flexibility and moderation of compensation**,
- **management overhead reductions and restraint**,
- **investment commitments for the sites that remain**,
- **retraining, redeployment, and social cushioning**.

But I need to be equally clear: these are not optional extras. They are the minimum ingredients of a viable deal.

## To the Works Council
You say closures are a threat, not a plan. I disagree. In a situation like this, they are a legitimate option if they are required to restore competitiveness.

You also say we have not shown that closures are the only way. That is correct: they are not the only way in every case. But you have not shown an alternative package that delivers equal savings with equal reliability and speed. That matters.

### What would move me
If you want me to rule out closures, then bring me a package that delivers:
- a **durable reduction in capacity**,
- a **real labour-cost contribution**,
- and **savings that are structurally equivalent** to site rationalisation.

Not promises. Not temporary pain deferral. Concrete numbers, durable effects, and execution certainty.

## To the Union
I hear your willingness to discuss moderation, bonuses, working time, and a restructuring fund. That is useful. But I cannot accept the condition that all of that only happens if closures are ruled out in advance.

That sequence does not work. We cannot guarantee the outcome before we know whether the measures are sufficient.

If labour wants to avoid compulsory redundancies and closures, then the package has to demonstrate that the economics still work. Until then, I will not tie management’s hands.

## To the State Government
I respect your regional responsibility, but “no one-sided closure strategy” is too vague. A company in this position needs more than political moderation. It needs a footprint that matches the market.

A blocking minority is not a business model. If public influence is used to prevent necessary restructuring, then it risks protecting jobs today by undermining them tomorrow.

## To the Family Holding
You are right that a managed decline is unacceptable. But stewardship also means backing the hard measures, not just the language of long-term value.

If you want market confidence, then we need a package that is visibly structural:
- lower fixed costs,
- less excess capacity,
- and a credible improvement in return on sales.

## To Investors
You are right to reject cosmetic compromise. The company needs front-loaded, measurable savings. That means management restraint, yes — but it also means labour-cost reductions and footprint adjustments that materially improve the economics.

## Bottom line
Management is not prepared to sign up to:
- unconditional site guarantees through 2030,
- a “no closures ever” rule,
- or a restructuring that depends only on temporary concessions.

We are prepared to negotiate a serious package, but it must include **real structural capacity reduction and durable labour-cost relief**. If the package does not do that, it is not a turnaround; it is delay.

If the other stakeholders want a deal, then bring a counterproposal that shows, in hard terms, how you achieve equivalent savings without closures and without compulsory redundancies. Otherwise, the reality is simple: the restructuring will have to include deeper interventions.
