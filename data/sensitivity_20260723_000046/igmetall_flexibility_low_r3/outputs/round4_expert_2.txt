### Independent industrial finance and plant-network assessment

I’ll answer the management-side question directly: **can the required savings be achieved without plant closures, and if so at what cost and with what risk?**

## Bottom line

**Possibly yes, but only if the savings gap is not too large and if all parties make material, quantified contributions.**  
In a large German automotive footprint, a closure-free package can usually capture a **substantial minority to a majority** of the savings from a hard footprint reset, but it typically:

- delivers **less total run-rate savings** than closures,
- takes **longer** to fully realize,
- and carries **higher execution risk** in the sense of undershooting target savings.

So the question is not whether non-closure restructuring can save money. It can. The question is whether it can save **enough, soon enough, and durably enough** to restore competitiveness.

---

## What is plausible industrially

A closure-free package can generate savings through:

- **headcount reduction via voluntary exits / early retirement**
- **working-time reductions or shift moderation**
- **overhead and management-layer cuts**
- **product and variant simplification**
- **procurement and SG&A reduction**
- **internal redeployment**
- **productivity gains**
- **owner / capital contribution** through lower distributions, balance-sheet support, or capex prioritization

That said, it usually cannot replicate all the savings from removing an entire plant if that plant is structurally underloaded.

### Reasonable estimate ranges
For a company in this situation, I would treat the following as **order-of-magnitude planning ranges**, not verified company numbers:

- **Closure-based restructuring:** 100% of the targeted footprint-related savings
- **Strong no-closure package:** roughly **50–80%** of that savings
- **Hybrid package:** roughly **70–90%** of that savings
- **Weak no-closure package:** roughly **20–40%**

These are broad industrial benchmarks, not a substitute for a site-by-site financial model.

---

## Why closures still matter economically

Plant closures are powerful because they eliminate **fixed-cost blocks** quickly:

- site overhead
- maintenance and utilities
- local management
- indirect labor
- logistics duplication
- underutilized capacity
- complexity from duplicated flows

If the problem is primarily **too much fixed plant capacity**, then avoiding closures leaves some structural costs in place.

If the problem is primarily **too much indirect labor, too many variants, and too much overhead**, then a no-closure package can get much farther.

So the key issue is the **composition of the savings gap**.

---

## Timing comparison

### Closure-based restructuring
Typical profile:
- first meaningful savings: faster
- full run-rate savings: often **12–36 months**
- conflict risk: higher

### Closure-free restructuring
Typical profile:
- some savings can start quickly if management/owner actions are immediate
- full run-rate savings: often **18–48 months**
- lower irreversible industrial damage
- but slower workforce reduction and more dependency on voluntary measures

So if management needs a very fast and deep fixed-cost reset, no-closure is weaker.

---

## What would need to be true for no-closure to work

A closure-free package is credible only if most of these are true:

1. **The savings gap is manageable**
   - If the gap is modest, no-closure is realistic.
   - If the gap is large and structural, it becomes difficult.

2. **Surplus labor can be absorbed**
   - through attrition, voluntary severance, early retirement, redeployment, and retraining.

3. **Working-time flexibility is real**
   - shift reductions, hour banking, temporary short-time, flexible scheduling.

4. **Management and owners contribute materially**
   - overhead cuts, executive restraint, capex discipline, dividend restraint or balance-sheet support.

5. **Product complexity is reduced**
   - fewer variants, fewer platform overlaps, fewer changeovers.

6. **Implementation discipline is strong**
   - milestones, transparency, and enforcement.

Without those, “no closures” becomes a slogan rather than a restructuring package.

---

## What makes a no-closure package fail

The main failure modes are predictable:

- voluntary exits are too small
- redeployment saturates
- productivity gains don’t materialize
- savings arrive too late
- too much overhead is left untouched
- plant utilization remains structurally low
- the company ends up with a softer but still uncompetitive footprint

In other words, a closure-free path can fail not because it is impossible, but because it may be **too weak or too slow** relative to the target.

---

## Industrial-financial judgment

### If the company’s savings target is modest to medium:
A no-closure package is **credible**, especially if management, owners, and labour all contribute.

### If the savings target is very large and the footprint is structurally oversized:
A no-closure package is **possible but unlikely to fully match** the savings of a hard reset.

### Best practical option:
A **hybrid package** is usually the most robust industrial solution:
- prioritize voluntary and internal measures,
- cut overhead and complexity aggressively,
- use targeted footprint changes only if truly necessary,
- and keep closures as a last-resort instrument rather than the opening move.

That tends to preserve more value than either extreme.

## Final assessment

**Can the required savings be achieved without closures?**  
**Sometimes yes, but not always fully.**

**Can a no-closure package be economically equivalent to a closure-based reset?**  
**Usually not if the overcapacity is truly structural and large.**

**What is the most likely feasible compromise?**  
A **hybrid package** with:
- strong management and owner contribution,
- bounded labour contribution,
- aggressive overhead and complexity reduction,
- voluntary workforce adjustment,
- and targeted footprint action only if the numbers truly require it.
