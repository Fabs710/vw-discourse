I will be just as direct in round 2:

**We do not accept plant closures in Germany. We do not accept compulsory redundancies. And we do not accept a restructuring that destroys the job-security framework and then asks the workforce to applaud “responsibility.”**

If management, investors, or owners want a solution, then they must stop talking as if closures and layoffs are the only serious tools available. That is not a plan; that is a threat.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on one point: **the company needs a durable solution, not a cosmetic patch**.  
I also agree that **the burden cannot fall on labour alone**. If the workforce is asked to contribute, then management, owners, and capital must contribute too. And I agree that prolonged conflict helps nobody.

But agreement ends there if the “solution” still means shutting plants, tearing up guarantees, and forcing people out.

## Where I disagree — and why

### 1) To management: “structural crisis” does not justify closure as a default
You keep saying the cost base is too high and capacity too large. We hear that. But you have not shown that German plant closures are the only way to address it.

There are still alternatives that must be exhausted first:
- capacity reductions through internal adjustment,
- working-time reductions and flexible shift models,
- temporary or negotiated wage moderation,
- redeployment across plants,
- voluntary exit packages,
- retraining and transfer measures,
- and a management contribution through overhead cuts and restraint.

If you come in with closures first, you are not solving the problem; you are shifting the damage onto workers and communities.

### 2) To the family holding and investors: financial discipline is not the same as social irresponsibility
You speak as if “value creation” automatically means closing sites and cutting jobs. That is too narrow and too short-sighted.

A company like this is not just a spreadsheet. It is:
- skills,
- suppliers,
- regional industrial ecosystems,
- long-term engineering capability,
- and trust built over decades.

Destroying that for a short-term margin fix can weaken the company’s future. You say co-determination does not change the arithmetic. I say **your arithmetic is incomplete if it ignores the long-term cost of conflict, skill loss, and broken trust**.

### 3) To the state government: your blocking position must be used to prevent irreversible damage
You are right to defend industrial employment and regional stability. But that means using your influence to stop any package that includes closures or forced layoffs. “No one-sided closure strategy” is too weak. We need a clear refusal of irreversible destruction.

### 4) To the union: shared sacrifice is only acceptable if it is bounded and conditional
We welcome the union’s support for job and site security. But let me be clear: employees should not be asked to pay for management’s failure through open-ended concessions.

Any contribution from labour must be:
- temporary or reversible,
- tied to binding guarantees,
- and matched by real commitments from management and owners.

## What we will accept
If you want a package that can even be discussed seriously by the workforce, it must include all of the following:

1. **Binding guarantees for all German plants through 2030**
   - no closures,
   - no relocation of production out of Germany,
   - and no hidden deindustrialisation by attrition.

2. **No compulsory redundancies**
   - workforce adjustment only through voluntary measures, retirement paths, internal transfers, and negotiated working-time tools.

3. **A real contribution from management and ownership**
   - overhead reductions,
   - executive restraint,
   - capital contribution to restructuring,
   - and a visible commitment to future products and investment in Germany.

4. **A genuine co-determination role**
   - the works council must be involved in shaping implementation, not presented with a finished plan after the fact.

5. **A credible industrial future**
   - investment in new models and technologies at the German sites,
   - not just promises to “consider” the future later.

## What is not acceptable
Let me state the red lines plainly:

- **No German plant closures.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No unconditional abolition of job-security guarantees.**
- **No fait accompli.**
- **No restructuring where labour pays and capital gets to call it responsibility.**

If management cannot show how savings are achieved without crossing those lines, then the proposal is not ready.

## On the claim that closures are “unavoidable”
That claim has not been proven. It is being asserted, not demonstrated.

So here is the challenge back to management and investors:

- Quantify the savings you believe are needed.
- Show what management itself is prepared to cut.
- Show what owners are prepared to contribute.
- Show what alternative capacity measures have actually been tested.
- Show why voluntary and negotiated instruments are insufficient.

Until then, closures and forced layoffs remain a choice — and a bad one.

## Practical next step
If you want a serious basis for negotiation, then bring back a **site-preserving package** with:
- quantified management savings,
- a capital contribution,
- investment commitments,
- and a 2030 security framework for all German plants and jobs.

If instead the next round is just a dressed-up version of “accept closures or take the blame,” then there is nothing to negotiate. In that case, expect continued resistance from the workforce, escalation through industrial action, and full use of our co-determination rights.

We are not here to manage decline. We are here to defend the industrial future of the German plants and the people who built this company.
