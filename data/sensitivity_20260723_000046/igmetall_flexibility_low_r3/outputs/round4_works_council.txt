## My position remains unchanged

- **No German plant closures.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No dismantling of the job-security framework.**
- **No weakening of co-determination in implementation.**

## To management

You say the workforce side is “refusing to engage with the actual scale of the problem.” That is simply not true.

We are engaging with it. What we refuse to do is accept your preferred instrument — closures and forced job cuts — before you have proven that every non-closure option is exhausted and insufficient.

The expert has confirmed the central point: **a closure-free restructuring is possible in principle**. That is the starting fact, not an aside. Your claim that closures must remain available is not evidence; it is your preference for the hardest lever.

What you still have not done is provide the hard numbers that justify irreversible damage:
- exact savings target,
- exact savings from management cuts,
- exact savings from overhead reduction,
- exact savings from portfolio simplification,
- exact savings from working-time and voluntary instruments,
- exact residual gap.

Until that is on the table, “closures may be necessary” is just a pressure tactic.

## To the family holding

You talk about competitiveness, margins, and value. Fine. But you continue to underprice the value destruction of breaking trust with the workforce and hollowing out German industrial capability.

A company does not become stronger by teaching its employees that historic guarantees can be discarded whenever the spreadsheet turns ugly. That produces:
- resistance,
- warning strikes,
- loss of know-how,
- weaker implementation,
- and a damaged social contract that will poison the next restructuring too.

So no, I do not accept your suggestion that preserving every site is somehow a refusal to deal with reality. The reality is that once you close a plant, you destroy a capability network, not just a cost line.

## To investors

You are right that cosmetic compromise is worthless. We agree on that.

Where we disagree is your apparent assumption that “harder” automatically means “better.” It does not. A closure-led plan can look cleaner on a slide deck and still destroy more value through conflict, execution failure, and long-term industrial damage.

The expert has already said what you need to hear: closure-free restructuring may be slower, but it can preserve capability and lower conflict risk. That is not weakness. That is industrial realism.

If you want to talk value, then price in:
- strike risk,
- disruption risk,
- reputational damage,
- loss of skill and apprenticeship capacity,
- and the long-term erosion of cooperation.

If you do that honestly, your certainty about closures should shrink.

If the state is serious about its regional responsibility, then it should not merely “support” a site-preserving outcome. It should use its blocking position to prevent irreversible damage unless and until non-closure alternatives are proven insufficient.

## To the union

We are aligned on the core red lines, but I want to sharpen one point:

“Bounded contributions” must never become the first step toward structural job destruction. The sequence has to be:
1. binding security,
2. then voluntary and reversible measures,
3. then implementation.

Not the other way around.

I will not endorse a package that says workers must first pay in wages, flexibility, or guarantees while management preserves the option to close plants later. That is not a bargain; it is leverage extraction.

## To management and capital on the compromise frames

### 1) Time-bound no-closure test phase
This is only acceptable if it is a **real no-closure commitment**, not a probation period for future shutdowns.

If the “test” simply means “give us concessions now and we may still close later,” then it is not a compromise. It is a delay mechanism for the same outcome.

### 2) No compulsory redundancies plus voluntary tools
This is the only structurally sensible direction, but only if it is treated seriously:
- voluntary exits,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time adjustments,
- product and portfolio rationalization,
- overhead reduction.

That is what restructuring looks like when you respect the workforce instead of threatening it.

### 3) Security-for-contribution exchange
This is the only frame that gets close to acceptable, but only if “security” is real:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no erosion of co-determination,
- no backdoor weakening of the job-security framework.

If those are missing, then there is nothing to exchange.

## What I agree with from the expert

I agree with the expert on one important point: a no-closure package must be **quantified, material, and enforceable**.

Good. That is exactly what we have been asking for all along.

But the expert also made clear that closure-free restructuring is **plausible** if the savings gap is manageable and contributions are real. That means management does **not** get to start from closure as the default and treat alternatives as an afterthought.

The burden of proof is on those proposing irreversible damage.

## What I reject

I reject the repeated attempt to redefine “responsible restructuring” as a package that keeps closures available from the outset.

From our side, that is not responsible. It is a hostage mechanism.

I also reject the idea that “voluntary only” is somehow unserious. Voluntary instruments, internal redeployment, reduced working time, and shared contribution can absolutely be part of a serious restructuring — if the company is willing to accept lower speed and real co-determined change.

That is the price of keeping industrial peace and preserving capability.
