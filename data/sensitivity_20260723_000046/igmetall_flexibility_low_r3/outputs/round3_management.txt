I’ll be blunt: the workforce side is still refusing to engage with the actual scale of the problem.

We all agree the company needs a durable solution. We all agree the burden cannot fall on labour alone. But “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no structural guarantee changes” is not a restructuring strategy. It is a demand that management solve a deep competitiveness gap while being barred in advance from using the tools that change the cost base.

## Where I agree
I agree with the expert on one point: a closure-free package can work in principle if the savings gap is not too large and if every side contributes materially. I also agree that conflict costs matter. Prolonged strikes would damage output, reputation, and the value of any settlement.

I also agree that management, owners, and capital must contribute. We are prepared to do that:
- overhead reduction,
- leadership restraint,
- simplification of structures,
- and investment commitments for the sites that remain.

That is not in dispute.

## Where I disagree
### 1) “No closures” cannot be an absolute starting point
The works council keeps saying closures are just a choice and not a necessity. That may be emotionally satisfying, but it is not enough economically.

If a plant network is structurally overcapacity and the cost base is materially above target, then selective closures or deep site restructuring remain legitimate options. You cannot simply declare them off the table and still expect a credible turnaround.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” is not enough if total employment must fall
You are using “voluntary only” as though it were a solution. It may be part of one, but not if the scale of the adjustment is large. A voluntary-only approach can easily be too slow, too uncertain, and too small to close the gap.

### 3) The sequence you demand is backwards
You want binding site guarantees first, then labour flexibility. We cannot accept that order.

Why? Because guarantees without proof of economic viability become a promise to preserve uncompetitive structures. The company cannot sign away its future before the savings are proven.

## On the works council’s latest argument
You say management is starting from a preferred solution and then justifying it afterward. No. Management is starting from the economic reality and testing which measures are sufficient.

You have not presented a quantified alternative that proves:
- the required savings target,
- the timing,
- the execution certainty,
- and the durability of savings
can be reached without closures or deeper restructuring.

Until you do, “site-preserving” is not a complete answer. It is a hope.

## On the union’s position
I appreciate that you are willing to discuss capped, temporary labour contributions. That is constructive.

But you still insist on:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no unconditional guarantee changes,
- and full protection of the collective framework.

Those red lines may be politically understandable, but they do not yet add up to a financially credible package unless the labour contribution is very substantial.

Let me be direct: if the contribution is only temporary wage moderation, bonus restraint, and a few flexibility measures, then it is unlikely to be enough. A serious restructuring needs durable cost reduction, not just a brief easing of pressure.

## On the state government
I respect the regional responsibility. But your role is not to freeze the industrial map in place.

You say closures are not the default. Fine. But you also say you won’t support “selective closures if necessary” before alternatives are tested. That sounds reasonable until you realize it can become a permanent veto against hard decisions.

The special governance structure exists to protect against reckless moves, not to block all economically necessary change.

## On the family holding and investors
I agree with you that cosmetic compromise is unacceptable.

I also agree that the company needs:
- real structural savings,
- capacity reduction,
- lower fixed costs,
- and a stronger return-on-sales path.

Where I diverge is this: some of you are willing to treat closures as the clean answer. I am not making closures the goal. I am saying they must remain available if the alternative package does not deliver equivalent results.

That is a very different position from “close plants first.” It is “do not rule out the measures that may actually solve the problem.”

## What I would accept
To be concrete, management is prepared to pursue a settlement if it contains:

- meaningful management and overhead cuts,
- a visible ownership contribution,
- labour-cost moderation that is real and measurable,
- capacity reduction across the network,
- and enough flexibility to restore competitiveness by 2030.

That package may or may not include closures. But I will not agree to a deal that excludes them in advance.

## What I will not accept
I will not accept:
- unconditional site guarantees through 2030,
- an advance ban on closures,
- a voluntary-only adjustment path that fails to deliver enough savings,
- or a package that delays the hard decisions and leaves the cost base uncompetitive.

That would be a cosmetic compromise dressed up as industrial peace.

## On the expert assessment
The expert has made the central point: closure-free restructuring can be plausible, but it is not automatically equivalent and often slower. That means the burden is on those arguing for a no-closure path to show hard numbers.

So here is my challenge back:
- quantify the savings from management cuts,
- quantify the savings from owner contribution,
- quantify the savings from labour contribution,
- quantify the savings from capacity reduction without closures,
- and show that the total is sufficient and timely.

If that can be shown, then we have a basis to talk. If not, then closures remain on the table as a necessary part of the solution.

## My bottom line
Management’s mandate is to restore competitiveness, not to preserve every site as-is.

We are open to negotiation. We are open to a serious labour contribution. We are open to site-preserving measures where they work.

But we are **not** prepared to pre-commit to:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and no structural footprint change.

Those are not principles of responsible restructuring. They are constraints that would make the restructuring fail.

If the other stakeholders want to avoid closures, then they need to bring a package that proves the economics work. Until then, selective closures and deeper restructuring remain necessary instruments, not taboos.
