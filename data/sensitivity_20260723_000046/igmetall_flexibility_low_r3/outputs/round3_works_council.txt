I’ll be very clear.

The moderator has correctly identified the core impasse, but I reject the implicit framing that the only serious question is how much of a closure package the workforce should tolerate. That is not the question. The question is whether management and owners are prepared to accept a **site-preserving, job-preserving restructuring** and stop treating German plants and workers as disposable cost items.

My answer remains unchanged:

- **No German plant closures.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No dismantling of the job-security framework.**
- **No settlement that makes labour carry the main burden while others merely “contribute” in words.**

## To management
You say closures are needed because the savings must be “large enough” and “fast enough.” That is exactly the problem: you are starting from a solution and then trying to justify it after the fact.

The expert has already said the crucial thing: **a no-closure path is plausible** if the company is willing to accept a longer horizon and real operational change. That is not a rejection of the no-closure option. It is a warning that you cannot have everything at once.

So let’s be honest about what you are actually demanding:
- faster savings,
- easier execution,
- and the right to shift the structural pain onto the workforce.

That is not inevitability. That is preference.

If management wants us to take your restructuring seriously, then bring a package that proves you have exhausted the non-closure options:
- overhead cuts,
- management restraint,
- portfolio simplification,
- working-time adjustments,
- voluntary exits,
- internal redeployment,
- and serious investment commitments.

Until then, closures are not “necessary”; they are simply the most aggressive option.

## To the family holding
You keep saying “trust the economics,” but your economics still ignore the long-term cost of destroying trust and capability.

A plant is not just a line on a balance sheet. It is:
- trained labour,
- supplier networks,
- know-how,
- apprenticeships,
- and regional industrial capacity.

Once you break that, you do not rebuild it easily. That is not sentimentality; it is industrial reality.

You say site guarantees cannot be unconditional. Fine — but the reverse is also true: **management cannot ask for unconditional worker sacrifice**. If you want labour flexibility, then you must offer binding guarantees, not a vague promise that maybe the sites survive if workers first give enough.

## To investors
You have taken the expert’s cautious wording and turned it into a justification for keeping closures on the table. That is not the same thing.

The expert did **not** say closures are inevitable. The expert said:
- no-closure packages can achieve a lot,
- the savings come slower,
- and the company must be willing to accept real labor and management contributions.

That supports negotiation, not a closure threat dressed up as financial discipline.

And let me be blunt: you are underestimating the value destruction of conflict. A strike-heavy, legitimacy-poor restructuring is not “risk-adjusted value.” It is self-inflicted damage.

## To the state government
You say you support a site-preserving restructuring, but you also say you cannot endorse “unconditional” security. That is too weak for the situation.

A state shareholder that claims responsibility for regional stability must do more than moderate the debate. It must use its blocking position to prevent irreversible damage. If you are serious, then you should say plainly:

- no closures as the basis of the plan,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and no weakening of co-determination.

Anything less leaves the workforce exposed while everyone else waits to see whether pressure works.

## To the union
We are aligned on the red lines. Where I would sharpen the point is this: “bounded concessions” must never become a backdoor to structural job destruction.

If labour is asked to contribute, the sequence must be:
1. binding site and job guarantees,
2. then limited, reversible labour measures,
3. then joint implementation.

Not the other way around.

And I will not endorse a package that says, in effect, “accept the cuts now and trust the company later.” We have seen what that trust is worth when historic guarantees are broken.

## On the facilitator’s proposed compromise frames
I reject all three as they are currently framed, because each one still smuggles in the possibility of closures, fallback closures, or conditional security that depends on labour first conceding the core issue.

### Proposal 1: time-bound no-closure test phase
A “test phase” sounds practical, but it is only acceptable if it is a **real no-closure commitment**, not a probationary period for future plant shutdowns. I will not agree to a plan where workers give concessions today and closures return tomorrow if some target is not met.

### Proposal 2: hybrid restructuring with closure fallback
No. A fallback mechanism for closures is still a closure mechanism. That is not a compromise from our point of view; it is a delayed threat.

### Proposal 3: exchange package linking labour contribution to binding security
This is the only frame that moves in the right direction, but only if “binding security” actually means:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no erosion of co-determination,
- and enforceable commitments, not political language.

If those guarantees are missing, then there is nothing to exchange.

## What I would consider
I remain open to a serious package, but only on these terms:

- **All German plants stay open.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **Workforce adjustments only through voluntary, negotiated instruments.**
- **Management and owners contribute materially and visibly.**
- **Co-determination is preserved in implementation.**
- **Investments and future models are committed to the German sites.**
- **Any labour contribution is capped, temporary, and reversible.**

That is the only basis on which the works council can even begin to discuss internal adjustment.

## What is not acceptable
Let me repeat the line management still has not crossed but is trying hard to blur:

- “selective closures”
- “deep site restructuring”
- “closure fallback”
- “conditional guarantees”
- “site-preserving if economically possible”

These are all different ways of saying: **the workforce must accept that some German plants may still be sacrificed**.

I do not accept that premise.

## My direct challenge
If you believe closures are unavoidable, then prove it with hard numbers **after** exhausting the non-closure options, not before. Show:
- exact savings needed,
- exact savings from management cuts,
- exact savings from portfolio and overhead simplification,
- exact savings from voluntary exits and internal redeployment,
- exact savings from labour cost moderation,
- and only then, if you still claim a gap exists, explain why.

Until that evidence exists, closure is not a necessity. It is a political choice.

## Bottom line
The workforce will not be bounced into accepting closures, forced redundancies, or the destruction of job security under the banner of competitiveness.

If management and owners want a deal, they should stop asking whether we can be persuaded to accept closures, and start answering the real question:

**How will you achieve the savings without closing plants, without forced layoffs, and without breaking the company’s social contract?**

That is the standard. Anything less is not a solution.
