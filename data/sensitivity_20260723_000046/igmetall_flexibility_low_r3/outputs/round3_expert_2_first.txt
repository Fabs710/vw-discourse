Here is the industrial-finance answer, stripped of bargaining language.

## Short conclusion

A **closure-free restructuring is possible in principle**, but only if three things are true at once:

1. the company’s required savings gap is **not so large** that only footprint removal can close it;
2. management, owners, and labour all make **quantified contributions**;
3. the package accepts that a no-closure path usually means **slower cash savings, more execution risk, and some permanent labour-cost adjustment**.

If management’s real requirement is a **very large, immediate, structural cost reset**, then a closure-free package is often **not financially equivalent** to closures. In that case, avoiding closures may preserve social capital but can also leave the company with insufficient savings, slower deleveraging, and ongoing capacity overhang.

The key point: **the answer depends on the size of the gap**. Without the company’s exact target, I cannot say closure-free is definitely sufficient. But I can say what a credible non-closure package would need to look like and what its limits are.

---

## What determines feasibility

For a large carmaker in Germany, the major cost levers are typically:

- headcount and overtime reduction,
- site overhead and plant support costs,
- labor flexibility and working-time arrangements,
- material and procurement savings,
- product complexity reduction,
- manufacturing efficiency and yield,
- and capital expenditure discipline.

If the company is carrying **structural overcapacity**, then savings from just wage moderation are usually not enough. You must also take out:

- fixed plant overhead,
- indirect labor,
- support functions,
- and complexity in the product/plant network.

That does **not automatically require a full plant closure**, but it often requires one or more of:
- partial line consolidation,
- shifting products between sites,
- capacity reduction via shorter shifts or reduced lines,
- and very substantial voluntary workforce exits.

---

## What a closure-free package would need to contribute

A plausible non-closure package usually requires contributions from all sides:

### 1) Management contribution
Management must typically deliver:
- deep overhead cuts,
- lower capex where not strategically essential,
- portfolio simplification,
- procurement and logistics savings,
- management pay restraint,
- and often a visible reduction in white-collar/support costs.

In a large OEM, management-side and non-production savings might realistically cover a **meaningful but not dominant** part of the gap. Think in the range of **hundreds of millions per year**, not multiples of billions unless there is major portfolio simplification.

### 2) Owner contribution
Owners can contribute through:
- accepting lower near-term distributions,
- supporting recapitalization or bridge funding if needed,
- tolerating a slower path to margin recovery,
- and backing a multi-year restructuring instead of forcing a one-year reset.

This matters for liquidity and covenant headroom, but owners do not create operating savings by themselves. They mainly buy time.

### 3) Labour contribution
If closures are off the table, labour usually has to contribute through some mix of:
- temporary wage restraint,
- reduction/removal of bonuses or supplementary payments,
- longer working-time flexibility,
- lower shift premiums,
- reduced guaranteed hours,
- voluntary exits,
- and suspension or redesign of legacy protections.

Important: if the labor contribution is only “symbolic,” the package usually fails on economics. For a large carmaker, labour contribution needs to be **material, recurrent, and measurable**.

---

## What non-closure restructuring can realistically achieve

A closure-free package can often achieve savings through:

- **voluntary exits and attrition**: effective, but slower;
- **working-time moderation**: useful for cash flow, but limited;
- **shift and line rationalization**: can be substantial if utilization is low;
- **indirect labor cuts**: often a major lever;
- **plant network balancing**: moving production without closure;
- **complexity reduction**: very important, but takes time.

However, there are limits:

- If a plant is **deeply underutilized**, keeping it open can preserve fixed costs that a closure would remove.
- If multiple plants perform similar functions, a no-closure package may still leave too much installed capacity.
- If products are low margin or near end-of-life, a closure may be the only fast way to remove the cost base.

So the non-closure option is strongest when the issue is **cost and utilization**, but weaker when the issue is **durable excess footprint**.

---

## The usual trade-off versus closures

### Closure-driven restructuring
Pros:
- faster removal of fixed costs,
- clearer capacity reset,
- stronger immediate margin improvement,
- easier to align production with demand.

Cons:
- high one-time cash costs,
- disruption to output and quality,
- loss of capability and know-how,
- supply-chain disturbance,
- industrial conflict risk,
- reputational damage,
- and possible longer-term value destruction if demand recovers or electrification strategy changes.

### Closure-free restructuring
Pros:
- lower disruption,
- better retention of know-how,
- less conflict and strike risk,
- more social stability,
- less one-time cash burn from severance and shutdown costs.

Cons:
- savings come more slowly,
- execution depends on discipline and cooperation,
- savings may be smaller,
- risk of “half-restructuring”,
- may not fully solve overcapacity.

---

## My judgment on the specific question

### Can the required savings be achieved without closures?
**Possibly yes, but only if the required savings are within the range that can be produced by:**
- strong management cost cuts,
- meaningful owner support,
- significant labour concessions,
- and aggressive network rationalization short of full closures.

### When would I doubt it?
I would doubt feasibility if:
- the company needs a **very large step-change** in annual fixed-cost base;
- German utilization is materially below efficient levels;
- the product mix cannot absorb current site structure;
- or the company needs savings quickly enough that voluntary measures cannot deliver in time.

In that case, a closure-free plan may be **politically attractive but financially incomplete**.

## What would I need to judge this robustly

To say whether closure-free restructuring is enough, I would need:

1. **current German fixed cost base**
2. **plant-by-plant utilization**
3. **annual volume by site and model**
4. **EBIT / contribution margin by product line**
5. **headcount split: direct, indirect, management**
6. **labor cost per vehicle and per hour**
7. **required annual savings target**
8. **one-time restructuring cash cost**
9. **expected demand outlook by platform**
10. **capex commitments required for EV transition**

Without those, anyone claiming “closures are unavoidable” or “closures are unnecessary” is talking too generally.

## Practical bottom line

From a plant-network and industrial-finance perspective:

- A **closure-free package can be credible** if the company is mainly trying to close a cost gap and improve flexibility.
- It becomes **much less credible** if the company has a **structural capacity surplus** that cannot be removed through labor and overhead measures alone.
- A no-closure deal is usually **slower, more complex, and more dependent on trust and enforcement**.
- A closure-based deal is usually **faster and more decisive**, but carries much higher conflict and long-term capability destruction risk.

So the real test is not ideology. It is arithmetic:

> **Can management, owners, and labour together close the savings gap through non-closure measures, and do so within the time the business can afford?**

If yes, then closure-free restructuring is economically defensible.  
If no, then avoiding closures may preserve peace now but leave the company under-restructured and still uncompetitive later.
