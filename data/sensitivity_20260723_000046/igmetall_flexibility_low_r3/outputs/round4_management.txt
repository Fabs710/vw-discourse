I’ll respond plainly.

The works council and union are still asking management to accept a “restructuring” that leaves the core cost problem structurally unresolved. I do not accept that. If the company is to remain competitive to 2030, we need **durable, material cost and capacity reduction**. A package that preserves every site, rules out compulsory redundancies, and bans closures in advance may feel safer, but it does **not** yet prove it closes the savings gap.

## Where I agree
I agree on three points:

1. **The burden cannot fall on labour alone.**  
   Management, owners, and capital must contribute visibly.

2. **A cosmetic compromise is useless.**  
   If the deal only buys time and leaves the cost base uncompetitive, we will be back in crisis.

3. **Conflict costs matter.**  
   A prolonged strike would damage output, reputation, and trust. That is not in anyone’s interest.

## Where I disagree
### 1) “No closures” is not a serious starting point
The works council keeps saying closures are just a choice. That is too convenient. We are not talking about sentiment; we are talking about a company with structural overcapacity and a cost base above target. If the savings gap is large enough, **selective closures or deep footprint reductions must remain available**. I will not rule them out in advance.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” does not solve the economics
The union’s willingness to discuss temporary contributions is constructive, but a voluntary-only approach may simply be too slow and too small. If employment must fall materially, then insisting on voluntary tools alone may leave the company with insufficient savings and too much excess capacity.

### 3) Guarantees first, economics later is the wrong sequence
The workforce says it wants binding security before discussing flexibility. I understand the desire, but management cannot sign away all restructuring tools before seeing a credible economic package. **Security must be earned by a package that actually restores competitiveness.**

## On the expert’s assessment
The expert was clear: a closure-free path is possible **in principle**, but only if:

- the savings gap is manageable,
- management, owners, and labour all make quantified contributions,
- and the company accepts a slower, more complex implementation.

That does **not** prove that a no-closure package is sufficient for this company. It only proves it is not impossible.

And that is exactly the point: the burden is now on those opposing closures to show a package that is not just morally appealing, but **arithmetically sufficient**.

## What I need to see
If the other side wants to keep closures off the table, then bring hard numbers:

- exact savings target,
- exact management savings,
- exact owner/holding contribution,
- exact labour contribution,
- exact savings from working-time and voluntary instruments,
- exact savings from overhead and portfolio simplification,
- and the residual gap.

Without that, “no closures” is not a solution. It is a demand that the company hope the numbers work out later.

## My position on the compromise frames
### Time-bound no-closure test phase
This is only acceptable if it is a **real test with binding metrics**, not a disguised veto on future structural action. If the company is expected to give up closures entirely while waiting for voluntary measures to maybe work, that is not a deal I can support.

### Hybrid restructuring
This is the most realistic route if it produces **real capacity reduction** and **real cost improvement**. But if the hybrid model is just a softer label for preserving excess footprint, then it fails the test.

### Exchange package with labour contribution for security
This is the right logic in principle: contributions in return for security. But “security” cannot mean an unconditional promise to preserve every site regardless of economics. It has to be tied to a package that genuinely closes the cost gap.

## To the works council
You asked how we will achieve savings with all German plants open. My answer is: **show me a package that proves it can be done at scale**. Until then, I will not pretend that plant closures are an absurdity. They are part of the available toolkit if the economics require them.

## To the union
I welcome a bounded labour contribution. But it must be **material, measurable, and durable**. If it is only symbolic, it does not solve the problem. Wage restraint alone will not fix structural overcapacity.

## To the state government
I respect your role and your blocking position. But that does not mean the company can be forced into an uneconomic settlement. A site-preserving solution is acceptable only if it is financially credible. If not, it becomes managed decline.

## To the family holding
You are right to reject cosmetics. But you are also right only if you are prepared to support **real structural measures**, not just slogans about competitiveness. That includes difficult footprint decisions if the numbers require them.

## To investors
You are right to demand risk-adjusted value. But value is not protected by freezing the footprint and hoping conflict disappears. If the cost base remains too high, the market will punish that just as surely as restructuring pain.

## Bottom line
I remain open to a negotiated solution. But it has to satisfy one condition:

> **It must deliver a durable reduction in cost and capacity sufficient to restore competitiveness by 2030.**

If a closure-free package can truly do that, quantify it and bring it forward. If it cannot, then closures and deeper restructuring remain on the table. I will not accept a settlement that is politically easier but economically too weak.
