Yes — from an industrial-operations and restructuring standpoint, **comparable savings can sometimes be achieved without plant closures, but only if the gap is moderate and the company is willing to accept a longer implementation timeline and more operational complexity**.

In this specific kind of situation, my assessment would be:

## Bottom line
- **If management needs a very large, durable cost reduction quickly**, then **avoiding closures entirely is usually difficult**.
- **If the required savings are significant but not extreme**, then a **no-closure package can plausibly close much of the gap** through a combination of:
  - capacity rationalization within sites,
  - reduced shifts and line-speed changes,
  - voluntary departures,
  - internal redeployment,
  - working-time moderation,
  - wage restraint / temporary concessions,
  - overhead reduction,
  - and capital/management contributions.

But:
- **the savings are usually slower to realize**,
- **the execution risk is higher**,
- and **the total structural savings are often somewhat smaller than a hard footprint reduction** unless there is very strong labor cooperation.

## What is realistic without closures
A closure-free package can deliver meaningful savings in four buckets:

### 1) Workforce cost reduction
This is the biggest lever if negotiated well.
- voluntary exits / early retirement
- attrition management
- no-replacement hiring freezes
- reduced overtime and agency labor
- temporary wage moderation
- bonus reduction / conversion into deferred or contingent elements
- more flexible shift patterns and working time accounts

**Typical effect:** material, but not immediate.  
**Timing:** 12–36 months for most of the savings, depending on legal and bargaining conditions.

### 2) Capacity reduction without site closure
You can reduce output materially while keeping the sites open:
- one-shift operations instead of two or three
- line consolidation within plants
- product-family simplification
- lower variants / fewer derivatives
- transfer of some lines or modules between plants without full closure
- removing redundant tooling and support functions

**Typical effect:** real savings in fixed overhead and indirect labor, but not as deep as shutting an entire plant.  
**Timing:** 12–24 months if product planning allows it; longer if model cycles are fixed.

### 3) Overhead and complexity reduction
Often underestimated.
- engineering simplification
- purchasing savings
- administrative headcount reduction
- shared service consolidation
- simplification of plant governance and support functions
- lower logistics complexity and inventory

**Typical effect:** significant, but requires strong central execution.  
**Timing:** 6–24 months.

### 4) Product and portfolio actions
- fewer variants
- smaller option matrix
- platform sharing
- model-line rationalization
- more standardization across brands or derivatives

**Typical effect:** can be very powerful, but it depends on future product cycles, so it is not instant.  
**Timing:** 2–4 years for full impact.

## The main constraint
The key question is not whether savings exist in theory — they do. The question is whether they are:

1. **large enough**
2. **fast enough**
3. **durable enough**
4. **credible enough to markets, investors, and lenders**

A closure-free solution tends to be strongest when:
- the company has **excess labor and complexity more than excess physical capacity**,
- there is **room to reduce shifts and utilization** without destroying the industrial base,
- and labor is prepared to make **bounded, quantified concessions**.

It is weakest when:
- a plant is structurally underutilized,
- fixed costs are high,
- the product mix is too narrow,
- or the required savings are so large that only footprint reduction can deliver them.

## Comparable savings: my estimate
Without seeing internal numbers, I would frame it this way:

- **A closure-free package can often capture roughly 50–80% of the savings of a severe plant-closure scenario** if labor and management both contribute meaningfully.
- **Getting to the full 100% equivalent is possible in some cases, but uncommon** and usually requires:
  - very strong concessions,
  - high voluntary exit volumes,
  - major complexity reduction,
  - and acceptance of a slower ramp.

In other words:  
**closure-free is plausible, but not automatically equivalent**.

## Timeline comparison
### If plants close
- Savings begin faster
- Structural fixed-cost reduction is clearer
- But execution risk is high: strikes, disruption, legal/political resistance, talent loss, supplier disruption
- Typical meaningful savings: **12–36 months**, depending on closure mechanics

### If plants remain open
- Lower disruption risk if negotiated
- But savings accumulate more gradually
- Typical meaningful savings: **18–48 months**
- Full run-rate benefits often take longer because you must work through labor law, redeployment, product cycles, and operational reconfiguration

## What would need to be true for a no-closure deal to work
A closure-free settlement would need all of the following:

1. **Quantified savings target**
   - The company must define the required annual run-rate savings clearly.

2. **Hard workforce contribution**
   - Not symbolic. Real reductions in cash cost and staffing load.

3. **Capacity reduction inside the network**
   - Even without closures, output must fall where demand is weak.

4. **Management and owner contribution**
   - Overhead, governance, and capital discipline must visibly share the burden.

5. **Product and investment commitments**
   - Otherwise labor will see only sacrifice, not a future.

6. **Sequenced implementation**
   - Immediate cost actions first
   - Product/portfolio changes next
   - Full structural simplification over the model cycle

## My practical judgment
If the company’s target is a **modest-to-material restructuring**, then **yes, a negotiated no-closure path can work**.

If the target is a **deep reset of a structurally uncompetitive German footprint**, then **no-closure is possible only if the labor contribution is unusually large and the company accepts a longer time horizon**. In many cases, that becomes harder than a selective closure.

## Final conclusion
So my assessment is:

- **Plausible?** Yes, in principle.
- **Equivalent to closures?** Sometimes, but not reliably.
- **Fast enough for a severe crisis?** Often not.
- **Most likely outcome if closures are fully off the table?** A larger negotiated package, but with either:
  - lower total savings than management wants, or
  - slower realization, or
  - both.
