I will be direct.

The state government is prepared to support a **serious restructuring**, but not a restructuring that is simply a euphemism for **German plant closures, mass job losses, and the erosion of co-determination**. Those are not acceptable starting points for us, and they are not acceptable end states either.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family holding, and investors on one fundamental point: **doing nothing is not an option**. The company needs a durable turnaround, and it needs to restore competitiveness.

I also agree with the works council and union that **the burden cannot fall on the workforce alone**. If employees are asked to contribute, then management, owners, and capital must make visible contributions too.

And I agree that a cosmetic compromise would be dangerous. A deal that merely postpones the problem would leave the company weaker in 12–24 months.

## Where I disagree
### 1) Plant closures are not the default solution
Management keeps presenting closures as if they were the natural consequence of economic reality. They are not. They are a decision. And for a company of this importance, with strong co-determination and a regional anchor shareholder, that decision must be justified with far more than “it is easier and faster.”

If there are still credible alternatives that preserve sites and jobs while delivering structural savings, they must be exhausted first.

### 2) Unconditional site and job guarantees are not enough if they are only rhetorical
I hear the workforce demand for security, and I understand it. But guarantees must be paired with an economically viable plan. At the same time, management cannot ask for concessions first and security later. That sequence will not build trust and will not be accepted.

### 3) A “blocking minority is not a business model” line misses the point
To the extent that this was directed at the state: our role is not to block necessary change for its own sake. Our role is to prevent irreversible damage to the regional economy and to ensure the company remains rooted here.

That role is legitimate, and it is part of the governance structure by design. Sidelining it would not create confidence; it would destroy trust.

## What I can support
I am open to a package with the following features:

- **capacity reduction** where it can be done without closing viable German plants,
- **voluntary workforce adjustment** rather than compulsory redundancies,
- **working-time flexibility** and negotiated labour-cost moderation,
- **management overhead cuts and restraint**,
- **executive contribution** and a visible owner contribution,
- **retraining, redeployment, and internal transfers**,
- **binding investment commitments** for the sites that remain,
- and a **credible product roadmap** for the German plants.

That is the direction I consider defensible.

## What I cannot support
I cannot support:
- a package centered on **German plant closures**,
- **compulsory redundancies** as a first resort,
- a restructuring that **gut​s regional employment**,
- or one that weakens the state’s co-determination and veto role.

If management wants our support, it must come back with a proposal that shows:
1. how the necessary savings are achieved,
2. how German jobs and sites are protected as far as economically possible,
3. how management, owners, and labour all contribute,
4. and how the company’s industrial future in the region is secured.

## My response to each party

### To Management
You have not yet proven that closures are unavoidable. You have argued that they are expedient. That is not the same thing. Before you ask for irreversible measures, show us the alternatives you have tested and why they fail. If there is a serious site-preserving package, I want to see it.

### To the Works Council
Your red lines are understandable, but I need to be honest: saying “no closures, no redundancies” without offering a quantitatively credible alternative will not be enough in the end. If you want the state to stand firmly with you, then we need a package that is economically convincing, not only morally right.

### To the Union
Your willingness to discuss bounded contributions is constructive. That is the right direction. But concessions from labour must be matched by real commitments from management and owners. Otherwise this becomes another case of employees paying to preserve mistakes made elsewhere.

### To the Family Holding
You are right that managed decline is unacceptable. But stewardship also means not forcing the company into a socially explosive restructuring that destroys the industrial base in Germany. Long-term value is not protected by burning trust.

### To Investors
You are right that cosmetic compromise is not enough. But you are wrong if you think that only closures can deliver credibility. A negotiated package with genuine structural changes, management restraint, and labour flexibility can also be credible — if it is serious.

## Bottom line
The state is prepared to work on a **site-preserving restructuring** that is socially negotiated and economically credible.

We are **not** prepared to endorse a plan whose core logic is:
- closures first,
- layoffs next,
- and then hope the region absorbs the damage.

If management wants support, it must bring a **quantified alternative** that protects plants and jobs in Germany as far as economically possible, shares the burden fairly, and secures the company’s long-term role as a regional economic anchor.

If that cannot be done, then I will not help legitimize a destructive shortcut.
