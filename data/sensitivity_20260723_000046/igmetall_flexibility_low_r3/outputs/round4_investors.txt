We agree on the **goal**: the company needs a durable restructuring that restores competitive margins and protects long-term value. We also agree that a cosmetic settlement would be worse than no deal.

But from a capital-markets perspective, the current discussion still has a problem: **too much of the burden of proof is being shifted onto investors and the market while the operating side avoids quantifying the gap.** We do not need another round of principled statements. We need numbers that show whether a closure-free package actually gets the company back to competitive margins.

## Where we agree

### With management
You are right that the status quo is not acceptable. If the German cost base remains structurally above target, then margins will stay impaired and the equity will remain under pressure.

You are also right on one critical point: **a settlement that only buys time is not enough**. The market will punish delay dressed up as peace.

### With the expert
The expert has provided the right framing: closure-free restructuring is **possible in principle**, but not always sufficient, and usually slower. That is exactly the issue. We are not ideologically attached to closures; we are attached to **risk-adjusted value creation**. If the economics work without closures, fine. If they do not, then a no-closure line becomes value-destructive.

### With the family holding
You are right that the company cannot preserve every structure and call that a strategy. Capital will not support permanent overcapacity. We also agree that value destruction from prolonged conflict matters and must be priced in.

### With the state government
You are right to insist that conflict risk and execution risk matter. A closure package that triggers prolonged industrial action can destroy more value than it creates.

## Where we disagree

### To the Works Council and Union
We respect the social and industrial logic of your position, but your current stance is still too absolute for us to support as a final outcome.

A blanket **“no closures, no compulsory redundancies”** line may be understandable, but it is **not yet a financially sufficient answer**. If the footprint is structurally too large, then refusing in advance to consider plant closures can lock in a cost base that stays uncompetitive and depresses shareholder value for years.

The expert has already said what matters: the feasibility of a closure-free package depends on the **size of the savings gap** and the **materiality of the contributions**. Until those are quantified, an absolute ban is not a credible value-preserving position.

That said, we also do **not** accept the opposite extreme: management cannot simply use “closures may be necessary” as a rhetorical weapon without proving that the alternative package fails.

## What we need to see from management

This is the key gap in the discussion. Management needs to quantify:

- the current annual savings target,
- the savings from overhead and management cuts,
- the savings from procurement, complexity reduction, and portfolio simplification,
- the savings from labor measures and voluntary exits,
- the contribution from owners in the form of lower distributions / support / balance-sheet flexibility,
- the timing of each measure,
- and the **residual gap** if closures are excluded.

Without that, “closures remain on the table” is not a business case. It is a posture.

## Our view on the compromise frames

### 1) Time-bound no-closure test phase
This is the most interesting proposal, but only if it is genuinely hard-nosed.

We would support a **time-bound no-closure phase** only if it includes:
- hard quarterly savings milestones,
- transparent reporting,
- material contributions from management, owners, and labor,
- and a review gate that is based on audited numbers, not sentiment.

What we will not support is a test phase that simply delays hard decisions while savings remain unproven.

### 2) Hybrid restructuring
This is potentially workable if it produces real savings and not just deferred conflict.

But a “hybrid” package that still leaves too much fixed cost in the system, or merely postpones the closure question, will not restore market confidence. Hybrid only works if it is numerically credible.

### 3) Exchange package with labor contribution for security
This is the most promising frame in principle, because it matches concessions to enforceable security.

However, “security” must be more than language. If the package is to avoid closures, then the guarantees and the savings need to be strong enough to justify that decision economically.

## Our hard line

We will **not** support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a settlement that preserves excess capacity and erodes margins,
- or a “site-preserving” deal that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

We will also **not** support an outcome where investors are asked to bankroll a peace deal that does not credibly restore returns.

## What would move us

We could support a closure-free or hybrid settlement if it shows:

1. **Material management and overhead cuts**
2. **Visible owner contribution**
3. **Substantial but bounded labor contribution**
4. **Meaningful capacity reduction**
5. **A credible timetable to return to competitive margins**
6. **Execution discipline with quarterly milestones**
7. **Protection against a prolonged strike war**

If that package closes the gap, we do not need closures. If it does not, then closures or deeper footprint reduction remain legitimate tools.

## Final position

Our position is simple:

- We support **durable restructuring**.
- We support **shared sacrifice**.
- We support **site-preserving solutions only if they are financially sufficient**.
- We do **not** support an unconditional no-closure rule without proof that it restores competitiveness.

So the next step should not be more argument about red lines. It should be a **quantified comparison** of the three packages:
1. closure-based,
2. no-closure with quantified contributions,
3. hybrid.
