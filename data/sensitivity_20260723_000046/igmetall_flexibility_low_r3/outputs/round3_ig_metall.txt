I’ll respond directly.

## Where I agree
I agree with the expert on one important point: **a closure-free restructuring can be possible in principle** if the savings gap is not extreme and if management, owners, and labour all make **real, quantified contributions**. That is not new to us — it is exactly why the union has already been willing to discuss bounded labour contributions.

I also agree that a cosmetic compromise is useless. If the company only buys time and leaves the cost base basically untouched, then we will be back here in a year with the same conflict, only more damaged.

And I agree that management, owners, and capital must contribute visibly. The workforce will not accept being the only side asked to carry the restructuring.

## Where I disagree
### 1) I do not accept the implication that closures are the default “realistic” answer
The expert was careful, but some others are already using that caution to keep closures on the table as if they were the natural baseline. They are not.

A no-closure path is not a fantasy. It is a serious option if:
- management cuts overhead and executive costs,
- owners support the restructuring financially,
- labour makes a bounded contribution,
- capacity is reduced through working-time and line adjustments,
- and the product/portfolio is simplified.

That is a real restructuring, not a soft patch.

### 2) I do not accept “we can’t rule out closures” as a negotiating principle
That phrase is doing too much work. It is a way of keeping the threat alive while asking the workforce to concede first. We reject that sequence.

If management wants a credible non-closure settlement, then it must show:
- the required savings target,
- the share coming from management and ownership,
- the share coming from labour,
- and why those measures are insufficient without closures.

Until then, closures are not a proven necessity; they are an unproven option being used as pressure.

### 3) I do not accept that the burden can be left uneven
The expert said a no-closure package can work only if labour, management, and owners all make quantified contributions. That is exactly right.

But let me be equally clear: **“contribution” is not the same as labour bearing the pain through wage restraint while capital keeps its flexibility and management keeps its privileges.**  
If the burden is not shared in substance, then it is not shared at all.

## To management
You say the company needs a “very large, immediate, structural cost reset.” Fine — then show the numbers.

So far, what we have is:
- a demand for deep savings,
- a refusal to preclude closures,
- and a broad claim that alternatives are too slow or uncertain.

That is not yet an argument; it is a preference for the hardest lever.

If you want us to take non-closure restructuring seriously, bring a **quantified package** with:
- management overhead reductions,
- executive restraint,
- portfolio simplification,
- working-time moderation,
- voluntary exits,
- internal transfers,
- and investment commitments.

Then we can judge whether the gap remains.

### What I am not prepared to do
I will not endorse a path where management says:
- “we need structural change,”
- but the structural change is mainly **job losses and closure threats**,
- while labour is asked to prove its goodwill first.

That is backwards.

## To the family holding
You speak about value and competitiveness, but you are underestimating the value destruction of destroying trust.

A company does not become stronger by turning its core workforce into a variable cost to be broken whenever margins tighten. That may produce a nicer short-term spreadsheet, but it weakens the social and industrial base that makes execution possible.

You say a no-closure package may be slower and less certain. True. But a closure-driven conflict is also slower, riskier, and expensive — just in a different way. If you want “risk-adjusted value,” then you must price in:
- strikes,
- plant disruption,
- loss of skills,
- reputational damage,
- and the long-term erosion of cooperation.

That is not a side issue. It is part of the economics.

## To the state government
You are right that guarantees must be paired with economic viability. But I think you are still too cautious in how you frame your role.

If the state is serious about industrial stability, then it should support a package that is:
- site-preserving,
- free of compulsory redundancies,
- and based on negotiated capacity reduction.

That is not the same as unconditional protection. It is a responsible industrial policy position.

What I do not want to hear is the state helping to normalize “selective closures if necessary” before the alternatives have been fully tested. That is just opening the door wider for management.

## To institutional investors
You ask for risk-adjusted value. Good. Then measure the risks honestly.

A strike-heavy restructuring with weak legitimacy is not value creation. If you push for closure simply because it is theoretically cleaner, you may get your saving faster on paper and lose more in execution and trust.

The expert has given you the key answer: closure-free restructuring can deliver a lot, though usually more slowly. That means the question is not whether it is perfect. The question is whether the company can afford a negotiated path that preserves capability and avoids social rupture.

My answer: **yes, if the burden is shared fairly and the savings are real.**

## On the proposed compromise frames
I remain skeptical of all “hybrid” formulations that keep closures in reserve as a fallback. From the workforce perspective, a fallback closure is still a closure threat. That is not a neutral technicality; it shapes bargaining power and trust.

If management wants labour flexibility, then it must give **binding security first**:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no erosion of the collective wage framework,
- and real investment commitments.

Then labour can discuss:
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- working-time adjustments,
- voluntary exits,
- and a restructuring contribution fund.

That sequence matters. We will not accept concessions first and security later.

## My position, unchanged but concrete
I remain open to a settlement if, and only if, it contains all of the following:

1. **No German plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Workforce adjustment only through voluntary, negotiated tools**
4. **Meaningful management cost cuts**
5. **Visible owner/holding contribution**
6. **Binding investment and product commitments for German sites**
7. **A capped, temporary, reversible labour contribution**
8. **Protection of the collective bargaining framework**

If those conditions are met, then the union can discuss a serious restructuring contribution from employees.

If they are not met, then the other side is not asking for shared sacrifice — it is asking labour to pay for a solution it did not create.

## Bottom line
The workforce is not blocking restructuring. We are blocking a **one-sided restructuring**.

If management and owners want a durable settlement, they should stop treating closures and forced layoffs as the benchmark of seriousness and start proving that a negotiated, site-preserving package cannot work.

Until then, our position stays where it is:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of collective protections,
- and no agreement that makes workers bear the main burden.
