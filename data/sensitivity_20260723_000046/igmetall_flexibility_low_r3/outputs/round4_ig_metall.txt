I will be equally direct.

## Where I agree

I agree with the works council on the **core red lines**:

- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No plant closures as the starting point or hidden fallback.**
- **No solution where labour pays first and everyone else contributes later in rhetoric only.**

I also agree with the expert that a **closure-free restructuring is possible in principle**, but only if the package is **quantified, material, and enforceable**, and only if management, owners, and labour all make real contributions.

That is exactly the kind of framework the union has already signaled openness to.

---

## Where I disagree

### 1) Management is still treating closures as the default “serious” option
That is not a neutral assessment; it is a preference for the hardest lever.

You keep saying closures must remain available. From the workforce side, that is not a technical footnote. It changes the entire bargaining climate because it means concessions are made under threat. We do not accept that as a legitimate basis for settlement.

If closures are to be discussed at all, then only **after**:
- management cuts,
- owner contribution,
- portfolio simplification,
- working-time adjustments,
- voluntary exits,
- and internal redeployment  
have all been fully quantified and shown insufficient.

Until then, closures are not proven necessity. They are bargaining pressure.

### 2) “Voluntary only” is not a trick, but it must be real and sufficiently large
I do not accept the management line that voluntary measures are automatically too weak. That depends on the scale, design, and sequencing.

A serious package can use:
- voluntary exits,
- partial retirement,
- internal redeployment,
- shift reductions,
- working-time moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- and temporary wage contribution,

if and only if it is backed by **binding site security** and meaningful management/owner participation.

What I will not accept is a fake “voluntary” package that is clearly designed to fail so closures can be reintroduced later.

### 3) The state government is still too cautious
I appreciate the state’s support for a site-preserving solution. But “support” is not enough if management is still pushing closure logic into the room.

If the state wants to be taken seriously as a stabilizing shareholder, then it should use its blocking position to insist on a **closure-free restructuring test phase** first, not merely say it prefers one.

### 4) The family holding and investors are underweighting conflict costs
You both speak as if the only question is whether a closure package gives faster savings on paper. That is incomplete.

A restructuring that triggers prolonged warning strikes, operational disruption, damaged trust, and loss of know-how can easily destroy the very value it claims to create.

So yes, value matters. But **value-adjusted execution risk** matters too. If you ignore that, you are not being financially disciplined; you are being one-dimensional.

---

## On the expert’s assessment

The expert’s answer is the right one:  
**a closure-free restructuring can work, but only if the savings gap is manageable and the contributions are real.**

That means the burden is now on management and capital to show, in hard numbers:

- what the required savings gap actually is,
- how much management can cut,
- how much owners will contribute,
- how much labour can reasonably contribute,
- and what remains after that.

If they cannot show that closures are still required after those measures, then they have no case for plant closures. Not a rhetorical case. A real one.

---

## To management

You say you need a “fast and durable reset.” Fine. But you still have not proven that closures are the only way to get there.

What you have shown is:
- a preference for speed,
- a preference for lower execution complexity,
- and a willingness to shift structural pain onto the workforce if needed.

That is not the same as inevitability.

If you want labour to consider a contribution, then bring a package with:
- management overhead cuts,
- executive restraint,
- portfolio simplification,
- operating efficiency gains,
- and a clear owner contribution.

Then we can judge whether a closure-free path closes the gap.

---

## To the works council

We are aligned on the fundamentals. Where I want to sharpen the point is this:

“Binding security” must not become a vague promise. It must be **enforceable** and **sequenced correctly**.

The sequence has to be:
1. binding protection of jobs and sites,
2. then bounded labour contribution,
3. then implementation.

Not the other way around. We will not ask workers to subsidize uncertainty.

And I want to be explicit: if the company wants wage restraint or flexibility, that is only negotiable in exchange for **real site security**, not “we’ll see later.”

---

## To the state government

If you are serious about industrial stability, then say plainly:

- no closures as the first resort,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and a structured no-closure test with hard metrics.

If the package fails the test, then we talk again. But the burden of proof must be on those proposing irreversible damage.

That is a legitimate and responsible governance position.

---

## To the family holding

You are right about one thing: managed decline is unacceptable.

But a closure-driven confrontation that tears up trust and industrial peace is also not a serious stewardship strategy. A company does not become more competitive by teaching its workforce that guarantees are disposable whenever margins tighten.

If you want resilience, then you need:
- durable relations,
- credible commitments,
- and shared sacrifice.

Not a one-sided reset dressed up as discipline.

---

## To investors

You are right to reject a cosmetic compromise.

But I reject the implication that only closures are “hard enough” to be credible. A real package can be hard on all sides without being destructive:
- labour contribution,
- management restraint,
- owner support,
- capacity reduction through non-closure tools,
- and binding investment commitments.

That is not softness. That is the only way a durable agreement is likely to survive implementation.

---

## What I would support

The union remains open to a serious settlement if it contains all of the following:

1. **No compulsory redundancies**
2. **No plant closures during the negotiated period**
3. **A binding job and site security framework**
4. **A capped, temporary labour contribution**
5. **Meaningful management overhead and leadership cuts**
6. **Visible owner contribution**
7. **Investment commitments for German sites**
8. **Protection of the collective wage framework**
9. **Enforceable milestones and transparency on savings**

That is a real restructuring package.

---

## What I will not support

I will not support:
- a deal that keeps closures as a standing threat,
- a package that makes labour carry the main burden,
- or a restructuring that treats wage standards and job security as expendable.

If the other side cannot live with that, then they are not asking for a fair compromise. They are asking labour to underwrite a solution it did not create.

---

## On the need for a specialist

I would support a focused expert input here:

[SUMMON: independent industrial conflict and bargaining implementation specialist to assess whether a quantified no-closure package with management cuts, owner contribution, and voluntary workforce adjustment can be implemented without triggering major conflict or backdoor layoffs.]

We need that not because we lack principles, but because implementation risk matters. A deal that looks sound and collapses in practice is no deal at all.

---

## Bottom line

The union is not blocking restructuring. We are blocking a **one-sided restructuring**.

If management and capital want a durable deal, they should stop treating closures and forced layoffs as the standard of seriousness and start proving, with numbers, that a closure-free path cannot work.

Until then, our position remains:

- **no compulsory redundancies,**
- **no plant closures,**
- **no weakening of collective protections,**
- and **a fair shared-sacrifice settlement only if all sides contribute materially.**
