The expert’s input is useful, and it broadly confirms what we have been saying: **a no-closure path can work only if the savings are large enough, the labor contribution is real, and the implementation timeline is accepted as longer and more complex.**

From our side, that is not a problem in principle. What matters is **whether the package gets the company back to competitive margins with sufficient certainty**.

## Where we agree
We agree with management, the family holding, and the expert on the key financial point:

- **The current German cost base is not competitive.**
- **A cosmetic compromise would destroy value.**
- **The burden cannot fall on labor alone.**
- **We need structural, not temporary, savings.**

We also agree with the union and works council on one practical point:
- **Prolonged open conflict is value-destructive.**
A strike-heavy, uncertain process would hurt margins and execution regardless of the final shape of the deal.

## Where we disagree
### 1) We do not accept “no closures” as a principle
The expert was careful, and rightly so: closure-free restructuring can sometimes deliver 50–80% of the savings of a hard footprint reset, but not always 100%, and often more slowly.

That is exactly why we will not support a blanket rule that rules out closures in advance.

If the company’s required savings are at the high end of the range, then selective closures or deep site restructuring may still be necessary. We are not prepared to subsidize an uncompetitive structure just because it is politically easier to defend.

### 2) We do not accept a deal that trades savings for delay
The expert’s timeline matters a lot. A closure-free package is typically slower:
- meaningful savings in 18–48 months,
- full benefits often longer.

That may be acceptable if the savings are still robust and the plan is credible. But if the deal simply postpones the hard decisions while preserving too much fixed cost, then it is not a turnaround. It is deferred underperformance.

### 3) We do not accept unconditional site/job guarantees if they block economic repair
We understand why the workforce wants security. But from an equity-holder perspective, guarantees are only valuable if they are consistent with sustainable economics.

If guarantees lock in excess cost, then they may protect jobs in the short term while eroding them in the medium term through weak margins, lost investment capacity, and strategic drift. That is not acceptable to us.

## Our reaction to each stakeholder

### To the Works Council
You are right that skills, trust, and regional ecosystems matter. But you are wrong to treat plant closures as inherently illegitimate.

Your position becomes acceptable only if it is paired with a package that can be shown, in hard numbers, to restore margins. Otherwise, “protect every site” is just a slogan with a price tag borne by shareholders.

### To the Union
Your willingness to discuss bounded concessions is constructive. But the concessions must be:
- durable,
- measurable,
- front-loaded enough to matter,
- and tied to actual cost reduction.

We do not want a temporary wage pause that leaves the structural problem untouched. If the package depends on labor giving up value, then we need evidence that the burden is producing a real and durable return.

### To Management
You are right to insist on structural change. But you need to be more specific and disciplined:
- What is the required savings target?
- What is the minimum acceptable timeline?
- What savings come from overhead, labor, product simplification, and capacity reduction?
- What is the fallback if the closure-free package underdelivers?

Without that, the market cannot assess whether this is a credible turnaround or an optimistic negotiation tactic.

### To the State Government
We respect the regional responsibility, but public influence cannot become a veto against necessary economic repair. “Site-preserving” is only defensible if the sites are economically sustainable after the restructuring.

### To the Family Holding
You are correct that managed decline is unacceptable. But stewardship also means backing the hard economic logic, not just the language of long-term value. If the numbers require deeper action, then we expect the ownership block to support it.

## What we would support
We would support a package if it contains:

- **quantified structural savings**
- **clear capacity reduction**
- **meaningful labor-cost contribution**
- **management and overhead cuts**
- **visible owner contribution**
- **credible investment commitments**
- **a realistic timeline to restore margins**

That package may be closure-free if the numbers truly work. But it must be proven, not assumed.

## Our bottom line
The expert has made the key point: **closure-free restructuring is plausible, but not automatically equivalent**. That means the burden of proof is now on those arguing for a no-closure solution.

If you can show:
- the savings are large enough,
- the timeline is acceptable,
- and the execution risk is manageable,

then we can back a negotiated package.

If you cannot, then we will not support a settlement that preserves too much capacity and leaves margins structurally impaired.

**We are not looking for the cheapest political compromise. We are looking for the highest risk-adjusted value outcome.**
