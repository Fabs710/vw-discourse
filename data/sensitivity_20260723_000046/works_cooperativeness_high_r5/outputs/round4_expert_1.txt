## 1) Core assessment

From an industrial-restructuring perspective, the key question is not whether closures are desirable, but whether the company can close its **structural cost and capacity gap** through non-closure measures quickly enough and durably enough.

### In general
- **Non-closure packages** can often capture a substantial share of savings, especially through:
  - voluntary exits
  - retirement windows
  - redeployment
  - shift consolidation
  - working-time reductions/flexibility
  - internal transfers
  - procurement and overhead cuts
  - product/volume reallocation
- But they usually do **not** fully eliminate:
  - fixed-site overhead
  - duplicated logistics and utilities
  - underutilized equipment and maintenance costs
  - excess indirect labor
  - complexity costs from too many lines/sites

### Closure-based measures
- Usually produce the **largest and most durable fixed-cost reset**
- But they carry:
  - higher one-time costs
  - longer lead time
  - greater execution and continuity risk
  - higher industrial-relations disruption
  - possible capacity and supply-chain transition risk

## 2) Decision rule: when is closure operationally justified?

A closure or site exit becomes operationally rational only when all three are true:

1. **Non-closure measures have been exhausted or quantified as insufficient**
2. The remaining **residual gap** is still material relative to site economics
3. The company can execute the transition without unacceptable continuity risk

### Practical threshold concept
I would treat a residual gap as “material” if, after all feasible non-closure measures:
- the site remains meaningfully above the target cost position, and/or
- the site cannot contribute competitively to the future product plan even after investment,
- and the shortfall is large enough that the company’s return on sales target remains out of reach.

Because we do not have the company’s actual baseline, I can only provide a rule of thumb:
- If non-closure measures can close **most but not all** of the gap, then a further capacity adjustment may be justified.
- If they close the gap only marginally, closure is more likely to be required.
- If they close the gap fully or nearly fully, closure is not operationally necessary.

## 4) Like-for-like comparison of options

### Option 1: Full non-closure package
**Typical savings**
- Moderate to high
- Often enough to reduce a large share of the gap

**Timing**
- Faster to launch
- Savings often phased over 12–36 months

**Risks**
- May not fully reset fixed costs
- Can become temporary if underlying footprint remains too large

**Best use**
- When the site is structurally viable but overstaffed or underutilized

### Option 2: Internal capacity reduction without closure
Examples:
- shift consolidation
- line rebalancing
- product reassignment
- function relocation
- internal site specialization

**Typical savings**
- Higher than pure non-closure
- Lower than full closure

**Timing**
- Medium-term

**Risks**
- Requires precise industrial planning
- May still leave excess fixed overhead

**Best use**
- When one site can be made viable by shrinking scope rather than exiting

### Option 3: Partial line exit / footprint reduction
Examples:
- remove a product line
- close one building/line
- transfer part of production elsewhere

**Typical savings**
- Material
- Usually stronger structural reset than internal adjustments

**Timing**
- Medium to long

**Risks**
- Transition complexity
- Logistics disruption
- Asset write-downs
- Potential underutilization of the remaining footprint

**Best use**
- When only part of the site is non-competitive

### Option 4: Full plant closure
**Typical savings**
- Highest fixed-cost reduction

**Timing**
- Longest lead time
- Often 18–48 months depending on tooling, product transfer, labor process, and supply-chain redesign

**Risks**
- Highest industrial-relations risk
- Highest continuity risk
- Highest one-time cost
- Potential loss of know-how and flexibility

**Best use**
- When the site has no viable long-term role even after all realistic adjustments

## 5) Execution-risk assessment

Closure-based restructuring is often economically attractive on paper but operationally fragile in practice. Key risks:

- delayed transfer of tooling and production
- loss of experienced labor during transition
- quality issues at receiving plants
- supplier disruption
- unplanned downtime
- litigation or labor conflict causing schedule slippage
- underestimation of severance, redundancy, and remediation costs

Non-closure packages carry different risks:
- insufficient savings
- temporary rather than durable savings
- internal inconsistency if too many guarantees are layered on top
- slower response to market deterioration
