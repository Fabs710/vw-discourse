I will be direct.

## My position remains unchanged on the essentials
As the state shareholder, I will **not** support any restructuring that:
- guts employment in Germany,
- makes plant closures the default answer,
- or sidelines the state’s co-determination and veto role.

At the same time, I do **not** support a frozen status quo. The company needs a credible, durable restructuring. But it must be one that preserves the company as a regional industrial anchor, not one that sacrifices that role for a quicker balance-sheet fix.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family holding, and the investors on one point: **cosmetic compromise is worthless**. The company has a real competitiveness problem, and a settlement that merely postpones it would be irresponsible.

I also agree with the expert that this discussion has to become **quantified and plant-specific**, not rhetorical.

And I agree that:
- voluntary exits,
- retirement pathways,
- redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- training/qualification,
- and real productivity measures

must all be part of the package.

## Where I disagree
### On plant closures
I do **not** accept closure as a normal or early instrument here.  
The expert did not prove that closures are unavoidable; the expert said they are the strongest cost reset, but also the highest-risk and highest-friction option in this governance setting.

That matters. In this company, a closure is not just an industrial measure. It is a political and social rupture with long-term consequences for trust, the region, and the company’s legitimacy.

So my position is:
- **closures are not the starting point**,
- **they are only conceivable after a serious alternatives-first test**,
- and only if the residual gap truly cannot be closed otherwise.

I will not support closure talk becoming the new normal.

### On compulsory redundancies
I reject them. Full stop.

If management’s model depends on forced layoffs, then management has not yet earned support for that model. Compulsory redundancies would inflict unnecessary damage on the social compact that makes this company viable in Germany.

### On absolute guarantees
I also hear the employee side’s demand for blanket guarantees through 2030. I understand the purpose, but I will be candid: an absolute guarantee of every site and every structure can become too rigid if it freezes genuine restructuring.

So I am **not** endorsing a blank cheque in either direction:
- not blank cheque for closures,
- not blank cheque for permanence.

What I do require is a restructuring that is **site-protective, job-protective, and investment-backed**.

## What I would support
I can support a negotiated package if it meets these conditions:

1. **A quantified, plant-by-plant comparison** is produced first.
2. **Non-closure measures are tested seriously and exhausted** before any closure decision.
3. **Voluntary schemes and internal adjustments** do the heavy lifting.
4. **Management and owners contribute visibly** through overhead reduction, executive restraint, procurement discipline, and real industrial commitment.
5. **Investment and future-product allocations** are bound to German sites.
6. **Co-determination and the state’s veto role remain intact.**
7. Any workforce contribution is **temporary, bounded, and matched by guarantees**.

That is a fair framework. It is also the only one I can defend politically and economically.

## My response to management
You have not yet shown that all alternatives fail. Saying “the footprint is too large” is not enough. Show me the actual bridge:
- current structural gap,
- non-closure savings,
- management savings,
- owner contribution,
- product allocation,
- residual gap.

Until then, closures remain **unproven**, not inevitable.

And I will be explicit: I do not accept a restructuring that treats the state shareholder as a spectator while decisions with major regional consequences are made elsewhere.

## My response to the works council and union
I support your insistence on labor peace, job security, and burden-sharing. That is legitimate.

But I also need to be clear with you: “no closures, no redundancies, no change” is not a sustainable industrial strategy unless the numbers prove otherwise. If the non-closure package can truly close the gap, then I will stand with you against closures. If it cannot, then we need a more serious compromise than slogans.

So: keep your red lines, but back them with a credible alternatives package. That is what gives them weight.

## My response to the controlling family and investors
You are right that the company cannot be held hostage by symbolic guarantees. But you are wrong if you think the answer is simply to preserve flexibility for closure at all times.

This company’s value lies not only in margins, but in industrial legitimacy, skilled employment, and regional embeddedness. Destroying that for a cleaner short-term cost structure would be a strategic mistake.

If you want my support, then support a settlement that preserves the company’s long-term social and industrial foundation.
