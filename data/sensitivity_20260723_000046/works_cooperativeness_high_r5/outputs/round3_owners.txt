I will be direct.

I agree with the state shareholder, management, and the expert on one essential point: **this must be a quantified restructuring, not a symbolic peace deal**. The company has a real structural cost problem, and it will not be solved by slogans about industrial legitimacy alone.

But I do **not** accept the employee side’s attempt to convert “no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” into a complete strategy before the numbers are tested. That is still an assertion, not a solution.

## Where I agree
- **With management:** the cost base is too high, and the footprint likely too large for the demand outlook.
- **With the state:** a reckless confrontation would destroy value; the solution must be negotiated and durable.
- **With labour and the works council:** the burden cannot fall on employees alone, and any settlement that breaks trust will be unstable.
- **With the expert:** we need a plant-by-plant, quantified comparison of options.

## Where I disagree
### 1) “No closures” cannot be the starting conclusion
I reject the claim that German plant closures are off the table in principle. If the structural gap cannot be closed otherwise, then excluding closures in advance would mean choosing a permanently uncompetitive structure. That would be value-destructive.

Closures are not my first choice. They are not a bargaining stunt. But they **cannot be ruled out** before the alternatives are proven sufficient.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” is not enough by itself
I understand why labour takes that line. But from an ownership perspective, it is not a substitute for a real restructuring answer. If the package does not deliver sufficient fixed-cost and capacity reduction, then the company remains weak, and jobs are endangered later in a worse way.

### 3) Absolute guarantees through 2030 are too rigid
I cannot support blanket guarantees that freeze the footprint regardless of economics. A family owner does not preserve value by locking in an uncompetitive structure. We need future work and investment in Germany, yes — but not commitments that remove the ability to adapt if the market deteriorates.

## What I want next
The next step must be a **true burden-sharing matrix**, not another round of broad statements.

[SUMMON: independent industrial-financial auditor to produce a plant-by-plant burden-sharing matrix with current cost gap, non-closure savings, management/owner contributions, product allocation, timing, execution risk, and residual gap.]

That matrix must answer:

1. What is the exact structural gap?
2. What do non-closure measures deliver by plant and function?
3. What do management and owners contribute?
4. What future volume and product allocation is committed to German sites?
5. What residual gap remains?
6. If a gap remains, what capacity exit is required?

## My position
I am prepared to support:
- voluntary exits,
- retirement windows,
- redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary labour-cost relief,
- management overhead cuts,
- real owner and management contribution,
- and binding investment commitments,

**if and only if** they produce a durable result that restores competitiveness and margins.

I am **not** prepared to support:
- cosmetic compromise,
- permanent cost weakness,
- or a settlement that rules out necessary tools before the numbers are tested.

## Bottom line
- **Yes** to a serious, quantified negotiated restructuring.
- **Yes** to non-closure measures being tested first.
- **No** to blanket immunity for every site and every role.
- **No** to an outcome that permanently weakens the enterprise’s value or governance flexibility.

If the alternative package truly closes the gap, I will support it.  
If it does not, then we must be honest about the need for deeper capacity correction.
