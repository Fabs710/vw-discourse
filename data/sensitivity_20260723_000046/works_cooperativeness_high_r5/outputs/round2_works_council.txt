Management is still trying to treat a structural problem as if the only serious answer were to threaten closures and layoffs. I do not accept that framing.

Let me be equally direct with everyone:

### To management
I agree with one point: the company needs a real industrial future and a durable competitiveness plan. I do **not** agree that this requires plant closures or breaking the job-security framework.

What you have not shown so far is a convincing case that every alternative has been exhausted. You have not presented a quantified comparison proving that:
- voluntary exits,
- retirement pathways,
- working-time adjustments,
- internal redeployment,
- temporary capacity reduction,
- management and owner contributions,
can **not** close the gap without shutting German sites.

Until that is shown, closures remain an unacceptable first resort.

And let me be clear: **no compulsory redundancies** is not a bargaining gimmick. It is a hard line. The same is true for keeping all German plants open through 2030. If you want a serious discussion, then bring a plan that preserves sites, avoids forced layoffs, and invests in future products here.

### To the institutional investors
You talk about “hard numbers,” but you are reducing the company to a margin exercise. A company like this does not survive by shredding trust in its workforce and hollowing out its industrial base. If you insist that closures are acceptable whenever the model is under pressure, you are rewarding short-term financial logic over long-term industrial value.

You say labour’s position is only a bargaining line. No — it is a legitimate red line backed by co-determination and the company’s social settlement. If the market wants stability, then it has to accept that the workforce is not disposable.

### To the family holding
You are right about one thing: cosmetic deals are not enough. But I reject the implication that “serious restructuring” means accepting plant closures as inevitable. Serious stewardship means preserving the industrial core and protecting the company’s long-term legitimacy. That means:
- no unilateral destruction of sites,
- no open-ended erosion of job security,
- and no deal that treats the workforce as the last and easiest source of savings.

If you want my support, then support a plan that keeps every German site open, ties any workforce contribution to binding investment commitments, and shares the burden fairly at the top.

### To the state government
I appreciate your support for negotiated change and your rejection of rushed closures. But I want to be explicit: “closures as a last resort” is still too weak if it becomes a path to normalizing closure talk. German sites should not be placed on a chopping block while management looks for easier headlines.

The state shareholder should use its blocking position to defend industrial continuity, not just to moderate the pace of cuts.

### What I am prepared to discuss
We are not refusing restructuring. We are refusing destruction. I can discuss:
- voluntary schemes only,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary cost contributions,
- internal redeployment,
- and productivity improvements,
**if and only if** they are tied to:
1. a binding guarantee that all German plants remain open through 2030,
2. no operational redundancies,
3. real investment and future-product commitments,
4. and a genuine co-determination role in shaping the implementation.

### What I will oppose
I will oppose any package that includes:
- German plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- dismantling of historic job security,
- or “flexibility” that is just a euphemism for making workers pay for management’s strategic failures.

If management and investors want a durable settlement, then stop demanding that the workforce absorb the company’s overcapacity alone. Bring forward a credible alternative that protects jobs and sites, and then we can talk about our contribution. Until then, I will continue to resist this course with every means available.
