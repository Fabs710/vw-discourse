We are at the point where words are not enough. I will respond directly and clearly.

### To management
I agree with you on one thing: the company needs a **real** restructuring, not cosmetic delay. I also agree that voluntary exits, internal redeployment, working-time tools, and capacity adjustments belong on the table.

But I do **not** accept your implicit premise that plant closures are somehow the natural baseline and that everything else is secondary. You have still not shown that closures, compulsory redundancies, and the weakening of the collective bargaining framework are the **only** way to close the gap.

What is missing is a serious, quantified comparison of alternatives. If you want the union to even consider a wage and flexibility contribution, then show us:
- what each plant contributes to overcapacity,
- what savings are achievable through reduced working time, short-time-style tools, transfer of work, and voluntary programmes,
- what management and owners are putting in,
- and why that package would not be enough.

Until then, closures remain a threat, not a solution.

And let me be explicit: I will not accept **compulsory redundancies**. That is not a negotiating detail; that is a line. If management insists that forced layoffs are unavoidable, then it is choosing confrontation.

### To the works council
I share your core concern: the workforce cannot be made to carry the full burden of a management crisis. Your demand that employees contribute only with binding guarantees is fair.

Where I want to sharpen the position is this: a blanket “no closures under any conditions” line is understandable as a bargaining stance, but we should not let it become a substitute for a credible alternative package. We need to be able to say not only what we reject, but what we can support.

So I support your red lines:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no unilateral destruction of sites,
- real co-determination,
- and serious investment commitments.

But I want the company to be confronted with a **better deal**, not just a defensive no. That means we should be ready to discuss:
- temporary wage moderation or the waiver of certain components,
- reduced bonus or special-payment elements,
- working-time flexibility,
- and a contribution to a restructuring fund,

**if and only if** those concessions are matched by:
- binding job security,
- no plant closures in Germany,
- a future product commitment,
- and a management/owner contribution that is visible and real.

### To the regional state government
Your insistence on negotiated change and exhausting alternatives is welcome. But I need to be direct: “closures as a last resort” can easily become a polite way of keeping the threat alive until employees cave. We cannot accept that logic.

If the state shareholder wants to protect the regional industrial base, then it should use its influence to press for a **labor-saving, site-preserving restructuring** before any closure discussion becomes normalised. That means backing:
- internal reallocation,
- investment commitments,
- training and qualification,
- and a fair burden-sharing package.

I appreciate that you do not want a frozen structure. Neither do we. But change must be socially sustainable. If the company tears up trust now, the damage will last far beyond this round of restructuring.

### To the controlling family
You say you want durable competitiveness, and I take that seriously. But competitiveness does not come from making workers the sole shock absorber.

I agree that cosmetic deals are useless. I disagree with the tone that suggests site closure is simply a technical option if the numbers do not fit. A family owner should understand that an industrial company is not just a spreadsheet — it is a social and productive system. Destroying that system to prove discipline would be a strategic mistake.

If you want the union to help deliver lower structural costs, then support a package that includes:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no closures,
- a wage and working-time contribution from labour,
- management restraint,
- and a binding industrial plan for German plants.

That is what shared sacrifice looks like. Anything less is workers being asked to pay for decisions they did not make.

### To the institutional investors
I hear your demand for hard numbers and durable savings. That is legitimate. What is not legitimate is treating every social guarantee as value destruction.

A company like this does not regain competitiveness by humiliating its workforce or blowing up labor peace. A strike cycle and a broken collective bargaining framework will destroy more value than a carefully balanced deal.

So yes, we are prepared to discuss contribution and flexibility. But we will not agree to a model where:
- cost cutting is one-sided,
- plant closures are normalised,
- and employees are left with insecurity while capital keeps its options.

If you want investor confidence, then support a deal that is:
- economically credible,
- industrially stable,
- and socially durable.

### Our position, plainly
We are still open to a negotiated settlement. But it must meet these conditions:

1. **No compulsory redundancies.**  
2. **No German plant closures as part of the package.**  
3. **Protection of the collective wage framework.**  
4. **Binding guarantees on jobs, sites, and future investment.**  
5. **A fair contribution from management and owners, not just workers.**  
6. **A real co-determination role in implementation.**

If management can bring a serious alternative that proves the savings can be delivered without forced layoffs or closures, then we are ready to discuss a workforce contribution in a responsible way. That contribution can include wage moderation, working-time flexibility, and internal adjustments.

But if the message remains “accept closures and layoffs or accept nothing,” then there is no basis for agreement. In that case, we will mobilise accordingly.
