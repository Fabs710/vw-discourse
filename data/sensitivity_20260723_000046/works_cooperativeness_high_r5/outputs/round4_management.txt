## Where I agree
I agree on three points:
1. **A quantified, plant-by-plant matrix is essential.**
2. **Management must contribute, and we will.**
3. **A cosmetic peace deal would be worthless.**

I also agree that we should distinguish between:
- internal capacity reduction,
- partial line exits,
- and outright plant closure.

That distinction matters. But it does **not** change the basic conclusion: if the residual structural gap remains material after all non-closure measures, then we need a real capacity correction, not a rhetorical one.

## Where I disagree — strongly

### 1) “No closures” is not a solution
The works council keeps repeating “no plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, all plants open through 2030.” That is a political slogan, not an industrial plan.

If you can show that the non-closure package closes the gap credibly and durably, fine — then closures are not needed. But you have not shown that. You have demanded that we assume it.

I won’t.

### 2) You keep shifting the burden of proof
You say management must prove that every alternative fails after management, owners, and product allocation have contributed. That is fair in principle — and we are doing exactly that through the matrix process.

But your real position is not “prove it”; it is “do not arrive at closures under any circumstances.” That is the red line you are trying to smuggle in before the numbers are known. I reject that.

### 3) A trigger mechanism is not a threat structure; it is discipline
The moderator’s alternatives-first model with a trigger is sensible. The workers council calls it a countdown clock to plant destruction. No — it is a discipline mechanism.

If the residual gap after the first phase is still material, we cannot pretend otherwise forever. A trigger simply means the parties agree in advance how to respond if the numbers do not work. That is responsible governance.

Without such a trigger, you are asking the company to accept open-ended underperformance and hope the cost problem vanishes. It won’t.

### 4) Internal adjustments are useful, but not sufficient by default
Voluntary exits, retirement windows, redeployment, working-time flexibility, temporary moderation, and internal transfers are all on the table. I support using them.

But none of those levers removes fixed plant cost if the footprint remains too large. They can ease the burden, improve transition, and buy time. They do not necessarily solve the structural issue.

That is the key point the employee side still refuses to face.

## On the moderator’s compromise options

### 1) Alternatives-first with a trigger mechanism
Yes — that is the right way to structure this.

But the trigger must be real:
- if the residual gap is material,
- and if non-closure measures plus management/owner/product contributions still do not close it,
- then the parties must be prepared to execute capacity reduction, including closure where necessary.

I will not agree to a trigger that is merely symbolic.

### 2) No forced redundancies, but negotiated footprint reduction
This is the most promising route.

If the workforce wants to avoid forced redundancies, then we can pursue:
- voluntary exits,
- retirement windows,
- redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- shift consolidation,
- product changes,
- and footprint reduction inside plants.

But again: if the result still leaves too much fixed cost, then deeper capacity measures remain on the table. “Negotiated footprint reduction” cannot become a euphemism for permanent inefficiency.

### 3) Jobs-and-investment pact with measurable burden-sharing
I support this only if it is tied to actual performance:
- management overhead cuts,
- owner contribution,
- binding product allocation,
- real workforce flexibility,
- and a clear test of whether the residual gap is closed.

It cannot be an open-ended job-security pact that locks in an uncompetitive structure.
