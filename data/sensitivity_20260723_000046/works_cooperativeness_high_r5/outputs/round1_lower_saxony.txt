I will be very clear: I agree that the company needs a durable restructuring, and I agree that competitiveness and future investment cannot be financed by wishful thinking. But I do **not** accept the idea that the only serious path is plant closures and a one-sided shift of the burden onto the workforce.

From the state’s perspective as a long-standing anchor shareholder, our priorities are straightforward:

- protect as many good industrial jobs in Germany as possible,
- preserve the company’s role as a regional economic anchor,
- ensure the restructuring is economically credible,
- and keep the co-determination and veto architecture intact.

Where I agree with management:
- The pressure is real.
- Overcapacity and cost gaps cannot be ignored.
- The company needs a structurally better cost position.
- Investment in future products is essential.

Where I disagree:
- A blanket framing of plant closures as “necessary” is not yet justified to me.
- A package that treats German sites as disposable would be politically and economically damaging.
- A solution that undermines long-term labor peace and co-determination would create a much bigger strategic problem than the one it claims to solve.

I also want to respond directly to the works council and union: your insistence on no compulsory redundancies and a binding industrial future is legitimate. That said, I would not support a purely defensive position if it simply freezes the current structure. The company does need change. But that change must be negotiated, balanced, and durable.

So my line is this:

1. **No outcome that removes German sites from the map without exhausting alternatives.**  
   If closures are being discussed at all, they must be a last resort, not the opening demand.

2. **A serious alternative package must be tested first.**  
   That includes working-time tools, voluntary exits, internal redeployment, targeted capacity reduction, and management’s own contribution.

3. **Any workforce contribution must come with binding guarantees.**  
   If employees are asked to give ground on cost or flexibility, then there must be credible commitments on jobs, investment, and future product allocation.

4. **The restructuring must be compatible with social partnership.**  
   A confrontational solution that permanently damages trust may satisfy a short-term balance sheet view, but it is not a good long-term answer for this company.

To management: if you want support from the state shareholder, you need to show that closures are truly unavoidable and that every less damaging route has been exhausted. “Competitiveness” is not a magic word that automatically overrides the regional economy, plant communities, and the governance balance that has sustained this company for decades.

To the works council and union: I will support you in resisting any attempt to impose mass layoffs or strip away co-determination through the back door. But I will also expect a constructive counterproposal that is concrete enough to close the cost gap. Saying “not our sites” is not enough on its own if there is no credible economic alternative.

So the position I am taking into this round is:

- **No to rushed closures.**
- **No to compulsory redundancies as the first instrument.**
- **Yes to a negotiated restructuring.**
- **Yes to real burden-sharing by all sides, including management and owners.**
- **Yes to a binding industrial plan for German plants.**
- **No to any settlement that weakens the state’s role or the workforce’s co-determination rights.**

If management wants my support, bring a package that proves you can restore competitiveness **without gutting the regional industrial base**. If the proposal still relies primarily on closures and layoffs, then I will oppose it.
