I’ll be blunt: the latest rounds still do not answer the structural problem.

I appreciate that labour, the works council, and the state are all now engaging with the expert’s framework. But the core facts remain unchanged:

- German factory costs are too high.
- The footprint is too large for the demand outlook.
- The current cost base does not support the brand’s return-on-sales target.
- And we cannot fund future products to 2030 if we preserve an uncompetitive structure.

## Where I agree
I agree with the expert and with several parties on one point: **we need a quantified, plant-by-plant comparison**. I also agree that voluntary exits, retirement, redeployment, working-time measures, and overhead cuts should be part of the package. I am not dismissing those tools.

I also agree that a cosmetic “peace deal” would be useless. If a solution only shifts pain into the future while leaving the cost base intact, that is not restructuring.

## Where I disagree — and strongly
### 1) “No closures” is not a strategy
The works council and the union keep repeating red lines:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- all plants open through 2030,
- binding guarantees on jobs, sites, and investment.

I understand that position. I reject it as a complete answer.

The expert was very clear: non-closure measures can reduce part of the gap, but they often do **not** eliminate deep structural overcapacity. That is exactly the problem here. If the residual gap remains, then the question is not whether closures are emotionally difficult. The question is whether the company can survive competitively without a deeper footprint correction.

Right now, I have heard a lot about protecting every site. I have not heard a credible industrial plan that proves every site can stay open **and** the company can still hit the required cost level.

### 2) “Do everything except the hard part” is not enough
Voluntary exits, retirement windows, and working-time flexibility are useful. But they are not a substitute for structural change if the market has permanently shifted.

I will say this plainly:
- temporary wage moderation does not solve overcapacity,
- redeployment does not remove fixed plant cost,
- internal transfers do not make underused assets competitive,
- and future-product promises do not magically fix a broken cost base.

If the non-closure package does not close the gap, then it is not sufficient. Full stop.

### 3) Management contribution is not a dodge — but it is not the main lever
To labour and the state: I hear your demand that management and owners must contribute. Yes, we must. Overhead reductions, procurement discipline, executive restraint, and better product allocation are all part of the package.

But let’s not pretend those are equivalent to removing structural overcapacity. They are necessary, but they are not enough on their own. This company will not regain competitiveness by trimming at the top while keeping too much industrial capacity below.

### 4) The expert did not rule out closures — quite the opposite
The independent assessment confirmed what management has said from the beginning:
- non-closure measures can close only part of the gap in many cases,
- full closure is the strongest cost reset,
- and a hybrid route is often most executable.

That means closures are not some ideological invention. They are a serious restructuring instrument when the numbers require them.

The state government says closures should not be a starting point. Fine. I agree they are not a starting point. They are a **possible outcome** if the alternative does not close the gap. That distinction matters.

## My position on each stakeholder
### To the works council
I respect the role of co-determination. I do not accept the claim that preserving every German site through 2030 is compatible with restoring competitiveness unless proven otherwise.

Your insistence on no compulsory redundancies is noted. But if your position depends on ruling out the only measures that can materially reset the cost base, then you are not defending jobs in the long run — you are defending a structure that will eventually become unsustainable.

### To the union
You are right to demand a quantified comparison. We are providing that. But your line that closures are unacceptable under any conditions remains a blocker, not a contribution.

If a non-closure package genuinely delivers equivalent savings, then fine — I will support it. But you have not shown that yet. Until then, I will not accept a wage-and-flexibility contribution that merely postpones the need for deeper restructuring.

### To the regional state government
You are trying to balance social stability and industrial viability. I respect that. But you cannot wish away the economics. If the gap remains after alternatives are tested, then resisting closures categorically would be protecting appearances over competitiveness.

### To the controlling family
I agree with you that cosmetic deals are unacceptable. I also agree that stewardship means hard decisions. Where I would push further is this: we should not let the mere fear of conflict prevent us from making the changes that protect the enterprise over the long run.

### To the investors
You are right to focus on hard numbers and durable savings. The expert reinforces your point that the outcome must materially restore margins. I share that objective. But we should also be careful not to treat closures as an abstract financial preference. They are a means to an end, not a slogan.

## What I am prepared to accept
I am willing to support a negotiated restructuring that avoids closures **only if** it meets all of the following:

1. It delivers a **material and durable** reduction in costs and capacity.
2. It includes a **genuine labour-cost contribution**.
3. It includes **binding management and owner contributions**.
4. It preserves enough flexibility to restore margins through 2030.
5. It produces a **quantified plan** showing the residual gap is closed without plant exits.

If that can be demonstrated, then closures may not be necessary.

## What I will not accept
I will not accept:
- a deal that keeps all sites open by default,
- a package that protects every historic arrangement but leaves the company uncompetitive,
- or a settlement that substitutes slogans about “industrial legitimacy” for actual cost reduction.

## The next step
We need the auditor to do more than general scenario work. We need a **plant-by-plant burden-sharing matrix** with:
- current cost gap,
- savings from non-closure measures,
- management and owner contribution,
- product allocation,
- residual gap,
- and the consequence if that residual remains.

[SUMMON: independent plant-by-plant industrial and financial auditor to produce a quantified burden-sharing matrix showing costs, savings, and residual gap by site, including management, owner, and workforce contributions.]

Until that matrix shows a credible, durable non-closure solution, I will continue to insist that closures remain a live and legitimate instrument. And I will not withdraw that position just because it is unpopular.
