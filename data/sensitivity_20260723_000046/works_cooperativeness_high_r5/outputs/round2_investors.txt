The expert’s assessment is useful, and it reinforces the central point from the capital markets: **a deal only matters if it materially restores margins, not if it merely creates the appearance of peace**.

## Where we agree
We agree with management on the diagnosis:
- the cost base is structurally too high,
- overcapacity is real,
- and the company needs durable action, not delay.

We also agree with the expert that:
- **non-closure measures can help**, but
- they are often **not enough on their own** for a deep structural gap,
- and a credible package must be **quantified, durable, and executable**.

That is exactly the standard we are applying.

## Where we disagree with labour and the state
I need to be blunt: the works council and union are still treating “no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” as if they were a complete strategy. They are not.

The expert has now confirmed what investors were saying from the start:
- voluntary exits, working-time changes, redeployment, and internal flexibility may close part of the gap,
- but they often do **not** remove enough fixed cost and excess capacity to restore competitive returns.

So if the employee side insists on:
- no closures,
- no forced redundancies,
- no material structural change,
while also demanding binding job and site guarantees, then that is **not** a viable turnaround package. It is a transfer of the problem into the future.

We will not support a settlement that leaves the company structurally overmanned and under-margined.

## Where we agree with the family and management — with conditions
We agree that a cosmetic compromise would be a mistake. But that cuts both ways:
- a cosmetic “social peace” deal that preserves too much cost is bad,
- and a confrontational closure plan that destroys value through delay, strikes, and governance deadlock is also bad.

The family holding is right that stewardship means hard decisions. Management is right that the outcome must materially reduce cost and capacity. But both must now prove they can deliver that **without unnecessary destruction of value**.

## What the expert’s assessment means for us
The expert’s core conclusion is the one that matters:
- **pure no-closure is likely underpowered unless the numbers are unusually favorable**,
- **pure closure is high-risk in this governance environment**,
- **a hybrid negotiated package is most executable**.

That is the only part of the discussion that looks investable.

So our position is:
- **We are open to alternatives to closures only if they are quantified and economically equivalent.**
- If they are not, then closures or other hard capacity measures may be necessary.
- But we will not back a package that rules out the very tools needed to restore returns.

## What we need to see next
Before we support any outcome, management must table a proper bridge:
1. the exact structural cost gap,
2. the savings from each non-closure lever,
3. what remains uncovered,
4. and whether the residual gap can be closed without plant action.

If the answer is that the non-closure package materially closes the gap, then fine — we support it.  
If the answer is that it does not, then employee vetoes cannot be allowed to freeze the economics.

## Our clear bottom line
We will support:
- decisive restructuring,
- durable cost reduction,
- real capacity correction,
- and a credible investment plan.

We will **not** support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a structure that remains uncompetitive,
- or an outcome where shareholder value is sacrificed to preserve every historic arrangement.

So the next round should stop arguing in slogans and start showing numbers. If labour wants to avoid closures, it must prove equivalence. If it cannot, then management and the controlling owners need to act accordingly.
