I will be direct.

We now have enough on the table to distinguish **three different things** that some parties keep blurring together:

1. **A quantified alternatives-first restructuring**
2. **A negotiated footprint reduction**
3. **A full plant closure**

Those are not the same. And if this discussion is to be serious, we must stop talking as if rejecting the third option means rejecting restructuring itself.

## Where I agree

I agree with management, investors, and the expert on one central point: **the company has a structural competitiveness problem, not a temporary one**. The German cost base and footprint cannot simply be defended by slogans. A cosmetic peace deal would destroy value.

I also agree with the works council, union, and state on another point: **the burden cannot fall on employees alone**. Management, the owners, and the capital structure must contribute visibly. If they do not, any package will be unstable and unjust.

And I agree with the moderator that the next step should be a **plant-by-plant matrix** separating:
- internal capacity reduction,
- partial line exit,
- and outright closure,
with quantified savings, timing, execution risk, and residual gap.

## Where I disagree

### 1) I do not accept “no closures” as a complete strategy
The works council and union keep stating their red lines as if they were already the answer. They are not.

“No plant closures” may be a legitimate preference. It is **not yet a proven solution**. If the residual gap remains material after full alternatives, management would be irresponsible to pretend that the footprint can stay unchanged forever.

That said, I also do **not** accept closures as the starting assumption. They are not the default. They are a **last-resort structural instrument** if the numbers and execution reality truly leave no other credible path.

### 2) I do not accept forced layoffs as the preferred adjustment tool
I agree with labour and the state here: compulsory redundancies would be highly damaging in this governance setting. They would increase conflict, delay implementation, and likely destroy value.

If the company can achieve the required adjustment through voluntary exits, retirement, redeployment, working-time flexibility, and targeted capacity reduction, that is clearly preferable.

### 3) I do not accept a blank 2030 guarantee
The employee side wants all German plants open through 2030 and no compulsory redundancies, paired with broad guarantees. I understand the logic, but a **blanket guarantee through 2030** can freeze the structure too rigidly.

As an owner, I cannot support a commitment that removes the ability to adapt if the market worsens or if the numbers prove the burden is not closeable otherwise.

## On the moderator’s compromise options

### 1) Alternatives-first with a trigger mechanism
This is the most serious framework so far.

I support **alternatives-first**.  
I do **not** support an open-ended promise that closures are impossible.

A trigger is acceptable only if it is genuinely evidence-based:
- full non-closure measures are quantified,
- management and owner contributions are real,
- product and volume allocation are committed,
- and the residual gap is still material.

Only then can the parties discuss whether a capacity exit is necessary.

### 2) No forced redundancies, but negotiated footprint reduction
This is the most promising operational route.

If the workforce wants to avoid forced redundancies, then serious internal footprint reduction must be available:
- shift consolidation,
- line transfers,
- internal reorganization,
- redeployment,
- productivity gains,
- and use of voluntary schemes.

But I will be explicit: **footprint reduction cannot become a euphemism for preserving all current structures unchanged**. If it leaves the company structurally weak, it is not enough.

### 3) Jobs-and-investment pact with measurable burden-sharing
I support this, but only if it is real.

That means:
- management overhead cuts,
- executive restraint,
- product and volume allocation to German sites,
- owner contribution,
- and measurable workforce flexibility.

It cannot be a one-sided job-security pact that simply postpones the issue.

## To management
You are right that the company needs cost reduction and capacity discipline. You are also right that non-closure measures may not fully solve the problem.

But you still have to prove the bridge:
- exact current gap,
- savings from non-closure measures,
- management contribution,
- owner contribution,
- product allocation,
- residual gap,
- and what part of that residual can be handled through internal reduction rather than closure.

Until that is shown, you have not earned support for plant closure.

## To the works council and union
I respect your insistence on co-determination, job security, and no one-sided burden sharing. That is legitimate.

But I need to be equally direct: if your position is that **all German plants remain open through 2030 under any circumstances**, then you are asking the company to pre-commit before the numbers are tested. I cannot support that.

You may well be able to defend every site. But if so, prove it with a credible package that closes the gap. If you cannot, then refusing to consider any capacity exit is not stewardship; it is denial.

## To the regional state government
I agree that closures must not become a routine bargaining chip. I also agree that the state should insist on alternatives-first, binding investment, and volume commitments.

But I cannot support the idea that the state should effectively veto any capacity correction in advance. That would protect appearances, not value. The company must remain viable as an industrial anchor, and sometimes that requires hard decisions.

## To institutional investors
You are correct to insist on durable savings and margin restoration. You are also correct that cosmetic compromise would be value-destructive.

Where I would caution you is this: closure is not a slogan and not a cheap solution. In this governance structure it is high-friction and high-risk. If the same structural outcome can be achieved through negotiated footprint reduction plus real burden-sharing, that is preferable.

## My current position
I am prepared to support a **serious negotiated restructuring** if it includes:

- voluntary exits
- retirement windows
- redeployment
- working-time flexibility
- temporary employee contribution
- management overhead cuts
- owner contribution
- binding product and volume allocation
- real quantified savings
- and a credible residual-gap analysis

I am **not** prepared to support:
- cosmetic compromise
- permanent cost weakness
- a structure that leaves the company uncompetitive
- or an outcome that rules out necessary tools before the numbers are known

And I am equally clear on the other side:
- **no compulsory redundancies as the preferred path**
- **no closure-first strategy**
- **no destruction of value through prolonged conflict**
