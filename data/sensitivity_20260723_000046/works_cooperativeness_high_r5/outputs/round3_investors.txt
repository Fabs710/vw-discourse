We agree with the direction of the discussion, but not with the employee side’s attempt to turn resistance into strategy.

## Our position

As capital-market shareholders, our standard is simple: **the restructuring must credibly restore competitive margins and lower the structural cost base**. If it does not, then it is not a solution, whatever label it carries.

## Where we agree

### With management
We agree that:
- the company has a **structural cost and capacity problem**,
- the current German footprint is likely too expensive for the demand outlook,
- and cosmetic compromise would be value-destructive.

We also agree that **voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time flexibility, and overhead reduction** should be part of the package. If those tools can remove a meaningful share of the gap, they should absolutely be used.

### With the expert
The expert’s point is the right one:  
- non-closure measures can deliver **real savings**,  
- but they often do **not fully reset fixed costs** in a structurally overcapacity automotive business,  
- and a **hybrid, negotiated package** is usually the most executable in this governance setup.

That is exactly why we have been insisting on numbers rather than slogans.

### With the family holding
We agree that stewardship means making hard calls if the economics require them. We do **not** support preserving every historic arrangement simply because it is uncomfortable to change.

### With the state
We agree that prolonged industrial conflict would destroy value, and that a negotiated settlement is preferable to a damaging strike cycle.

## Where we disagree

### With the works council and union
Your repeated insistence on:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- all German plants open through 2030,

is **not yet a restructuring solution**. It is a red line list.

That would be acceptable only if you could demonstrate an **economically equivalent** alternative. So far, you have not done that. The expert has now confirmed why: non-closure tools may help materially, but they often leave a residual gap.

That means your position, as stated, still risks leaving the company:
- overmanned,
- overcapacity,
- and under-margined.

We will not support a deal that simply pushes the problem into the future.

## What we need next
The discussion needs to move from principles to a **quantified bridge**:

1. The current structural cost gap, by plant and function.
2. Savings from:
   - voluntary exits,
   - retirement pathways,
   - internal redeployment,
   - working-time changes,
   - wage moderation,
   - management overhead cuts,
   - owner contribution,
   - product/volume allocation.
3. The residual gap after those measures.
4. What additional capacity action would be required if the gap remains.

Without that, this is still rhetoric.

## Our bottom line

We are prepared to support:
- a serious negotiated restructuring,
- meaningful labour-side flexibility,
- management and owner burden-sharing,
- and investment in future products and German operations,

**but only if the final package materially and durably restores competitiveness.**

We are **not** prepared to support:
- a cosmetic peace deal,
- an outcome that keeps the cost base structurally uncompetitive,
- or a package that rules out necessary tools before the numbers are tested.

So our stance is clear:
- **test non-closure measures first,**
- **quantify the residual gap honestly,**
- and if that gap remains material, do not pretend that closures or other hard capacity actions can be ruled out in advance.

If labour wants to avoid closures, it must prove that the alternative truly closes the gap. If it cannot, then shareholders should not be asked to accept permanent margin impairment for the sake of a political formula.
