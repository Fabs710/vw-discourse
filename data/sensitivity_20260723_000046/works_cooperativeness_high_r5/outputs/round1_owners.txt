Let me respond plainly.

I hear three things from the other sides:

1. The workforce will not accept a one-sided cut package.
2. Management believes the current structure is not competitive enough to sustain itself.
3. The state shareholder wants restructuring, but not at the price of destroying the industrial base or social partnership.

On the core diagnosis, I am closer to management than to the veto-first positions. If the company’s German operations remain materially above target on cost and capacity, then preserving every historic arrangement simply postpones the problem and increases the eventual damage. That would be irresponsible stewardship.

But I also do **not** support a simplistic “closures first” approach. A plant closure is a serious step for a family owner and for a long-term anchor shareholder. It should not be treated as a slogan or as leverage. It must be justified by a credible business case and by a clear showing that less destructive alternatives cannot deliver the needed effect.

So my position is this:

- I support a **real restructuring**, not cosmetic change.
- I support **durable cost reduction and capacity correction**.
- I do **not** support permanent value destruction through a prolonged conflict.
- I do **not** support settlements that weaken governance control or make the enterprise ungovernable.

### To the works council and union
I agree with you on one point: a settlement that simply dumps the burden on employees is neither fair nor durable. If you are being asked for wage moderation, flexibility, or reduced guarantees, then there must be a serious industrial plan and visible contributions from management and capital as well.

Where I disagree is your opening red line on **no plant closures** and **no compulsory redundancies** as absolute positions. Those may be understandable bargaining lines, but they are not yet a solution to a structural competitiveness problem. If the numbers require capacity to come out, then the question is not whether change happens, but how it is achieved with the least damage.

A credible employee counterproposal would need to show:
- materially lower fixed costs,
- a real reduction in excess capacity,
- verifiable savings over time,
- and enough flexibility to restore margins.

If that package can be delivered without closures, fine. But the burden is on you to show it is economically equivalent, not merely politically preferable.

### To management
I agree that “keep everything open and hope for the best” is not serious. But I will be watching carefully for overreach.

If management uses competitiveness as a blanket justification for actions that go beyond what is necessary, then I will not support it. A family owner cannot accept a restructuring that damages the long-term franchise, provokes unnecessary escalation, or creates a reputational and industrial mess that undermines the company’s ability to invest and execute.

So the standard for me is not “toughness” for its own sake. It is:
- durable margin restoration,
- sustainable competitiveness,
- and stable governance.

### To the state shareholder
I understand and share your concern for the regional industrial base. But a position that rules out closures in advance also weakens the credibility of the restructuring. If every hard choice is off the table, then the burden falls back onto wages, time, and guarantees alone — which is exactly what the workforce will resist, and rightly so.

So I am not endorsing a political veto on change. I am saying that any plant action must be justified by necessity and should come only after exhausting alternatives. That is a demanding standard, but not a frozen one.

### What would move me
I would be prepared to support a settlement if it has all of the following:
- a credible path to lower structural cost,
- meaningful labour contribution,
- no open-ended erosion of governance control,
- protection of long-term investment capacity,
- and a settlement robust enough to preserve labour peace.

What will **not** move me:
- a package that merely delays the crisis,
- a package that permanently impairs the company’s value,
- or a package that turns the family holding into a passive spectator while the business is structurally weakened.

So, in short: I am open to a negotiated restructuring, but not to a settlement that sacrifices the company’s long-term competitiveness for the appearance of short-term peace. If the counterproposal can really restore margins and competitiveness, I will support it. If not, then we should not pretend that slogans about job security solve the underlying problem.
