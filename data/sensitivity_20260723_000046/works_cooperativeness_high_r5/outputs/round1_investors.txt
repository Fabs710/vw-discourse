From the capital markets side, I’ll be blunt: we are not here to preserve the status quo, and we are not here to subsidize a structurally uncompetitive cost base.

We agree with management on the diagnosis in one important respect: if German operations remain above target on labor and fixed costs, then margins will stay under pressure and the market will continue to discount the equity. That is not acceptable. Shareholders need a credible return to competitive returns on sales, not another round of postponement.

Where I differ is on how the burden is framed and how much confidence I can place in the current posture of the debate.

### To management
Your case for structural action is credible in principle. What matters to us is whether it becomes a **verifiable and durable** improvement in margins, not just a narrative of urgency.

So far, you have said the right things about competitiveness, but investors will judge you on specifics:
- How much cost comes out?
- When?
- Is it recurring or one-off?
- Does the package materially improve ROS?
- Does it reduce complexity and fixed-cost leakage permanently?

If the answer is vague, then the market will treat it as a cosmetic process. That would be a problem.

I also want to be clear: if management cannot secure the required savings without plant closures, then that may be economically necessary. But if closures are being used as a negotiating weapon rather than as a last-resort efficiency measure, that destroys credibility. Investors will not reward brinkmanship for its own sake. We want a settlement that is hard-nosed and executable.

### To the works council and union
I understand why you are resisting compulsory redundancies and closures. But your opening line of “no plant closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” is not a business solution by itself. It is a bargaining position. If it leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive, then it simply transfers the problem to shareholders, future investment, and ultimately the company’s viability.

That is the core issue for us. We will **not** support an outcome that preserves every site and guarantee while margins remain impaired. If the only result is a more expensive, slower-moving company with the same structural overcapacity, then value destruction continues.

That said, I do agree with you on one point: employees should not be the only party making sacrifices. Any credible settlement must include:
- management discipline,
- a visible burden-sharing contribution from owners/capital,
- and a serious industrial plan.

But that does not change the fact that the numbers must work.

### To the regional state government
Your insistence on a negotiated solution and on exhausting alternatives is understandable and, in some respects, prudent. A prolonged conflict would be value-destructive. So yes, we would prefer a settlement over a strike cycle.

But I need to push back on the implicit premise that plant closures are politically unacceptable unless every other option has been exhausted to a near-impossible standard. The market will not wait for a perfect social compromise if the company is bleeding competitiveness.

If the alternative package can genuinely deliver the same savings through voluntary exits, working-time changes, capacity reductions, and wage moderation, then fine — that is better than closure. But if not, then excluding closures in advance is effectively choosing a weaker margin outcome. We cannot support that.

### To the controlling family
You are right to focus on long-term stewardship and not just short-term noise. But long-term stewardship means accepting hard restructuring when the business model demands it. It does **not** mean protecting every historic arrangement.

I agree with your point that a purely cosmetic deal is worse than no deal. But I would also say this: a deal that preserves too much cost and too much capacity is also a cosmetic deal, just dressed up as social peace.

What would move us is not rhetoric about seriousness. It is a package that clearly restores competitiveness:
- durable reduction in fixed costs,
- real capacity correction,
- protection of future investment,
- and enough certainty to support the share price.

### Our bottom line
We are open to a negotiated outcome, and we would welcome one that avoids unnecessary disruption. But we will not support a settlement that:
- leaves the cost base uncompetitive,
- structurally impairs margins,
- or substitutes vague promises for hard savings.

The market is already watching margins and execution closely. If this process delivers a credible, durable path back to competitiveness, investors will support it. If it becomes a cosmetic compromise, prolonged conflict, or a freeze on necessary change, we will not.

So our position is:
- **Yes** to decisive structural savings.
- **Yes** to a negotiated settlement if it is real.
- **No** to any outcome that keeps the company structurally too expensive.
- **No** to value-destructive delay.

If the other stakeholders want investor confidence, they need to show us a deal that actually restores returns, not just one that sounds socially acceptable.
