Below is a neutral restructuring assessment, based on the facts you provided and standard industrial turnaround practice. I am not taking a stakeholder position.

## Executive view

On the information available, **a non-closure restructuring can sometimes deliver a large share of the required savings, but closure-based restructuring is usually the faster and more certain route to deep structural capacity reduction**. The key issue is not whether alternatives exist; it is whether they are **economically equivalent, executable within the needed time, and durable under governance constraints**.

Given the company’s described situation — high German fixed costs, overcapacity, weak margins, and several veto points — I would frame the choice as:

- **Closure-based scenario:** higher one-time cost, higher social and execution risk, but **larger and more durable capacity/fixed-cost reduction**.
- **Non-closure scenario:** lower immediate disruption, lower legal/industrial conflict risk, but usually **smaller savings, slower realization, and a greater risk of partial or temporary relief only**.
- **Hybrid negotiated scenario:** often the most realistic in co-determined German manufacturing, combining **voluntary exits, working-time tools, product allocation, and selective footprint reduction** if the numbers cannot be closed otherwise.

## What can realistically be saved without closures?

Without plant closures and forced redundancies, savings typically come from five buckets:

1. **Workforce reduction through voluntary exits/retirement**
   - Effective for headcount, less effective for fixed overhead.
   - Works best in older workforces.
   - Typical impact: meaningful but often insufficient alone for a large overcapacity problem.

2. **Working-time and pay flexibility**
   - Can reduce annual labor cost and preserve skills.
   - Usually temporary or bounded unless renegotiated structurally.
   - Good for near-term relief; weaker as a permanent fix.

3. **Redeployment and internal transfers**
   - Preserves employment and know-how.
   - Does not by itself remove excess site costs.
   - Useful only if there is enough internal demand elsewhere.

4. **Product and volume reallocation**
   - Can improve utilization without closure.
   - Depends on whether new models are genuinely assigned to German plants.
   - Often constrained by capital intensity and platform economics.

5. **Management, procurement, and overhead reductions**
   - Necessary, but rarely sufficient to close a large structural gap in manufacturing.
   - Usually incremental, not transformative.

### Practical estimate
For a large carmaker with high domestic costs, **non-closure measures might close perhaps 30–60% of the structural gap** if implemented well and if the company is lucky on product allocation. They can do more if management is willing to accept deeper labor concessions and longer implementation time, but that increases industrial-relations risk.

By contrast, **closure of one or more plants can account for a disproportionate share of the remaining gap** because it removes:
- site fixed costs,
- duplicate overhead,
- underutilized equipment,
- local logistics and utilities burden,
- and often the need to maintain parallel complexity.

That is why closures are so frequently proposed when the gap is structural rather than cyclical.

## Closure-based scenario: advantages and disadvantages

### Advantages
- **Largest permanent cost reduction**
- **Strongest reduction in excess capacity**
- Better chance of restoring margin quickly
- Cleaner footprint for future investment
- Easier to align assets with new product allocations

### Disadvantages
- High one-time restructuring cost:
  - severance,
  - decommissioning,
  - remediation,
  - transfer costs,
  - potential legal/consultation delay
- Significant execution risk:
  - industrial action,
  - governance deadlock,
  - delayed ramp-down,
  - loss of know-how
- Reputational and continuity risk
- Can damage labor trust and future flexibility
- If closure timing is wrong, savings arrive too late

### Restructuring timing
A closure-based plan usually takes **12–36 months** to generate the full savings, depending on:
- consultation requirements,
- litigation or labor conflict,
- asset disposal,
- transfer of production,
- and how much volume can be moved elsewhere.

## Non-closure scenario: advantages and disadvantages

### Advantages
- Lower immediate conflict
- Higher chance of a negotiated agreement
- Preserves skills and optionality
- Lower social and political disruption
- Faster to implement if based on voluntary schemes and working-time tools

### Disadvantages
- Often **insufficient on fixed-cost removal**
- Savings may be temporary rather than structural
- Risk of “cost-shuffling” rather than true capacity correction
- Can leave the company with the same footprint and weaker margins
- May postpone a needed decision, increasing long-term risk

### Restructuring timing
Non-closure measures can start quickly — sometimes within **weeks to months** — but the savings are often **gradual and partially reversible** unless accompanied by binding capacity changes and product reallocation.

## Like-for-like comparison

### 1) Direct cost savings
- **Closure-based:** higher, more certain, permanent
- **Non-closure:** lower, more contingent, sometimes temporary

### 2) Speed to benefit
- **Closure-based:** slower to launch, faster to deliver once executed
- **Non-closure:** faster to launch, slower to reach full structural effect

### 3) Execution risk
- **Closure-based:** high
- **Non-closure:** moderate, but risk of under-delivery is high

### 4) Labor and asset implications
- **Closure-based:** major labor displacement; asset write-downs; potential stranded equipment
- **Non-closure:** lower labor shock; assets remain in place, but may stay underutilized

### 5) Long-term competitiveness
- **Closure-based:** better if overcapacity is real and persistent
- **Non-closure:** better only if the gap can be closed through productivity, volume allocation, and flexibility without sacrificing margins

## My assessment of the current bargaining positions

From a restructuring standpoint:

- **Management’s case** is credible if the cost gap is truly structural and the current footprint cannot be made competitive with labor flexibility alone.
- **Labor’s case** is also credible insofar as it demands proof that alternatives have been fully modeled and tested.
- The crucial missing element is a **quantified bridge**:
  - what the current gap is,
  - how much each non-closure lever can deliver,
  - and what remains unreconciled.

Without that, closure threats are not yet proven necessary, but neither is the non-closure alternative proven sufficient.

## What I would require before calling a non-closure plan credible

A serious non-closure plan must show all of the following:

1. **A quantified cost gap**
   - by plant,
   - by function,
   - by product line.

2. **A quantified savings bridge**
   - voluntary exits,
   - working-time changes,
   - wage moderation,
   - overhead cuts,
   - procurement and logistics savings,
   - capacity reallocation.

3. **A binding industrial plan**
   - which products go where,
   - what volumes each site will receive,
   - and what capex backs that plan.

4. **Durability**
   - not just a 1–2 year bridge, but a path that survives through the market cycle.

5. **Execution governance**
   - who can veto,
   - what happens if savings under-deliver,
   - and what triggers a second-stage measure.

If this cannot be demonstrated, a no-closure solution is not yet an industrial solution; it is a postponement.
