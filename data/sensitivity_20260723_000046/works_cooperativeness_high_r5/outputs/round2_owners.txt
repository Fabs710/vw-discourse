I will be direct.

We are now at the point where everyone has stated their preferred red lines, but the central question remains unresolved: **how do we restore competitiveness in a way that is durable, credible, and executable?** I am not interested in a compromise that merely postpones the problem or leaves the company structurally weak.

## Where I agree

### With management
I agree that the company faces a structural competitiveness problem, not just a temporary downturn. I also agree that we cannot preserve every historic arrangement and expect margins to recover. That would be irresponsible stewardship.

I also agree that voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time tools, and negotiated flexibility should be part of the package.

### With labour and the state
I agree that a one-sided burden on employees would be both unfair and unstable. I also agree that a prolonged industrial conflict would destroy value. A settlement that breaks labour peace and damages the company’s social legitimacy would be costly for everyone, including the owners.

I further agree that any workforce contribution should be matched by visible contributions from management and, where appropriate, capital.

## Where I disagree

### On blanket “no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies”
I do **not** accept categorical red lines that rule out the tools before we know whether the alternatives are sufficient.

A blanket refusal of closures or compulsory redundancies may be understandable as a bargaining position, but it is not yet an answer to a structural cost and capacity problem. If the economic gap cannot be closed any other way, then ruling out those measures in advance would amount to choosing value erosion over restructuring.

That said, I am not endorsing closures as a first move or as leverage. They are serious measures and should be treated as such.

### On all German plants being guaranteed open through 2030
I cannot support an absolute guarantee that freezes the structure regardless of economics. That would remove needed flexibility and risk locking in an uncompetitive footprint. We need investment in Germany, yes — but not at the price of making the company incapable of competing.

### On “closures as a last resort” becoming an empty formula
To the state shareholder: I understand your point that closures must not be the default answer. I agree. But “last resort” cannot mean “never.” If the counterproposal does not close the cost and capacity gap, then insisting on no closures would be a refusal to confront reality.

## What I need to see
What matters now is a **quantified, side-by-side comparison** of options.

I want management to table, in concrete form:

1. The cost and capacity gap that must be closed.
2. What savings come from:
   - voluntary exits,
   - retirement pathways,
   - internal redeployment,
   - working-time measures,
   - wage moderation or temporary labour-cost relief,
   - management contribution.
3. What additional savings would still be missing under that alternative package.
4. What role, if any, plant closures would play if the gap remains.

Without that, this discussion stays rhetorical.

## My position on labour’s counterproposal
If labour can genuinely deliver:
- material labour-cost relief,
- a real and durable capacity correction,
- verifiable savings over time,
- and enough flexibility to restore margins,

then I am prepared to support a negotiated solution that avoids closures and forced layoffs.

But if the proposal is essentially:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no structural change of consequence,
- and guarantees that eliminate real flexibility,

then I will not support it. That would not be stewardship; it would be drift.

## To management
You should not overplay your hand. If you pursue a package that is unnecessarily aggressive, you will create the very instability that undermines value. I would not support a solution that damages the company’s long-term franchise through avoidable confrontation or reputational harm.

So I am not giving you a blank cheque for plant closures. I am giving you support for a **serious restructuring**, provided it is demonstrably necessary and proportionate.

## To labour and the state
You are right to insist on binding commitments, investment, and co-determination. Those are legitimate conditions. But you must also acknowledge that the company cannot be made competitive by guarantees alone.

If you want to avoid closures, then bring forward a package that is economically equivalent. Otherwise, you are asking the shareholders to accept structural weakness in the name of symbolism.

## Bottom line
My position is:

- **Yes** to a real restructuring.
- **Yes** to negotiated solutions that materially reduce costs and capacity.
- **Yes** to investment and future products in Germany.
- **No** to cosmetic deals.
- **No** to any settlement that permanently weakens governance control or locks in uncompetitive structures.
- **No** to blanket positions that exclude necessary tools before the numbers are tested.

If needed, I support the immediate commissioning of a neutral quantified comparison of the restructuring options.
