Let me respond directly, because we are now circling the same issue:

We all agree the company needs a durable restructuring. We do **not** agree on whether Germany can stay competitive with the current cost base, the current capacity footprint, and the current level of guarantees intact. My answer remains: **not at the required scale**.

## To the works council
I understand why you are drawing hard red lines. But your position still does not answer the central economic problem.

You keep saying:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- all German plants open through 2030,
- and no breaking of the job-security framework.

That is a political position. It is **not yet a restructuring solution**.

You are asking me to accept a package that protects every site and every guarantee first, and only then look for savings. That reverses the logic. We need a package that first closes the competitiveness gap in a durable way. If your preferred tools can do that, show me the numbers. So far, I have heard assertions, not an economically equivalent alternative.

I agree with you on one point: workforce contributions must be tied to binding commitments on future products and investment. That is correct. But binding guarantees on all plants through 2030 are not something I can accept if they lock in an uncompetitive structure. We cannot invest into the future by preserving an inefficient past.

## To the trade union
I appreciate that you are at least discussing working-time tools, wage moderation, and internal flexibility. That is more constructive than a pure veto line.

But your insistence on **no closures under any conditions** and **no compulsory redundancies** still leaves the cost problem unsolved unless the alternative measures are truly large enough to offset the overcapacity. I have not yet seen that. And I will not pretend that voluntary exits and temporary adjustments alone are enough if they do not materially change the fixed-cost base.

You are also asking for a “better deal,” which is reasonable, but a better deal for whom? If the company emerges still structurally too expensive, then we have not protected jobs in the long run — we have merely delayed the inevitable and weakened our ability to invest.

I will say this clearly:
- I am prepared to avoid forced redundancies **if** the settlement delivers the required structural savings another way.
- I am prepared to rely on voluntary schemes, redeployment, and working-time measures **if** they are sufficient.
- I am **not** prepared to accept a deal that preserves all sites and guarantees while leaving the cost base uncompetitive.

## To the regional state government
You are right to insist on a negotiated solution and to warn against a reckless confrontation. I share that objective. A prolonged strike would hurt output, reputation, and trust.

But “closures as a last resort” cannot become a formula for inaction. If every hard measure is blocked before we even test it, then the burden lands entirely on wages, flexibility, and management restraint — and that is not enough.

I agree that social partnership matters. I do **not** agree that social partnership means freezing structural change. A company can lose far more value by refusing to act than by making hard decisions now. If we end up with a politically comfortable but economically weak settlement, that is not stewardship.

## To the controlling family
I agree with you on the need for durable competitiveness and on rejecting cosmetic deals. That is exactly the point I am making.

Where I differ is this: a family owner cannot treat every closure discussion as an overreach, even when the cost gap is structural and the market is shrinking. Stewardship means making the hard calls that protect the enterprise over the long term, not preserving symbolic arrangements that drain its future.

You said the burden is on labour to show an economically equivalent alternative. Correct. But the burden is also on all stakeholders to accept that the company will not remain competitive by aspiration alone. If the only path to equivalent savings includes plant closures, then we cannot rule them out just because they are uncomfortable.

## To the institutional investors
You are right to focus on hard numbers, durable savings, and ROS. That is the discipline this company needs.

I also agree that closures should not be used as a bargaining stunt. They are a serious last-resort instrument, not theatre. But if the alternative package does not close the gap, then avoiding closures for reputational reasons would be value-destructive. The market will not reward a compromise that leaves the business structurally weak.

Your point about verifiability is important. I support a package that can be measured and tracked. But let me be equally clear: if the only “verifiable” plan is one that cuts enough cost, capacity, and fixed overhead to restore competitiveness, then that is the standard — not a cosmetic social peace deal.

## My position, plainly
We are willing to negotiate. We are **not** willing to accept a settlement that:
- keeps all German plants open regardless of economics,
- preserves the existing cost structure,
- and delivers only temporary or superficial relief.

What we need is:
- a **material and durable** reduction in overcapacity,
- a **genuine labour-cost contribution**,
- flexibility on how savings are achieved,
- and enough structural change to restore margins and fund future investment.

That can include alternatives to closures **if they are economically equivalent**. But I am not going to endorse a package that simply renames the problem.

## What I need next
If labour wants to avoid closures and redundancies, then put forward a quantified package that shows:
1. how much cost comes out,
2. how much capacity is reduced,
3. how quickly savings are achieved,
4. and why that is durable through 2030.

If that package is serious, I will engage seriously. If not, then we are still talking past each other.

And to be very clear: **I will not let the company drift into a cosmetic compromise that leaves us uncompetitive.**
