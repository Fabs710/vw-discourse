We agree with the direction of the debate on one point: this cannot end in a cosmetic settlement. A deal that preserves the current German cost base, leaves overcapacity untouched, and merely postpones the problem would destroy value. That is unacceptable for us.

## Where we agree

### With management
- The company has a **structural** cost and capacity problem.
- Voluntary exits, retirement windows, redeployment, working-time flexibility, overhead cuts, and product/volume allocation are all legitimate parts of the solution.
- A quantified, plant-by-plant matrix is necessary.

### With the expert
- Non-closure measures can generate real savings, but often **do not fully reset the fixed-cost base** in a materially overcapacity automotive structure.
- A hybrid route is usually more executable than a pure closure strategy.
- The residual gap matters.

### With the family holding
- Stewardship means hard decisions if the economics require them.
- A sentimental “keep everything” approach is not a strategy.

## Where we disagree

### With the works council and union
Your insistence on:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- all German plants open through 2030,

is **not yet a solution**. It is a red line list.

We understand why you are taking that position, and we agree that labor should not carry the burden alone. But for shareholders, the test is simple: **does the final package restore competitive margins on a durable basis?**

If the answer is no, then “no closures” becomes a request to preserve an uncompetitive structure. We cannot support that.

You say closures are a threat, not a solution. That is only true if the alternatives actually close the gap. So far, nobody has demonstrated that.

### With the regional state government
We agree that closures should not be the casual starting point. But we do **not** agree that the state can effectively preclude capacity correction in advance. If the residual gap remains material after full alternatives and burden-sharing, then the company must retain the ability to act.

### With the controlling family
We agree that symbolic peace is worthless. Where we differ is that we are not prepared to treat closure as an ideological endpoint. It is only justified if the quantified evidence shows that alternatives still leave a materially impaired cost base.

## What we need now

The discussion has to stop circling around slogans and move to a decision-grade comparison:

1. **Current structural gap**
2. **Savings from full non-closure measures**
3. **Management contribution**
4. **Owner contribution**
5. **Product and volume allocation to German sites**
6. **Residual gap**
7. **What capacity action is needed if the residual remains material**

That matrix must distinguish clearly between:
- internal capacity reduction,
- partial line exit,
- and full plant closure.

## Our bottom line

We are prepared to support a negotiated restructuring if it:

- materially and durably lowers the cost base,
- includes real management and owner burden-sharing,
- uses non-closure tools first,
- and leaves the company with a credible path back to competitive margins.

We are **not** prepared to support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a package that preserves structural overcapacity,
- or an outcome that rules out necessary tools before the numbers are tested.

So our position is unchanged:
- **Test non-closure measures first.**
- **Quantify the residual gap honestly.**
- **If the gap remains material, capacity reduction cannot be excluded.**

That is the only stance that protects long-term shareholder value.
