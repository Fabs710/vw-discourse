## 1. Decision context

The company faces a structural competitiveness problem in Germany: excess capacity, high fixed costs, and weaker returns amid a shrinking European market and stronger low-cost competition. Management seeks a durable restructuring that materially lowers cost and capacity; labour-side stakeholders seek to avoid closures and forced layoffs, while still accepting that change is necessary.

The governance setting makes this unusually constrained. Co-determination gives employees an effective veto over plant closure, the controlling family can block or shape major decisions through the supervisory board, and the regional state has blocking-minority leverage and political legitimacy concerns. As the interaction progressed, the issue became less “whether restructuring” and more “what tools remain legitimate after the numbers are tested.”

## 2. Stakeholder assembly

- **Group & brand management**: highest operational standing; driving the restructuring and accountable for competitiveness.
- **Works council**: strong legitimacy and formal veto power through co-determination; plant-level workforce representative.
- **Industrial union**: bargaining authority over collective agreements and strike power; high mobilisation capacity.
- **Regional state government**: significant political legitimacy and shareholder veto position; seeks industrial stability.
- **Controlling family holding**: high governance power through voting control and board influence.
- **Institutional investors**: weaker formal blocking power individually, but material capital-market legitimacy and value pressure.
- **Independent expert / auditor**: no decision power, but high informational legitimacy; asked to define the matrix and decision rules.

## 3. Final positions

### Group & brand management
Management insists that a material residual gap may still require capacity reduction, including closure, if non-closure measures do not close the cost gap. It supports quantified alternatives first, but rejects any blanket “no closures” commitment before the numbers are tested.

### Works council
The works council is the strongest labour-side blocker and rejects plant closures and compulsory redundancies as red lines. It supports voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time flexibility, and temporary concessions only if management, owners, and product allocation contribute first and if all German plants remain open through 2030.

### Industrial union
The union aligns closely with the works council on no compulsory redundancies, burden sharing, and real co-determination. It supports an alternatives-first process and a jobs-and-investment pact, but rejects trigger mechanisms that function as a countdown to plant destruction.

### Regional state government
The state supports negotiation, site protection, and a serious industrial plan, but does not want to freeze restructuring entirely. It accepts burden-sharing and alternatives-first sequencing, while stopping short of endorsing closures as a routine fallback.

### Controlling family holding
The family accepts that the German footprint cannot simply be preserved unchanged, and it sides with management on the need for a credible cost reset. But it also accepts the need for visible owner and management contributions, and it prefers negotiated footprint reduction before full closure.

### Institutional investors
Investors prioritise durable margin restoration and reject cosmetic compromise. They support quantified non-closure measures first, but insist that capacity reduction remains legitimate if the residual gap stays material.

### Independent expert / auditor
The expert does not claim closures are necessary on the available record, because no plant-level data were provided. It states that non-closure measures can plausibly close a meaningful share of the gap, but that a residual gap may still justify partial exit or closure if it remains material and operationally manageable.

## 4. Genuine conflicts

1. **Closure as last resort vs. closure as unacceptable**
   - Management, family, and investors treat closure as a legitimate fallback if quantified evidence shows non-closure options are insufficient.
   - Works council and union treat closure as illegitimate unless it is proven unavoidable, and in practice aim to exclude it altogether.

2. **Trigger mechanism vs. permanent threat**
   - Management and investors see a trigger as disciplined sequencing.
   - Labour and the state view it as a standing threat that normalises plant destruction.

3. **Residual gap legitimacy**
   - Capital-side actors believe a material residual gap justifies capacity correction.
   - Labour-side actors argue that the residual gap must be evaluated only after every management, owner, and allocation lever is exhausted, and even then not necessarily via closure.

4. **Blanket guarantees vs. adaptive flexibility**
   - Labour wants all German plants open through 2030 and no forced redundancies.
   - Management and owners view such guarantees as too rigid and potentially value-destructive.

5. **Who bears proof burden**
   - Labour insists management must prove why alternatives fail.
   - Management insists the matrix must be joint and outcome-neutral, not a mechanism for pre-committing to no closure.

## 5. Areas of convergence

- All parties accept that the status quo is not viable.
- All sides now support a quantified plant-by-plant analysis.
- Voluntary exits, retirement windows, redeployment, working-time flexibility, training, and internal adjustments are broadly acceptable.
- There is shared recognition that a one-sided employee burden would not be durable.
- Most parties agree that cosmetic compromise would be worse than no deal.
- There is broad support for some combination of management, owner, product, and workforce contributions.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker

1. **Speed vs. durability**
   - Non-closure measures can be launched faster.
   - Closures or partial exits may produce deeper structural savings but increase delay and conflict.

2. **Flexibility vs. security**
   - Management and investors need room to adapt.
   - Labour seeks binding guarantees on jobs, sites, and investment.

3. **Competitiveness vs. social legitimacy**
   - Stronger cost resets improve competitiveness.
   - Aggressive downsizing risks governance breakdown, strike escalation, and loss of trust.

4. **Short-term peace vs. long-term restructuring**
   - A broad guarantee package may buy peace.
   - But if it prevents necessary adjustment, it may only postpone a larger crisis.

5. **Internal reduction vs. plant exit**
   - Internal footprint reduction preserves continuity and legitimacy.
   - Closure yields stronger fixed-cost relief but at much higher social and execution cost.

## 7. Integrative pathways

### Pathway A: Alternatives-first with hard data gate
- Deliver a plant-by-plant matrix showing current gap, non-closure savings, management/owner/product contributions, and residual gap.
- Commit to non-closure measures first.
- If the residual gap remains material, move to a second-stage review of internal footprint reduction before any closure discussion.

This is the strongest procedural bridge because it preserves labour’s demand for proof while leaving management a credible fallback.

### Pathway B: No forced redundancies, but site-specific footprint reduction
- Exclude compulsory redundancies.
- Allow internal capacity reduction, shift consolidation, line transfers, and product reassignment.
- Keep closure on the table only as a jointly reviewed, evidence-based last resort.

This may be the most practical middle ground if the parties can distinguish internal shrinkage from site exit.

### Pathway C: Jobs-and-investment pact with conditional review
- Labour grants temporary flexibility and moderated cost relief.
- Management, owners, and investors commit to investment, product allocation, and overhead cuts.
- A review clause reopens the issue if targets are missed, but only through the agreed matrix.

This is the best route if the priority is to lock in cooperation while postponing any irreversible decision.

## 8. Open questions

- What is the residual gap, by site, after all non-closure measures and top-side contributions?
- How much of the gap can be closed by management overhead cuts, owner contributions, and product allocation shifts?
- What threshold residual gap would justify internal capacity reduction rather than closure?
- What counts as “material” residual gap in economic and operational terms?
- How durable can workforce contributions be without binding job and site guarantees?
- What social-impact and execution-risk tests must be passed before any closure could be considered?
- How will co-determination and the state’s blocking rights be handled if the matrix still shows a gap?

## 9. Recommended next step

Commission the independent auditor to produce the plant-by-plant matrix immediately, with a strict separation between:
- internal capacity reduction,
- partial line exit,
- and full closure.

The matrix should include quantified savings, one-time costs, timing, execution risk, and the residual gap after management/owner/product contributions. The next round should then be limited to one question: **can the gap be closed credibly and durably without compulsory redundancies or plant closures?**

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction

1. **The real dispute is no longer about restructuring in principle.**  
   Early rounds showed broad agreement that change is necessary. The deeper disagreement emerged only later: what counts as a legitimate endpoint if non-closure measures do not fully close the gap.

2. **“No closures” and “last resort closures” are not just different preferences; they are incompatible decision logics.**  
   Labour treats closure as a governance failure and a threat structure; management and investors treat it as an economically legitimate fallback. This is the core structural incompatibility.

3. **A trigger mechanism became the symbolic fault line.**  
   What management described as disciplined sequencing, labour interpreted as a standing countdown to plant destruction. That distinction only became visible through the back-and-forth.

4. **The separation between internal capacity reduction and outright closure matters more than initially framed.**  
   The interaction repeatedly exposed that participants were mixing together line transfers, footprint reduction, and site exit. The expert’s intervention clarified that these are distinct decision categories with different economics and social consequences.

5. **The burden-of-proof battle is central to legitimacy.**  
   Labour does not merely want a better outcome; it wants management to prove that alternatives fail before any closure can be discussed. Management, family, and investors want the matrix to remain open-ended and outcome-neutral.

6. **The state’s role is genuinely ambiguous.**  
   It is not simply pro-labour or pro-management. It seeks industrial stability and regional protection, but cannot credibly endorse a permanent no-change commitment if competitiveness collapses. This middle position became clearer only in the later rounds.

7. **A cosmetic deal is now widely seen as the worst outcome.**  
   Even the parties most opposed to closure accept that a symbolic compromise that preserves overcapacity would destroy value. The debate has matured from positional bargaining to conditional realism.
