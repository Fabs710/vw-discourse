## 2) Threshold logic: what counts as “acceptable” vs “requires exit”
I would use a four-step test for each site:

### A. Retain and optimize
Keep the site open if, after all feasible non-closure actions:
- the site can still earn an acceptable return on capital or at least meet its allocated cost target,
- utilization can be brought to a viable level,
- and future product allocation supports sustained load.

### B. Internal capacity reduction
Use this if:
- the site is basically viable, but too broad or too expensive in its current form,
- the residual gap is modest to moderate,
- and shrinkage can be achieved without dismantling the plant’s core economics.

Examples:
- shift reduction
- line consolidation
- function relocation
- internal specialization

### C. Partial line exit / footprint reduction
Use this if:
- one part of the site is non-competitive,
- but the remaining footprint can still operate efficiently,
- and the savings from removing one line or function are material.

### D. Full closure
This becomes operationally rational only if:
1. all realistic non-closure measures are exhausted,
2. the remaining residual gap is still material,
3. the site has no credible long-term industrial role,
4. and transition risk is manageable.

## 4) Practical rule of thumb on residual gap
Because there are no actual numbers, I can only give a rule of thumb.

A **small residual gap** usually means:
- non-closure measures are probably enough,
- and closure would be excessive.

A **moderate residual gap** means:
- internal capacity reduction or partial line exit is likely the right discussion,
- not immediate full closure.

A **large residual gap** means:
- if product allocation and burden-sharing cannot close it,
- closure becomes economically plausible despite governance friction.

The key is not the label “gap,” but whether the site can reach:
- acceptable unit cost,
- acceptable fixed-cost absorption,
- and acceptable utilization under the future product plan.

## 5) How to separate internal reduction from closure
This distinction matters.

### Internal capacity reduction
The plant remains open, but with:
- fewer shifts,
- fewer lines,
- less indirect labor,
- lower overhead,
- and a narrower product role.

### Closure
The site exits as an operating unit:
- tooling removed or transferred,
- workforce separated or redeployed,
- local fixed overhead eliminated,
- site sold, repurposed, or mothballed.

These are not equivalent. Internal reduction preserves some fixed overhead; closure removes most of it but at much higher transition cost.
