[Works Council (Betriebsrat) (Plant-level employee representation under co-determination) — Round 1]
We are prepared to discuss a restructuring, but not at the expense of our people and our sites.

Our position is clear:

- No plant closures in Germany.
- No compulsory redundancies.
- No breaking of the historic job-security framework.
- Any contribution from the workforce must be tied to binding guarantees for jobs, sites, and investment.
- The works council must have a real co-determination role in shaping any restructuring path.

We recognise the company faces serious competitive pressure. We do not deny the need for action. But the burden cannot be placed mainly on the workforce while management asks employees to pay for strategic mistakes. A solution that destroys jobs, closes plants, or turns long-standing guarantees into scraps of paper is unacceptable.

If management wants a durable settlement, then we need:

1. A binding guarantee that all German plants remain open through 2030.
2. No operational layoffs; departures only through natural attrition, retirement, and agreed voluntary schemes.
3. A credible industrial plan with future products and investments for German sites.
4. A fair burden-sharing approach, including management’s own contribution, before asking employees for sacrifices.
5. A genuine say for employee representatives in any restructuring decisions.

If those conditions are not on the table, then I will oppose the proposal clearly and forcefully. We have the means to do that, and we will use them.

## Executive view

On the information available, **a non-closure restructuring can sometimes deliver a large share of the required savings, but closure-based restructuring is usually the faster and more certain route to deep structural capacity reduction**. The key issue is not whether alternatives exist; it is whether they are **economically equivalent, executable within the needed time, and durable under governance constraints**.

## What can realistically be saved without closures?

Without plant closures and forced redundancies, savings typically come from five buckets:

1. **Workforce reduction through voluntary exits/retirement**
   - Effective for headcount, less effective for fixed overhead.
   - Works best in older workforces.
   - Typical impact: meaningful but often insufficient alone for a large overcapacity problem.

2. **Working-time and pay flexibility**
   - Can reduce annual labor cost and preserve skills.
   - Usually temporary or bounded unless renegotiated structurally.
   - Good for near-term relief; weaker as a permanent fix.

3. **Redeployment and internal transfers**
   - Preserves employment and know-how.
   - Does not by itself remove excess site costs.
   - Useful only if there is enough internal demand elsewhere.

4. **Product and volume reallocation**
   - Can improve utilization without closure.
   - Depends on whether new models are genuinely assigned to German plants.
   - Often constrained by capital intensity and platform economics.

5. **Management, procurement, and overhead reductions**
   - Necessary, but rarely sufficient to close a large structural gap in manufacturing.
   - Usually incremental, not transformative.

### Practical estimate
For a large carmaker with high domestic costs, **non-closure measures might close perhaps 30–60% of the structural gap** if implemented well and if the company is lucky on product allocation. They can do more if management is willing to accept deeper labor concessions and longer implementation time, but that increases industrial-relations risk.

By contrast, **closure of one or more plants can account for a disproportionate share of the remaining gap** because it removes:
- site fixed costs,
- duplicate overhead,
- underutilized equipment,
- local logistics and utilities burden,
- and often the need to maintain parallel complexity.

### Advantages

- **Strongest reduction in excess capacity**

  - severance,
  - decommissioning,
  - remediation,

  - industrial action,

  - delayed ramp-down,
  - loss of know-how
- Reputational and continuity risk
- Can damage labor trust and future flexibility

- asset disposal,
- transfer of production,

### Advantages
- Lower immediate conflict
- Higher chance of a negotiated agreement
- Preserves skills and optionality
- Lower social and political disruption
- Faster to implement if based on voluntary schemes and working-time tools

### Disadvantages

- Savings may be temporary rather than structural
- Risk of “cost-shuffling” rather than true capacity correction

Non-closure measures can start quickly — sometimes within **weeks to months** — but the savings are often **gradual and partially reversible** unless accompanied by binding capacity changes and product reallocation.

- procurement and logistics savings,
- capacity rationalization inside plants,
- shifts in product mix to higher-margin or higher-volume programs.

**Typical effect:** these measures can produce **material but incomplete savings**. They are often fastest to implement and least disruptive in the first 12–24 months.

### 2) But they often do not solve a structural fixed-cost problem
If the issue is **persistent overcapacity**, then the company is carrying:
- too much fixed plant cost,
- too much indirect labor,
- too much support infrastructure,
- and too much capital tied to underutilized assets.

In that case, non-closure measures tend to:
- improve the cost base,
- but **not fully reset the fixed-cost structure**.

That is especially true where:
- plant utilization is low,
- one or more plants have legacy layouts or poor logistics,
- EV transitions have reduced labor intensity but not reduced overhead proportionately,
- and domestic labor costs remain above peers.

### 3) Closures are usually the deepest fix, but also the most expensive to execute
A closure can remove a large amount of fixed cost, but the transaction cost is high:
- severance and social plan costs,
- decommissioning and site remediation,
- tooling relocation,
- supply-chain disruption,
- write-downs,
- labor conflict,
- delay from legal and governance blocking points,
- and possible loss of know-how or future flexibility.

So closures are not automatically superior; they are **more decisive but higher risk**.

## Plant-by-plant options matrix: how to think about it

Because no plant-specific data were provided, I cannot rank the actual sites by name. But I can give the **decision matrix that should be applied to each German plant**.

### A. Core variables for each plant
For each site, assess:

1. **Capacity utilization**
   - high / medium / low
2. **Product strategic value**
   - current model importance, future EV allocation, platform relevance
3. **Fixed-cost intensity**
   - indirect labor, maintenance, utilities, logistics, depreciation
4. **Labor productivity and flexibility**
   - output per employee, shift flexibility, union/work-rule rigidity
5. **Logistics and supply-chain fit**
   - proximity to suppliers, inbound/outbound cost
6. **Investment requirement**
   - capex needed to keep site competitive
7. **Social/governance resistance**
   - co-determination risk, state-shareholder veto, political sensitivity
8. **Closure feasibility**
   - legal, contractual, political, and operational
9. **Alternative-measure headroom**
   - how much cost can be removed without closure

### B. Practical decision categories by plant
Each plant generally falls into one of four buckets:

#### 1) Core future plant
- High utilization
- Strong future product allocation
- Competitive logistics
- Good EV/platform fit

**Best option:** retain, invest, and extract savings through efficiency and labor flexibility.  
**Likely consequence:** moderate savings, high strategic value, low disruption.

#### 2) Maintain-but-shrink plant
- Medium utilization
- Some future products, but too much capacity
- Can be downsized through line consolidation or SKU reduction

**Best option:** preserve site, reduce footprint, consolidate shifts, cut overhead.  
**Likely consequence:** meaningful savings, moderate execution risk, some workforce reduction.

#### 3) Candidate for partial exit / function transfer
- Low utilization
- Weak future product pipeline
- Duplicative capabilities
- High fixed cost relative to output

**Best option:** transfer some functions, reduce sharply, possibly end one production stream.  
**Likely consequence:** larger savings, higher labor conflict, higher transition risk.

#### 4) Closure candidate
- Persistently low utilization
- Poor future product case
- High reinvestment requirement
- Weak logistics or subscale structure
- Minimal strategic necessity

**Best option:** closure or full conversion to a smaller role, if alternatives fail.  
**Likely consequence:** deepest fixed-cost reduction, but highest political, labor, and implementation cost.

## Can non-closure measures close the gap?

### My estimate, in general automotive cases:
A robust non-closure package can often close:
- **30–60% of the structural gap** in a first round,
- sometimes more if the gap is mainly overhead, utilization, and temporary labor,
- sometimes less if the problem is a genuinely oversized plant network.

That means:

- If the gap is **small to moderate**, a negotiated package may be enough.
- If the gap is **large and persistent**, non-closure measures usually **do not fully solve it**.

### What determines whether they are enough
Non-closure measures are more likely to be sufficient if:
- the company has near-term model launches,
- EV ramp-up will restore utilization,
- there is a high share of avoidable overhead,
- supply-chain savings are still available,
- and the employer can secure real labor flexibility.

They are less likely to be sufficient if:
- one or more plants are chronically underfilled,
- the product pipeline is weak,
- the German footprint is materially larger than demand,
- and labor cost remains structurally above peers.

## Consequences of each restructuring route

### 1) Non-closure, negotiated restructuring
**Operational consequences**
- Less disruption
- Better retention of know-how
- Easier to preserve future flexibility
- Lower immediate supply-chain shock

**Financial consequences**
- Moderate savings
- Lower one-time charges
- Slower fixed-cost reset
- Risk of under-delivery if volume recovery disappoints

**Main risk**
- The company may end up with a “better but still too expensive” structure.

### 2) Partial plant reduction / line shutdown
**Operational consequences**
- Better fit than blanket closure
- Can remove subscale capacity while keeping strategic functions
- Allows targeted transfer of work

**Financial consequences**
- Larger structural savings than pure labor concessions
- Moderate to high restructuring cost
- Better fixed-cost absorption if the surviving footprint is filled

**Main risk**
- Requires precise execution and strong industrial planning.

### 3) Full plant closure
**Operational consequences**
- Maximum structural simplification
- Risk of production interruptions, supplier disruption, and loss of resilience
- Potential knowledge loss if not managed carefully

**Financial consequences**
- Highest long-term fixed-cost relief
- Highest near-term cash cost
- Write-downs and severance can be significant

**Main risk**
- Governance blockage, industrial action, and timeline slippage can erase part of the theoretical financial benefit.

## Governance and execution reality

Given the governance structure you described, **closure is not just an economic decision**. It is a **high-friction decision with veto points**. That matters because a theoretically optimal closure can become a poor real-world option if:
- it takes too long,
- it triggers prolonged strike action,
- it is blocked in the supervisory board,
- or it forces a costly fallback settlement later.

So even if closure is economically attractive on paper, management must ask:
- Can it be executed?
- At what delay cost?
- What is the likelihood of watered-down compromise?

In this environment, the **credible path is often staged**:
1. exhaustive non-closure package,
2. targeted footprint reduction,
3. closure only where residual gap remains clearly unsolved.

## 1. Decision context

The company faces a structural competitiveness problem in Germany: excess capacity, high fixed costs, and weaker returns amid a shrinking European market and stronger low-cost competition. ## 4. Genuine conflicts

1. **Closure as last resort vs. closure as unacceptable**
   - Management, family, and investors treat closure as a legitimate fallback if quantified evidence shows non-closure options are insufficient.
   - Works council and union treat closure as illegitimate unless it is proven unavoidable, and in practice aim to exclude it altogether.

2. **Trigger mechanism vs. permanent threat**
   - Management and investors see a trigger as disciplined sequencing.
   - Labour and the state view it as a standing threat that normalises plant destruction.

3. **Residual gap legitimacy**
   - Capital-side actors believe a material residual gap justifies capacity correction.
   - Labour-side actors argue that the residual gap must be evaluated only after every management, owner, and allocation lever is exhausted, and even then not necessarily via closure.

4. **Blanket guarantees vs. adaptive flexibility**
   - Labour wants all German plants open through 2030 and no forced redundancies.
   - Management and owners view such guarantees as too rigid and potentially value-destructive.

5. **Who bears proof burden**
   - Labour insists management must prove why alternatives fail.
   - Management insists the matrix must be joint and outcome-neutral, not a mechanism for pre-committing to no closure.

## 5. Areas of convergence

- All parties accept that the status quo is not viable.

- There is shared recognition that a one-sided employee burden would not be durable.
- Most parties agree that cosmetic compromise would be worse than no deal.
- There is broad support for some combination of management, owner, product, and workforce contributions.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker

1. **Speed vs. durability**
   - Non-closure measures can be launched faster.
   - Closures or partial exits may produce deeper structural savings but increase delay and conflict.

2. **Flexibility vs. security**
   - Management and investors need room to adapt.
   - Labour seeks binding guarantees on jobs, sites, and investment.

3. **Competitiveness vs. social legitimacy**
   - Stronger cost resets improve competitiveness.
   - Aggressive downsizing risks governance breakdown, strike escalation, and loss of trust.

4. **Short-term peace vs. long-term restructuring**
   - A broad guarantee package may buy peace.
   - But if it prevents necessary adjustment, it may only postpone a larger crisis.

5. **Internal reduction vs. plant exit**
   - Internal footprint reduction preserves continuity and legitimacy.
   - Closure yields stronger fixed-cost relief but at much higher social and execution cost.

## 7. Integrative pathways

### Pathway A: Alternatives-first with hard data gate
- Deliver a plant-by-plant matrix showing current gap, non-closure savings, management/owner/product contributions, and residual gap.
- Commit to non-closure measures first.
- If the residual gap remains material, move to a second-stage review of internal footprint reduction before any closure discussion.

This is the strongest procedural bridge because it preserves labour’s demand for proof while leaving management a credible fallback.

### Pathway B: No forced redundancies, but site-specific footprint reduction
- Exclude compulsory redundancies.
- Allow internal capacity reduction, shift consolidation, line transfers, and product reassignment.
- Keep closure on the table only as a jointly reviewed, evidence-based last resort.

This may be the most practical middle ground if the parties can distinguish internal shrinkage from site exit.

### Pathway C: Jobs-and-investment pact with conditional review
- Labour grants temporary flexibility and moderated cost relief.
- Management, owners, and investors commit to investment, product allocation, and overhead cuts.
- A review clause reopens the issue if targets are missed, but only through the agreed matrix.

This is the best route if the priority is to lock in cooperation while postponing any irreversible decision.
