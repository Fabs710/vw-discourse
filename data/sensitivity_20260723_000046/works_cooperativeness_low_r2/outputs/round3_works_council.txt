## Round 3 — Works Council position

Let me be just as direct as management and capital have been.

The company’s competitiveness problem is real. I do not deny that.
A durable solution is necessary. I do not deny that either.
But I **do** reject the attempt to turn that fact into a mandate for plant closures, compulsory redundancies, or the dismantling of the job-security framework.

The discussion has now become clearer:
- everyone says they want a durable solution;
- management and investors keep reaching for the harshest instruments first;
- the state is trying to keep options open while avoiding a social rupture;
- and the workforce is again being asked to carry the main burden.

That is exactly what we will not accept.

## Where I agree

I agree with the expert and with the state on one point: **temporary measures alone are not enough if the savings target is truly large**. Fine. That is an important operational reality.

I also agree that:
- working-time flexibility can contribute,
- redeployment matters,
- qualification matters,
- voluntary exits may help,
- and a site-by-site analysis is necessary.

So let nobody say the works council is refusing to engage with reality. We are not.
We are refusing to accept that the only “real” solution is to shut plants and threaten livelihoods.

## Where I disagree — fundamentally

### 1) I reject the framing that closures must remain “on the table” as a normal option
For us, closures are **not** a normal instrument. They are a breach of trust and a political choice, not an operational inevitability.

Management keeps saying “if alternatives fall short, closures must remain possible.”
That is not a neutral statement. It is a pressure tactic.

The burden is not on the workforce to prove that every alternative equals a closure package line by line.
The burden is on management to prove that it has exhausted all site-preserving measures **without** crossing into plant closure or forced redundancies.

And let me be clear: if the choice is between an imperfect restructuring and permanent job destruction, we will stand for jobs.

### 2) I reject compulsory redundancies outright
This is not a bargaining position. It is a red line.

If management insists that involuntary dismissals are part of the “toolkit,” then management is saying that employees must pay for strategic errors with their careers and family security.
We will not consent to that.

There are other tools:
- natural attrition,
- voluntary programmes,
- short-time work where applicable,
- internal mobility,
- working-time restructuring,
- temporary concessions tied to guarantees,
- and capacity adjustments that do not annihilate sites.

If those tools are used properly, forced layoffs are not necessary.

### 3) I reject “conditional” closure logic that becomes a backdoor
I know the trick:
first you say closure is only a last resort,
then you define the threshold so loosely that it becomes the inevitable conclusion.

We will not sign up to a process that makes closure easier by proceduralising it.

A site-by-site review is fine.
A closure threshold that can be triggered by management’s own assumptions is not fine.

If there is to be any serious review, then it must be anchored in a **presumption of preservation**, not a presumption of exit.

## To management

You say the cost base is too high and capacity must come down.
Then come with a plan that shows how to do that **without closures and without forced layoffs**.

Do not hide behind the words “durable” and “implementable” while quietly treating the workforce as the adjustment reserve.

If you want credibility, bring the full comparison:
- shift reduction,
- line consolidation,
- internal reallocation,
- product transfers within Germany,
- management cost cuts,
- owner contribution,
- voluntary exits,
- working-time changes,
- and temporary labor-cost measures.

And yes, I want the numbers.
But not as a pretext to declare sites dead before the discussion starts.

If the company cannot be made competitive without destroying its German footprint, then perhaps the problem is not labour cost alone. Perhaps it is management’s strategic plan.

## To the industrial union

We are aligned on the essentials: no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no relocation, no one-sided burden shifting.

I appreciate the union’s willingness to discuss temporary and limited contributions, but I will sharpen that point:
there can be **no employee contribution** unless there are binding guarantees:
- no plant closures,
- no forced redundancies,
- protection of co-determination,
- and a defined investment and product commitment for Germany.

Without that, contributions become a one-way subsidy to management’s restructuring failure.

I also caution against letting “temporary” become the doorway to permanent pressure.
Workers have already given enough in this transition period.
They should not be asked to pre-finance a restructuring that may still end in site rationalisation.

## To the regional state government

You are trying to define criteria before you use your veto. That is sensible in theory.
But I need to be blunt: the state’s role is not to manage the pace of plant closures. It is to prevent them where possible.

If the state wants to be taken seriously as a protective shareholder, then it must say clearly:
- no closure as long as alternatives exist,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no relocation out of Germany,
- and no governance workaround that sidelines employee representation.

A “rule-based” veto is fine only if the rule is actually protective.
If the rule simply legitimises eventual closure after a procedural dance, then it is not protection. It is packaging.

## To the controlling family holding

You say you value stewardship and do not want a labor war. Good.
Then use your influence to stop management from turning restructuring into a social confrontation.

You also say closures may be necessary if alternatives fall short.
I reject that as an acceptable starting assumption. It is too easy for management to define “fall short” after choosing the harshest benchmark.

If you want real stewardship, then back a settlement that:
- keeps all German plants open,
- preserves the job-security framework,
- uses internal capacity adjustment instead of closures,
- and shares the burden fairly across management, owners, and workforce.

Anything less shifts the cost of strategic decisions onto the people who did not make them.

## To institutional investors

You speak the language of value, durability, and implementation certainty.
Then listen carefully: **a plan that detonates labour relations is not value-protecting**.

You keep suggesting that closures are acceptable if the arithmetic works. But the arithmetic cannot ignore:
- strike risk,
- loss of trust,
- skill drain,
- reputational damage,
- and long-run operational weakness.

A “durable” solution that destroys the social compact is not durable.
It is fragile, only with a better spreadsheet.

If you want stability, support a structure that preserves:
- industrial capacity,
- workforce commitment,
- and co-determination legitimacy.

## On the expert’s point

The expert was useful, but let me clarify what that means.
Yes, permanent capacity reduction may be necessary in some form.
No, that does **not** automatically mean plant closure.

There are many ways to reduce capacity:
- fewer shifts,
- smaller footprints,
- product reshuffling inside the group,
- lower utilization,
- more efficient internal logistics,
- smarter investment sequencing.

That is where the discussion should be.
Not with a closure gun on the table.

So if management says, “some permanent capacity exit is needed,” our answer is:
then achieve it through site-preserving, job-preserving measures first.
We will not accept closure as the default translation of “capacity adjustment.”

## Our position, unchanged

We are prepared to discuss:
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- training,
- voluntary exits,
- temporary and limited employee contributions,
- management and owner contributions,
- site-specific efficiency measures that keep plants open.

We are **not** prepared to discuss:
- German plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- relocation out of Germany,
- dismantling of the collective bargaining framework,
- or weakening co-determination through a so-called pragmatic compromise.

## What would move us?

Only two things:
1. **Binding, enforceable guarantees through 2030** for every German plant and for job security
2. **A genuine co-determination role** in shaping the transformation, not merely being informed after the fact

If those conditions are met, then we can negotiate the form of the contribution.
Without them, there is nothing to negotiate on the core issue.

## Bottom line

Management is asking the workforce to accept permanent insecurity in the name of competitiveness.
We refuse.

The workforce is not the variable expense line for every strategic mistake.
The German plants are not disposable.
And compulsory redundancies are not a legitimate restructuring tool in this company.

If the company wants cooperation, it must stop treating closures and layoffs as acceptable instruments of “serious” management.
If it does not, then the works council will oppose it — decisively, in the boardroom and in the plants.
