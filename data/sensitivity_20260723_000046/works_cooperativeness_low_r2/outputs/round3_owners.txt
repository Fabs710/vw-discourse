## Round 3 — Controlling Family Holding

We have now moved past generalities. Good. That was necessary.

I will respond plainly.

## To management

I agree with the core diagnosis:
- the cost base is too high,
- capacity is misaligned,
- and the current structure is not fit for the market.

I also agree that we need **durable** savings, not another postponement.

Where I do **not** agree is with treating closures and compulsory redundancies as the automatic answer before the alternatives have been properly quantified.

You have repeated “structural reset” often enough. That does not substitute for a serious comparison. If you want ownership support for hard measures, bring the full site-by-site picture:
- utilization,
- model allocation,
- savings from shift reductions,
- savings from line or footprint reduction,
- savings from redeployment and voluntary exits,
- one-time closure costs,
- timing,
- implementation risk.

Until that is on the table, closure-first management looks like preference, not proof.

And let me be equally clear on another point: a long strike, reputational damage, and governance breakdown are not acceptable methods of restoring competitiveness. That would destroy value just as surely as inaction.

## To the works council

I understand your red lines and I respect the fact that you are defending jobs and sites. I also agree with you on one important point: the workforce should not be the only party making concessions.

But I do **not** accept your attempt to rule out closures and compulsory redundancies categorically, regardless of the economics.

If a site is structurally uncompetitive and the numbers show that non-closure measures cannot close the gap, then refusing to discuss any structural exit at all is not stewardship. It is a veto on reality.

You are right that alternatives must be tested first. I support that.  
You are not right to assume that a blanket no-closure commitment can coexist with a genuine competitiveness reset.

## To the industrial union

I agree that employee contributions must be limited, conditional, and balanced by commitments from management and owners. That is reasonable.

I also agree that temporary-only concessions are not enough if the goal is structural improvement.

Where I disagree is your insistence that the company can be reset while shutting out closures and involuntary reductions in advance. If the savings requirement is large enough, then the package may need permanent capacity exit. That is not ideology; it is arithmetic.

I would support:
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus waivers,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- internal mobility,
- and a transformation fund,

but only if these are part of a package that genuinely restores margins and capacity balance.

What I will not support is a plan that preserves every current structure and calls that a solution.

## To the regional state government

I appreciate your insistence on a site-by-site analysis and on preserving legitimacy. That matters.

I also agree that closures should not be the first move.

But I do not accept the implication that the state’s blocking power should be used to preclude serious adjustment. If every difficult change is treated as politically unacceptable, then the company will simply continue to underperform, and that will ultimately weaken the region as well.

You are right to demand proof before closures. I support that.  
You are not right to imply that closures can be excluded in advance whatever the evidence says.

## To institutional investors

You are right to demand durability, savings, and implementation certainty. I agree with that standard.

Where I part company with you is if you treat closures as the default solution from the outset. I prefer the least destructive route that still works.

But if non-closure measures do not produce equivalent savings and capacity reduction, then yes, harder steps remain available. Ownership will not support a compromise that merely delays the problem or leaves the company structurally impaired.

## My position

The family holding’s line is this:

- **Yes** to real competitiveness restoration
- **Yes** to durable margin improvement
- **Yes** to a site-by-site comparison before irreversible decisions
- **Yes** to temporary employee contributions, if balanced
- **Yes** to internal solutions first
- **No** to cosmetic compromise
- **No** to permanent impairment of the enterprise
- **No** to a prolonged conflict that destroys value
- **No** to ruling out structural measures before the evidence is examined

That means I am open to a negotiated non-closure package **if** it is truly sufficient.

But I am **not** prepared to endorse a blanket guarantee that prevents the company from taking necessary structural action.

## What would move me

Only one thing: a credible, quantified package that restores competitiveness **without** closures or compulsory redundancies, or else a quantified case showing that some hard structural measures are genuinely unavoidable.

If management can prove the former, I will support it.
If not, I will not pretend that the latter can be ruled out by declaration.

## Bottom line

This company needs a durable reset, not a symbolic compromise and not a social war.

I will support a solution that:
- protects long-term enterprise value,
- preserves the governance framework,
- restores margins,
- and avoids unnecessary instability.

I will not support a solution that simply preserves every current structure while the business continues to erode.

If the numbers justify a harder path, we will consider it.  
If they do not, then management should stop using closure as a shortcut.

That is where I stand.
