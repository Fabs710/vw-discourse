## Round 4 — Industrial Union position

We have now heard enough repetition to separate what is serious from what is not.

I will start with the point of agreement: **yes, the company has a real structural problem, and yes, temporary measures alone will not solve it.** That was true in Round 3, and it is still true now. So nobody should accuse the union of denying reality.

But I must be equally clear: **the existence of a structural problem does not justify plant closures or compulsory redundancies by default.** That leap is still not proven. Management keeps asserting it; capital keeps echoing it; but assertion is not evidence.

## To management

Your argument still boils down to this: because the savings target is large, you want to reserve the right to close plants and force layoffs if your preferred package is not accepted.

I reject that logic.

Why? Because you have not shown that the alternatives are insufficient. You have not shown, plant by plant, that:
- shift reductions,
- line consolidation,
- internal product reallocation,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus waivers,
- and management/owner contributions

cannot deliver a durable, meaningful reduction.

You keep asking the workforce to accept “durable” change, while offering them only insecurity in return. That is not restructuring; that is offloading risk.

And let me say this plainly: if management’s “durable” plan only works by turning German plants into bargaining chips, then the problem is not labour resistance. The problem is your chosen restructuring logic.

## To the works council

We are aligned on the essentials:
- no closures as a starting point,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no relocation out of Germany,
- no weakening of co-determination,
- no one-sided burden shifting.

I also agree with you that employee contributions must be **temporary, limited, and conditional**. On that, we stand together.

Where I want to sharpen the approach is this: we should not make the discussion look like “all or nothing” if it can be turned into a fair exchange.

That means I am prepared to discuss a package such as:
- temporary wage moderation,
- a bonus freeze or waiver,
- working-time flexibility,
- a joint transformation fund,
- and voluntary exit options,

**if and only if** those are tied to binding guarantees:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no plant closures during the agreement period,
- no relocation of production out of Germany,
- and a defined investment and future-production commitment.

Without that, there is nothing to trade.

## To the regional state government

Your insistence on a site-by-site matrix is right. But I want to be very direct: a review is only useful if it is designed to **test preservation first**, not to generate a pretext for closure.

So if the state wants to be taken seriously as a protective anchor shareholder, it should not hide behind “last resort” language. It should insist on:
- a presumption of site preservation,
- full testing of non-closure measures,
- transparent thresholds,
- and no closure decision unless the evidence is truly overwhelming.

I do appreciate that you are not rubber-stamping management. That matters.

But I also need to say this: if the state is willing to accept closures once a managerial benchmark is met, then the benchmark itself becomes the battlefield. That is where this can go wrong. The threshold must not be defined so aggressively that closure becomes the automatic result.

## To the controlling family holding

You talk about stewardship. Good. Then stewardship has to mean more than preserving balance-sheet flexibility.

A steward does not create a conflict that destroys trust, triggers strikes, and weakens the productive base, simply to preserve the option of structural exits. That is not prudence; that is escalation.

If the family genuinely wants long-term enterprise value, then it should support a solution that:
- preserves German industrial capacity,
- avoids forced layoffs,
- protects the collective framework,
- and requires management to do the harder work of redesigning the operation rather than cutting it away.

I am not saying there can never be any capacity adjustment. I am saying that the family should stop treating closure as the default answer when the social and operational costs are so high.

## To institutional investors

You are right about one thing: value matters. So does implementation certainty. But you are still underestimating the value destruction that comes from industrial conflict.

A plan that provokes:
- warning strikes,
- prolonged confrontation,
- skill loss,
- reputational damage,
- and loss of trust in future negotiations

is not “durable” just because it cuts cost faster on paper.

If you want risk-adjusted value, then you should care about:
- labour peace,
- workforce commitment,
- reliable execution,
- and a stable German industrial base.

That is why I am not rejecting contribution. I am rejecting one-sided contribution.

## On the expert input

The expert has said something useful: closure is the cleanest way to remove capacity, but not the only way, and non-closure measures can remove part of the gap.

That is exactly the point.

The expert did **not** say:
- closures are inevitable,
- compulsory redundancies are required,
- or that all alternatives are exhausted.

So the expert’s analysis does not move me toward accepting your hard line. It reinforces the need for a **serious quantified comparison**.

[SUMMON: Independent industrial restructuring analyst to provide a plant-by-plant comparison of utilization, fixed-cost burden, closure costs, voluntary-exit potential, and savings from shift/line reduction versus full closure.]

We need that comparison before anyone claims inevitability.

## What would actually move the union

I will be concrete. We can discuss a serious labour contribution if management and owners commit to all of the following:

1. **No compulsory redundancies**
2. **No plant closures during the agreement period**
3. **No relocation of production out of Germany**
4. **Binding investment commitments for German sites**
5. **Future product allocation for Germany**
6. **Joint governance of any transformation fund**
7. **Temporary, sunsetted employee contributions only**

In return, we can discuss:
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus waivers,
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- training,
- voluntary exits,
- and targeted capacity adjustments that do not destroy sites.

That is a fair exchange.  
What is not fair is asking workers to pay now while management keeps the right to close plants later.

## My line is unchanged

The union will not support:
- plant closures as a normal restructuring tool,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or a “conditional” process that is really just a delayed closure decision.

We are prepared to help fix the company.  
We are not prepared to finance our own displacement.

## Bottom line

If management wants a settlement, it must stop demanding advance consent for irreversible damage.

Bring us a quantified, site-by-site package that proves non-closure measures cannot work, and then we will examine it critically. Until then, the burden of proof remains where it belongs: with management and capital, not with the workforce.

And if the discussion continues to rely on pressure rather than proof, then the workforce will respond accordingly.
