## Round 4 — Controlling Family Holding

We are at the point where positions are fully explicit, so I will be equally explicit.

My priority remains the same: **restore competitiveness and margins, protect long-term enterprise value, and avoid a strike-driven or governance-driven destruction of value**. I will support serious restructuring. I will not support a process that turns into permanent industrial instability or weakens ownership control.

## Where I agree

### With management
I agree with the diagnosis:
- the cost base is too high,
- capacity is misaligned,
- and the current structure is not fit for the market.

I also agree that **temporary measures alone will not be sufficient** if the gap is truly structural. The expert has confirmed that point, and I accept it.

### With the state
I agree that a **site-by-site review** is necessary.  
I also agree that closures should not be the first move. They should only come into question after alternatives are tested seriously and transparently.

### With the union and works council
I agree that the workforce should not be the only constituency making sacrifices. Any credible settlement must include:
- management contribution,
- owner contribution,
- and a real employee contribution only if it is conditional and limited.

## Where I disagree

### 1) I do not accept a blanket “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” doctrine
This is the central point.

The works council and union are trying to turn those into absolute principles. I cannot accept that. If a plant is structurally uncompetitive, has weak future allocation, and non-closure measures do not close the gap, then **closing that site or reducing headcount materially must remain possible**.

A governance structure that rules out hard decisions in advance is not stewardship. It is self-protection dressed up as principle.

### 2) I do not accept that “presumption of preservation” can be the default if it prevents competitiveness
I understand the political logic, but the company is not being asked to run a social programme. It is being asked to compete in a brutal market.

A presumption of preservation is acceptable only as a **starting point for analysis**, not as a veto over outcomes. If the numbers say a site cannot be made viable, then preserving it anyway would simply transfer value destruction into the future.

### 3) I do not accept that the state’s veto should be used to block justified structural action
The state has a legitimate role. I respect that. But that role is not to freeze the industrial footprint regardless of economics.

If the evidence shows a hard step is necessary, then the state should not turn its veto into a tool for delaying reality. The consequence of such delay is often worse: deeper losses, weaker margins, and a larger eventual shock.

### 4) I do not accept the idea that strike risk alone should discipline management into accepting an uncompetitive compromise
A prolonged strike would indeed destroy value. I recognize that risk. But the answer cannot be to avoid all hard decisions because conflict is uncomfortable.

The answer is to design a package that is economically credible **and** implementable. If employee representatives offer only a no-closure/no-layoff boundary without proving equivalent savings, then they are not protecting value; they are shifting the burden back onto the company’s future.

## My view on the current impasse

The core problem is not whether change is needed. Everyone now accepts that.

The problem is that the employee side wants **binding site and job security first**, while management wants **economic proof first**. The state and ownership are caught in between.

My position is:
- **first, quantify everything site by site**
- **second, test non-closure measures honestly**
- **third, only then determine whether any site-specific hard step is unavoidable**

But I will be clear: if the evidence shows a site is not viable, I will not support governance paralysis just to avoid confrontation.

## [SUMMON: Independent plant-level restructuring analyst to produce a site-by-site matrix of utilization, fixed-cost burden, future model allocation, one-time closure costs, savings from shift/line reduction, and the volume of voluntary exits realistically achievable within 12–24 months.]

That analysis is what should determine where preservation is realistic and where it is not.

## What I can support

I would support a package built around:
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary exits,
- temporary employee contributions,
- management overhead cuts,
- owner contribution,
- targeted shift reduction,
- line consolidation,
- and a strict site-by-site review.

I can also support a **temporary no-forced-redundancy period** if it is paired with:
- real labor-cost relief,
- real productivity measures,
- and a binding review mechanism.

But I cannot support a permanent or blanket commitment that makes structural adjustment impossible.

## What would move me

Only two things would move me materially:

1. **A credible non-closure package that demonstrably restores competitiveness at the required scale and duration**
2. **Or a quantified site-by-site case showing that certain hard measures are genuinely unavoidable**

Absent one of those, I will not shift toward a blanket protection package.

## Bottom line

I am not here to endorse closures for their own sake.  
I am also not here to endorse a compromise that preserves every current footprint while the company continues to erode.

The family holding will support:
- a real competitiveness reset,
- durable margin improvement,
- and a settlement that avoids unnecessary instability.

But we will **not** support:
- a closure taboo,
- a forced-layoff taboo,
- or a governance posture that prevents the company from taking necessary structural action if the numbers demand it.

That is our line.
