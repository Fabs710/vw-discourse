Let me be equally clear in Round 3.

The discussion is now fully exposed: everyone agrees the company has a competitiveness problem, but the employee side still wants to turn “no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” into untouchable principles. That is not a restructuring strategy. It is a refusal to confront the scale of the problem.

## To the Works Council
I agree with you on one point: the workforce should not be the only party contributing to the reset.

But I fundamentally reject your red lines.

You keep saying:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no relocation,
- no structural job losses.

That is not a credible answer to a company with:
- a materially uncompetitive German cost base,
- shrinking demand in Europe,
- excess capacity,
- and a brand that is missing its return-on-sales target.

If you want a serious settlement, then you cannot pre-emptively ban the tools that may be necessary to restore competitiveness.

You also say the burden is on management to prove alternatives first. Fine — we accept that burden. But that does not mean the burden can never be met. And it certainly does not mean the workforce gets a veto over any outcome that contains real structural change.

Your position assumes that every plant can be preserved if only management works harder at the margins. That may be politically convenient, but it is not economically convincing.

## To the Industrial Union
I appreciate that you are willing to discuss temporary concessions, working-time flexibility, voluntary exits, and a transformation fund. That is a constructive shift in tone.

But your core line remains insufficient:
- temporary measures alone will not solve a structural overcapacity problem,
- and temporary employee contributions are not enough if the cost base remains too high.

I agree with that. Where I disagree with you is your insistence that all change must happen without closure risk or compulsory redundancies.

That would only be acceptable if you can show, with numbers, that the required savings and capacity reduction can be achieved permanently without those instruments.

So far, you have not done that.

If you want to argue that non-closure measures can fully replace structural exits, then show me:
- how the savings match the required scale,
- how the timing is fast enough,
- how the effect is durable through 2030,
- and how the capacity gap is actually closed.

Until then, saying “no closures” is not a solution. It is just a boundary.

## To the Regional State Government
I respect your insistence on a site-by-site analysis, and I support that.

But your line is still too evasive. You say closures should be a last resort, not a starting point. I can work with that. What I cannot accept is turning “last resort” into “never.”

The state cannot simultaneously demand:
- full industrial presence,
- full competitiveness,
- no forced redundancies,
- no closures,
- and no relocation,
while refusing to explain who pays for the difference.

That arithmetic does not work.

If you want to play a real steering role, then help shape a package that brings down the cost base in a way that is durable and implementable. But do not pretend that a political veto can substitute for an economic plan.

## To the Controlling Family Holding
You are right to insist on evidence rather than slogans. I agree.

But I also need to be equally blunt: if ownership supports a line that preserves every current structure in Germany regardless of economics, then it is not stewardship — it is inertia.

We do not need a cosmetic compromise. We need a structural reset.

You keep asking for quantified comparisons. Fine. We will provide them. But let me be clear in advance: if the numbers show that some sites are structurally uncompetitive and that non-closure measures do not close the gap, then closures and/or compulsory headcount reductions must remain available.

That is not a threat. It is economic reality.

And if the family wants to protect long-term value, then it should support the measures that actually restore margins — not the ones that merely postpone the pain.

## To Institutional Investors
You are right about one thing: the solution must be economically sufficient, durable, and implementable.

That is exactly why cosmetic middle-ground solutions are not acceptable.

But I reject your implication that avoiding closures is always preferable if it reduces friction. Not if the result is a weak cost base and a permanently impaired margin structure.

You say you support alternatives if they deliver equivalent savings. Good. Then we are aligned in principle.

The real question is whether the alternatives the employee side is willing to accept are actually equivalent in scale and durability.

So far, the answer is no.

## On the expert analysis
The specialist has already made the key point: in a mature auto network, temporary concessions, working-time changes, and voluntary exits can help, but they usually do **not** eliminate a structural overcapacity problem by themselves.

That is the operational reality.

So when the employee side says:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- internal solutions first,
- temporary contributions only,

they are describing a package that may reduce tension, but not one that necessarily restores competitiveness.

That is the gap that still has not been closed.

## What management will accept
Let me restate our position without ambiguity:

We are prepared to work with:
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary exits,
- temporary employee contributions,
- targeted capacity reductions,
- and a quantified site-by-site review.

We are **not** prepared to accept:
- a blanket ban on closures,
- a blanket ban on compulsory redundancies,
- a permanent job guarantee through 2030 if it blocks structural adjustment,
- or a deal that leaves the cost base uncompetitive.

## What would actually move us
Only a credible, quantified package that delivers:
- permanent cost reduction,
- permanent capacity adjustment,
- and durable margin improvement

without destroying the company’s future investment ability.

If the employee side can produce a non-closure package that genuinely matches the scale and permanence of what is needed, we will consider it seriously.

But until that exists, closures and compulsory redundancies must remain on the table.

## Bottom line
I will not pretend that protecting every current site is the same as solving the crisis.

It is not.

The company needs a structural reset, not a symbolic compromise.  
It needs lower costs, lower overcapacity, and better margins.  
It needs room to invest in future products and technology.

If the employee side wants to avoid the hardest measures, then it needs to bring a package that actually works at the scale required.

Otherwise, management will continue to pursue the restructuring the business needs — and we will not retreat from that simply because the alternatives are uncomfortable.
