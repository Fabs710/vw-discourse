Management keeps saying “structural necessity” as if repeating the phrase makes plant closures and compulsory redundancies acceptable. It does not.

Let me be equally direct in Round 2:

## What we agree on
Yes, the company has a competitiveness problem.
Yes, cost reduction is needed.
Yes, the solution must be durable, not symbolic.
Yes, we need future investment and a viable 2030 plan.

But none of that justifies making German plants pay with closures and forced layoffs while management asks the workforce to absorb the full shock of strategic failure.

## What we reject
I reject the idea that:
- closures are “on the table” as a normal option,
- compulsory redundancies are somehow a legitimate planning tool,
- and the workforce must first prove that every alternative is mathematically identical to a closure package before job security is respected.

That is backwards. The burden is on management to prove that it has exhausted all alternatives that preserve sites and jobs.

And let me be crystal clear: **no German plant closure, no compulsory redundancies, no relocation of production out of Germany**. Those are not bargaining chips to us. They are red lines.

## On management’s argument
You say the cost base is too high. Then show us the full package, site by site:
- where exactly are the savings coming from,
- what capacity is genuinely needed,
- what internal redeployment has been used,
- what working-time tools are available,
- what management and shareholder contributions are on the table,
- and why closures are unavoidable despite all that.

Until then, closure is not a solution; it is a choice.

You also talk about “certainty.” The most certain way to destroy trust, trigger strikes, and damage value is to threaten plants and then expect the workforce to cooperate quietly. If you want industrial peace, you need to stop treating the workforce as disposable.

## To the union
We are aligned on the essentials: no closures, no forced redundancies, shared burden. Where I sharpen the point is this: the workforce has already shown willingness to discuss flexibility, qualification, redeployment, and temporary measures. That willingness should not be mistaken for openness to structural job destruction.

Any employee contribution must be:
- limited,
- temporary,
- and conditional on binding site and job guarantees.

No guarantees, no contribution. That is the line.

## To the regional state government
You are right to say the state cannot be sidelined. But I want to be frank: your veto only matters if you are prepared to use it to stop closures and forced layoffs, not just to demand “dialogue.”

If the board is serious about social partnership, then the state should back a settlement with:
- binding investment commitments,
- protection of all German sites,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and real long-term industrial planning.

Otherwise, the workforce will quite rightly see governance as decorative.

## To the family holding
You say you do not want value destruction. Good. Then do not back a restructuring that destroys the company’s social foundation in Germany. A long strike is not caused by the workforce defending itself; it is caused by management attacking historic guarantees.

If ownership wants stability, it should support a settlement based on:
- job guarantees through 2030,
- no plant closures,
- no forced layoffs,
- internal transformation instead of destruction,
- and a real commitment to future production in Germany.

Anything less is not stewardship; it is short-term pressure dressed up as strategy.

## To institutional investors
You keep asking for “certainty” and “durable savings.” Let me give you certainty: a plan that breaks co-determination, tears up guarantees, and closes plants will not produce a stable workforce or a stable company. It will produce conflict, reputational damage, and long-term weakness.

If you want value protected, then back a solution that preserves industrial capacity and skills. Asset-stripping the German base is not a credible route to long-term enterprise value.

## Our position
We are prepared to discuss:
- working-time adjustments,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- temporary wage-related measures,
- capacity adjustments that do not involve closures,
- and a fair contribution from management and shareholders.

We are **not** prepared to discuss:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or the dismantling of job-security guarantees.

If management brings a package that respects those limits and includes binding guarantees through 2030, then we can talk about the form of contribution. Without that, we will resist — in the boardroom and in the plants.

## Bottom line
This company does not need a social massacre to become competitive.

It needs:
- management discipline,
- fair burden-sharing,
- real investment,
- and respect for the workforce.

If the other side insists that only closures and layoffs can solve this, then they are not presenting an economic necessity. They are presenting a political choice — and one we will block.
