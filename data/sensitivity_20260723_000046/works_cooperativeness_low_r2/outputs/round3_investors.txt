## Round 3 — Dispersed capital-market shareholders

We are aligned with one basic point that everyone now seems to acknowledge: **the company needs a durable competitiveness reset, not cosmetic delay**. That is the only reason we are still in this discussion.

But let’s be equally clear: **our priority is risk-adjusted shareholder value**, not preserving every current footprint. If the final package does not credibly restore margins and lower the structural cost base, we will not support it.

## To management
We agree with you on the diagnosis:
- the German cost base is too high,
- capacity is misaligned,
- market conditions are tougher,
- and the brand is missing its return-on-sales target.

That said, you still have not done enough to justify your preferred instruments. Repeating “structural reset” is not a substitute for a quantified path.

What we need from you is not more rhetoric, but a **site-by-site comparison** of:
- closure,
- partial footprint reduction,
- shift or line elimination,
- redeployment,
- voluntary exits,
- working-time changes,
- and temporary employee contributions,

with:
- recurring annual savings,
- one-time costs,
- timing,
- and implementation risk.

We are **not** attached to closures as an ideology. If you can deliver the same savings with less disruption, fine. But if the alternatives do not deliver durable, meaningful cost reduction, then you need to say so plainly.

What we will not back is a settlement that:
- keeps an uncompetitive cost base,
- avoids hard decisions,
- and hopes markets will reward ambiguity.

That would be value-destructive.

## To the works council
We agree with you on one thing: the workforce should not be the sole shock absorber of the crisis.

We also agree that temporary concessions alone are not enough if the company’s structural problem is serious.

Where we disagree sharply is your attempt to rule out closures and compulsory redundancies in advance. That position is not credible if the company genuinely needs a major permanent reset.

You say closures are a political choice, not an economic necessity. Sometimes that is true. But if the numbers show that a site is structurally uncompetitive and non-closure measures do not close the gap, then refusing even to consider closure becomes a veto on competitiveness.

That is not acceptable to us.

We are open to:
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary exits,
- temporary employee contributions,
- and internal capacity adjustments.

But those must be **durable and sufficient**. If they are not, then the package is too weak.

And to be explicit: **a blanket ban on compulsory redundancies is not acceptable if overcapacity remains unresolved.** We dislike the disruption of layoffs, but we dislike ongoing margin erosion more.

## To the industrial union
You are right to insist on numbers, and you are right that temporary-only measures do not solve a structural problem.

Where we differ is your insistence that the answer must avoid closures and forced redundancies. That is only defensible if the alternative package delivers equivalent permanent savings and capacity reduction.

So far, that case has not been made.

We can support:
- bonus moderation,
- temporary wage relief,
- working-time corridors,
- voluntary exit packages,
- internal mobility,
- and a transformation fund,

but only if these are part of a package that actually restores competitiveness.

If your red lines prevent the company from achieving a structurally lower cost base, then they are not protecting value — they are eroding it.

## To the regional state government
We agree that closures should not be the first resort.

We also agree that the state has a legitimate governance role and that a site-by-site analysis is necessary before any irreversible decision.

Where we differ is the implication that a closure can be blocked simply because it is politically difficult. If the analysis shows a site is not viable, then the state should not force the company to carry dead weight indefinitely.

Capital markets will not reward a structure that keeps excess capacity alive for political comfort.

We would support a staged process:
1. quantified site-by-site review,
2. testing of non-closure options,
3. then, only if the gap remains, hard structural action.

That is a reasonable sequencing. What is not reasonable is pre-committing to no closures regardless of the numbers.

## To the controlling family holding
We agree with your rejection of a cosmetic compromise and your concern about long labor conflict. A strike spiral would destroy value.

We also agree that the least destructive workable solution is preferable.

But your language still leaves too much room for delay. “Exhaust alternatives” must not become code for indefinite avoidance of necessary action.

If the quantified comparison shows that non-closure measures can truly deliver equivalent savings, we would prefer that route.

If they cannot, then the company must keep closures and other hard measures available. Otherwise the enterprise stays structurally impaired, and that is not stewardship — it is drift.

## Our position

Our support depends on one thing: **a credible, durable, quantified return to competitive margins**.

So we can support:
- temporary employee contributions,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment and qualification,
- working-time flexibility,
- targeted capacity reductions,
- and even a non-closure restructuring if it is truly sufficient.

We cannot support:
- a cosmetic settlement,
- a deal that preserves excess cost structure,
- or a package that bans the instruments needed to fix the business.

And to be blunt: **if the final deal leaves the company’s cost base uncompetitive, we will treat that as a failure, regardless of how socially elegant it looks.**

## What would move us
Only a quantified package that shows:
- permanent cost reduction,
- capacity aligned with demand,
- implementation certainty,
- and a clear path back to acceptable margins.

If that can be achieved without closures, great.
If not, then closures and compulsory headcount reductions must remain available.

That is the line we are holding.

## Bottom line
We are not here to defend every plant.
We are here to protect enterprise value.

A plan that restores competitiveness is supportable.
A plan that postpones the hard decisions is not.

If management brings a real, durable, numbers-backed package, we will consider it.
If not, then the company should not expect capital markets to endorse a compromise that leaves the problem intact.
