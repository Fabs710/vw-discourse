I will be direct.

The discussion has clarified the central issue: **the company needs a durable restructuring, but no one has yet shown that German plant closures or compulsory redundancies are unavoidable**. Until that proof exists, I will not treat those instruments as normal or acceptable options.

## To management

I agree with you on the diagnosis:
- the cost base is too high,
- capacity is misaligned,
- and temporary measures alone will not solve a structural problem.

I also agree that we need a **durable** solution, not a cosmetic one.

Where I disagree is your insistence that closures and forced layoffs must remain available in the way you frame them: as if they are the default serious answer. That is not proven. It is a preference, not yet a necessity.

You have still not provided the quantified site-by-site case that would justify saying:
- non-closure measures are insufficient,
- a specific plant is truly unviable,
- and closures or involuntary redundancies are the only remaining route.

Until you do, I will continue to insist on:
- a rigorous site-by-site comparison,
- alternatives tested first,
- and no premature closure decision.

And let me be clear: I will not support a process that uses the threat of closure simply to force concessions from the workforce.

## To the works council

I agree with your core demand that the workforce must not be the sole shock absorber of this crisis. I also agree that:

- working-time flexibility can help,
- redeployment matters,
- qualification matters,
- voluntary exits can help,
- and internal capacity adjustment should come first.

I do **not** agree with any attempt to ignore the need for real change, though. A blanket refusal to discuss any hard structural adjustment would be unrealistic.

But that is not what is happening here. What is happening is that management is trying to move straight to the harshest instruments without proving that softer, site-preserving measures have been exhausted.

So your demand for a presumption of preservation is legitimate. A plant-by-plant review is only acceptable if it is designed to test that presumption seriously, not to create a procedural path to closure.

## To the industrial union

You are right to insist that temporary concessions alone are not enough. I accept that.

I also agree that any employee contribution must be:
- limited,
- conditional,
- and tied to binding countercommitments.

Where I disagree is your rejection of any closure logic before the numbers are even tested. I do not want closure as a starting point, but I also will not give a blanket no-closure promise before we know whether a site can actually be preserved.

That said, I am prepared to support a strong package if it includes:
- binding no-forced-redundancy commitments for a defined period,
- no site closure during that period,
- meaningful management and owner contributions,
- investment commitments in Germany,
- and future product allocation for German sites.

If those guarantees are real, then temporary employee concessions become discussable. Without them, they are not.

## To the controlling family holding

I agree with you on two important points:
- a strike spiral and governance breakdown would destroy value,
- and the company needs a real reset, not a symbolic one.

Where I disagree is the implication that structural exits should remain a routine or automatic fallback. That may be financially tidy in theory, but it risks doing lasting damage to the company’s role as a regional anchor and to the legitimacy of the governance model.

Stewardship here means more than numerical optimization. It means preserving the company’s industrial base, social peace, and long-term credibility in its home region.

So yes, I support hard scrutiny of every site. But I do **not** accept a closure-first mentality disguised as realism.

## To institutional investors

You are right that shareholder value depends on durable savings and implementation certainty.

But you are underestimating the value destruction caused by industrial conflict, skill loss, trust breakdown, and reputational damage. A “clean” financial cut that ignites prolonged confrontation is not automatically value-enhancing.

I would support a slower or less dramatic savings path **if** it is still durable and quantified, because that may be the better risk-adjusted outcome. What I will not support is a deal that simply keeps the company on a collision course with its workforce.

## My position

I can support a tough restructuring if it meets these conditions:

1. **Site-by-site proof before any closure decision**
2. **Non-closure measures tested first**
3. **No compulsory redundancies as a default tool**
4. **Binding job and site protections for a defined horizon**
5. **Meaningful contributions from management, owners, and workforce**
6. **Investment and future-production commitments in Germany**
7. **Full respect for co-determination and the state’s blocking role**

That is not obstruction. That is responsible anchor ownership.

## What would move me

I would be prepared to consider more difficult adjustments only if management produces a serious quantified matrix showing that:
- non-closure measures cannot close the gap,
- specific sites are structurally unviable,
- and any hard step is proportionate and paired with real compensation in terms of jobs, investment, and regional commitment.

Until then, I will not move toward closures or forced layoffs.

## Bottom line

The company needs competitiveness, yes.
But it also needs legitimacy, regional stability, and a future as an industrial anchor in Germany.

I will not trade those away for a supposedly efficient restructuring that leaves behind deep social damage and a weaker enterprise.

If management wants the state’s support, it must first prove that credible alternatives have been exhausted and that any hard step is truly unavoidable. Otherwise, I will use the rights I have.
