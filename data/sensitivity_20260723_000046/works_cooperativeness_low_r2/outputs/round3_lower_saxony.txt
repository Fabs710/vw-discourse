## Round 3 — Regional State Government position

Let me be very clear: I will not support a restructuring that treats German plants as disposable or uses “competitiveness” as a blanket justification for gutting regional employment.

I recognize the reality of the situation:
- the company has a serious cost and capacity problem,
- the status quo is not sustainable,
- and some form of durable adjustment is needed.

On that, I agree with management, the family holder, and the capital side.

But I disagree sharply with the way management keeps framing the choice: as if closures and compulsory redundancies were simply the responsible, grown-up answer. That is not yet proven, and I do not accept it as a starting point.

### To management
You still have not shown that plant closures are unavoidable.

The expert has made an important point: temporary measures alone are not enough if the gap is truly large. I accept that. But the same expert also made clear that the required permanent adjustment could be achieved through different combinations of measures, depending on the scale of the savings target.

So I expect, before any closure talk becomes serious:
- a site-by-site utilization picture,
- a quantified comparison of closure versus non-closure options,
- the savings from shift deletion, footprint reduction, redeployment, voluntary exits, and labor-cost measures,
- and a realistic implementation timeline.

Until that is on the table, “closure” is not necessity. It is management preference.

And I need to say this directly: I will not back a package that destroys jobs in the state just because it is the easiest path on a spreadsheet.

### To the works council and union
I support your core refusal to let the workforce bear the whole burden.

You are right that:
- no one should be forced into layoffs by default,
- temporary measures can contribute,
- internal solutions must come first,
- and the company’s German industrial base matters.

Where I differ slightly is that I will not turn “no closures ever” into a formal principle before the numbers are tested. That would be irresponsible from a governance perspective.

But I am also not prepared to let management define “last resort” so loosely that closure becomes the predetermined outcome.

So my line is:
- alternatives first,
- closures only if they are truly proven unavoidable,
- and no bypassing of co-determination or the state’s veto role.

### To the controlling family holding
I agree with your insistence on stewardship rather than short-term optics.

But stewardship here means more than restoring margins. It also means preserving the company’s legitimacy in its home region and not detonating the social compact that has made this company durable for decades.

A plan that provokes a long strike, destroys trust, and hollows out German capacity is not stewardship. It is value destruction in another form.

So yes, I support hard scrutiny of alternatives. But I will not support a closure-first doctrine masquerading as realism.

### To institutional investors
You are right to insist on durability and implementation certainty.

But I reject the idea that the only way to get that certainty is by forcing through closures and involuntary cuts. In this governance structure, that approach may produce years of conflict and a weaker company, not a stronger one.

If you want real value protection, then you should support a package that:
- delivers quantified savings,
- preserves industrial peace,
- protects future investment,
- and keeps the company anchored in Germany.

That may mean slower or less dramatic savings than you want at the outset. But a stable solution is worth more than a brutal one that blows up execution.

## My actual position

I can support a tough restructuring, but only if it follows these principles:

1. **Site-by-site proof before any closure decision**
2. **Non-closure measures tested first**
3. **No compulsory redundancies as the default tool**
4. **Binding job and site guarantees where feasible**
5. **Meaningful contributions from management, owners, and workforce**
6. **Investment commitments for German future production**
7. **Full respect for co-determination and the state’s blocking rights**

That is not obstruction. That is responsible ownership and public stewardship.

I am prepared to support:
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary exits,
- temporary employee contributions,
- targeted capacity reduction,
- and a negotiated transformation package.

I am **not** prepared to support:
- plant closures as a first resort,
- compulsory redundancies as a routine restructuring instrument,
- production relocation out of Germany,
- or any deal that sidelines the state’s role.

## What would move me
I would be open to a more difficult settlement if management can prove, with a serious site-by-site matrix, that:
- non-closure measures cannot achieve enough savings,
- a specific site is truly unviable,
- and the proposed remedy is proportionate and accompanied by binding compensation in terms of jobs, investment, and regional commitment.

Until then, I will continue to resist any move toward closures or forced layoffs.

## Bottom line
The company needs competitiveness, yes.
But it also needs legitimacy, regional stability, and a future as an industrial anchor in Germany.

I will not trade those away for a supposedly clean restructuring that leaves behind deep social damage and a weaker long-term enterprise.

If management wants the state’s support, it must first prove that it has exhausted credible alternatives and that any hard step is truly unavoidable. Otherwise, I will use the rights I have — and I mean that.
