[Works Council (Betriebsrat) (Plant-level employee representation under co-determination) — Round 1]

We will not accept plant closures in Germany, and we will not accept compulsory redundancies.

Our priorities are:

- No forced layoffs
- No plant closures or relocation of production out of Germany

Management must stop treating the workforce as a cost item to be cut. If there is to be restructuring, it has to be socially responsible, legally robust, and future-oriented. Otherwise, we will resist it with all the means available to us.

## What is operationally plausible

### 1) Non-closure alternatives can work only if the required savings are moderate
Tools that avoid plant closure include:
- reducing shifts,
- deleting a line or a shift at a plant,
- moving models between plants,
- using natural attrition and voluntary exits,
- internal redeployment,
- shorter working time,
- temporary pay moderation or bonus sacrifice.

These can produce real savings, but the ceiling is limited.

In practice:
- **Working-time measures** mainly reduce labor cost, not fixed overhead.
- **Voluntary exits** are useful, but they are slower and uncertain in uptake.
- **Redeployment/training** preserves employment but does not reduce total payroll much unless there is genuine surplus labor.
- **Capacity reduction without closure** can work if a site can be reconfigured to a smaller footprint and lower overhead, but the savings are weaker than full site exit because utilities, maintenance, logistics, and supervisory overhead remain.

So if management needs only a **mid-single-digit percentage** reduction in German operating cost, a no-closure package may be enough.  
If it needs a **large structural reset**, it is usually not enough.

### 2) Plant closure is the highest-savings option, but only if the plant is truly underutilized
A closure delivers the full removal of:
- direct labor,
- indirect labor,
- maintenance,
- utilities,
- local plant overhead,
- inbound/outbound logistics complexity,
- and depreciation burden if assets are written down or mothballed.

But the savings depend on whether the plant’s output can be absorbed elsewhere without creating new bottlenecks or excessive transfer cost. The closure is operationally compelling when:
- the site is underutilized,
- the product mix is mature or can be relocated,
- the plant has limited future model assignment,
- and the receiving plants have spare capacity.

Closure is much less attractive if:
- the site has specialized tooling,
- the workforce has scarce skills,
- the product is EV-sensitive and logistics-heavy,
- or the network is already tight.

- supplier disruption,

## Relative savings ranking
In most automotive networks, the usual ranking is:

1. **Full plant closure**  
   Highest recurring savings, highest labor disruption, highest implementation friction.

2. **Full line elimination or shift deletion at a plant**  
   Often the best compromise if the site is too large to keep unchanged but too politically sensitive to close.

3. **Footprint reduction / partial consolidation**  
   Good if the plant can be shrunk and still operate efficiently.

4. **Working-time and labor-cost concessions**  
   Helpful, but usually insufficient alone for structural overcapacity.

5. **Voluntary exit programs**  
   Useful as a transition tool, but not dependable as the sole source of permanent savings.

## Timing
This matters a lot.

- **Temporary concessions** can be implemented relatively quickly, but savings begin to expire unless renewed.
- **Voluntary exits** take months to years to fully realize.
- **Capacity reductions and product transfers** typically take 12–36 months depending on tooling and homologation.
- **Plant closure** is the longest and most disruptive, often requiring multi-year run-down, supplier transition, severance processing, and asset disposition.

So if the company needs near-term earnings relief, it may need a **bridge package** first, then a structural package. A bridge package alone is not a final answer.

- A package relying only on **temporary concessions, working-time changes, and voluntary measures** is usually **not durable enough** to solve a structural overcapacity problem in a mature carmaker.

- **retain**

## 1) Core decision logic

For each plant, assess five variables:

1. **Utilization**
   - Current and projected annual load
   - Whether the site is running near efficient scale
   - Whether the line can be loaded with future products

2. **Fixed-cost burden**
   - Plant overhead per vehicle
   - Utilities, maintenance, indirect labor, logistics, depreciation-like operating burden
   - How much of the cost base is truly fixed vs. adjustable

3. **Future model allocation**
   - Is the plant assigned one or more future programs?
   - Are those programs volume-stable or uncertain?
   - Is there a credible path to fill the site beyond the next 12–24 months?

4. **Savings from non-closure measures**
   - Shift deletion
   - Line consolidation
   - Footprint reduction
   - Working-time flexibility
   - Voluntary exits
   - Redeployment
   - Wage moderation / bonus moderation

5. **One-time transition cost**
   - Closure / decommissioning
   - Severance
   - Transfer or dual-running costs
   - Tooling relocation
   - Supply-chain disruption
   - Start-up duplication elsewhere

The correct choice is the one with the best **durable savings per unit of implementation risk**.

---

## 2) Practical thresholds by outcome

### A. Retain
A plant is usually a retention candidate if:

- utilization is reasonably high, or can be restored with known future volume
- at least one future model is allocated with meaningful volume
- fixed-cost burden can be brought down through shift/line optimization
- non-closure measures can close most of the gap
- logistics and supply resilience are strategically important

**Typical operational profile**
- Moderate savings from internal efficiency
- No need for irreversible action
- Best for sites with strategic product relevance

### B. Consolidate / footprint reduce
A plant is a consolidation candidate if:

- utilization is weak but not hopeless
- future allocation exists, but not enough to justify full current footprint
- the site can be downsized to a smaller, stable core
- shift deletion or line consolidation captures a large part of the savings
- the site remains useful after capacity removal

**Typical operational profile**
- Significant fixed-cost reduction
- Lower social and implementation cost than closure
- Often the best answer when a site is “too big for its volume, but not obsolete”

### C. Close
A plant becomes a closure candidate if:

- utilization is persistently low even after realistic consolidation
- future product allocation is absent or too small
- fixed costs remain structurally too high at any viable footprint
- savings from partial measures are materially inferior to the residual burden
- the site has no credible long-term role in the production network

**Typical operational profile**
- Highest permanent savings
- Highest one-time cost
- Highest conflict and execution risk
- Only justified when underutilization and poor future allocation are structural, not cyclical

## 3) Simple matrix thresholds

Here is a practical decision table.

| Criterion | Retain | Consolidate | Close |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Utilization | Adequate | Weak but recoverable | Persistently low |
| Future model allocation | Clear | Partial / limited | None or insufficient |
| Fixed-cost burden | Manageable | High but reducible | Structurally excessive |
| Savings from non-closure measures | High share of gap | Moderate share of gap | Low share of gap |
| Closure cost vs. ongoing burden | Not justified | Usually not justified | Justified |
| Implementation risk | Low | Moderate | High but acceptable if economics are strong |

A plant should not be closed just because it is expensive. It should be closed only if the **remaining economic burden after consolidation is still materially worse than the value of keeping it open**.

- **Closure** is the only measure that fully removes the fixed cost base of a deeply underloaded site.

So if management is asking for a large durable reset, then:
- temporary measures alone are not enough,
- one plant may be a consolidation case,
- and one or more plants may still be closure cases if their future load is too weak.

The key is that **the burden must be proven at site level**.

It wants quantified site-level analysis and keeps closure/headcount reduction available if the numbers justify it, while also valuing industrial peace and long-term legitimacy.

### Institutional Investors
Investors want risk-adjusted value creation: durable savings, implementation certainty, and a lower cost base. They are open to slower or less dramatic savings if conflict risk falls, but they reject cosmetic settlements and want closure or involuntary reductions to remain possible where a site is structurally uncompetitive.

### Independent Expert
The expert concludes that temporary labor concessions can bridge cash pressure but not solve structural overcapacity. It recommends a plant-by-plant matrix using utilization, fixed-cost burden, future model allocation, savings from non-closure measures, and one-time closure costs to distinguish retain, consolidate, and close.

## 4. Genuine conflicts

1. **Closure as last resort vs. unacceptable instrument**  
   Management, family, and investors treat closures as a legitimate fallback; the works council and union treat them as a red line, not merely a last resort.

2. **Forced redundancies**  
   Capital-side actors see compulsory headcount reduction as potentially necessary; employee-side actors reject it categorically.

3. **Presumption of preservation vs. presumption of economic necessity**  
   Worker and state positions start from site retention; management and investors start from the need for structural cost removal.

4. **Who bears the burden**  
   Labor wants management/owners to contribute first and avoid one-sided sacrifice; capital wants labor to share more materially and durably.

5. **Threshold for “unviable” sites**  
   No shared rule exists for when a plant crosses from difficult to non-viable, which is now the core procedural battleground.

## 5. Areas of convergence

- All parties accept the diagnosis that **inaction is not an option**.
- All parties agree the solution must be **durable**, not cosmetic.
- There is broad acceptance that **temporary measures alone are insufficient**.
- Everyone supports, at least in principle, a **site-by-site assessment**.
- There is shared recognition that **labor peace, implementation certainty, and future investment capacity** matter.
- Several non-closure tools are broadly acknowledged as useful: working-time flexibility, redeployment, qualification, voluntary exits, and shift/line reductions.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker

- **Speed and savings vs. conflict and legitimacy**: closures may deliver the clearest structural savings, but they risk strikes, governance vetoes, and reputational damage.
- **Permanent cost reduction vs. employment stability**: the more the company protects sites and jobs, the less immediate room it may have to restore margins.
- **Analytical rigor vs. bargaining power**: a site-by-site matrix could create legitimacy, but parties fear it will be used tactically to justify pre-decided outcomes.
- **Short-term cash relief vs. long-term structural adjustment**: employee concessions help bridge, but they do not by themselves solve overcapacity.
- **Governance feasibility vs. managerial discretion**: the company cannot restructure effectively unless the supervisory-board coalition can carry the decision.

## 7. Integrative pathways

1. **Time-limited preservation pact**
   - No closures and no compulsory redundancies for a fixed period.
   - In exchange: temporary wage moderation, bonus moderation, working-time flexibility, voluntary exits, management/owner contribution, and binding German investment commitments.

2. **Site-by-site retain / consolidate / last-resort close matrix**
   - Common criteria: utilization, future model allocation, fixed-cost burden, savings from non-closure measures, closure costs, and implementation risk.
   - This turns the debate from ideology into threshold-based classification.

3. **Sequenced restructuring**
   - Phase 1: bridge measures only.
   - Phase 2: quantified review by site.
   - Phase 3: hard measures only if agreed thresholds are not met.
   - This may reduce immediate conflict while preserving a fallback path.

4. **Conditional exchange model**
   - Stronger employee concessions are exchanged for stronger guarantees: no relocation out of Germany, no forced redundancies for a defined period, product allocation, and monitored investment commitments.

## 8. Open questions

- Which specific sites, if any, fail the viability test even after shift reduction and footprint consolidation?
- What exact savings target must be met, and by when, to satisfy management and investors?
- What level of temporary labor contribution is politically and legally acceptable to the workforce?
- What objective threshold will the state and family consider sufficient to justify a hard step?
- Can the company credibly commit to future product allocation and German investment while retaining flexibility?
- How should closure costs, transition costs, and labor conflict risk be weighted against permanent savings?

## 9. Recommended next step

Adopt a **formal site-by-site decision matrix** before any final commitment on closures or compulsory redundancies. The matrix should score each plant on utilization, future model allocation, fixed-cost burden, savings from consolidation, voluntary-exit potential, one-time closure costs, and implementation risk.  

Parallel to that analysis, negotiate a **time-limited bridge package** of temporary measures and owner/management contributions, contingent on binding countercommitments on jobs, investment, and no premature relocation.

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction

- The real deadlock is **not whether restructuring is needed**, but **which instruments are legitimate to achieve it** and **who must prove necessity**.
- The most important shift in the dialogue was from abstract “restructuring vs. no restructuring” to **site-specific viability thresholds**.
- Temporary employee concessions were repeatedly shown to be **useful but insufficient**, a distinction that only became sharp after the expert’s intervention.
- The state and family holder emerged as true **hinge actors**: both support restructuring, but only if it is filtered through governance legitimacy and site-level evidence.
- A practical compromise may depend less on agreement about closures in principle than on agreement about **what counts as exhausted alternatives**.
- The employee side’s strongest position is not simple refusal; it is the demand for a **presumption of preservation plus quantified proof before irreversible steps**.
- The capital side’s strongest position is not closure advocacy per se; it is the insistence on **durable structural savings and risk-adjusted value**, which can in some cases be achieved without closure if consolidation is deep enough.
- The interaction revealed that “no forced redundancies” and “no closures” are being treated by labor not as bargaining points but as **identity-level red lines**, making them structurally harder to trade than temporary wage concessions.
