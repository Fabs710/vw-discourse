From an industrial-operations standpoint, the main issue is whether the company can achieve the required **durable cost reduction and capacity alignment** without plant closures and compulsory redundancies, and if so, **how much of the gap those alternatives can realistically close**.

## My assessment

### 1) Temporary measures alone are unlikely to be sufficient
The employee side is right on the narrow point that working-time flexibility, redeployment, qualification, voluntary exits, and temporary wage moderation can all contribute. But in a mature automotive network with persistent overcapacity, those tools usually deliver only **partial, time-limited relief**.

Typical order of magnitude:
- **Working-time changes / shift reduction:** meaningful if a plant is underutilized, but savings depend on headcount and shift premium structure.
- **Voluntary exits:** useful for lowering structural headcount, but slow and limited by take-up.
- **Wage moderation / bonus waivers:** can reduce annual labor cost, but usually not enough to offset a large fixed-cost gap.
- **Redeployment / training:** operationally necessary, but mostly a reallocation tool, not a savings engine.

If management truly needs a **large permanent reset**, the non-closure package has to be unusually deep and very well enforced to match closure-level savings.

### 2) Closure is not the only way to remove capacity, but it is the cleanest way
Operationally, there are several ways to reduce capacity without full plant closure:
- deleting shifts,
- idling or removing lines,
- consolidating production into fewer halls,
- transferring models to other sites,
- shrinking footprint and utilities load,
- reducing indirect labor and logistics overhead.

These can be effective if a plant is only marginally over-sized. But if a site has:
- poor utilization,
- high fixed overhead,
- unfavorable logistics,
- limited model pipeline,
- and significant future capex needs,

then partial measures often leave a **residual cost burden** that is still too high.

That is why management keeps closures on the table: not because closure is ideal, but because it is the most decisive way to eliminate fixed cost, surplus indirect labor, and stranded assets.

### 3) “No forced redundancies” is feasible only if attrition and voluntary exits are enough
A no-compulsory-redundancy commitment can work if:
- natural attrition is high,
- retirement eligible cohorts are large,
- and the company has enough time.

If the adjustment has to happen quickly, that commitment becomes much harder. Then the company must either:
- pay for very generous voluntary programs, or
- accept that headcount will not fall fast enough.

So the key operational question is not ideological; it is:
**How many FTEs can be removed by voluntary means within the required timing window?**

If the required reduction is, say, several thousand jobs over a short horizon, voluntary programs alone may not be sufficient unless heavily subsidized.

### 4) The right test is site-by-site, not abstract
The state’s call for a site-by-site matrix is operationally correct. Each plant should be tested on:

- current and forecast utilization,
- model allocation certainty,
- labor intensity per unit,
- logistics cost,
- energy and overhead cost,
- capex required to stay competitive,
- ability to absorb shift loss,
- and closure or consolidation cost.

A plant with:
- a stable model,
- strong utilization,
- and low incremental capex requirement

is a very different case from a plant with:
- no future product,
- chronic underutilization,
- and high fixed overhead.

Without that matrix, the debate stays ideological.

## What a realistic compromise would look like
If the goal is to avoid immediate closures, the most credible operational package would be:

1. **12–18 months bridge period**
   - working-time flexibility
   - temporary bonus moderation
   - voluntary exits
   - redeployment/training
   - management overhead reduction

2. **Binding site-by-site review**
   - predefined utilization and savings thresholds
   - transparent comparison of closure vs. partial consolidation vs. shift deletion

3. **Conditional structural action**
   - if a site cannot reach a viable cost position after full non-closure measures, then partial consolidation or closure remains available

That is the only structure I see as operationally credible.

## Bottom line
From a plant-economics perspective:

- The employee side is right that **closures are not the starting point**.
- Management is right that **temporary measures alone are unlikely to close a large structural gap**.
- The decisive question is whether **non-closure measures can deliver equivalent durable savings at the required speed**.

In most automotive restructuring cases, the answer is: **only partly**.
