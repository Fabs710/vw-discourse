## Round 3 — Industrial Union position

I’ll be equally direct.

We are not here to defend the status quo at any price. We are here to defend **jobs, collective standards, and a fair restructuring**. Those are not slogans. They are the conditions for any settlement that can actually hold.

What I hear from management and the capital side is still the same basic pattern:
- acknowledge the crisis,
- demand “durable” savings,
- keep closures and forced redundancies available,
- and ask the workforce to pre-pay the adjustment.

That is not a shared-sacrifice package. That is burden shifting.

## Where I agree

I agree with the expert and with the state on one central point: **temporary measures alone will not solve a structural overcapacity problem** if the savings requirement is truly large.

So yes:
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary exits,
- and temporary employee contributions

can all be part of a serious package.

I also agree that we need **quantified site-by-site analysis**. Management should have put that on the table earlier, and I support the call for a proper comparison of:
- shift or line reduction,
- footprint shrinkage,
- internal redeployment,
- voluntary exits,
- labor-cost measures,
- and closure costs.

That is not the problem.

The problem is what management wants to do with that analysis before it is even completed: use it to justify plant closures and the threat of compulsory layoffs.

## Where I disagree — fundamentally

### 1) Closures are not a normal instrument here
I reject the idea that plant closures are simply an ordinary restructuring tool.

This company is not facing a situation where closure should be the starting assumption. It is facing a situation where management wants to treat German sites as the first place to cut because that is politically and financially easiest.

That is not the same thing as necessity.

If alternatives can preserve sites and still deliver serious savings, then closures are not justified. And the burden is on management to prove otherwise — with hard numbers, not just repeated phrases like “structural reset.”

### 2) Compulsory redundancies are unacceptable
I want to say this clearly and without ambiguity: **compulsory redundancies are not acceptable to us**.

Not as a first resort.
Not as a default fallback.
Not as a bargaining threat.

If the company insists on using involuntary layoffs as a management tool, then it is choosing to make employees pay for the restructuring with their livelihoods. That is exactly what we will fight.

There are other ways:
- natural attrition,
- voluntary severance,
- early retirement where legally possible,
- internal mobility,
- reduced working hours,
- and negotiated labor-cost relief.

If those are used seriously, forced layoffs should not be necessary.

### 3) “Conditional closure” can become a backdoor to closure
I also want to be cautious about the state’s idea of a conditional threshold.

A site-by-site matrix is useful only if it is designed to **test preservation first**, not to create a procedural path to closure.

We know how this works in practice:
- define the benchmark around the harshest management scenario,
- declare alternatives insufficient,
- and then say closure is merely the result of the numbers.

We will not accept a process that turns a political choice into a supposedly neutral technical outcome.

If there is to be a threshold, then it must be very strict:
- non-closure measures must be thoroughly exhausted,
- management must show that the savings gap is truly irreducible,
- and the burden of proof must remain high.

## To management

You keep saying the package must be “durable” and “implementable.” Fine. But durability cannot mean permanent insecurity for workers.

If you want our cooperation, then bring a package that meets all of these conditions:

- **No German plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Protection of the collective bargaining framework**
- **Binding site and job guarantees**
- **Management and owner contributions**
- **Temporary and limited employee contributions only**
- **Real investment commitments in Germany**
- **Internal solutions first**

You say the workforce must accept “meaningful and durable” relief. We are prepared to discuss that — but only if it is paired with binding guarantees and fair burden sharing.

I am willing to talk about:
- bonus waivers,
- temporary wage moderation,
- working-time corridors,
- and a jointly governed transformation fund

if and only if the company commits to:
- no closures,
- no forced layoffs,
- and a defined future-production plan for Germany.

Without that, you are asking us to subsidize our own insecurity.

## To the works council

We are aligned on the essentials:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no relocation out of Germany,
- no dismantling of the collective framework,
- and no one-sided burden shifting.

I also agree strongly that employee contributions must be **limited, temporary, and conditional**.

Where I want to sharpen the point is this: temporary measures are acceptable only if they are paired with **binding countercommitments** from management, owners, and the state.

That means:
- site security,
- job security,
- investment commitments,
- and a clear future product allocation for German plants.

If those are not there, then “temporary contribution” becomes a permanent wage sacrifice with no guarantee of anything in return.

## To the regional state government

I appreciate that you are not simply rubber-stamping management’s position. That matters.

But I need to be frank: a “rule-based” veto is only meaningful if the rules are genuinely protective of jobs and sites.

I can support a framework that says:
- alternatives must be fully tested,
- closures are only a last resort,
- and a site-specific justification is required.

What I cannot support is a framework that quietly normalizes closure once management has set an aggressive benchmark.

If the state wants to be a serious stakeholder, then it should back:
- no closures as long as viable alternatives exist,
- no forced redundancies,
- and a negotiated path that preserves industrial capacity in Germany.

## To the controlling family holding

You say stewardship means avoiding value destruction. I agree.

But value destruction is not only a strike. Value destruction is also a restructuring that blows up labor peace, destroys trust, and weakens the company’s production base.

So if ownership wants stewardship, then it needs to support a deal that is not just financially neat on paper but socially and industrially sustainable in reality.

That means:
- no plant closures unless truly unavoidable,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and a fair contribution from management and owners as well.

If the family backs a package that shifts the pain overwhelmingly onto workers, then that is not stewardship. That is merely choosing which form of damage you prefer.

## To institutional investors

You are right that the outcome must be durable and economically sufficient. We are not disputing that.

But you are wrong if you think the only credible way to restore value is through closure and layoff pressure.

A plan that:
- preserves sites,
- protects skills,
- avoids a strike spiral,
- and includes real employee contributions

can be more valuable than a “clean” cut that triggers years of conflict and capacity loss.

You should understand this: a workforce that feels treated fairly is more likely to deliver the productivity and flexibility management says it needs.

If you want durable value, then support a settlement that is:
- economically real,
- socially fair,
- and operationally stable.

## Our concrete position

We remain prepared to discuss a serious restructuring package, but only under these conditions:

1. **No German plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Protection of the collective wage framework**
4. **Binding job and site guarantees**
5. **Temporary, limited employee contributions**
6. **Meaningful contributions from management and owners**
7. **Investment and future-production commitments in Germany**
8. **Internal solutions first: working time, redeployment, qualification, capacity adjustment**

What we can discuss:
- bonus waivers
- temporary wage moderation
- working-time flexibility
- voluntary exit packages
- retraining and internal mobility
- a transformation fund with joint governance

What we cannot accept:
- closure threats as leverage
- permanent insecurity
- one-sided wage concessions
- or a restructuring that makes workers absorb management’s strategic failure

## Bottom line

We are willing to contribute.
We are not willing to be sacrificed.

If management and capital want a settlement, they need to stop treating closures and compulsory redundancies as acceptable “tools” and start treating workers as partners in a fair transition.

If they insist otherwise, then they should be prepared for conflict — because we will not sign away jobs and wage standards just to make the spreadsheet look cleaner.
