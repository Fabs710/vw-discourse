We are now at the point where vague positions are no longer helpful.

I will respond directly to each side.

## To management
I agree with the diagnosis: the company has a real competitiveness problem, and a cosmetic fix would be value-destructive. I also agree that capacity must come down and that labour-cost relief has to be part of the answer.

Where I do **not** agree is in treating plant closures and compulsory redundancies as the default route.

You still have not shown, in a sufficiently quantified way, that all lower-damage options have been exhausted. Repeating “structural necessity” is not enough. If you want ownership support for hard measures, bring the evidence:
- site-by-site utilization,
- savings from shift reductions,
- savings from footprint shrinkage,
- savings from voluntary exits,
- savings from redeployment and working-time changes,
- one-time closure costs,
- and implementation timing.

Until that is on the table, closure-first management looks like convenience, not necessity.

I also reject the idea that a settlement must sacrifice labour peace to restore margins. A prolonged strike spiral would destroy value as surely as an uncompetitive cost base.

## To the works council and union
I understand your red lines, and I agree with part of your stance:
- no one wants reckless mass disruption,
- no one wants a destructive tear-up of social partnership,
- and employees should not be made the sole shock absorber.

But I do **not** support turning “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” into an absolute principle regardless of economics. If a site is structurally uneconomic and alternatives cannot deliver equivalent durable savings, then refusing to discuss any closure at all is not stewardship; it is a veto on reality.

So I am not with you on a blanket guarantee through 2030 if it prevents a genuine structural reset. Temporary measures, redeployment, and voluntary tools are worth serious discussion. But if the savings fall short, then hard decisions remain necessary.

## To the regional state government
I respect the governance role and the legitimacy of defending regional industry. I also agree that the company is not a pure quarterly asset.

Where I disagree is in treating the state veto as a reason to preclude serious adjustment. The company needs to remain competitive internationally. If every difficult change is blocked in the name of regional protection, the result will be a weaker company, not a stronger region.

That said, I do support your demand for a site-by-site analysis before any closure is justified. That is precisely the standard management should meet. If a closure is truly unavoidable, it should be proven, not asserted.

## To institutional investors
You are right that the outcome must be economically sufficient, durable, and implementable. I agree with that standard.

Where I part company with you is the assumption that the only credible route is to keep closures and layoffs fully open from the outset. If a non-closure package can deliver the same structural effect, it is preferable because it reduces instability and protects long-term value.

But if it cannot, then yes, closures must remain a live option. The family holding will not support a settlement that merely postpones the problem or protects every current structure at the expense of margins.

## Our position
The controlling owner’s position is not “no change.” It is:
- **real competitiveness restoration**
- **durable margin improvement**
- **avoidance of unnecessary value destruction**
- **no prolonged labor war**
- **no governance bypass**
- **no permanent impairment of the enterprise**

That means we are prepared to support:
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary exits,
- targeted capacity reduction,
- and a negotiated employee contribution,

provided these are real, durable, and sufficient.

What we will **not** support is:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a blanket preservation of every site,
- or a restructuring that ignores the company’s economic reality.

## What must happen next
Before anyone asks for final alignment, management should deliver a clear matrix:
1. current and projected utilization by site,
2. savings from non-closure measures,
3. savings from partial footprint reduction,
4. savings from closure,
5. one-time costs,
6. timing and risk.

Only then can we distinguish between:
- a serious alternative to closures,
- and a delaying tactic dressed up as compromise.

If the analysis shows a credible way to restore competitiveness without closures, I am open to that.
If it does not, then I will not pretend that closures can be ruled out in advance.

That is where I stand.
