We are aligned on one central point: **the company needs a durable competitiveness reset, not a cosmetic fix.** On that, management is right. The market reality does not negotiate with us.

But I am not prepared to support a restructuring that damages the enterprise’s long-term value by triggering a prolonged internal conflict or undermining the company’s German industrial base without exhausting better alternatives first.

My position is therefore:

### Where I agree
- **Cost reduction is necessary.**
- **Capacity must be adjusted.**
- **Labour-cost contributions can be part of a solution.**
- **A purely temporary, symbolic compromise is not enough.**
- **The company must regain margins to fund future investment.**

### Where I disagree
- I do **not** accept that plant closures are the starting point.
- I do **not** accept compulsory redundancies as the default instrument.
- I do **not** accept a solution that permanently weakens the company’s social legitimacy or creates a destructive strike spiral.
- I do **not** accept that governance constraints from co-determination and the state are obstacles to be brushed aside.

### To management
If you want the owners to back a hard restructuring, then bring forward a package that is:
- **credible in magnitude**
- **durable in effect**
- **legally workable**
- and **less value-destructive than closures**

That means you should first exhaust alternatives such as:
- working-time flexibility,
- targeted capacity reductions,
- voluntary exit programmes,
- redeployment,
- qualification and internal mobility,
- and a negotiated employee contribution.

If, after that, you can demonstrate site-by-site that a closure is unavoidable, then that discussion can happen. But I am not going to endorse closures as a first resort.

### To the works council and union
Your insistence on **no compulsory redundancies** and protection of skills is understandable and, in principle, consistent with long-term stewardship. But I would caution against turning every plant into a permanent red line regardless of economics. If the company’s competitiveness is not restored, the value at risk will ultimately be larger and more permanent than any one restructuring measure.

So yes:
- no unnecessary closures,
- no blunt layoffs,
- but also no refusal to accept structural change where it is genuinely required.

### To the regional state government
Your role as a blocking minority and governance actor matters, and I recognise the legitimacy of protecting regional employment and industrial presence. But the state should not interpret that role as a veto on any serious adjustment. The company cannot be held hostage to preserving every current footprint unchanged. The real test is whether the overall package strengthens the enterprise over the long term.

### Bottom line
I am **supportive of a restructuring that restores competitiveness**, but I am **not yet supportive of plant closures or forced redundancies** as the path chosen now.

My standard is simple:
1. show me a **credible alternative package** with equivalent savings and real durability, and I will support it;
2. if that fails, show me **site-specific necessity** backed by evidence, not just managerial preference;
3. but do not expect family ownership to back any plan that creates avoidable value destruction, labour war, or irreversible damage to the company’s core German base.

We are prepared to back hard decisions. We are not prepared to back reckless ones.
