I will be direct.

The expert analysis confirms what management has been saying from the start: **non-closure measures can help, but they are unlikely to be sufficient if we are serious about restoring competitiveness at scale**. That is the central point, and it cannot be wished away by repeating “no closures” as a principle.

### Where I agree
I agree with the capital markets, the family owner, and even parts of the labour side on one important issue: **a cosmetic settlement is useless**. If we do not materially reduce the cost base and capacity, we will not restore return on sales, and we will not free the investment room needed for 2030.

I also agree that **prolonged conflict destroys value**. A long strike spiral would hurt output, customers, reputation, and cash flow. That is precisely why we need a settlement that is structurally strong enough to end the crisis, not merely postpone it.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the idea that:
- plant closures are automatically the wrong instrument,
- compulsory redundancies are off the table in all circumstances,
- or that working-time changes, redeployment, overhead cuts, and limited wage concessions can by themselves solve a large structural overcapacity problem.

The expert was clear: those tools can deliver **several hundred million euros per year** if pursued hard. That is meaningful, but it is **not the same thing as a deep structural reset** if the gap is large. If the cost base remains above target, then we have not solved the problem — we have only softened it.

So I reject the attempt to turn “no closures” into a veto on serious restructuring. That would effectively force shareholders and the business to absorb a competitiveness deficit indefinitely. That is not acceptable.

### To the union and works council
I hear the argument about trust, social partnership, and the regional footprint. But trust cannot be a substitute for economics.

If you want us to avoid closures, then you need to help deliver **equivalent, durable savings**. Not symbolic savings. Not time-limited concessions. Durable savings that close the competitiveness gap.

That means:
- real labor-cost reductions,
- real flexibility,
- real headcount adjustment,
- and real productivity gains.

If the package preserves every site, every guarantee, and every legacy arrangement, then it is not a restructuring. It is a delay.

### To the regional state government
I understand the political constraints, but I will not pretend they change the financial reality. A package that prioritizes the regional footprint over the company’s cost structure is not a viable industrial strategy. If the business stays uncompetitive, the footprint erodes anyway — only slower, with weaker investment and more uncertainty.

So I do not accept the proposition that plant closures are inherently a failure of management imagination. They may be necessary if the alternative savings are not enough.

### To the capital markets and family owners
You are right to insist on **measurable, durable, implementation-ready savings**. But I would also caution that capital markets must not confuse “no closure” with “no credibility.” If management can demonstrate a package of non-closure measures that truly removes enough fixed cost and headcount, then fine. But the burden of proof is on those claiming that route can deliver equivalent savings.

### My position
Management’s position remains this:

- **Capacity must come down.**
- **Labour costs must come down.**
- **The company must regain structural competitiveness.**
- **Savings must be durable and enforceable.**
- **Future investment depends on a cost base that actually works.**

I am open on the method, but not on the objective. If alternatives to closures can deliver truly equivalent savings, I will consider them. But if they cannot, then closures remain on the table. That is not an escalation for its own sake; it is a consequence of the numbers.

### What I need next
I want the other parties to stop speaking in absolutes and engage with the actual required savings:
- How much recurring cost reduction are you willing to commit to?
- Over what timeframe?
- Which measures are structural, not reversible?
- What headcount reduction is acceptable?
- And if you reject closures, what exactly replaces the fixed-cost removal they provide?

Until those questions are answered, “no closures” is just a slogan. And slogans do not restore competitiveness.
