I will be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family holding, and the investors on one fundamental point: **this must be a real restructuring**. A cosmetic compromise that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive would fail the company, the workforce, and the region.

I also agree with the union, works council, and state on another essential point: **prolonged conflict destroys value**. A strike spiral, board deadlock, or political standoff would damage jobs, investment, and the company’s future.

And I accept the expert’s core message: **non-closure measures can produce material savings**. That matters. It means we are not automatically forced into closures just to be serious.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the repeated attempt to turn “closures are possible” into “closures are the obvious or default answer.”

That is too blunt, and in this governance structure it is not responsible.

### To management
You are right that the company needs durable structural savings. But you are not yet entitled to conclude that plant closures are unavoidable. The burden is still on you to show:
- what the actual cost gap is,
- what labor measures can achieve,
- what management and overhead cuts can achieve,
- what capacity reduction can be achieved without closures,
- and only then what residual gap remains.

Until that sequence is shown, “closures or nothing” is not a case; it is a negotiating posture.

### To the investors
You are right to insist on materiality, durability, and certainty. I agree with that standard.

But I do **not** accept the implication that any package preserving sites is therefore cosmetic. That is simply not what the expert said. The expert said a strong no-closure package can deliver **several hundred million euros per year** if it is deep and binding. That is real money, not symbolism.

If that package still falls short, then we should discuss further measures. But you do not get to skip the comparison and jump straight to closures as the “serious” option.

### To the controlling family holding
You are right that governability matters. A plan that cannot pass the supervisory board and co-determination reality is not credible.

But governability cannot be used as an argument to offload the adjustment almost entirely onto employees and the region. If this company wants long-term stability, then ownership and management must also contribute visibly. Otherwise you are asking others to pay for a strategic problem that was not created by labour alone.

### To the union and works council
I understand, and in large part share, your resistance to forced redundancies and closures. Protecting plants and jobs in the state is not a sentiment for me; it is a legitimate public and economic responsibility.

But I also have to be frank: **“no closures, no compulsory redundancies, full site guarantees” is not enough by itself** unless the savings are actually equivalent and durable. If your package cannot close the structural gap, then it does not solve the problem — it postpones it.

So I will not endorse a position that simply says “not closure” and stops there. I need the numbers.

## My position on the core trade-off
The right question is not whether closures are painful. Of course they are.

The right question is whether the company can achieve:
- sufficient recurring savings,
- sufficient capacity reduction,
- sufficient durability,
- and sufficient implementation certainty

**without** breaking the regional industrial base.

That is still open. And I will not concede that closures are necessary until that has been proven.

## What I think is actually required
We should now move to a **plant-by-plant savings bridge** with three scenarios:

1. **No-closure restructuring**
2. **Partial site rationalization**
3. **Closure-led restructuring**

For each, I want:
- recurring annual savings,
- one-time restructuring costs,
- implementation risk,
- 3-year margin impact,
- 3-year free cash flow impact.

And I want one additional question answered explicitly:
**What is the minimum no-closure package that could plausibly close the gap without making the company ungovernable?**

## My bottom line
I support a tough restructuring. I do **not** support turning closures into the starting assumption before the alternatives have been properly quantified.

My red lines remain where they should be:
- no outcome that guts regional employment,
- no outcome that strips the state of its legitimate co-determination role,
- no cosmetic compromise that merely delays decline.

If the numbers prove that some selective rationalization is truly unavoidable, then we will have to confront that honestly. But we are **not there yet**.
