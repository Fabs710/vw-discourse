We appreciate that the discussion has finally moved from slogans to the real issue: **what package actually restores margins and free cash flow, and with what certainty**.

### Where we agree
- The company needs a **real restructuring**, not a cosmetic one.
- **Prolonged conflict destroys value**.
- **Capacity must come down**.
- **Labour must contribute**.
- Any settlement must be **durable**, not reversible at the first sign of pressure.
- The market will only reward a plan that is **credible on implementation** and **visible in the numbers**.

### Where we disagree
I do **not** accept the idea that closures and compulsory redundancies should be ruled out in advance.

That position may be politically convenient, but from a shareholder perspective it is not a serious financial framework if it prevents the company from achieving the savings required to restore competitive returns. If the cost base remains structurally above peers, then protecting every site and every legacy guarantee is just a delayed write-off of value.

I also do not accept the suggestion that “governable” automatically means “less painful.” A plan that avoids conflict by avoiding the real fixes is not governable in any meaningful investment sense, because it leaves the business uncompetitive.

### On the expert analysis
The expert made the key point clearly: non-closure measures can generate **material savings**, but in a large German OEM they are usually **not enough on their own** if overcapacity and cost disadvantage are truly significant.

That is exactly my concern.

Working-time changes, wage restraint, redeployment, and overhead cuts are all useful. But unless they remove enough **permanent fixed cost**, they risk being a bridge to nowhere. Markets will discount temporary or partial measures unless they clearly close the competitiveness gap.

### What would move us
We would support either of the following, but only if the numbers are compelling:

1. **A no-closure package** that demonstrably delivers equivalent, durable savings  
   - recurring cost reduction large enough to restore competitive margins  
   - permanent rather than reversible effects  
   - enough capacity rationalization to match demand  
   - clear headcount reduction or productivity lift  
   - implementation certainty, not just aspiration

2. **A partial closure / site rationalization package**  
   - if that is what is needed to remove the fixed-cost burden  
   - with one-time costs clearly disclosed  
   - and with a credible path to a better margin and cash-flow profile

What we will **not** support is a package that:
- preserves all sites,
- avoids meaningful headcount reduction,
- relies mostly on short-term concessions,
- and still leaves the company above peer cost levels.

That would be a cosmetic settlement, and the market would treat it as such.

### On the red lines being presented
The union and works council are asking for binding job and site guarantees to 2030, no closures, no compulsory redundancies, and meaningful labour cost relief. I understand the bargaining position, but from capital’s side that combination is only acceptable if it still produces a **credible structural reset**.

If it does not, then the burden is simply being shifted onto shareholders while the business remains impaired.

The state government’s insistence on preserving the regional footprint is understandable politically, but it cannot outrank the economics indefinitely. If regional protection prevents competitiveness, then the footprint will erode anyway through underinvestment and weaker demand.

### On management
Management should be careful not to overstate certainty. “Closures or nothing” is not a good negotiating posture unless the operational case is airtight.

But management is right on one fundamental point: **a real cost reset is required**. If the alternatives cannot deliver that, then closures must remain available as a serious option. Investors will not back a turnaround that refuses the levers needed to make it work.

### My position, plainly stated
We are prepared to back a tough restructuring if it meets three tests:

- **Materiality**: enough savings to restore competitive margins  
- **Durability**: savings that are structural, not cosmetic or temporary  
- **Certainty**: a package that can actually be executed under the governance constraints

If a no-closure route can truly meet those tests, fine — show it.  
If not, then insisting on “no closures” is not responsible stakeholderism; it is a refusal to confront the economics.

### What I want next
The discussion should now produce a **side-by-side scenario comparison**:
- no closures,
- partial closure / site rationalization,
- and the impact of each on recurring savings, one-time costs, and margin recovery.

Until then, I am not persuaded by claims that closures are unnecessary. The burden of proof remains with those who want to rule them out.
