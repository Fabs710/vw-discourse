Below is a finance-style restructuring bridge based on the facts given and the expert’s ranges. Because no plant-by-plant volumes, headcount, or cost data were supplied, this is necessarily **illustrative**, not a literal site valuation.

## Executive conclusion

- A **no-closure package can be financially meaningful** and may be the only route that is realistically governable under the company’s co-determination and political constraints.
- But in most cases it will **not fully equal** the fixed-cost reset from **partial site rationalization**, and it is even less likely to match a **closure-led** package.
- The central issue is not whether no-closure measures save money — they do — but whether they save **enough, durably, after one-time costs and implementation friction**.

My practical conclusion is:

- **No-closure** is plausible if the structural gap is roughly **€500m–€700m recurring annual savings or less**.
- If the gap is meaningfully larger, then **selective rationalization** becomes much more likely to be necessary.
- A closure-led solution may be strongest financially, but it is also the **most execution-risky** in this governance setting.

## Scenario 1: No-closure package

### What it means
- No plant closures
- No compulsory redundancies
- Working-time concessions
- Wage restraint / allowance cuts
- Attrition-based headcount reduction
- Redeployment
- Overhead cuts
- Shift reductions and complexity reduction inside existing sites

### Illustrative savings bridge

| Site / lever | Recurring annual savings |
|---|---:|
| Assembly plant A – shift reduction, productivity | €50m–€100m |
| Assembly plant B – shift reduction, productivity | €40m–€90m |
| Component/powertrain plant – working time, productivity | €60m–€120m |
| Central overhead / engineering / admin | €100m–€200m |
| Wage restraint / legacy allowance removal | €100m–€200m |
| Attrition / redeployment / hiring freeze | €50m–€100m |
| **Total recurring annual savings** | **€400m–€810m** |

### One-time restructuring costs
| Item | Estimated cost |
|---|---:|
| Retirement / attrition incentives | €80m–€200m |
| Redeployment / training / qualification | €20m–€60m |
| Reorganization / IT / process change | €40m–€100m |
| Social plan / transitional payments | €80m–€200m |
| **Total one-time costs** | **€220m–€560m** |

### Implementation risk
**Medium to high**
- The savings are real, but depend heavily on labor consent and on disciplined execution.
- Some savings may be delayed if they require repeated bargaining or voluntary uptake.
- There is also a risk that “temporary” concessions are partly reversed later.

### 3-year margin impact
If executed well, this could improve operating margin by roughly **1 to 2 percentage points** over three years, depending on revenue base and volume stability.

### 3-year free cash flow impact
- Year 1: often weak because one-time costs hit early
- Year 2: transition year
- Year 3: materially better if the package is embedded

A reasonable cumulative 3-year FCF improvement is roughly:
- **€500m–€1.3bn net**, after restructuring costs

- No broad shutdown of the German base
- Continued labor contribution and overhead reduction
- More fixed-cost removal than in the no-closure case

### What it means
- One or more full plant closures
- Larger headcount reduction
- Deeper capacity reset
- Simplified footprint and lower fixed cost

### One-time restructuring costs
| Item | Estimated cost |
|---|---:|

**Very high**

### 3-year free cash flow impact
- Year 1 and Year 2 often heavily burdened by restructuring costs

Cumulative 3-year FCF can be positive, but the path is volatile and politically fragile.

# 2) What is the minimum no-closure package that could plausibly close the gap?

To be credible, a no-closure package would typically need all of the following:

## A. Labor contribution
- pay restraint
- reduction of bonuses / special payments
- working-time flexibility without full compensation
- reduction or removal of legacy guarantees

**Likely recurring benefit:** **€100m–€250m**

## B. Workforce reduction without compulsory layoffs
- attrition
- retirement programs
- no replacement hiring in non-critical areas
- internal redeployment

**Likely recurring benefit:** **€50m–€150m**

## C. Overhead reset
- management layers
- administration
- engineering/support simplification
- procurement, IT, finance, HR consolidation

**Likely recurring benefit:** **€100m–€200m**

## D. Production productivity / shift reduction
- fewer shifts
- higher utilization
- lower overtime
- reduced temporary labor
- tighter complexity management

**Likely recurring benefit:** **€75m–€200m**

## E. Durability
This is crucial. Without a multi-year binding agreement, the package is not financeable as a structural fix.

### Practical minimum
A no-closure package would need to generate roughly:

- **€500m–€700m recurring annual savings**

to be a serious substitute for site rationalization at this scale.

If the structural gap is materially above that, no-closure alone becomes progressively harder to defend financially.

---

# 3) What this means in plain language

## No-closure can work if:
- the real gap is moderate,
- labor concessions are deep,
- overhead is cut hard,
- capacity is reduced inside existing sites,
- and the agreement is durable.

## No-closure becomes doubtful if:
- the cost gap is very large,
- fixed-cost base is too high,
- capacity has to come out permanently,
- and concessions are too reversible or too slow.

## Partial rationalization is usually the best compromise if:
- no-closure savings are real but insufficient,
- governance blocks a full closure-led reset,
- and the company needs a durable cost reset without a full industrial rupture.

---

# 4) Direct answer to the key question

## “What is the minimum no-closure package that could plausibly close the gap without making the company ungovernable?”

A plausible minimum would require:

- **€500m–€700m recurring annual savings**
- a **multi-year binding agreement**
- visible contributions from **labor, management, and overhead**
- **capacity reduction** through shifts and utilization cuts
- headcount reduction via **attrition/redeployment/retirement**
- enough durability to survive beyond one bargaining cycle

If the required gap is higher than that, then a no-closure route may still be politically possible, but it becomes much less likely to be financially sufficient.

---

# 5) Bottom line

My restrained finance view is:

- **No-closure is not cosmetic by default.**
- **It can be materially meaningful.**
- But **it is not automatically equivalent** to site rationalization.
- If the company needs a deep reset, **partial rationalization is often the most realistic economic compromise** under German governance constraints.
