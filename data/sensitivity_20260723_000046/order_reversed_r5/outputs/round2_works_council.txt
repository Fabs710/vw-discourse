I will be equally clear: **the workforce will not accept plant closures or compulsory redundancies as the price of this restructuring.**

The discussion so far confirms something important: everyone agrees the company needs a **real** turnaround, not a cosmetic one. On that point, I agree. The cost base must be addressed. Capacity must reflect demand. The business needs room to invest in future products.

But there is a hard line here, and I will not move it by repeating the word “credible.”

## Where I agree
I agree with the expert that:
- working-time changes can create savings,
- wage concessions and bonus restraint can contribute,
- redeployment and overhead reduction can help,
- and selective capacity measures may be needed.

I also agree that the company faces serious competitive pressure and that delay would be harmful.

## Where I strongly disagree
What I reject is the leap from “the company needs savings” to “therefore German plants must be closed and jobs cut.”

That is not an economic necessity. It is a choice.

And I reject the idea that a package is only credible if it includes plant closures or forced redundancies. Credibility cannot mean breaking trust, destroying sites, and tearing up the job-security framework that has held this company together.

## To management
You keep saying alternatives may not be enough. Then show the numbers plant by plant, site by site.

Until you prove that every other option is exhausted, “closures are necessary” is just an assertion. And let me be frank: management has not earned the right to demand that workers pay first while the rest of the structure remains protected.

If you want real savings, then present a package that includes:
- management overhead cuts,
- executive and top-management restraint,
- internal reorganization,
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment and qualification,
- and a fair employee contribution.

But **not** closures. **Not** forced layoffs.

## To the investors
You keep talking about margins and free cash flow as if the only thing that matters is what looks clean on paper. But a plan that triggers strikes, blockade, political resistance, and a collapse in trust is not more credible just because it is harsher.

A package that cannot pass the supervisory board in reality is not a viable package. So no, “closure-first” is not automatically the serious option. It may simply be the most destructive one.

## To the controlling family and the state
If you want a durable turnaround, then stop asking the workforce alone to absorb the pain.

We are prepared to talk about sacrifice, but only under these conditions:
- **no plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **binding site and job guarantees to 2030**
- **a real co-determination role in shaping the plan**
- **investment commitments for German plants**
- **contributions from owners and management, not just labour**

Without those guarantees, there is no basis for an agreement.

## On the expert’s assessment
The expert confirmed something the others seem eager to ignore: non-closure measures can produce **material recurring savings**. Not symbolic savings. Real savings. That means closures are not proven to be unavoidable.

The burden of proof is on those who want to break red lines. And so far, that burden has not been met.

## My position
I am prepared to negotiate a tough restructuring.

I am **not** prepared to negotiate away:
- German plant security,
- the prohibition on compulsory redundancies,
- or the co-determination framework that protects the workforce from unilateral decisions.

If management insists on closures or forced layoffs, it should expect resistance, not cooperation. The workforce is organized, the peace obligation is over, and we will defend every site.

So the next step is not more pressure. It is a serious package that proves competitiveness can be restored **without** sacrificing plants and jobs. Until then, my answer remains no.
