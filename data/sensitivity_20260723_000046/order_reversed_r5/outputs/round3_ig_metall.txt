## Where I agree
I agree with all sides on one core point: **this must be a real restructuring, not a cosmetic compromise**.  
The company has a genuine competitiveness problem, and it does need:
- lower costs,
- better capacity alignment,
- and room for future investment.

I also agree with the expert’s assessment that **working-time changes, wage restraint, redeployment, and overhead cuts can produce material savings**. That is not trivial. It is a real contribution, and I do not deny it.

So let me be clear: the union is **not** pretending there is no problem.

## Where I disagree
What I reject is the jump from “non-closure measures may not be enough alone” to “therefore closures and compulsory redundancies become acceptable.”

That leap is not an economic law. It is a **choice about burden-sharing**.

And on that point I remain firm:
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no plant closures**
- **no restructuring that forces employees to bear the main pain while management, owners, and capital avoid equivalent sacrifice**

That is not a negotiating tactic. It is the line the workforce will defend.

## On management
You keep saying the numbers require closures unless we produce “equivalent savings.” Fine — then engage seriously with the actual package options instead of treating closures as the default proof of seriousness.

The expert did **not** say a no-closure approach is pointless. The expert said it can yield **several hundred million euros per year** if pursued hard. That is a real amount. It may not be enough for every scenario, but it is enough to justify a serious negotiation before anyone starts talking about shutting plants.

If management wants an agreement, it must show a willingness to combine:
- working-time reductions,
- wage restraint and bonus sacrifice,
- redeployment and qualification,
- productivity improvements,
- overhead cuts,
- and selective capacity reduction

— without destroying sites or breaking the collective wage framework.

If management refuses that path, then it is not proving necessity. It is choosing confrontation.

## On the investors
I understand your focus on margins and free cash flow. But I do not accept the idea that “credible” simply means “painful enough for workers.”

A credible restructuring must also be **implementable**.  
And in this governance structure, a closure-first or forced-layoff-first approach is not automatically more credible — it may be less so, because it risks:
- industrial conflict,
- co-determination blockage,
- political resistance,
- and a long-term collapse of trust.

So no, I do not accept the claim that the harshest option is therefore the most responsible one.

## On the family owner
You are right about one thing: a plan that cannot pass the real supervisory-board and co-determination structure is not credible.

But that cuts both ways. It also means you cannot simply demand the workforce absorb the costs while ownership stays protected. If you want a durable settlement, then contributions have to come from the top as well:
- executive restraint,
- management overhead cuts,
- internal discipline,
- and binding investment commitments for the sites that remain.

The burden cannot be pushed downward only.

## On the state government
I agree with your concern for regional employment and industrial capability. That is legitimate.

But I also need to be frank: preserving the footprint only matters if the company remains viable. So the right answer is not “nothing changes,” but **change without destruction**.

That means:
- real employee contribution,
- real management contribution,
- real productivity gains,
- and a restructuring that preserves key sites and industrial capability.

What I cannot support is a plan that uses “competitiveness” as a blanket justification for closures before alternatives have been fully exhausted.

## On the expert’s numbers
The expert actually supports the union’s position more than the others want to admit.

Yes, closures are the strongest lever.  
But the expert also says:
- non-closure measures can deliver **meaningful recurring savings**,
- selective capacity reductions can go a long way,
- and the outcome depends on actual plant data, utilization, and product allocation.

That means closures are **not proven unavoidable**. They are merely the strongest lever on paper.

That is not enough to justify breaking our red lines.

## What we are prepared to discuss
We remain prepared to negotiate a tough settlement, but only under these conditions:
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no plant closures**
- **binding site and job guarantees**
- **a meaningful employee contribution**
- **working-time and capacity adjustments**
- **redeployment and qualification**
- **management and owner contributions**
- **investment and product commitments for German sites**

That is a serious restructuring. It is not denial. It is fair burden-sharing.

## What we will not accept
We will not accept:
- a closure-led strategy,
- forced layoffs dressed up as necessity,
- or a deal where workers sacrifice jobs and wages while the rest of the governance structure remains largely untouched.

If that is the route management insists on, then it should expect resistance, not cooperation. The peace obligation is over, and the workforce is organized.

## Bottom line
We are ready for a hard settlement.  
We are **not** ready to sign up to a restructuring that solves competitiveness by **breaking the workforce**.

If the others want a real deal, then bring forward a package that proves equivalent savings **without** closures and forced layoffs. If you cannot do that, then your argument is not that this is the only viable route — it is that this is the most socially destructive one.
