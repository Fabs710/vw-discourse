## Core impasse
The dispute is not over whether restructuring is needed. The impasse is over **the acceptable instrument of restructuring**:

- **Management / investors / family / state** want proof of **durable, material savings** and are unwilling to rule out closures.
- **Union / works council** want **no closures and no compulsory redundancies**, and will only accept a package that proves equivalent savings another way.
- The **state and family** are trying to preserve governability, while still demanding credible cost reduction.
- Everyone now agrees on the need for numbers, but not on what level of savings justifies site action.

So the deadlock is: **how to achieve sufficient structural savings with implementation certainty, without triggering a governance and industrial-relations breakdown**.

---

## Facilitator proposals: possible compromise paths

### Proposal 1: Phased restructuring with an automatic review gate
A first-stage package would focus on:
- labor concessions,
- management overhead cuts,
- productivity improvements,
- voluntary attrition / retirement,
- shift and utilization reductions.

A second-stage review would be triggered only if the agreed savings target is not met by a fixed date. At that point, **selective site rationalization becomes a conditional fallback**, not an upfront demand.

**Why this may bridge the gap:**  
It gives the union and works council a genuine no-closure first attempt, while giving investors, management, and the family a credible backstop if the gap remains open.

---

### Proposal 2: No-closure package with binding minimum savings target
Parties could negotiate a no-closure deal only if it includes:
- a binding annual savings floor,
- measurable headcount and overhead reductions,
- explicit capacity reduction,
- multi-year durability,
- and independent verification.

If the package does not reach the floor, the agreement would not be treated as complete.

**Why this may bridge the gap:**  
It preserves the no-closure principle for labour, while addressing the financial concern that “material” is not necessarily “sufficient.”

---

### Proposal 3: Partial rationalization limited to one clearly underutilized area
Instead of debating closures in the abstract, the parties could isolate **one site, line, or function** where utilization and fixed costs are most clearly misaligned. The rest of the footprint would be protected under a broader restructuring settlement.

**Why this may bridge the gap:**  
It converts an all-or-nothing dispute into a contained transaction, reducing the risk of a company-wide breakdown while still delivering visible structural savings.

## One pointed question to each stakeholder

### To management
**What minimum recurring savings target do you require before you would accept a no-closure package as structurally sufficient?**

### To the union
**Which specific savings levers, and at what quantified scale, are you prepared to accept so that “no closures” is not just a principle but a financially credible plan?**

### To the works council
**Which plant-level flexibilities can you realistically support now without crossing your red lines, and where do you see the hard limit?**

### To the controlling family holding
**Are you prepared to endorse a phased plan that preserves sites initially but authorizes selective rationalization automatically if the savings target is missed?**

### To the regional state government
**What level of restructuring is politically acceptable to you if it is the only way to secure the company’s long-term viability in the region?**

### To the institutional investors
**Would you support a no-closure settlement if an independent review confirmed that it meets a clearly defined minimum savings threshold and durability test?**

### To the expert
**What exact savings floor would make a no-closure package financeable enough to defer site rationalization, and what verification mechanism would you recommend?**
