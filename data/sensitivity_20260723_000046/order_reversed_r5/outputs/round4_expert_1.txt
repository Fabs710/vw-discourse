Below is a finance-style restructuring assessment, using the information available and making the assumptions explicit.

## Executive view

A **credible no-closure package can generate material savings** at a large German carmaker, but in most cases it will **not fully match** the cost-reset effect of a package that includes at least **selective site rationalization**.  

In German automotive restructurings, the realistic range is usually:

- **No-closure package:** roughly **€300m to €700m recurring annual savings** if it is deep, binding, and includes wage restraint, working-time changes, overhead cuts, productivity measures, and capacity reduction without plant closure.
- **Partial rationalization:** roughly **€600m to €1.2bn recurring annual savings**, depending on whether one plant, a line, or major functions are removed.
- **Closure-led restructuring:** potentially **€1bn+ recurring annual savings**, but with the highest one-time costs and the highest execution risk.

The key point is that **non-closure measures can be substantial**, but they are usually **more limited on fixed-cost removal and capacity reset** than site closures.

# 1) What can realistically be saved without closures?

## Main no-closure levers
A no-closure package in Germany typically draws on:

1. **Working-time reductions / flexible hours**
2. **Wage restraint or temporary pay concessions**
3. **Removal of legacy guarantees or special allowances**
4. **Redundancy through attrition / hiring freeze**
5. **Redeployment and internal mobility**
6. **Overhead reductions**
7. **Productivity improvement / line balancing**
8. **Selective capacity reduction within sites**  
   - fewer shifts  
   - fewer models per site  
   - lower technical utilization  
   - outsourcing of non-core activities

## Realistic savings scale
For a large carmaker with several German plants, the savings are usually:

- **Working-time and wage measures:**  
  around **€100m to €300m per year** recurring, depending on workforce size and the depth of concessions.
  
- **Overhead and management cuts:**  
  around **€100m to €250m per year** recurring if headquarters, functions, and indirect labor are reduced materially.
  
- **Productivity / line efficiency:**  
  around **€50m to €150m per year** recurring, but often ramps up slowly.
  
- **Capacity reduction without closure:**  
  around **€50m to €200m per year** recurring, depending on whether shifts and ancillary costs are taken out.

### Total no-closure recurring savings
A deep package could therefore reach roughly:

- **Low case:** €250m–€400m per year
- **Base case:** €400m–€700m per year
- **High case:** €700m–€900m per year

In practice, the **base case** is the most realistic if there are strong co-determination constraints and a desire to avoid forced redundancies.

# 2) What is the minimum no-closure package that could plausibly close the gap?

If management believes the structural gap is large, then a **credible minimum no-closure package** would usually need all of the following:

## A. Labor cost contribution
- temporary or multi-year **pay restraint**
- removal/reduction of bonuses or special payments
- extension of working time without full compensation
- reduced paid time guarantees or legacy protections

**Typical contribution:** **€100m–€250m per year**

## B. Workforce reduction without compulsory layoffs
- attrition
- retirement programs
- internal transfer / redeployment
- no replacement hiring in non-critical areas

**Typical contribution:** **€50m–€150m per year**, but slower to realize

## C. Overhead reset
- management-layer reduction
- engineering/admin consolidation
- procurement, IT, finance, HR simplification

**Typical contribution:** **€100m–€200m per year**

## D. Production productivity and shift reduction
- fewer shifts
- better line utilization
- higher labor productivity
- reduced overtime and temporary labor

**Typical contribution:** **€75m–€200m per year**

## E. Binding durability
Without binding duration, the package is not financeable.  
You would need:
- a **multi-year collective agreement**
- site-level guarantees with conditions
- restrictions on reopening the package too soon

### Practical minimum
A no-closure package would need to generate at least **€500m–€700m recurring annual savings** to be regarded as a serious substitute for site rationalization at a company of this scale and cost position.

If the actual structural gap is bigger than that, then no-closure alone is unlikely to be enough.

# 4) Gross vs net savings

This distinction matters.

## Gross savings
These are the cost reductions announced:
- lower wages
- fewer shifts
- overhead cuts
- reduced plant costs

## Net savings
These subtract:
- one-time restructuring costs
- transitional inefficiencies
- duplicated overhead during ramp-down
- lost volume from disruption
- labor conflict costs
- sometimes lower pricing power if capacity is removed badly

### Typical haircut from gross to net
- **No-closure:** gross savings may be reduced by **15% to 30%** in net terms over 3 years
- **Partial rationalization:** gross-to-net haircut often **20% to 35%**
- **Closure-led:** gross-to-net haircut can be **30%+** in the first 3 years because of disruption and severance costs

# 5) What is realistic under German legal and collective-bargaining constraints?

## Constraints that matter
- Co-determination gives the workforce real blocking power on major restructuring.
- Plant closures are politically and legally difficult.
- Collective agreements limit unilateral wage or working-time changes.
- Compulsory redundancies are possible in principle, but usually costly, slow, and conflict-heavy.
- Site guarantees and social plans can materially delay and raise costs.

## Practical implication
A company like this is much more likely to achieve:
- **large no-closure concessions**, or
- **partial rationalization by negotiated settlement**

than a fast, clean closure-led reset.

So from an implementation standpoint:
- **No-closure packages are more governable**
- **Closure-led packages are more powerful on paper but harder to execute**
- **Partial rationalization is often the only route that is both economically meaningful and legally executable**

# 6) Bottom-line assessment

## Can savings come without closures?
**Yes.**
A serious no-closure package can produce **material recurring savings**, likely in the **€400m–€700m** range, and possibly somewhat above that if concessions are deep.

## Can no-closure fully replace plant closures?
**Sometimes, but not reliably.**
If the structural cost gap is large and fixed-cost burden is heavy, no-closure measures often fall short of full equivalence.

## What is the most likely economically credible compromise?
A package with:
- substantial labor contribution,
- overhead reduction,
- capacity reduction,
- and **selective site rationalization if needed**

is usually the most durable balance of:
- savings,
- implementability,
- and governance feasibility.
