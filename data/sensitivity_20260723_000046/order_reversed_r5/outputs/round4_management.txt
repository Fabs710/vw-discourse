I will be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on the following:

- The company needs a **real restructuring**, not a cosmetic settlement.
- **Prolonged conflict destroys value**.
- **Labour must contribute**.
- The package must be **durable**, **implementable**, and capable of restoring competitiveness.
- We cannot make decisions on slogans; we need **numbers** and a **plant-by-plant view**.

That part is not in dispute.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the attempt to frame **“no closures, no compulsory redundancies, full site guarantees”** as a credible endpoint before the savings gap has been proven closed.

That is not a restructuring plan. It is a constraint package.

Yes, the expert has confirmed that non-closure measures can generate **material savings**. I accept that. But “material” is not the same as “sufficient.” And in a company with this level of overcapacity and a cost base above target, **material but incomplete** does not restore competitiveness.

### To the union and works council
You keep saying closures are not proven unavoidable. Fine — but the burden of proof does not disappear just because the answer is uncomfortable.

If you want to exclude closures, then show a package that:
- removes enough **fixed cost**,
- cuts enough **capacity**,
- reduces enough **headcount cost**,
- and does so **durably**, not for one bargaining cycle.

Working-time changes, wage restraint, redeployment, and productivity gains are useful. I am not denying that. But they do **not automatically close a structural cost gap** of the kind we are facing. If your package still leaves us above peer cost levels, then it is not a solution.

### To the state government
I respect the regional and political responsibility. But I reject the idea that preserving every site is the same as protecting the industrial base.

It is not.

A site that remains open at an uncompetitive cost, with too much installed capacity and too much fixed overhead, is not protected forever. It just becomes a slower decline with more underinvestment. That is exactly what I am trying to prevent.

So no, regional concern cannot be allowed to become an effective veto on structural change.

### To the controlling family holding
You are right that governability matters. A plan that cannot pass the supervisory board and co-determination structure is not credible.

But governability is not an argument for avoiding hard choices. It is an argument for designing a package that is tough enough to matter **and** structured enough to pass.

I agree with your insistence on sequencing:
1. quantify the cost gap,
2. quantify what non-closure measures can do,
3. quantify what remains,
4. then determine whether site rationalization is needed.

That is exactly what management is asking for.

### To the investors
You are right to reject cosmetic deals. I agree completely.

But I do not accept the suggestion that every no-closure route is automatically weak and every closure-led route automatically serious. The key question is whether the package is **sufficiently structural**.

If a no-closure package can truly deliver:
- recurring savings,
- durability,
- capacity reduction,
- and a credible margin path,

then it is acceptable. If it cannot, then closures remain on the table because we need a real reset, not wishful thinking.

## My position
Let me state it plainly.

Management will support a tough restructuring only if it delivers:

- **real and measurable savings**
- **durable fixed-cost reduction**
- **meaningful capacity reduction**
- **a genuine labour-cost contribution**
- **implementation certainty**
- **room to invest in future products and technology**

We are open on the method, but not on the objective.

## What I will not accept
I will not accept:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a settlement that merely postpones the problem,
- or a package that preserves all sites while leaving the company structurally above target cost.

I also will not accept the idea that management should absorb the burden of overcapacity indefinitely because closures are politically difficult. That is not a turnaround. That is drift.

**What is the minimum no-closure package that would actually close the structural gap without leaving the company uncompetitive?**

Until that is answered, nobody should pretend “no closures” is a finished solution.

## Bottom line
I support restructuring.  
I support labour contribution.  
I support implementation that can pass governance realities.

I do **not** support a settlement that protects every legacy arrangement and calls it strategy.

If the no-closure route truly closes the gap, prove it.  
If it does not, then selective site rationalization stays on the table.

That is the responsible position.
