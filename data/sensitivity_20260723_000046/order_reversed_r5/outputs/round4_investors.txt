We agree on the diagnosis, but not yet on the remedy.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family holding, and the state on one essential point: **the company needs a real restructuring, not a cosmetic deal**.  
I also agree with the union and works council that **prolonged conflict destroys value**. A drawn-out strike, board deadlock, or political stalemate would be a poor outcome for shareholders as well as everyone else.

And I agree with the expert: **non-closure measures can generate material savings**. That matters. It means a no-closure route is not automatically unserious.

## Where I disagree
What I do **not** accept is the repeated tendency to treat closures as either politically forbidden or economically automatic. Both extremes are wrong.

- To the union and works council: **“no closures, no compulsory redundancies” is not a sufficient answer by itself**. If that package does not remove enough fixed cost and capacity, then it simply preserves an uncompetitive structure and delays value destruction.
- To management: **“closures or nothing” is also too blunt** unless the company has actually shown that a non-closure package cannot bridge the gap.
- To the state and the family owner: governability matters, but **governability is not the same as preserving every legacy arrangement**.

## My shareholder view
We are prepared to back a tough restructuring if it meets three tests:

1. **Materiality** — enough recurring savings to restore competitive margins  
2. **Durability** — savings that are structural, not temporary goodwill  
3. **Certainty** — a package that can actually be executed under this governance setup

That is the standard. Nothing less will be enough for capital markets.

## On the expert analysis
The expert has already made the key point:
- a **no-closure package can be material**, but it is often hard to make fully equivalent to a package with site rationalization;
- **partial closure / rationalization** is usually the best balance of durability and credibility;
- **full closure-led restructuring** may be strongest on paper, but it is the riskiest to implement here.

That is exactly why I am not willing to rule closures out in advance, but I also do not want them used as a negotiating club before the numbers are proven.

## What would move us
We would support either of these, but only with hard evidence:

### 1) A no-closure package
Only if it demonstrates:
- recurring savings large enough to restore margins,
- permanent rather than reversible changes,
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- credible headcount and productivity impact,
- and contractual durability for several years.

### 2) Partial rationalization
If the no-closure route cannot do the job, then a selective site or line rationalization may be the right answer.  
That would be painful, but from a shareholder perspective it is preferable to preserving an uncompetitive cost base.

## What we will not support
We will not support a settlement that:
- preserves all sites,
- avoids meaningful headcount reduction,
- relies mainly on temporary concessions,
- and still leaves the business structurally above peer cost levels.

That would be a cosmetic compromise, and the market would price it accordingly.

## On burden-sharing
I agree with the union that employees should not bear the whole burden. But burden-sharing must be real, not rhetorical.

So yes, we expect:
- labour contribution,
- management overhead cuts,
- executive restraint,
- and productivity improvements.

But those measures must still add up to a structurally better cost base. Otherwise shareholders are simply financing delay.

## My position, plainly stated
- **We do not support a cosmetic no-closure deal.**
- **We do not support closure for its own sake.**
- **We do support whichever package credibly restores competitiveness and margins, with enough certainty to be investable.**

## What I want next
I want the discussion to move from principle to numbers:
- compare a **no-closure** package against a **partial rationalization** package,
- quantify recurring savings, one-time costs, implementation risk, and 3-year cash-flow impact,
- and show whether the no-closure route truly closes the structural gap.

If no one can show that, then the case for keeping every site open is not a financial case — it is just resistance to change.
