We are now at the point where abstract statements are not enough. I will respond directly.

### Where I agree
I agree with the institutional investors on the core financial test: **if the package does not materially improve margins, free cash flow, and competitiveness, it is not acceptable**.

I also agree with management that:
- **capacity must be adjusted to demand**, and
- **the cost base must come down structurally**, not just temporarily.

And I agree with the union and works council on one important point: **prolonged conflict would destroy value**. A strike spiral, reputational damage, and execution paralysis would hurt everyone, including the owners.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the claim that plant closures and compulsory redundancies are the only credible route. That is too narrow, and in this governance structure it is also strategically short-sighted.

The company is not operating in a simple unilateral setting. We have:
- co-determination with an effective workforce veto,
- a special-law/state position,
- a controlling owner with a long-term horizon,
- and a market that will punish delay but also punishes instability.

That means a plan that is “financially clean” on paper but **unimplementable in reality** is not credible. If it triggers a hard blockage, then it fails the business test.

### On the union and works council red lines
I understand the resistance to closures and forced redundancies, but I cannot endorse a position that makes them categorically impossible **before** we have seen whether equivalent structural savings can be achieved by other means.

At the same time, I also do not support a settlement that merely protects every legacy arrangement and shifts the burden onto capital. That would be value-destructive and unfair.

So I am not siding with either extreme:
- not a closure-first confrontation,
- not a preservation-first settlement.

### What I think is required
A viable package has to do four things:
1. **Lower the cost base materially**
2. **Reduce capacity to match a weaker market**
3. **Deliver a durable labour contribution**
4. **Remain governable and stable enough to execute**

If the parties can show that equivalent savings can be achieved through:
- working-time changes,
- a meaningful wage and bonus contribution,
- redeployment,
- internal reorganization,
- and targeted capacity adjustments,

then that should be preferred over closures. But I stress: **equivalent savings** is the key phrase. Not aspirational savings. Not symbolic savings.

### On management
Management cannot simply say “closures or nothing” and expect support. That is not governance; that is escalation. If management wants the owners and stakeholders behind it, it must present a package that can actually be carried through without blowing up the industrial relations system.

### On capital markets
I hear the demand for hard numbers and I agree with the principle. But capital markets also need to understand that a technically elegant plan that cannot pass the supervisory board, the co-determination structure, or the political constraints is not a viable plan. The eventual settlement must be **financially credible and institutionally executable**.

### My bottom line
I will support a tough restructuring **only if** it:
- produces real and measurable savings,
- restores competitiveness,
- avoids prolonged destabilisation,
- and preserves the company’s long-term strategic capacity.

I will **not** support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a conflict that destroys value,
- or a package that weakens governance and leaves the enterprise permanently impaired.

So the next step is not more rhetoric. It is this: bring forward a package that quantifies the savings and shows exactly how they are achieved under the actual governance constraints. If closures are required, show why alternatives fail. If closures are not required, prove that the savings are truly equivalent and durable.

That is the standard I will apply.
