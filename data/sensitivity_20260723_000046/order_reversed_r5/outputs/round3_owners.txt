We have now reached the point where repetition stops being useful. I will respond plainly.

## Where I agree

I agree with the investors, management, and the union on the basic diagnosis:

- the company needs a **real restructuring**,
- the cost base is too high,
- capacity must come down,
- labour must contribute,
- and prolonged conflict would destroy value.

I also agree with the state and the works council on one important governance fact: a plan that cannot be implemented in the real supervisory-board and co-determination structure is not credible, no matter how elegant it looks in a spreadsheet.

That is the correct starting point.

## Where I disagree

I do **not** accept the attempt to turn “closures and compulsory redundancies” into a default conclusion.

That is still too simplistic. It may be the strongest lever on paper, but it is not automatically the right lever in this ownership and governance setup. A package that triggers hard blockage, industrial escalation, and lasting political damage can destroy value faster than it creates savings.

So I reject both extremes:

- not **closure-first regardless of feasibility**,
- and not **preserve-everything regardless of economics**.

## On the investors’ argument

You are right on one point: a cosmetic settlement would be unacceptable. If the package does not materially improve margins, free cash flow, and competitiveness, then it fails.

But you are too quick to assume that only closures can deliver that outcome. The expert already said non-closure measures can deliver **material recurring savings** — potentially several hundred million euros per year if pursued hard.

That is not trivial. It may or may not be enough. But it is enough to justify a serious comparison before anyone declares closures necessary.

So I agree with your demand for hard numbers, but not with your implication that closure is the obvious benchmark and everything else is secondary.

## On management

Management is correct to insist on structural change. Temporary measures alone will not solve a deep competitiveness gap.

Where I disagree is the tendency to present closures as the only proof of seriousness. That is not governance discipline; it is negotiating leverage.

If management wants support from ownership, it needs to show:
- where the savings come from,
- how durable they are,
- what can be done through labour terms,
- what can be done through overhead reduction,
- what can be done through capacity reallocation,
- and only then whether any remaining gap truly requires site rationalization.

That sequence matters.

## On the union and works council

I understand the resistance to closures and forced redundancies. I also understand the political and social stakes.

But I am not prepared to endorse a position that says, in effect, “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, full site protection” before the savings requirement has been met. That would shift the burden onto the company’s owners and future competitiveness.

At the same time, I do **not** accept the opposite claim that employees should absorb the pain while management and owners stay untouched. There must be visible contributions from:
- labour,
- management overhead,
- executive restraint,
- and internal productivity improvements.

If the workforce is asked for a material contribution, that contribution must be matched by seriousness at the top.

## On the state government

Your concern for regional employment and industrial capability is legitimate. But it cannot be allowed to become a veto on all meaningful restructuring.

If the package fails competitively, the region loses anyway — through weaker investment, lower innovation, and gradual erosion. So I support your insistence that the company remain rooted in Germany, but not at any price.

A preserved footprint that is structurally uncompetitive is not a victory. It is deferred decline.

## What I think is actually required

The right next step is not another round of principled declarations. It is a quantified comparison between:

1. **A no-closure package**
   - working-time changes
   - wage and bonus restraint
   - redeployment
   - productivity improvements
   - overhead cuts
   - selective capacity reduction

2. **A partial rationalization package**
   - site or line consolidation where necessary
   - deeper fixed-cost removal
   - combined with labour contributions

The question is simple: can the first package deliver **equivalent, durable savings**?

If yes, that is preferable.  
If no, then we have to face the fact that some form of site rationalization may be unavoidable.

## My position, clearly stated

I will support a tough restructuring only if it produces:

- **real and measurable savings**
- **durable competitiveness gains**
- **implementation certainty**
- **labour peace after settlement**
- and **no permanent impairment of enterprise value**

I will **not** support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a settlement that merely postpones the problem,
- or a conflict-driven outcome that damages the company’s long-term value.
