I will be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the repeated diagnosis that this is a **real restructuring problem**, not something that can be solved with slogans or a one-off gesture.

I also agree with the expert and the others that:
- **non-closure measures can create material savings**,
- **capacity has to be reduced**,
- **labour will have to contribute**,
- and **prolonged conflict would damage the company**.

That is all true. I am not denying the competitive pressure, and I am not pretending the workforce can contribute nothing.

## Where I disagree
What I do **not** accept is the leap from “non-closure measures may not be enough alone” to “therefore closures and forced layoffs are now acceptable.”

That is not an economic law. It is a choice about who carries the burden.

And on that point my position is unchanged:

- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no plant closures**
- **no settlement that shifts the main pain onto employees while owners, capital, and management escape equivalent sacrifice**

That is the line. It remains the line.

## To management
You keep demanding proof that a no-closure route can close the gap. Fair enough — but you have not yet shown that closures are unavoidable either.

The expert’s assessment was clear:
- no-closure measures can generate **several hundred million euros per year** if pursued hard;
- they may not fully substitute for site rationalization in every case;
- but that means they are a **serious bargaining basis**, not a fake one.

So if you want our agreement, stop treating closures as the default benchmark of seriousness. Bring a package that combines:
- working-time flexibility,
- wage restraint and bonus sacrifice,
- redeployment and qualification,
- productivity improvements,
- overhead cuts,
- and selective capacity reduction

— while preserving jobs and sites.

If you refuse that path, then you are not proving necessity; you are choosing confrontation.

## To the investors
You are right about one thing: a cosmetic compromise would be worthless. On that, I agree completely.

But I reject your framing that any no-closure deal is automatically cosmetic or weak. A credible settlement must be **implementable**, and in this company that means it has to survive:
- co-determination,
- political reality,
- industrial relations,
- and workforce acceptance.

A closure-first or forced-layoff-first approach is not automatically more credible. It may actually be less credible, because it risks a strike spiral and long-lasting trust destruction.

So I do not accept the claim that the harshest option is therefore the responsible one.

## To the controlling family holding
You are right that a plan must pass the real governance structure. I agree.

But governability cannot be used as a one-way argument to push costs down onto labour while ownership and top management remain largely untouched. If the company wants a durable settlement, then the top also has to contribute:
- executive restraint,
- management overhead cuts,
- and binding investment commitments for the sites that remain.

If the burden only goes downward, there will be no fair settlement.

## To the state government
I understand and respect the concern for regional employment and industrial capability. That is legitimate.

But I have to be equally clear: preserving a footprint only matters if the company remains viable. So the answer is not “nothing changes,” but **change without destruction**.

That means real employee contribution, real management contribution, and a restructuring that preserves key sites and industrial capability. Not a closure-driven solution sold as inevitability.

## On the expert’s analysis
The expert’s own conclusion does **not** justify ignoring our red lines.

Yes:
- closures are the strongest lever on paper;
- partial rationalization is stronger on durability;
- full closure-led restructuring is strongest economically but riskiest to execute.

That does **not** mean closures are the obvious answer here. It means the company must prove that the no-closure route is insufficient.

So the burden of proof is still on those demanding plant closures.

## What we are prepared to discuss
We remain prepared to negotiate a hard settlement if it includes:
- a real employee contribution,
- working-time and capacity adjustments,
- redeployment and qualification,
- management and owner contributions,
- and binding investment commitments for German sites.

But the settlement must stay within these limits:
- **no closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **collective wage framework preserved**
- **durable job and site security**

That is not a soft position. It is a serious one.

## What we will not accept
We will not accept:
- a closure-led strategy,
- forced layoffs dressed up as necessity,
- or a deal where workers sacrifice jobs and wages while the rest of the governance structure avoids equivalent responsibility.

If that is the route management insists on, then it should expect resistance, not cooperation. The peace obligation is over, and the workforce is organized.

## Bottom line
We are ready for a hard settlement.  
We are **not** ready to sign away German plants, job security, and the collective wage framework because closures are the easiest lever on a spreadsheet.

If you want agreement, bring a package that proves equivalent savings **without** closures and forced redundancies. If you cannot do that, then the honest conclusion is not that closures are unavoidable — it is that management is unwilling to share the burden fairly.
