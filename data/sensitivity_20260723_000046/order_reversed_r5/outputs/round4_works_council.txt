We have now reached the point where everyone is forced to show their hand.

## Where I agree
I agree with all parties on the diagnosis that the company needs a **real restructuring**.  
I also agree that:
- **prolonged conflict destroys value**,
- **capacity must be brought in line with demand**,
- **labour must contribute**,
- and any settlement must be **durable and implementable**.

I do not deny the competitive pressure. I do not deny that the business needs a cost reset. I do not deny that the company needs room to invest in future products.

So let nobody pretend the works council is denying reality. We are not.

## Where I disagree
What I reject is the repeated attempt to convert “the company needs savings” into “therefore German plant closures and compulsory redundancies are acceptable.”

That is not a necessity. It is a choice.

And I will be equally clear: **we do not accept closures, and we do not accept forced layoffs.**  
That remains my line.

## To management
You keep saying the numbers require closures unless we produce “equivalent savings.” Fine. Then show the actual plant-by-plant proof.

So far, what I hear is:
- closures are the strongest lever,
- closures are the cleanest lever,
- closures are the easiest lever.

That is not the same as **unavoidable**.

The expert has already confirmed that a serious no-closure package can produce **material recurring savings** — not symbolic, material. That matters. It means the burden of proof is still on those demanding closures.

If you want to argue that closures are necessary, then show:
- which sites,
- what fixed costs,
- what utilization,
- what savings cannot be achieved otherwise,
- and why every non-closure option fails.

Until then, “closures or nothing” is not a case. It is a threat.

## To the investors
You keep speaking as if credibility is measured by how much pain is imposed on workers. That is not credibility. That is one-sidedness.

A credible restructuring must also be **implementable**. And in this governance structure, implementation means:
- co-determination reality,
- supervisory-board reality,
- political reality,
- and industrial-relations reality.

A package that triggers strikes, blockages, and lasting distrust is not more credible just because it is harsher on labour. It may be less credible because it cannot be carried through.

So no, I do not accept the claim that the harshest option is therefore the responsible one.

## To the family owner
You are right about one thing: a plan that cannot pass the real governance structure is not credible.

But that also means the answer cannot be to force through a solution that the workforce and the state will not accept. Co-determination is not decoration. It is a structural power in this company, and any serious plan has to respect that.

If you want long-term stability, then the burden must be shared:
- management overhead cuts,
- executive restraint,
- owner responsibility,
- and binding investment commitments for the sites that remain.

Do not ask employees alone to pay for a strategic problem created at the top.

## To the state government
I appreciate your concern for regional employment and industrial capability. On that, we are aligned.

But let me be equally clear: if “competitiveness” is used to justify closures before alternatives have been exhausted, then that is not industrial strategy. That is the beginning of deindustrialization by spreadsheet.

The state should not endorse that.

## On the expert analysis
The expert’s conclusion actually strengthens my position.

Yes, closures produce the biggest savings on paper.  
But the expert also said:
- non-closure measures can deliver **several hundred million euros per year**,
- selective capacity cuts can go a long way,
- and the outcome depends on actual plant data and utilization.

That means closures are **not proven unavoidable**. They are simply the strongest lever.

And I do not accept that “strongest lever” equals “must use it.” Not when the social, political, and co-determination consequences are so severe.

## What we are prepared to discuss
We are prepared to discuss a hard restructuring if it includes:
- **no closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **binding site and job guarantees to 2030**
- **real employee contribution**
- **working-time flexibility**
- **redeployment and qualification**
- **management and owner contributions**
- **investment commitments for German plants**

That is not a soft package. That is a serious package.

## What we will not accept
We will not accept:
- a closure-led strategy,
- forced layoffs dressed up as necessity,
- or a deal where the workforce sacrifices jobs and wages while ownership and management avoid equivalent responsibility.

If that is the route management insists on, then it should expect resistance, not consent. The workforce is organized, warning strikes have already shown that, and the peace obligation is over.

## Bottom line
We are ready for a tough settlement.  
We are **not** ready to sign away German plants, job security, and co-determination to solve a problem that has not been proven unsolvable any other way.

If you want our agreement, bring a package that proves equivalent savings **without** closures and forced layoffs. Until then, my answer remains no.
