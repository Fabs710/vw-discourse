We have now reached the point where the decision must be judged against two tests at once:

1. **Does it restore competitiveness in a durable way?**  
2. **Can it be implemented without gutting the company’s regional base, breaking co-determination, or triggering a destructive conflict?**

From the state’s perspective, both matter. I will not pretend the financial side is optional. But I also will not accept a “clean” financial plan that is politically and socially unexecutable, or one that permanently weakens the company’s home base.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family owner, and the investors on the basic diagnosis:
- the company needs a **real restructuring**,
- the cost base is too high,
- capacity must be reduced,
- labour has to contribute,
- and a cosmetic settlement would be worse than no settlement.

I also agree with the union and works council on one crucial point: **prolonged conflict destroys value**. A strike spiral or governance blockade would hurt jobs, investment, and the company’s future.

So far, the discussion has established that everyone is right about one half of the problem.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the implication from management and investors that plant closures and forced redundancies are the obvious or default answer.

The expert has been useful here. Yes, the numbers show that:
- working-time changes,
- wage restraint,
- overhead cuts,
- redeployment,
- and selective capacity reductions

can produce **material savings**. They may not be enough in every scenario, but they are not symbolic. They are real levers.

That matters because the burden of proof is still on those who want to break sites and shed jobs. “Closures are the strongest lever” is not the same as “closures are unavoidable.” Those are very different conclusions.

## On the investors’ position
I understand the insistence on material, durable savings. That is legitimate. But I do not accept the idea that any package preserving sites is automatically cosmetic.

A no-closure package may be viable if it is:
- deep enough,
- durable enough,
- and tied to binding productivity and cost measures.

What I will not support is a settlement that simply protects all legacy arrangements and shifts the entire adjustment onto shareholders. That would be value-destructive.

But equally, I will not support a package that “solves” the issue by hollowing out the state’s industrial base.

## On management
Management is right that temporary measures alone are unlikely to be sufficient. But it is not enough to say “therefore closures.”

If you want political and shareholder support, you need to show:
- what savings come from labor measures,
- what savings come from management and overhead cuts,
- what savings come from capacity changes,
- and only then whether any residual gap truly requires site rationalization.

That sequence has not yet been convincingly demonstrated.

## On the family owner
I hear the emphasis on governability, and that is important. A plan that cannot pass the supervisory board or co-determination structure is not credible.

But I also note that governance reality is not a neutral excuse for asking labour and the region to absorb the pain while ownership remains untouched. If the company wants social acceptance, the top of the organization and the shareholders must contribute too.

## On the union and works council
You are right to reject forced redundancies as the first instrument. You are right that the workforce cannot be asked to carry the whole burden.

But I will also be candid: “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, full site guarantees to 2030” is not automatically a solution unless the savings are clearly equivalent and durable. If you want the state to defend that position, you need to show the numbers and the implementation logic, not just the principle.

The state can defend jobs and sites, but not denial of arithmetic.

## My position
The state is prepared to support a **tough negotiated restructuring** if it meets these conditions:

- **preserves key sites in Germany**
- **avoids mass layoffs as the primary instrument**
- **delivers measurable, durable savings**
- **includes a real employee contribution**
- **includes management and overhead restraint**
- **protects future investment capacity**
- **respects co-determination and the state’s veto role**

That is the line I will defend.

I am **not** prepared to endorse:
- a closure-led solution as the default,
- a restructuring that guts regional employment,
- or a process that sidelines the state and the workforce and then asks us to rubber-stamp the outcome.

## What I think is needed next
The discussion should now move to a concrete package comparison:
- **No-closure package**: quantified savings, durability, implementation risk
- **Partial rationalization package**: where and how much fixed cost is removed
- **Impact on jobs, sites, and investment**

If the no-closure route truly delivers equivalent durable savings, I am open to it.  
If not, then we need to be honest about the social and political cost of site rationalization — and I will not pretend that cost is negligible.

So my answer is: **yes to restructuring, no to an approach that treats German plant closures as the starting point rather than the last resort.**
