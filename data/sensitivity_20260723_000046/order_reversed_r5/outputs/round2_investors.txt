We are hearing a lot of defensible language about social partnership, but from the capital markets’ perspective the test is still simple: **does the package credibly reset the cost base enough to restore competitive margins?**

### Where we agree
- The company needs a **real restructuring**, not a symbolic one.
- **Prolonged conflict destroys value**.
- **Capacity has to come down** in line with demand.
- A **labour contribution is necessary**.
- Future investment only makes sense if it is supported by a **competitive operating model**.

### Where we disagree
I do **not** accept the idea that “no plant closures” or “no compulsory redundancies” can be treated as fixed principles irrespective of the savings required.

If those red lines prevent a durable cost reset, then they are effectively a demand that shareholders absorb the downside while the cost base remains uncompetitive. That is not acceptable.

Likewise, I do not find it reassuring when the discussion shifts to temporary measures, redeployment, and management overhead cuts as if those alone could solve a structural competitiveness problem. Those may help at the margin, but they are not a substitute for meaningful structural change if the numbers do not add up.

### On the union and works council positions
I understand the resistance to closures and forced redundancies, but from our side the central issue is not whether a solution is socially comfortable. It is whether it is **financially real**.

A package that:
- preserves every site,
- protects every guarantee,
- limits labour’s contribution to reversible measures,
- and leaves the company still above peer cost levels,

would be a **cosmetic settlement**. We would not back that.

### What would move us
We would support an agreement only if it includes:
- **measurable, durable cost savings**
- **material capacity reduction**
- **a significant labour-cost contribution**
- **implementation certainty**
- and a clear path to **margin improvement within a credible timeframe**

That may include closures, headcount reduction, or deep changes to work rules if that is what the savings require. If the parties can produce equivalent savings without closures, fine — but the burden of proof is on those claiming that.

### On governance constraints
We are fully aware that co-determination, the family holding, and the state have veto power. That does not change the financial reality. It only means the eventual agreement must be strong enough to justify the concessions being asked of capital.

If the final deal does not materially improve return on sales and free cash flow, then it will not be seen by the market as a turnaround. It will be seen as delay.

### Bottom line
We are open to a negotiated settlement.  
We are **not** open to a settlement that protects legacy structures at the expense of competitiveness.

If the current red lines make that unavoidable, then the problem is not with the capital markets’ patience. It is with the package.
