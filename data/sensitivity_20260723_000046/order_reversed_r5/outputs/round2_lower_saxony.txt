We appreciate the directness from the other shareholder groups and management. Let me be equally direct from the state’s perspective.

### Where I agree
I agree that:
- the company needs a **real restructuring**, not a symbolic one;
- the cost base must come down;
- capacity has to reflect market reality;
- and prolonged conflict would damage value for everyone.

I also accept that the state cannot demand that nothing changes. That would not be serious.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the framing that plant closures and forced redundancies are simply “tools on the table” in the same way as working-time changes or internal reorganization.

For us, closures are not an abstract efficiency measure. They mean:
- lasting damage to regional employment,
- loss of industrial capability,
- weakening of supplier networks,
- and a political and social cost that cannot be brushed aside as a side effect.

So if the logic of the package is “we only get competitiveness by shutting sites and shedding jobs on a large scale,” then I say plainly: **that is not an acceptable starting point**. It may be the easiest answer on paper, but it is not the best answer for the company, the region, or the governance structure.

### What would move us
I am prepared to support a tough restructuring if it does all of the following:
- **preserves key sites in Germany**
- **avoids mass layoffs as the primary instrument**
- **delivers measurable savings**
- **includes a real employee contribution**
- **protects the company’s long-term investment capacity**
- and remains politically and institutionally executable

That means the burden cannot fall on employees alone, but it also cannot fall on the state to simply endorse closures because management prefers a cleaner spreadsheet.

### On the capital markets’ position
I hear the demand for “equivalent savings.” That is fair in principle. But “equivalent” cannot be defined in a way that ignores governance reality and regional consequences. A plan that looks stronger on a slide deck but cannot survive the supervisory board, co-determination, and public scrutiny is not a real plan.

So yes, the savings must be real. But they must be achieved through a package that the company can actually carry through without destroying the social basis of its operations.

### On management
Management is right that temporary measures alone are unlikely to solve a structural challenge. But management is not entitled to treat closures as the default proof of seriousness.

If you want support, bring a package that shows:
- which savings come from operations,
- which come from labour terms,
- which come from management discipline and overhead,
- and how you preserve the company’s regional backbone.

If the answer is simply “cut sites and jobs until the numbers work,” then you are asking the state to co-sign a long-term weakening of the company’s home base. I will not do that.

### On the union and works council
I agree with the insistence on social partnership, but it has to be paired with credible change. The workforce cannot expect to keep every legacy guarantee unchanged while everyone else absorbs the adjustment. There must be a negotiated contribution from labour.

At the same time, labour is right that any solution must be fair and durable. A forced break with the workforce would create instability that could undermine the turnaround itself.

### My bottom line
I am **open to a tough settlement**.  
I am **not open to a settlement that hollowes out the regional footprint of the company**.

So the test for the next round is simple:
- show me a package that produces **real competitiveness gains**
- **without making plant closures and mass layoffs the core instrument**
- and without sidelining the state’s co-determination and veto role.

If that cannot be done, then the burden is on those demanding closures to explain why they believe a socially destructive route is necessary when alternatives have not been exhausted.
