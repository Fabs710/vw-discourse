We are aligned on the destination: **this company must become structurally competitive again**. Where I differ is on the route that gets us there.

I agree with the institutional investors and the controlling family on one essential point: **a cosmetic settlement is useless**. If the package does not materially reduce the cost base, improve utilization, and restore return on sales, then it is not a restructuring. It is deferral.

Where I must push back is on the idea that we can achieve the necessary turnaround through temporary, reversible measures alone. **We cannot keep a cost structure built for a different market reality and still expect to compete against lower-cost producers and a shrinking European demand base.** Working-time tweaks, training, and phased adjustments may be part of the mix, but they are not sufficient on their own.

To the regional state government: I understand the political and social sensitivity. But **preserving every site at all costs is not a strategy**. If the company remains uncompetitive, the regional footprint will erode anyway—just more slowly, with weaker investment, weaker product cycles, and continuing uncertainty. That outcome is worse for the region, not better.

So let me be explicit:
- **Capacity must come down.**
- **Labour costs must come down.**
- **The company must regain room to invest in future products and technology.**
- **We need a durable, enforceable agreement, not a symbolic one.**

I am open on the means, but not on the objective. If there is a negotiated package that delivers equivalent structural savings without closures, I will consider it seriously. But it has to be **credible, measurable, and durable**. If it only postpones the adjustment, I will not regard it as acceptable.

And I want to be frank: management cannot carry the burden alone, and neither can shareholders. The workforce side must make a real contribution if the business is to secure its future. Without that, we do not have a turnaround. We have a delay.
