I will be equally direct.

We are not here to preserve sentiment. We are here to restore this business to a cost base that can survive in a shrinking, brutal market and fund the investment needed through 2030. That requires **durable structural savings**, not a comfort agreement dressed up as restructuring.

## Where I agree
I agree on four points:

1. **A cosmetic deal is worthless.**  
   If the package does not materially improve margins, free cash flow, and return on sales, it fails.

2. **Prolonged conflict destroys value.**  
   A long strike or board deadlock would hurt output, customers, reputation, and cash.

3. **Labour must contribute.**  
   There is no credible turnaround in which employees absorb nothing.

4. **Governance reality matters.**  
   A plan that cannot pass the supervisory board and co-determination structure is not a real plan.

So yes, we all agree on the diagnosis.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** agree that this means plant closures and compulsory redundancies should be ruled out in advance.

That position may be politically convenient, but it is economically insufficient. The expert has now made the point clearly: working-time changes, wage restraint, redeployment, overhead cuts, and selective capacity reductions can produce **material savings**, but in a company with this level of overcapacity and cost disadvantage they are often **not enough on their own**.

That is the central issue.

### To the union and works council
You keep saying “no closures” and “no forced layoffs” as if those were self-justifying principles. They are not.

If you want to exclude the strongest structural tools, then you must show how you achieve **equivalent, durable fixed-cost removal**. Not vague promises. Not reversible concessions. Not temporary pain relief.

I have not yet heard a package from labour that closes the gap with enough certainty. What I hear is:
- wage restraint,
- working-time changes,
- redeployment,
- some productivity gains,
- and site protection.

That may help. It does not automatically solve the problem.

### To the state government
I respect the regional and political responsibility, but I reject the idea that preserving the footprint can outrank competitiveness indefinitely.

A plant kept open at an uncompetitive cost is not a victory for the region. It is a delayed erosion of the same industrial base you want to protect. If the company cannot reset its structure, the investment will not come, and the erosion will happen anyway.

So I will not accept “no closures” as a default answer simply because the consequences are painful. Restructuring is painful. That is why it is restructuring.

### To the family owner
You are right that a package must be executable under our governance structure. But that does **not** mean we should limit ourselves to whatever is easiest to approve. If we do that, we lock in the current cost problem.

Your argument that closures are not automatically the right lever is fair. But you go too far when you imply that because alternatives exist, closure is off the table. That is not what the numbers say.

The question is not whether non-closure measures can contribute. They can. The question is whether they can contribute **enough**.

### To the capital markets
You are right to insist on hard savings and durability. I agree.

But I also caution against treating closure as the only serious benchmark. The right benchmark is whether the package:
- materially lowers the cost base,
- reduces capacity to match demand,
- improves margins,
- and is actually implementable.

If a no-closure package can do that, fine. But the burden of proof is on those claiming that it can.

## What the expert actually means
The expert’s analysis is not a blank cheque for “no closures.” It says something much narrower:

- non-closure measures can produce **several hundred million euros per year** if pursued hard,
- but they often do **not fully substitute** for the fixed-cost removal achieved by site rationalization,
- and the more severe the structural gap, the more likely some rationalization becomes necessary.

That is exactly my view.

So let’s stop pretending the expert’s answer supports an all-sites-preserved settlement. It does not. It supports a serious comparison, and if the no-closure route falls short, then we must be willing to act.

## My position
Management will support a tough restructuring only if it delivers:

- **real and measurable savings**
- **durable structural cost reduction**
- **meaningful capacity reduction**
- **a genuine labour-cost contribution**
- **implementation certainty**
- **room to invest in future products and technology**

We are open on the method, but not on the objective.

I will not accept:

I also will not accept the idea that management should simply absorb the cost of overcapacity forever because closures are politically hard. That would be a failure of responsibility.
