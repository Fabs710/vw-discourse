### Where I agree
I agree with all sides on one basic point: **this cannot be a cosmetic settlement**. The company has a real competitiveness problem, and yes, **capacity, costs, and future investment capacity** all have to be addressed.

I also agree with the expert’s broad conclusion that:
- **working-time changes, wage concessions, redeployment, and overhead cuts can produce meaningful savings**,  
- but **they are unlikely to be enough on their own** if management is truly talking about a large structural gap.

So I am not pretending this can be solved by slogans or by preserving everything unchanged.

### Where I disagree
What I reject is the leap from “non-closure measures may not be sufficient alone” to “therefore plant closures and compulsory redundancies become acceptable default tools.”

That is not a neutral economic conclusion. That is a **choice about who bears the burden**.

And that is exactly where I draw the line:
- **no forced redundancies**,
- **no plant closures as the first or default solution**,
- **no restructuring in which employees carry the main pain while owners and management keep their options intact**.

### To management
You keep saying the numbers require closures unless we produce “equivalent savings.” Fine — then engage seriously with what the expert actually said.

The expert did **not** say closures are the only route. They said alternative measures can deliver **several hundred million euros per year** if pursued hard. That is not trivial. That is a real contribution.

So the question is not whether labour must contribute. We already said it must. The question is whether management is willing to accept a package that includes:
- **working-time reductions**,
- **temporary or medium-term wage concessions**,
- **bonus restraint**,
- **redeployment and internal mobility**,
- **management overhead cuts**,
- **productivity improvements**,
- and **capacity reduction without site destruction**.

If you refuse to seriously combine those measures, then you are not proving closures are necessary — you are just choosing the harshest instrument first.

### To the investors
I understand your focus on returns and free cash flow. But you keep treating “credible” as if it only means “painful enough for workers.”

That is not a business test. A credible package must also be **implementable**.

A plan that triggers:
- industrial conflict,
- co-determination blockage,
- political resistance,
- and long-term trust damage

may look cleaner on a spreadsheet, but it is not automatically more credible in reality. If you want durable savings, you need a settlement that can actually be carried through.

And no, a restructuring is not made more credible simply because it threatens more jobs. It is made credible by being **durable, enforceable, and socially stable enough to execute**.

### To the controlling family and regional government
You both say you want a durable turnaround and stable governance. Then you cannot ask the workforce to shoulder the adjustment alone.

If you want employees to accept sacrifices, then there must be visible contributions from:
- **owners**,
- **management**,
- and **top-level compensation and overhead structures**.

That means:
- executive restraint,
- lower management costs,
- serious internal restructuring above the shop floor,
- and a binding investment commitment for the sites that remain.

If the message is “the workforce must give ground so the ownership model can stay untouched,” then we are not talking about fairness. We are talking about one-sided adjustment.

### On the expert’s numbers
The expert’s assessment actually supports our position more than yours in one key respect:

Yes, closures can create the largest savings. But the expert also said:
- non-closure levers can deliver **material recurring savings**,
- selective capacity reductions can go a long way,
- and the final outcome depends on actual plant data, utilization, and product allocation.

That means there is room for a negotiated package **before** anyone declares closures unavoidable.

So I want to be very clear:  
**You have not shown that plant closures are unavoidable.**  
You have shown only that they are the strongest lever on paper.

That is not enough to justify breaking red lines.

We will not accept:
- a closure-first strategy,
- forced layoffs dressed up as necessity,

If that is the direction management insists on, then you should expect resistance, not cooperation. The union has already shown it can mobilize, and the peace obligation is over.

We are ready for a hard settlement.
