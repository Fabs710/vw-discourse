## To management
I agree that the company needs a **durable** competitiveness solution. I do **not** dispute the cost problem, the market pressure, or the need to restore investment capacity.

But I disagree with your framing that the only “serious” answer is to keep plant closures and forced redundancies on the table as a quasi-default option. In a company with our governance structure, social legitimacy, and regional responsibility, that is not a responsible starting point.

What you have still not shown is:
- a convincing comparison of savings from closures versus non-closure alternatives,
- the contribution management itself is prepared to make,
- and why a negotiated restructuring cannot close the gap without lasting damage to German industrial capacity.

You keep saying “capacity must come out of the system.” That may be true. But **capacity reduction is not identical to plant closure**, and the burden cannot be shifted onto the regions and workforce as if they were disposable.

If you want the state to take your case seriously, bring:
1. the quantified cost gap,
2. the savings from each restructuring path,
3. the operational and regional consequences,
4. and the management/owner-side contribution.

Without that, your insistence on closures remains a demand, not a demonstrated necessity.

## To the works council and union
I agree with you on the central point: **no compulsory redundancies as a first response**, and no settlement that treats workers as the adjustment buffer.

I also agree that co-determination and site protection are not decorative principles; they are part of the company’s stability. If they are undermined, the company loses trust and industrial peace.

Where I would sharpen the discussion is this: we should not just repeat red lines. We need to force management to quantify a **real alternative package** that protects sites and jobs while still delivering material savings. That means:
- working-time adjustments,
- voluntary instruments,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- temporary and strictly limited employee contributions,
- and investment commitments.

I am open to that route. I am **not** open to a one-sided solution that cuts jobs first and asks for social acceptance later.

## To the controlling family holding
I agree that a prolonged conflict would destroy value. That is exactly why I do not want management to drive this into a strike-and-closure spiral.

But I disagree if “exhaust every alternative” is treated as weakness or delay. In this case, exhausting alternatives is not sentimentality; it is prudent ownership. If there is a credible way to preserve sites, protect employment, and still restore competitiveness, that is the superior long-term outcome.

What I will not support is a logic that says:
- if the numbers are difficult,
- then regional employment and co-determination must simply give way.

That would damage the company’s legitimacy and, in the medium term, its value.

## To the institutional investors
You are right to demand numbers and durability. I support that discipline.

But I do not accept the implication that preserving all German industrial capacity is automatically irrational. A company this important cannot be managed like a short-cycle balance sheet exercise. Regional embeddedness, skilled employment, and labour peace are part of long-term value creation.

If you want an answer to your return-on-sales concern, then support a package that is:
- quantified,
- enforceable,
- durable,
- and socially workable.

Do not assume that closures are the only serious instrument. They may be one instrument, but they are not yet justified as the necessary one.

## My position
As anchor shareholder and political actor, my position remains:

- **Yes** to a restructuring that restores competitiveness.
- **Yes** to a negotiated package with real savings.
- **No** to plant closures in Germany as the starting point.
- **No** to compulsory redundancies.
- **No** to sidelining the state’s co-determination and veto role.
- **No** to a solution that guts regional employment and weakens the company as an economic anchor.

## What I would support
I would support a settlement if it includes:
- a quantified savings package,
- capacity adjustment without closures where possible,
- working-time and flexibility measures,
- voluntary departures and redeployment,
- qualification and transformation investment,
- a temporary employee contribution tied to binding guarantees,
- management restraint and owner-side responsibility,
- and clear commitments on future products and plant investment.

## What I would block
I would block any deal that:
- closes German plants as the first or default answer,
- forces mass layoffs,
- or reduces the state to a bystander while irreversible damage is done to our regional industrial base.

So the next step is not rhetorical pressure. It is a **hard, quantified comparison of options**. If management cannot demonstrate that non-destructive measures are insufficient, then it should stop presenting closures as inevitability.
