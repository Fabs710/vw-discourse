I will respond plainly and from my position as the state’s anchor shareholder.

## First: where I agree

I agree with the expert, the family holding, the union, and the works council on one central point: **cosmetic measures will not solve this company’s structural problem**. We need a **durable, quantified restructuring**, not a public-relations compromise.

I also agree that prolonged conflict would be value-destructive. A strike spiral, a governance deadlock, or a rushed closure plan would all damage the company, its workforce, and the regional economy.

And yes: **the actual savings gap still has not been pinned down**. That is the key issue. Without that, nobody can honestly claim closures are necessary, or that they are avoidable.

## Where I disagree

### To management
I do **not** accept the way you keep presenting closures and forced redundancies as the default serious answer.

You have not yet demonstrated that:
- non-closure measures are insufficient,
- hybrid measures cannot close the gap,
- or that the cost of a closure-led fight would not wipe out much of the benefit.

You keep saying “capacity must come out of the system.” That may be true. But **capacity reduction is not automatically plant closure**, and you still have not shown why the burden must fall first and foremost on German sites and jobs.

You are right that a weak settlement is no solution. But you are wrong if you think that only closures can be serious. That is a claim, not yet a proof.

### To the trade union and works council
I support your red line against **forced redundancies** and your insistence that co-determination is not decorative. I also support site protection as a matter of regional responsibility.

But I will say just as clearly: I cannot support a position that says all German sites must be protected **regardless of the economic facts**. If the numbers show that some footprint adjustment is unavoidable, then we must face that honestly and in a negotiated way.

What I cannot accept from management is one-sided pain. What I also cannot accept from labour is a guarantee framework that leaves the underlying competitiveness problem untouched.

### To the investors
You are right to insist that the company must restore margins and investment capacity. But you should not confuse discipline with maximal destruction. A company like this does not create long-term value by detonating trust and regional legitimacy.

If you push too hard for closures as the benchmark, you may get a mathematically tidy plan and a practically ungovernable conflict.

### To the family holding
You are right not to start from closures. But I want to be more explicit than you have been: **closures should not be treated as the neutral fallback unless the numbers prove there is no workable alternative**. Keeping them permanently in the foreground makes a negotiated settlement harder, not easier.

## My position

The state will only support a restructuring that meets all of the following:

1. **It is quantitatively credible**
   - We need a real scenario comparison:
     - no-closure,
     - hybrid,
     - closure-led.
   - With annual savings, one-off costs, employment impact, disruption risk, and effect on margins.

2. **It protects core employment and sites in the state**
   - I am not here to sign off on regional industrial hollowing-out.
   - No settlement that guts the company as an economic anchor will be acceptable.

3. **It preserves co-determination and the state’s veto role**
   - Any attempt to sideline the governance rights attached to our stake would cross a line.

4. **It shares the burden fairly**
   - Management, owners, and labour all need to contribute.
   - I do not accept a plan where the workforce is the only adjustment buffer.

5. **It is durable**
   - Temporary relief that simply postpones the crisis is not enough.

## What I am prepared to support

I am prepared to support a **hybrid restructuring** if — and only if — it is deep enough to be real. That can include:
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary departures,
- redeployment,
- qualification and transformation measures,
- temporary and strictly limited employee contributions,
- management restraint,
- overhead simplification,
- productivity improvements,
- and binding investment commitments.

But I am **not** prepared to accept closures as the first move, or forced redundancies as the easy answer.

## What I am prepared to block

I will block any outcome that:
- uses German plant closures as the starting point,
- forces mass layoffs,
- or turns the state into a passive observer while lasting damage is done to our industrial base.

## What must happen next

The discussion is now at the point where rhetoric is no longer useful.

We need a **board-level scenario table** that answers:
- What is the annual savings gap?
- What comes from management-side savings?
- What comes from owner-side contributions?
- What comes from workforce measures?
- What is the one-off restructuring cost?
- What is the employment effect?
- What is the implementation risk?
- What is the return-on-sales effect?

## Bottom line

I will support a hard restructuring if it protects the company’s long-term health **without gutting regional employment or undermining co-determination**.

I will **not** support closure-first logic without proof.

I will **not** support forced layoffs.

And I will **not** support any settlement that weakens the state’s legitimate role as a long-term anchor shareholder and guardian of regional industrial stability.
