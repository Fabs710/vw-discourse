I appreciate the clarity from all sides. Let me be equally direct.

The controlling family holding shares the assessment that **the company needs a durable competitiveness reset**, not a temporary patch. If the German cost base remains structurally too high, value erosion will continue, and that is not acceptable for long-term owners or for the company’s future investment capacity.

Where I agree:
- I agree with management that **the problem is structural**, not cosmetic.
- I agree that **durable margin improvement** is necessary.
- I agree with the state government and employee representatives that **stability, legitimacy, and labour peace matter**. A prolonged conflict would destroy value.
- I agree that any workforce contribution must be tied to **credible commitments on investment, jobs, and future product allocation**.

Where I disagree:
- I do **not** accept the proposition that plant closures and forced redundancies are the only credible route.
- I do **not** accept a “maximum pressure first” strategy that risks a prolonged confrontation, industrial action, and reputational damage before alternatives are fully tested.
- I do **not** accept shifting the burden primarily onto employees while preserving management flexibility and treating the workforce as the adjustment reservoir.

From a shareholder perspective, a deal that technically reduces cost but **breaks trust, triggers lengthy strikes, or permanently damages the company’s social fabric and German industrial base** is not a good outcome. It may look tough in the short term and still destroy value in the medium term.

So my position is this:
1. **A deep restructuring is necessary.**
2. **A negotiated solution is strongly preferable to imposed closures.**
3. **The first preference should be a quantified alternative package** that achieves meaningful savings through:
   - capacity reductions,
   - working-time flexibility,
   - voluntary departures,
   - redeployment and qualification,
   - temporary employee contributions,
   - and management/owner-side restraint where appropriate.
4. If management claims closures are unavoidable, then it must show, in a transparent and quantifiable way, **why all credible alternatives fail** and why the value destruction from conflict is lower than the value destruction from maintaining excess capacity.

I want to stress one point very clearly: **I will not support a solution that permanently impairs the enterprise’s value or destabilizes governance**. That includes any approach that undermines the long-term ownership position or turns the restructuring into a political or industrial confrontation that weakens the company more than it helps.

So the burden now is on management to present a **hard, credible numbers-based case** and on the employee side to present an **alternative that is not just a refusal, but a real structural contribution**. If that alternative can close the gap with sufficient durability, I will take it seriously. If not, then the company will need a more forceful adjustment — but even then, it should be pursued in a way that minimizes long-term damage and preserves the possibility of labour peace.

In short:  
**I back competitiveness, not gratuitous destruction.**  
**I back restructuring, not open-ended confrontation.**  
**I back a durable settlement, but not at any price.**
