Here is a board-level scenario view, using the assumptions already stated by the expert and the negotiation record.

## Executive conclusion

A **no-closure package can plausibly close the gap only if the true annual gap is in the moderate band**:
- roughly **€0.8–1.5bn per year**.

If the gap is **larger than that**, then:
- a no-closure deal becomes fragile,
- a **deep hybrid** is the most realistic negotiated route,
- and **some footprint reduction / site rationalization starts to look economically necessary**.

The critical point is that **management-side and owner-side contributions help, but rarely enough on their own**. The main adjustable lever remains the workforce-cost and capacity base. If that lever cannot be made deep and durable enough without closures, then the economics push toward hybrid or closure-led restructuring.

## 1) Scenario comparison

### A. No-closure restructuring
No German plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, with savings coming from:
- working-time flexibility
- voluntary exits
- redeployment
- qualification/training
- productivity improvements
- overhead simplification
- temporary labor-cost contribution

**Annual run-rate savings:**  
- **€0.6–1.4bn**

**One-off restructuring costs:**  
- **€1.0–2.5bn**

**Employment impact:**  
- **8,000–15,000 fewer jobs**
- mostly attrition, voluntary departures, internal transfers

**Implementation risk:**  
- **Medium to high**
- strong dependence on labor cooperation
- savings can fade after 18–36 months if concessions are temporary

**ROS effect:**  
- **+0.5 to +1.5 percentage points**

**Assessment:**  
Viable only if the gap is moderate and management accepts slower margin repair. It is the lowest-conflict path, but also the least powerful structurally.

---

### B. Hybrid restructuring
No closure as the default, but with:
- capacity reduction
- product or shift consolidation
- partial site specialization
- selective line or function exits
- voluntary exits and redeployment
- working-time changes
- management restraint
- owner-side support

**Annual run-rate savings:**  
- **€1.2–2.4bn**

**One-off restructuring costs:**  
- **€1.8–4.0bn**

**Employment impact:**  
- **15,000–30,000 fewer jobs**

**Implementation risk:**  
- **Medium**
- more complex than no-closure, but materially more credible on economics
- lower conflict risk than direct closures if well negotiated

**ROS effect:**  
- **+1.0 to +2.5 percentage points**

**Assessment:**  
This is the most plausible negotiated outcome if management needs a real cost reset but wants to avoid the highest-disruption route. It is usually the best balance of savings, feasibility, and governance acceptability.

---

### C. Closure-led restructuring
One or more German plants close, with footprint reduction as the main fixed-cost lever.

**Annual run-rate savings:**  
- **€1.8–3.5bn**

**One-off restructuring costs:**  
- **€3.0–6.5bn**

**Employment impact:**  
- **25,000–45,000 fewer jobs**
- plus possible indirect regional losses

**Implementation risk:**  
- **High**
- strike, veto, legal, and political conflict risk is substantial
- realized savings may be reduced by delay and disruption

**ROS effect:**  
- **+1.5 to +3.5 percentage points** on paper
- but practical realized benefit can be lower

**Assessment:**  
Best on pure fixed-cost removal, but worst on execution friction and value destruction risk. Economically justified mainly if the gap is truly large and non-closure measures are insufficient.

## 2) Management-side and owner-side contributions

A realistic settlement usually needs contributions from all three sides.

### Management-side
Typical annual effect:
- **€150–400m**

Likely sources:
- overhead simplification
- executive/admin cuts
- procurement savings
- capex discipline
- portfolio rationalization

### Owner-side
Typical annual or quasi-annual equivalent:
- **€100–300m**

Likely forms:
- investment commitments
- dividend restraint
- intra-group support
- balance-sheet support

### Workforce-side
Typical annual effect:
- **€300–900m**

Likely sources:
- temporary wage restraint
- bonus forgoing
- working-time changes
- productivity measures
- voluntary exit programs

### Combined implication
Even at the high end, management + owner + workforce contributions usually close **part, not all**, of a large structural gap. That is why footprint reduction remains the key unresolved issue.

---

## 3) What this means for the bargaining range

### If the gap is moderate
A **deep no-closure or strong hybrid** package can work.

Likely structure:
- limited or no closures
- no compulsory redundancies
- time-bounded labour contribution
- management and owner contributions
- capacity reduction through shifts, lines, and product allocation rather than full site shutdowns

### If the gap is large
A no-closure solution becomes harder to sustain.

Likely outcome:
- hybrid becomes the practical compromise
- some footprint rationalization becomes increasingly likely
- closure threats gain credibility, even if they are not the preferred first choice

---

## 4) Risk view

### No-closure
- Lowest conflict risk
- Highest risk of insufficient structural savings
- Best politically
- weakest if the cost gap is large

### Hybrid
- Best all-round tradeoff
- moderate implementation complexity
- most likely to preserve enough value while still restoring margins

### Closure-led
- strongest fixed-cost removal
- highest disruption, veto, and strike risk
- could destroy more value than it creates if conflict is severe

---

## 5) Bottom line

### Can a no-closure package close the gap durably?
- **Yes, possibly** if the gap is **moderate** and the package is deep enough.
- **Probably not** if the gap is **large** and fixed costs are materially above demand-linked needs.

### Most likely economically viable settlement
- A **deep hybrid restructuring** is the most defensible base case.

### When closures become economically compelling
- If the gap is large,
- the workforce contribution cannot be made durable enough,
- and management/owner contributions still leave a material shortfall,
then **some footprint reduction becomes difficult to avoid**.
