## Board-level scenario comparison: closure-led vs hybrid vs no-closure

Below is a **defensible industrial-restructuring estimate** rather than a company-specific audit. Because the exact plant network, labor agreements, utilization, and model mix are not provided, the numbers should be read as **scenario ranges** for a large German OEM with multiple domestic plants, high fixed costs, and a material overcapacity problem.

### Core assumption on the gap
Management’s claim that the German footprint is structurally uncompetitive is plausible. For a group this size, the relevant question is usually whether the required gap is:

- **moderate**: roughly **€0.8–1.5 billion annual run-rate improvement**
- **large**: roughly **€1.5–3.0 billion annual run-rate improvement**

If the gap is in the first band, a strong hybrid package can often work.  
If it is in the second band, full no-closure solutions become much harder, and some footprint reduction starts to look economically necessary.

---

## 1) Scenario table

### A. No-closure restructuring
No German plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, reliance on working-time flexibility, voluntary exits, productivity, overhead simplification, redeployment, and temporary labor-cost contributions.

**Estimated annual run-rate savings**
- **€0.6–1.4 billion**

**Estimated one-off costs**
- **€1.0–2.5 billion**
  - severance/voluntary exit incentives
  - retraining and redeployment
  - temporary production dislocation
  - IT/process simplification
  - likely labor peace payments

**Employment impact**
- **8,000–15,000 fewer jobs**
  - mostly voluntary departures, attrition, internal transfers
  - low immediate redundancy risk if negotiated properly

**Implementation risk**
- **Medium to high**
  - requires very high labor participation
  - savings depend on adherence to temporary measures
  - risk that concessions decay after 18–36 months

**Return-on-sales effect**
- Typically **+0.5 to +1.5 percentage points**
  - enough if the starting margin gap is moderate
  - usually insufficient if the underlying problem is large overcapacity

**Industrial logic**
- Preserves sites and labor peace better than other options
- But fixed-cost reduction is limited unless productivity gains are very deep
- Most exposed to the criticism that it postpones structural adjustment

---

### B. Hybrid restructuring
No closure as the default, but with **capacity reduction, partial footprint consolidation, shift elimination, product relocation, and selected site specialization**. This can include one plant shrinking materially or one line/site exiting a function without a full closure.

**Estimated annual run-rate savings**
- **€1.2–2.4 billion**

**Estimated one-off costs**
- **€1.8–4.0 billion**
  - higher transition costs than no-closure
  - retooling and product migration
  - severance/voluntary exits
  - logistics and supply-chain redesign
  - possible limited strike-related losses if poorly handled

**Employment impact**
- **15,000–30,000 fewer jobs**
  - mix of voluntary exits, redeployment, and some forced separations if negotiations fail
  - site counts may remain unchanged, but site activity becomes thinner

**Implementation risk**
- **Medium**
  - still complex, but more credible on economics than pure no-closure
  - fewer governance shocks than outright closures
  - lower conflict risk than closure-led plans, if negotiated

**Return-on-sales effect**
- Typically **+1.0 to +2.5 percentage points**
  - often the most plausible path to restore target margins without maximum conflict
  - strong enough if the gap is moderate-to-upper moderate

**Industrial logic**
- Usually the best compromise between value, feasibility, and labor acceptance
- Captures some fixed-cost removal while avoiding the full political and operational cost of closures
- Most likely negotiation form if the gap is not extreme

---

### C. Closure-led restructuring
One or more German plants close, with footprint reduction as the main mechanism for fixed-cost removal.

**Estimated annual run-rate savings**
- **€1.8–3.5 billion**

**Estimated one-off costs**
- **€3.0–6.5 billion**
  - closure costs, severance, site shutdown, remediation
  - supply-chain reconfiguration
  - write-downs and impairment risk
  - likely labor conflict cost
  - potential customer and reputational damage

**Employment impact**
- **25,000–45,000 fewer jobs**
  - depending on plant size and supply-chain effects
  - additional indirect regional employment losses are possible

**Implementation risk**
- **High**
  - strongest legal, co-determination, and labor resistance
  - elevated strike/veto risk
  - high probability of delay and plan dilution
  - likely larger operational disruption than on paper

**Return-on-sales effect**
- Potentially **+1.5 to +3.5 percentage points**
  - but the realized benefit can be materially reduced by conflict, lost volume, and execution delays

**Industrial logic**
- Best on pure fixed-cost removal
- Worst on implementation friction and value destruction risk
- Economically attractive only if the gap is truly large and alternative savings are insufficient

---

## 2) What the numbers imply

### If the gap is moderate
If the structural gap is about **€0.8–1.5 billion per year**, then:

- a **strong no-closure** package can sometimes close most of it
- a **hybrid** package can usually close it credibly
- **closures are not economically necessary**
- but closure threats may still be used as bargaining leverage

### If the gap is large
If the gap is about **€1.5–3.0 billion per year**, then:

- no-closure becomes strained unless labor concessions are unusually deep and durable
- a **hybrid** is the most plausible negotiated route
- **some footprint reduction or site rationalization becomes more likely**
- pure no-closure risks becoming a delay strategy rather than a solution

---

## 3) Management-side and owner-side contributions

A realistic settlement usually needs contributions from all three sides.

### Management-side contributions
Typical annual effect:
- **€150–400 million**

Examples:
- overhead cuts
- executive and administrative simplification
- capex discipline
- procurement savings
- portfolio rationalization
- slower nonessential spend

### Owner-side contributions
Typical annual or quasi-annual effect:
- **€100–300 million equivalent**
- often via investment commitments, temporary dividend restraint, intra-group support, or balance-sheet support

This is useful, but rarely enough by itself to solve a major fixed-cost problem.

### Workforce-side contributions
Typical annual effect:
- **€300–900 million**
- via temporary wage restraint, working-time flexibility, bonus forgoing, productivity measures, and voluntary exit programs

This is the largest adjustable bucket in a negotiated solution, but it is usually time-bounded and politically constrained.

**Key point:**  
Even at the high end, these contributions often cover only **part** of a large gap. That is why the question of footprint reduction remains central.

---

## 4) Constraints and execution risks

### No-closure
Main risk: savings fade once temporary concessions expire.  
Best for preserving peace, weaker for deep structural reset.

### Hybrid
Main risk: complexity.  
If product allocation, headcount, and shift changes are not tightly sequenced, expected savings can leak away.

### Closure-led
Main risk: conflict and delay.  
The theoretical savings may not be fully realized because of:
- strikes
- governance vetoes
- legal delay
- customer disturbance
- reputational damage
- loss of know-how

In practice, closure-led outcomes often underperform the spreadsheet if conflict is intense.

---

## 5) Practical conclusion

### Most likely economically viable settlement
A **deep hybrid** is the most defensible base case.

It is usually the best balance of:
- annual savings
- implementation feasibility
- governance acceptability
- and value preservation

### When no-closure works
Only if:
- the gap is moderate,
- labor is willing to make time-bounded concessions,
- management and owners also contribute,
- and the company can accept a slower margin repair.

### When closures become hard to avoid
If:
- the gap is large,
- capacity is materially above demand,
- and labor-side concessions cannot be made deep or durable enough,
then some footprint reduction becomes economically more convincing.

---

## 6) Bottom-line answer to the key question
**Can a no-closure package close the gap durably after management-side and owner-side contributions?**

- **Yes, possibly** if the gap is moderate and the package is deep.
- **Probably not** if the gap is large and fixed-cost excess is structurally high.

So from an industrial restructuring perspective, the decisive issue is not whether closures are desirable. It is whether the company’s cost gap is small enough that a negotiated non-closure or hybrid package can cover it **durably** after all parties contribute.
