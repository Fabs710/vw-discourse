I will speak plainly.

We have now heard enough about “structural necessity” to know that management wants to keep the threat of closures and forced layoffs alive as leverage. I reject that logic.

## To management

You say the company needs a durable cost reset. I do not dispute the need for change. I dispute your chosen method.

What you have **not** shown is that German plant closures are necessary rather than simply convenient for management. The expert said the decisive question is the size of the gap. That is exactly why closures cannot be used as a starting point or as a pressure tactic. First prove that non-destructive measures cannot close the gap. You have not done that.

You also keep speaking as if a “hybrid” solution is acceptable only if it includes site rationalization. That is not acceptable to us. A hybrid must mean:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and a real reduction in cost through working-time, productivity, redeployment, qualification, voluntary measures, and management/owner contributions.

If your numbers only work by closing plants, then you are asking the workforce to pay for a management strategy failure by destroying livelihoods and regional industrial capacity. That is not restructuring. That is demolition.

## To investors

You are right about one thing: cosmetic compromise is useless. But do not confuse “serious” with “destructive.”

A company like this does not create value by provoking a major labour conflict, breaking trust, and weakening the industrial base that makes the brand possible. If you push for closures now, you may get a short-term accounting win and a long-term strategic loss.

The expert’s ranges are not proof that closures are needed. They are only proof that management has not yet presented a detailed, credible alternative. Until they do, closures remain a choice, not a necessity.

## To the family holding

You say you do not prefer closures, but will not rule them out. That is exactly the problem. Keeping closures on the table while asking labour to trust the process is not a neutral position; it is pressure.

If you want a negotiated settlement, then you must force management to put forward a real non-closure package first, not use closure threats as the default benchmark. You have influence. Use it to stop management from presenting irreversible damage as inevitability.

## To the state government

You are right to reject a one-sided solution. But we need more than words about regional responsibility. Use your blocking power. The state must not become a witness to the dismantling of German sites under the banner of competitiveness.

If you stand for industrial stability, then you must insist on:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- binding site protection,
- and a restructuring plan built around employment security.

Otherwise “regional responsibility” becomes a slogan.

## To the trade union

We are aligned on the core red lines:
- no plant closures
- no compulsory redundancies
- no dismantling of the collective framework

That remains the correct line. I agree with you that temporary working-time reductions, voluntary instruments, redeployment, qualification, and a limited workforce contribution can be discussed — but only if they are tied to binding guarantees. And those guarantees must be real, not temporary escape hatches.

However, I want to sharpen one point: if the current debate is turned into a numbers game with closure as the reference case, management will always argue that only closure “saves enough.” That framing is exactly what we must refuse. We should not validate the idea that job destruction is the baseline from which everything else is measured.

## Our position

The works council’s position remains unchanged and clear:

1. **No German plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **No weakening of co-determination**
4. **No open-ended insecurity for the workforce**
5. **A negotiated restructuring only if jobs and sites are protected**

## What we are prepared to discuss

We remain open to a serious alternative package, but only within those boundaries:
- working-time flexibility
- voluntary departures only
- redeployment across sites
- qualification and transformation measures
- temporary, strictly limited workforce contributions
- management restraint
- owner-side responsibility
- binding investment commitments

## What we reject

We reject:
- closures “if needed”
- forced layoffs “as a last resort”
- permanent wage degradation
- a deal that lets management keep strategic freedom while the workforce loses security
- any attempt to hollow out the job-security framework

## What is still missing

Management has still not answered the basic question:

**How much of the gap can be closed without closures, without forced layoffs, and without destroying trust?**

Before anyone talks about inevitability, I want the actual numbers on:
- savings from working-time changes,
- savings from voluntary exits and redeployment,
- savings from overhead simplification,
- management contributions,
- owner contributions,
- and the investment effects.

If management cannot produce that, then it is not entitled to demand sacrifices from the workforce.

## My bottom line

I will not soften my position under pressure:
- no closures,
- no forced redundancies,
- no dismantling of the social contract.

If management continues to push closure scenarios as leverage, then it will continue to face resistance, including industrial action. We are not here to bless a restructuring that destroys the very workforce that built this company.

If the others truly want a durable solution, then they should stop treating plant closures as the starting point and start treating **employment security as a condition of any legitimate deal**.
