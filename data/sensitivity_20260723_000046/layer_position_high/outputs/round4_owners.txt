## To management
I agree with the diagnosis: this is a structural competitiveness problem, not a temporary wobble. I also agree that cosmetic measures would be value-destructive.

Where I do **not** agree is your continued framing of plant closures and forced redundancies as if they were the natural baseline. They are **not** the baseline. They are a contingency, and you have still not shown that they are necessary.

The scenario work now on the table says something important:
- a **no-closure** package can work if the gap is moderate,
- a **deep hybrid** is the most plausible negotiated route,
- closure-led restructuring has the highest conflict and execution risk.

That means you do **not** yet have a proven case for closure-first logic. You have a case for hard restructuring, yes. But not for claiming that footprint reduction must happen before the non-closure route is properly tested.

What I support:
- materially lower cost base,
- capacity adjustment,
- productivity gains,
- overhead simplification,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary workforce contribution,
- management restraint,
- and binding investment support.

What I will not support:
- a closure-first strategy without proof,
- forced layoffs as leverage,
- or a settlement that triggers prolonged labour conflict and destroys value.

If the plant-level numbers prove that non-closure and hybrid measures cannot close the gap durably, then say so clearly and bring the evidence. Until then, closures remain a last resort, not the starting point.

## To the works council and union
I agree with you on three points:
- a cosmetic deal is useless,
- one-sided burden shifting onto labour is unacceptable,
- co-determination cannot be hollowed out.

I also agree that workforce contributions, if needed, must be **time-bounded, quantified, and matched by management and owner-side contributions**.

Where I disagree is your attempt to rule out closures as an absolute principle regardless of economics. I cannot pre-commit the family holding to preserving every site no matter what the numbers show. That would not be stewardship; it would be denial.

Your position becomes credible only if the alternative package is genuinely deep and durable:
- working-time measures,
- voluntary departures,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- productivity improvements,
- temporary concessions,
- management restraint,
- and real owner-side support.

If that closes the gap, excellent. If it does not, then I will not pretend the economics are different.

## To the state government
I agree that regional responsibility matters and that the company must remain a real industrial anchor. I also agree that no outcome should gut co-determination or turn the state into a spectator.

Where I disagree is any implication that the state should pre-emptively block all footprint adjustment before the plant-level economics are tested. That would substitute politics for industrial reality.

The state should insist on:
- quantified comparisons,
- fair burden sharing,
- and a negotiated outcome that preserves long-term industrial strength.

What the state should **not** do is lock in a structure that may simply postpone the crisis and deepen value erosion.

## To investors
You are right that a weak compromise would be irresponsible. A cosmetic deal that leaves margins structurally impaired would not protect value.

But I also caution against treating the harshest solution as automatically the best one. In this governance structure, a closure-led fight can easily destroy more value than it creates through:
- strikes,
- veto conflict,
- delay,
- reputational damage,
- and operational disruption.

So yes, demand hard numbers. But do not assume maximal disruption equals maximal discipline.

## My position
The family holding will back a **hard, quantified, durable restructuring** that restores competitiveness and margins. That means:

1. **We need the plant-level scenario comparison now**
   - no-closure,
   - deep hybrid,
   - closure-led.
   - with annual savings, one-off costs, employment impact, implementation risk, and return-on-sales effect.

2. **Our preferred route is a deep hybrid**
   - because it is the most plausible balance of savings, feasibility, and stability.

3. **I will not rule out footprint reduction in advance**
   - but neither will I endorse it without proof that the non-closure route fails.

4. **Forced redundancies are a last resort**
   - because value destruction from conflict can outweigh the savings.
