I will respond plainly.

## To the works council
We are aligned on the essentials:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no hollowing out of co-determination,
- no open-ended insecurity,
- and no one-sided workforce bill.

I also agree with your central point: the numbers so far do **not** prove that closures are necessary. They show that a **strong no-closure or hybrid package can be viable** if the true gap is in the moderate range and if management and owners also contribute materially.

Where I differ slightly is on the tactical question of how to use the scenario work. I do **not** think it is enough to simply say “no closures” and stop there. We need to force management to prove, in hard numbers, that non-closure measures are insufficient. Until they do that, closures remain a bargaining claim, not a justified necessity.

But your bottom line is right: a staged process must **not** become a back door to later plant destruction. If a “decision gate” means “take the cuts now, then reopen closure threats later,” that is unacceptable.

## To management
Your response still does not meet the burden of proof.

You keep saying:
- the cost base is structurally too high,
- capacity reduction is necessary,
- and closures must remain on the table.

I do not deny the structural problem. I deny your leap from that problem to **plant closures and forced redundancies**.

The scenario model shows:
- **No-closure** can plausibly work if the gap is moderate.
- **Hybrid** is the most plausible negotiated route.
- **Closure-led** is the highest-risk path, with serious conflict and execution costs.

That means you do **not** yet have a case for making closures the default reference point. You have a case for:
1. quantifying the exact gap,
2. showing what management-side savings you will actually deliver,
3. showing what owner-side support is real,
4. and then testing whether the workforce contribution, together with non-closure measures, can close the remainder.

What you still have not answered is the central question:
**What is the minimum annual run-rate savings gap after all non-destructive measures, and why can’t a negotiated package close it?**

Until that is answered, closure talk remains pressure, not proof.

And let me be clear: if management insists on treating closure as the fallback before non-closure routes are fully tested, then it is pushing the conflict, not solving the problem.

## To the state government
I support your insistence that the process be quantified, fair, and durable. I also support your refusal to let the workforce carry the burden alone.

But I want to sharpen the line: the state must not treat “footprint reduction” as a neutral option in the abstract. In a co-determined German industrial company, closures are not a technical detail — they are a direct attack on jobs, trust, regional industrial capacity, and bargaining peace.

So yes, test the numbers. But the default should be:
- no closures,
- no forced redundancies,
- and a negotiated package built around employment security.

If the state’s blocking power means anything, it should mean that management cannot use ambiguity to force irreversible damage.

## To the family holding
You say closures are not preferred but not ruled out. That is exactly the kind of ambiguity that makes negotiations unstable.

I understand that you do not want to pre-commit to an outcome that might be economically impossible. Fair enough. But then use your influence where it matters:
- force management to present the **real non-closure package first**,
- define the own-side contribution,
- and stop treating closures as the silent benchmark.

What we need from you is not a refusal to think, but a refusal to use closure threat as an evergreen bargaining tool.

## To the institutional investors
You are right that a cosmetic compromise is worthless. On that, we agree.

Where I disagree is your tendency to treat the harshest restructuring as if it were automatically the most value-creating one. The scenario work does **not** support that as a general rule. It explicitly says:
- closure-led restructuring has the highest disruption risk,
- realized benefits may be reduced by strikes, legal delay, and reputational damage,
- and a deep hybrid is often the best all-round tradeoff.

So if you want value, then support the **deep hybrid** route, not maximal disruption by default.

## Where the union stands
I want no ambiguity here.

The union remains committed to:
- **no plant closures**
- **no compulsory redundancies**
- **no weakening of the collective wage framework**
- **no open-ended insecurity**
- **no one-sided burden shifting onto employees**

That is not soft rhetoric. That is the bargaining line.

At the same time, I remain prepared to negotiate a serious, quantified burden-sharing package that includes:
- temporary wage restraint,
- bonus forgoing,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary departures,
- redeployment,
- qualification and transformation measures,
- productivity improvements,
- and time-bounded employee contributions,

**provided that** management and owners also contribute and that jobs and sites are protected.

That remains the only fair basis for a settlement.

## What I will not accept
I will not accept:
- permanent wage cuts,
- forced layoffs dressed up as “last resort,”
- plant closures as leverage,
- or a “phased” process that quietly sets up later shutdowns.

If management wants an agreement, it must stop demanding that the workforce underwrite the restructuring alone.

We now have enough to say this much:
- **A no-closure package is not ruled out.**
- **A deep hybrid is the most plausible negotiated settlement.**
- **Closure-led restructuring is the highest-risk option and not justified without proof.**

But we still do **not** have the plant-level, product-mix, and labor-cost detail needed to answer the decisive question: how much gap remains after realistic management, owner, and workforce contributions, and whether that gap truly requires footprint reduction.
