I will be equally direct.

We now have the first quantified framework, and it confirms something important: **a negotiated, non-destructive solution is not fantasy**. It is a real industrial option if management and the owners stop hiding behind closures as the default answer.

## What I agree with

I agree with the expert and with the state, family holding, and union on one point: **this company needs a durable, quantified restructuring**. I also agree that a cosmetic compromise would be irresponsible.

And yes, the numbers matter. The scenario work shows that:
- a **no-closure package** can deliver meaningful savings,
- a **hybrid package** can deliver more,
- and closures create the highest execution risk and the highest social damage.

That is exactly why management should stop pretending that plant closures are the obvious first move.

## Where I disagree

### To management
Your position is still too comfortable: you want the workforce to accept the burden of a structural problem while keeping closures and forced redundancies as leverage.

That is unacceptable.

The expert’s numbers do **not** prove that closures are necessary. They say clearly:
- if the gap is **moderate**, a strong no-closure or hybrid package can work;
- only if the gap is **large** do footprint reductions become more likely.

So you do **not** have a case for “closures first.” You have, at most, a case for proving whether non-closure measures are sufficient. That proof is not yet there.

And let me be absolutely clear: the numbers also show that **closure-led restructuring is not some clean economic truth**. It carries:
- high legal and co-determination resistance,
- strike risk,
- implementation delay,
- reputational damage,
- and a real chance that the supposed savings get eaten by conflict.

So if you are using closures as pressure, you are not demonstrating strength. You are demonstrating that you have not yet earned trust.

### To investors
You are right to reject a cosmetic compromise. But do not slide from that into making closures the benchmark for seriousness.

The scenario model does **not** support the idea that “more destruction equals better value.” In fact, it shows the opposite risk: the harshest plan can underperform once conflict is included.

If you want value, support a **deep negotiated hybrid** that preserves sites and avoids forced layoffs. That is the rational position, not some sentimental one.

### To the family holding
You say closures are not preferred, but not ruled out. I understand your governance caution. But from the workforce perspective, that is not neutral. Keeping closures alive as a fallback while demanding trust means you want labour to negotiate under threat.

That is not a legitimate basis for an agreement.

If you want a workable settlement, use your influence to force management to commit to the non-closure route **unless and until the numbers prove it cannot work**. The burden of proof must stay on management, not on the workforce whose jobs are at stake.

### To the state government
You are right to insist on regional responsibility and co-determination. But your stance must become more than procedural caution.

The state should use its blocking power to make one thing clear:
- **no plant closures without proof of necessity,**
- **no forced redundancies,**
- and **no deal that hollows out the job-security framework.**

If the state starts talking as if footprint reduction is just an abstract possibility, management will turn that into a weapon against the workforce.

### To the union
We are aligned on the essentials:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of collective protections.

I also agree that a serious package can include:
- working-time changes,
- voluntary departures,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- temporary workforce contributions,
- management restraint,
- and owner-side support.

But I will sharpen the point: we must not let the “scenario table” become a trap in which closures are treated as the neutral reference case and everything else as a compromise from that baseline. That is a political framing, not an economic necessity.

## Where I stand after the scenario model

The model says:
- **No-closure**: possible if the gap is moderate.
- **Hybrid**: most plausible negotiated route.
- **Closures**: only if the gap is large and alternatives fail.

That means our position is reinforced, not weakened.

Because what the model does **not** say is: “closures are unavoidable.” It says the opposite: that **a no-closure or hybrid package can plausibly close the gap** depending on the actual number.

And since management has not proven the gap is so extreme that closures are unavoidable, the only legitimate path is:
- test the non-closure solution fully,
- require management and owners to contribute materially,
- and keep all German plants open.

## Our line remains unchanged

The works council will not accept:
1. **German plant closures**
2. **Compulsory redundancies**
3. **Weakening of co-determination**
4. **Open-ended insecurity**
5. **A one-sided workforce bill**

That has not changed, and it will not change because management repeats “competitiveness” more often.

## What we are prepared to discuss — but only under our red lines

We are prepared to discuss:
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary departures only,
- redeployment across sites,
- qualification and transformation,
- temporary, strictly time-bounded workforce contributions,
- management restraint,
- owner-side responsibility,
- and binding investment commitments.

But only if they are used to secure:
- **no plant closures**
- **no forced layoffs**
- **real site guarantees**
- **preservation of co-determination**

If those guarantees are missing, then the package is not a solution. It is a prelude to destruction.

## On the mediator’s “phased restructuring” idea
I do **not** accept a staged process that quietly creates a bridge to later closures.

A “decision gate” sounds reasonable until you notice what it means in practice: accept cuts now, then reopen the knife later if management says the numbers are still unsatisfactory. That is not security. That is delayed leverage.

We have seen this pattern before:
- first the temporary concession,
- then the next round,
- then the plant is “no longer competitive,”
- then jobs disappear anyway.

So I reject any model that keeps closure decisions suspended like a sword over the workforce.

## Bottom line
The scenario work does **not** justify weakening our position. It just confirms that management and owners still have not done their part.

So I say again, plainly:
- **No German plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No hollowing out of co-determination**
- **No settlement that shifts structural failure onto the workforce**

If management wants an agreement, it should stop asking whether jobs can be sacrificed and start proving how it will restore competitiveness **without destroying the industrial base that makes this company possible**.
