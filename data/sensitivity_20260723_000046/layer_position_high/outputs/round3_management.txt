## To the Works Council
You keep saying “no closures, no forced redundancies, no weakening of co-determination.” I hear the red lines. But I do **not** accept the conclusion that they can be guaranteed up front while we still fix a structurally uncompetitive cost base.

You are trying to separate “capacity reduction” from “site rationalization” as if the former can always be achieved without the latter. That may be politically attractive, but it is not a credible industrial answer unless you can show, in numbers, that it closes the gap durably.

What I agree with:
- A cosmetic deal would be useless.
- Voluntary instruments, redeployment, working-time flexibility, and qualification are part of the solution.
- No one benefits from prolonged conflict.

What I reject:
- A blanket no-closure commitment before we know whether the economics allow it.
- A guarantee regime that locks in today’s cost structure through 2030.
- The idea that management should first rule out the only measures that can reliably remove fixed cost.

You keep asking for management-side and owner-side contributions. Fine — those exist, and we are prepared to discuss them. But they cannot substitute for a meaningful workforce contribution if the company is to restore competitiveness. We need a package that actually changes the cost base.

## To the Trade Union
I agree with you on one point: a purely destructive approach would be bad management. But you are still treating “no closures” as if it were the same thing as a serious restructuring. It is not.

You say the burden must be shared and time-limited. On that, we are not far apart in principle. But “time-limited” does not solve the structural problem unless the contribution is large enough and linked to measurable capacity and cost changes. Temporary bonus restraint and short-term concessions are not enough if fixed costs remain too high.

What I disagree with is your attempt to pre-empt the outcome by demanding:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no permanent cost reset,
- and binding guarantees first.

That sequencing does not work. The guarantees have to be the result of a credible restructuring, not a substitute for it.

## To the State Government
I agree that governance legitimacy matters. I agree that a value-destructive conflict would be harmful. I agree that the company is not a balance-sheet exercise alone.

But I do **not** agree that the state’s blocking position should be used to freeze structural adjustment until a no-closure solution is proven by others. If you want to defend long-term industrial value, then you must also accept that preserving all sites at all costs is not the same thing as protecting value.

You are right to ask for quantified comparisons. I support that. But the answer may still be that some footprint reduction is necessary. If so, state influence should be used to secure the best possible negotiated outcome — not to deny economic reality.

## To the Controlling Family Holding
I agree with you more than with most others here: closures should not be the starting point. But I do not accept your implication that they should be pushed to the margins unless absolutely unavoidable.

“Not preferred” is not the same as “not needed.” We need the family to back a process that is hard-headed, not one that becomes hostage to the wish that a hybrid package will somehow magically eliminate the cost gap.

You say management must prove necessity. Fair enough — and that is exactly why I want the scenario work accelerated. But if the numbers show that non-closure measures do not close the gap, then we must not pretend otherwise.

## To the Institutional Investors
You are right to demand numbers, durability, and return improvement. I support that fully.

What I do not accept is the notion that value only follows the harshest possible restructuring. A hybrid package can be value-accretive if it is sufficiently deep. But if we are forced into a choice between:
- a weak no-closure compromise, or
- a deeper package with some footprint adjustment,

then I will choose the deeper package.

Because the purpose here is not to avoid discomfort. It is to restore competitiveness, cut overcapacity and labour costs, and fund the company’s future through 2030.

## My position, plainly
I remain committed to a **durable structural reset**. That means:

- a materially lower cost base,
- real capacity reduction,
- productivity improvement,
- and a credible path back to the brand’s return-on-sales target.

I am **not** prepared to lock in a settlement that preserves all current German sites regardless of economics. That would be a delay, not a solution.

I am **open** to a negotiated hybrid package if it produces the savings we need. That package may include:
- working-time changes,
- voluntary departures,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- temporary employee contributions,
- management restraint,
- and owner-side support.

But I need one thing very clearly stated: if the numbers show that these measures are insufficient, then plant rationalization stays on the table. I will not pretend otherwise.
