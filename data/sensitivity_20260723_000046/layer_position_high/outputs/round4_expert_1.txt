## What the current exchange implies, analytically

The parties are converging on the same basic modeling frame:
1. **No-closure / no-forced-redundancy package**
2. **Deep hybrid package**
3. **Closure-led package**

The real question is not whether restructuring is needed, but whether the **residual cost gap after non-closure levers** is small enough to avoid footprint reduction.

## The key modeling takeaway

Based on typical industrial restructuring economics for a large German automotive manufacturer, the scenarios usually break down like this:

### 1) No-closure package
Can work if the gap is **moderate**, typically through:
- voluntary exits
- working-time reduction/flexibility
- wage restraint / temporary contributions
- redeployment
- overhead simplification
- procurement / logistics / efficiency gains
- management restraint

**Indicative effect:** often enough to close roughly **30–60%** of the structural gap, sometimes more if headcount can be reduced via attrition and if capacity is underutilized enough to permit shift reductions.

### 2) Deep hybrid package
Adds:
- line specialization
- shift elimination
- partial site downsizing
- product reallocations
- selective footprint consolidation without immediate full closure

**Indicative effect:** often closes **60–85%** of the gap, and is usually the most realistic negotiated middle path.

### 3) Closure-led package
Delivers the highest gross savings, but also:
- high one-off closure costs
- disruption risk
- relocation / requalification costs
- legal and industrial-action delays
- possible loss of execution momentum

In many German co-determined settings, the **realized net benefit** can be materially below the headline savings because of conflict and delay.

# Practical quantified ranges

Without plant-level data, I can only give defensible ranges, not a single point estimate.

## A. Annual run-rate savings needed
For a large German passenger-car footprint under pressure, the annual savings requirement to restore competitiveness is commonly in the rough range of:

- **Low/moderate gap:** ~€300m–€700m per year
- **Material gap:** ~€700m–€1.5bn per year
- **Severe gap:** above ~€1.5bn per year

If management is signaling “structural overcapacity” and “well above target German factory costs,” the case is often in the **material gap** range rather than the low one.

## B. One-off restructuring costs
Typical one-off costs by option:

- **No-closure:** ~€200m–€700m  
- **Deep hybrid:** ~€500m–€1.5bn  
- **Closure-led:** ~€1.0bn–€3.0bn+  

Why closure is expensive:
- severance
- decommissioning
- write-downs
- relocation
- supply-chain disruption
- tooling transfer
- parallel-run costs

## C. Employment impact
A large German carmaker restructuring of this type often involves:

- **No-closure:** ~5%–10% workforce reduction, mostly via voluntary exits / attrition
- **Deep hybrid:** ~10%–20%
- **Closure-led:** ~20%+ in affected footprint, depending on the number and size of plants

These are broad ranges, not predictions.

# What is most plausible?

## If the gap is moderate
A **no-closure package** can plausibly work if:
- headcount can fall materially without forced layoffs
- shift patterns can be changed
- product complexity can be reduced
- management and owner contributions are real, not symbolic

In that case, the residual gap after non-closure levers may be small enough to avoid footprint reduction.

## If the gap is large
A **hybrid package** is usually the best economic compromise:
- it preserves more of the footprint than closure-led restructuring
- but still generates structural savings beyond labor concessions alone

If the gap is too large for the hybrid package, then footprint reduction becomes more likely from a strictly industrial economics standpoint.

# The main sensitivity: implementation slippage

The biggest risk to the non-closure path is not the headline design; it is **execution**.

A model should test:
- strike days
- delayed start of savings
- lower-than-planned voluntary exit uptake
- slower redeployment
- weaker productivity gains
- owner-side support being smaller than assumed

A common sensitivity is that **20%–30% slippage in implementation can erase most of the advantage of a weak compromise**.

That is why a negotiated package must be:
- quantified
- time-bounded
- enforceable
- and tied to milestones

---

# Minimum conditions for a credible no-closure alternative

A no-closure alternative becomes credible only if it delivers all four of these:

1. **Structural savings**, not just temporary wage relief  
2. **Material headcount reduction** without forced layoffs  
3. **Meaningful productivity / overhead simplification**  
4. **A verified residual gap that is small enough** to keep margins on target  

If the residual gap remains above roughly **€300m–€500m per year**, depending on the company’s margin target and EV investment needs, then no-closure alone is often too weak.

# My analytical conclusion

From an industrial restructuring standpoint:

- The employee side is right that **closures are not automatically necessary**.
- Management is right that **non-closure measures must be shown to close the gap**, not just asserted.
- The most economically plausible negotiated route is usually a **deep hybrid**, not a pure no-closure package and not a closure-first shock plan.
- Whether all German sites can stay open depends on the **actual residual annual gap after management, owner, and workforce contributions**.
