Below is a neutral industrial-finance and operations assessment of the **tradeoff between a non-closure package and a closure-led restructuring** for the scenario as given.

## 1) Bottom line

From a restructuring economics standpoint:

- A **closure-led package** is usually the strongest tool for **permanent fixed-cost reduction and capacity removal**.
- A **non-closure package** can work only if it is **large, durable, and enforceable** enough to remove a comparable amount of cost and capacity through other means.
- In large automotive networks, **labor-cost concessions alone are rarely enough** if the core problem is **structural overcapacity plus high fixed site cost**.
- The decisive question is not whether closures are unpleasant, but whether the non-closure path can produce **equivalent annual savings, equivalent capacity reduction, and equivalent durability through 2030**.

On the governance side, the case’s German co-determination and special shareholder framework means:
- **plant closures/relocations require an exceptionally high approval threshold**,
- employee-side stakeholders have an **effective veto** through the supervisory board,
- and a negotiated solution is materially more realistic than a forced one.

## 2) Economic comparison

### A. Closure-led restructuring
**Typical strengths**
- Removes **fixed cost** most decisively.
- Cuts **installed capacity** rather than merely reducing its cost.
- Improves utilization at remaining plants if volumes are transferred well.
- Creates a cleaner long-term footprint.

**Typical costs**
- High **one-time restructuring charges**:
  - severance / social plan costs,
  - decommissioning,
  - asset impairments,
  - transfer and relocation costs,
  - possible remediation.
- Execution risk:
  - ramp-up issues at receiving plants,
  - supplier disruption,
  - quality risk during transfer,
  - industrial conflict.

**Economic logic**
If the company has true structural overcapacity, closure is often the most direct way to restore margin because it removes overhead that continues even when output falls.

### B. Non-closure package
This can include:
- working-time flexibility,
- reduced bonuses or temporary wage concessions,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- shifts/product reallocation,
- temporary capacity idling,
- management compensation restraint,
- owner support,
- product/investment commitments.

**Typical strengths**
- Lower immediate disruption.
- Better labor-relations compatibility.
- Can improve cash flow fast if concessions are front-loaded.
- Can preserve skills and industrial base.

**Typical limitations**
- Often reduces **labor cost** more than **fixed site cost**.
- If plants remain open, overhead and underutilization can continue.
- Savings may expire when temporary agreements lapse.
- If overcapacity is real, the company can end up with a smaller cost relief than needed.

**Economic logic**
A non-closure package is strongest when the problem is mostly **labor cost**, not network size. If the problem is **too many plants for the market**, it usually needs permanent capacity removal as well.

## 3) Site-level savings: what each path usually changes

### Closure-led path
At site level, closure can remove:
- plant management and support overhead,
- utilities and maintenance baselines,
- logistics and internal handling costs,
- duplicated planning and quality functions,
- depreciation burden if assets are written down or retired,
- part of salaried headcount.

It can also reduce direct labor if production is transferred rather than replaced.

### Non-closure path
At site level, non-closure measures usually reduce:
- direct labor cost per vehicle,
- overtime and premium pay,
- some indirect labor cost through attrition,
- short-term cash outflow.

But if the site remains open, you often still carry:
- much of the fixed overhead,
- a substantial portion of maintenance and support cost,
- underutilized assets,
- and residual network complexity.

### Practical implication
A non-closure package may save a lot in gross labor spend, but still fail to eliminate enough **site-level fixed cost** to restore competitiveness if the plant is materially underloaded.

## 4) Implementation cost and timing

### Closure-led restructuring
**Upfront cost**
- Usually highest.

**Timing**
- Often **12–36 months** to realize most savings, sometimes longer.
- Not “instant,” because transfer, negotiation, customer continuity, and shutdown sequencing take time.

**Risk profile**
- High short-term disruption, but stronger permanent cleanup if executed successfully.

### Non-closure package
**Upfront cost**
- Lower than closure, at least initially.

**Timing**
- Faster to implement for:
  - wage restraint,
  - bonus cuts,
  - hiring freeze,
  - working-time changes,
  - voluntary exits.
- Savings can show in the same fiscal year.

**Risk profile**
- Lower immediate disruption, but risk of under-delivery if the measures are temporary or too small.

## 5) Employment effects

### Closure-led
- Permanent loss of jobs at the affected site.
- Potential partial preservation via transfers, but usually not full.
- Can still result in group-wide headcount reduction if capacity is genuinely removed.
- Strong local social and political impact.

### Non-closure
- Better chance to preserve sites and employment.
- Can still produce significant headcount reduction through:
  - voluntary exits,
  - retirement,
  - attrition,
  - redeployment.
- More compatible with industrial peace.

### Core tradeoff
If the company needs a large reduction in labor and capacity, then preserving all sites usually means:
- either much deeper concessions elsewhere,
- or a longer period of weaker economics.

## 6) Transition risks

### Closure-led risks
- strikes and work stoppages,
- supplier instability,
- know-how loss,
- launch delays,
- reputational damage,
- potential spillover into other plants.

### Non-closure risks
- agreement may be too weak to solve the cost problem,
- temporary measures may later expire,
- repeated bargaining cycles can re-open the issue,
- capacity remains too high, so the company continues to bleed margin.

### Industrial-finance judgment
Closure is politically and operationally riskier in the short run.  
Non-closure is economically riskier if it does not truly remove enough cost and capacity.

## 7) Governance and legal constraints in this case

I’ll stay at the level of general governance mechanics, not specific legal advice.

### What matters here
The scenario says:
- the supervisory board is **20 seats, split 50/50** between shareholder and employee representatives;
- a **two-thirds majority** is required for plant closures or relocations;
- the workforce therefore has an **effective veto**;
- a controlling family holding company has majority voting influence;
- a regional state government has a **blocking-minority stake** and board representation under a special statute.

### Practical consequence
A unilateral closure-led restructuring is **governance-fragile**:
- it likely cannot pass without employee-side consent and broader stakeholder alignment,
- and it would face very high conflict costs if pushed against co-determination.

### Labor relations consequence
Once the collective-bargaining peace obligation has ended, strike risk becomes real.  
That does not change the governance veto structure, but it increases the cost of any coercive strategy.

### Result
The institutional setup makes a **negotiated package** much more feasible than an imposed closure program.

## 8) What a credible non-closure package would need

If management wants to avoid closures and still achieve a serious restructuring, the package must likely include all of the following:

1. **Large enough capacity reduction**
   - even without formal closure, the network must shed output and overhead.

2. **Permanent or semi-permanent headcount reduction**
   - through voluntary exits, retirement, attrition, and redeployment.

3. **Material labor-cost contribution**
   - temporary wage restraint, bonus reduction, or working-time changes.

4. **Site-specific productivity improvements**
   - line balancing, automation, process simplification, indirect headcount reduction.

5. **Binding guarantees in return**
   - site and employment guarantees,
   - investment and product commitments,
   - clear expiry and reversal terms.

6. **Burden-sharing from management and owners**
   - visible executive restraint and governance support.

Without these, a “no closure” package is likely to underperform.

## 9) Reasoned scenario view

### Scenario 1: Closure-led
Most likely outcome:
- highest durable annual savings,
- largest capacity cleanup,
- highest political/labor conflict,
- highest one-time charges.

### Scenario 2: Non-closure only
Most likely outcome:
- lower conflict,
- lower upfront charges,
- weaker fixed-cost reduction,
- savings may be insufficient if overcapacity is structural.

### Scenario 3: Hybrid package
This is often the most realistic in German automotive restructurings:
- no immediate plant closure,
- but meaningful capacity reduction,
- some labor concessions,
- voluntary exits and redeployment,
- targeted site adjustments,
- binding investment commitments,
- staged review with hard triggers.

This can be the best compromise if closure is legally and politically blocked, but it must still be large enough to matter.

## 10) Quantitative framing, with uncertainty

Because the case provides no plant-level financials, exact numbers are not knowable. But as a general industrial benchmark:

- A **closure-led** action can deliver **high-hundreds of millions to low billions of euros per year** in durable group-level savings if a major plant or equivalent capacity is removed.
- A **non-closure package** can also generate material savings, but more often in the **lower hundreds of millions to mid-hundreds of millions annually** unless it includes substantial permanent headcount reduction and real capacity trimming.

That said, these are only broad ranges. Actual savings depend on:
- plant utilization,
- fixed cost per site,
- product mix,
- labor agreements,
- severance terms,
- and transfer costs.

## 11) Final assessment

### If the company’s problem is mainly labor cost:
A non-closure package could plausibly work.

### If the company’s problem is structural overcapacity plus high fixed cost:
A closure-led or closure-equivalent package is usually economically stronger.

### In this case:
Given the described governance vetoes, co-determination, and special shareholder constraints, **a negotiated hybrid is the most realistic path**. A pure closure-led strategy may be economically persuasive in abstract, but institutionally hard to execute. A pure non-closure strategy may be politically easier, but economically may not go far enough.

So the key test is simple:
**Can the non-closure package remove enough cost and capacity to match the financial effect of closure?**
If not, it is a delay, not a substitute.
