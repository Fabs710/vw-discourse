I’ll be equally direct.

## Where I agree

I agree with all parties on one core point: **a cosmetic deal is useless**.

I also agree that:
- industrial peace has economic value,
- a one-sided solution would be destabilizing,
- temporary employee contributions must be matched by broader burden-sharing,
- and any restructuring has to be measurable and durable, not rhetorical.

I also accept the experts’ point that the debate must be grounded in numbers, not slogans.

## Where I disagree — clearly

### 1) On “no closures” as a red line
The works council and union keep presenting “no German plant closures” as if it were a legitimate starting condition for solving a structural cost problem. It is not.

If the network is too large for the market, then **capacity has to come out**. If you forbid closures, then you must show how you will remove equivalent fixed cost and capacity through other means. So far, I have heard principles, not a package that closes the gap.

I am not interested in preserving every site for its own sake. I am interested in restoring a viable business.

### 2) On compulsory redundancies
I am open to avoiding forced layoffs **if** we can achieve the necessary headcount reduction through voluntary exits, attrition, redeployment, and internal capacity changes at sufficient scale. But I will not accept a package that rules out forced reductions in advance while failing to deliver durable savings.

If voluntary measures plus flexibility plus temporary concessions are enough, then show it. If not, we need stronger structural instruments.

### 3) On the claim that management is using closures as mere intimidation
That is not fair.

We are using closures as a serious restructuring option because the economics are serious. This is not theater. German factory costs are above target; the market is shrinking; the brand is missing its return-on-sales target; and we need funds for 2030 product and technology investment.

If we end up with a “deal” that leaves the cost base uncompetitive, then we will have protected the structure and sacrificed the future.

### 4) On burden-sharing
The union and works council are right that employees should not carry the burden alone. I agree. But burden-sharing must be real, not symbolic.

That means:
- meaningful labour-cost relief,
- capacity reduction,
- productivity improvement,
- and durable structural savings.

It also means management, owners, and governance must contribute visibly. On that point, I accept the challenge. But I will not let that become an excuse for avoiding hard changes on the shop floor and in the footprint.

## On the expert assessments

The experts have been useful because they made the central issue plain:

- **closure-led restructuring** is usually stronger on fixed-cost removal and capacity reduction,
- **internal adjustment** can help, but often under-delivers if the problem is structural overcapacity,
- a **hybrid** is institutionally more realistic in this governance setup.

That is exactly the right framing.

But let me be clear: “hybrid” only matters if it produces **real** savings. It is not acceptable if it becomes a code word for keeping all sites open while asking for small, temporary concessions that expire before the economics improve.

A hybrid package must still be big enough to matter.

## What I need from the employee side

If you want to avoid closures, then stop answering with red lines alone and bring a **site-by-site quantified counterproposal** that covers:

- annual savings by measure,
- one-time costs,
- capacity removed,
- headcount impact,
- duration through 2030,
- and how the package closes the ROS gap.

And I do not mean a loose outline. I mean a package that can be stress-tested against the actual cost base.

That package must include hard answers on:
- how much labor cost comes out,
- how much overhead comes out,
- how much capacity is actually taken off the network,
- and what happens if the savings fall short.

Without that, “no closures” is not a solution. It is a refusal to choose.
