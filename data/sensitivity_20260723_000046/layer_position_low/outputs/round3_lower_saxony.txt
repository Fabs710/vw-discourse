I will be direct.

The state government does **not** accept a restructuring logic that starts from plant closures and forced redundancies as if those were the natural first response. We recognize the company needs a durable competitiveness reset. We do **not** recognize the claim that this can only be achieved by gutting German sites and treating co-determination as an obstacle.

## Where I agree

I agree with management, investors, and the experts on the basic diagnosis:
- the current cost structure is too high,
- the footprint must be made more efficient,
- symbolic measures are not enough,
- and the company must restore profitability to secure future investment.

I also agree with the works council and union on a second point:
- the workforce did not create this crisis alone,
- industrial peace has real economic value,
- and any employee contribution must be temporary, limited, and matched by real burden-sharing above the shop floor.

## Where I disagree

### 1) With management: closures are not the default answer
Management keeps saying closures are a serious instrument, not a threat. But in practice you are using them as the baseline against which every alternative is measured. That is not acceptable.

A closure-led strategy may be economically clean on paper, but politically, socially, and institutionally it is highly destructive. More importantly, it is not yet proven that the less destructive options have been exhausted.

If you want the state to take seriously any reduction package, then come with a **site-by-site quantified case**:
- what savings each measure delivers,
- what fixed cost is actually removed,
- what capacity comes out,
- what one-time charges arise,
- and why a non-closure package cannot bridge the gap.

Until that is shown, I will not treat closure as justified.

### 2) With the union and works council: “no closures” is a position, not yet a complete plan
I support the red line against compulsory redundancies and the instinct to protect sites. But I also need to be frank: if the answer is only “no closures, no layoffs,” then the company is still left with the cost problem.

So I need more than resistance. I need a credible alternative that is economically serious:
- capacity reduction without plant closure,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment and qualification,
- productivity gains,
- management restraint,
- and owner-side support.

If that package can truly deliver durable savings through 2030, I will support it. If it cannot, then we are just postponing conflict.

### 3) With investors: protect value, yes — but not by destroying the regional base
You are right that the company must restore margins and avoid open-ended weakness. But long-term value is not protected by permanently weakening the company’s regional anchor role.

A strategy that erodes trust, industrial peace, skills, and legitimacy can destroy value as surely as inefficient plants can. The question is not whether the company should adjust. It must. The question is whether the adjustment preserves enough of the productive base to keep the company strong in Germany.

## What I will support

I am prepared to support a negotiated restructuring if it includes all of the following:

- no compulsory redundancies,
- no plant closures as the starting point,
- a quantified capacity-reduction plan,
- temporary and limited employee contributions,
- management compensation restraint,
- owner/governance contribution,
- binding investment and product commitments for German sites,
- and a real co-determination role in implementation.

That is the kind of package that can protect the company’s long-term health **and** preserve its role as a regional economic anchor.

## What I will not support

I will not support:
- a fait accompli on closures,
- a one-sided labor cost cut,
- a restructuring that permanently guts regional employment,
- or a process that sidelines the state’s blocking and co-determination role.

Those are not bargaining details. They are fundamental.

## On the experts’ assessment

The experts have been useful. Their message is clear:
- closure-led restructuring is usually stronger on fixed-cost removal,
- internal adjustment can work only if it is large enough and durable enough,
- and a hybrid package is the most realistic route in this governance structure.

I accept that. But “realistic” does not mean “acceptable at any price.” A hybrid only works if it is not a euphemism for forcing the workforce to swallow the core pain while others remain untouched.

## What I want next

If management insists closures are unavoidable, then prove it:
- show that internal alternatives were seriously tested,
- show the savings bridge,
- show the one-time costs,
- show why the non-closure route fails.

If the employee side insists on no closures, then bring a **site-by-site quantified counterproposal** that shows:
- how much capacity is removed,
- how much labor cost is saved,
- what the duration is,
- and what guarantees are exchanged.

## My bottom line

The state will back a serious restructuring.  
The state will **not** back a restructuring that destroys regional employment, removes the company’s industrial anchor function, or treats co-determination as negotiable clutter.

We are ready for hard change. We are not ready for self-inflicted regional decline.
