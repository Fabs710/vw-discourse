## Bottom line

- **Closure-led restructuring**: strongest on permanent fixed-cost removal, but highest one-time cost, highest labor/political execution risk, and weakest governance feasibility.

## Interpretation

### 1) Closure-led restructuring
**Economic logic:**  
Best instrument for removing **fixed cost**, not just labor cost. In automotive manufacturing, that matters because underutilized plants carry depreciation, maintenance, utilities, logistics duplication, and overhead that do not fall much unless capacity is actually removed.

**Main tradeoff:**  
You gain durable savings, but you pay through:
- high severance and decommissioning costs,
- potential production disruption,
- labor conflict,
- and major governance resistance under co-determination and the special statute.

### 2) Internal-adjustment-only
**Economic logic:**  
Can reduce cash cost quickly through:
- temporary wage restraint,
- working-time changes,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- productivity measures,
- management restraint.

**Main tradeoff:**  
It preserves sites and industrial peace better, but does **not** usually remove enough fixed cost if the network is structurally too large. That means it risks being a temporary bridge rather than a durable solution.

### 3) Hybrid
**Economic logic:**  
Combines some permanent capacity reduction with negotiated employee contributions and operational measures.

**Main tradeoff:**  
It is less powerful than full closure-led restructuring, but much more feasible in this governance environment. It is often the only option that can plausibly pass through:
- co-determination,
- the supervisory board’s supermajority requirement for plant closures,
- works council resistance,
- union strike capacity,
- and the state/family blocking positions.

## Employment and site effects

- **Closure-led** would have the largest direct employment losses and strongest regional effects.
- **Internal-only** preserves the most jobs and sites, but may leave long-run job security exposed if the company remains uncompetitive.
- **Hybrid** preserves more employment than closure-led while still reducing some structural overcapacity.

## Limits of temporary wage concessions

A temporary employee contribution can help finance transition, but it has hard limits:
- it improves cash flow,
- it can buy time,
- it can be paired with guarantees,
- but it does **not** substitute for permanent capacity and fixed-cost reduction.

As a rule of thumb, temporary labor concessions are useful as a **bridge**, not as the core answer to structural overcapacity.

## Governance and legal constraints

Within the facts you supplied, the decision is constrained by:
- **parity co-determination** on the supervisory board,
- a **two-thirds requirement** for plant closures/relocations,
- the workforce’s effective veto,
- the controlling family’s voting power,
- and the regional state’s blocking/minority role under the special statute.

That makes a pure closure strategy economically strong but institutionally fragile. A negotiated hybrid is therefore the most realistic path if the goal is both:
1. meaningful savings, and  
2. a decision that can actually be implemented.

## Practical conclusion

If the question is “what is best on pure industrial economics?” the answer is **closure-led**.

If the question is “what best preserves jobs and sites in the short run?” the answer is **internal-adjustment-only**, but with the greatest risk of falling short on durable competitiveness.
