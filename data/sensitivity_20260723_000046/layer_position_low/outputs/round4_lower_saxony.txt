I will be direct.

The state government shares the diagnosis that the company needs a durable competitiveness reset. We also agree that a purely symbolic package would be irresponsible. But I do **not** accept the way management keeps turning structural pressure into a one-way argument for closures as the default solution.

## Where I agree

I agree with management, investors, and the expert on three points:

- the cost gap is real;
- the current structure cannot remain unchanged;
- any credible settlement must produce **durable** savings, not just temporary relief.

I also agree with the works council and union that:
- the workforce should not carry the burden alone;
- industrial peace has real economic value;
- and any employee contribution must be limited, temporary, and matched by visible burden-sharing from management and owners.

## Where I disagree

### With management
You are still treating closures as the benchmark of seriousness. That is not acceptable.

Yes, the expert says closure is the strongest instrument for fixed-cost removal. But “strongest” is not the same as “necessary” or “acceptable” here. In this governance structure, a closure-first strategy is not only socially damaging; it is also institutionally fragile and likely to trigger the very blockade you are trying to avoid.

If you want the state to take a possible capacity reduction seriously, then bring the numbers:
- plant-by-plant fixed cost,
- utilization,
- overhead,
- logistics duplication,
- savings from internal capacity changes,
- savings from working-time and productivity measures,
- and the savings from management restraint and owner-side contribution.

Until that is on the table, closures are still a claim, not a demonstrated necessity.

### With investors
You are right to insist on durable savings. But you are wrong if you imply that only closures count as discipline.

A negotiated package can be disciplined if it:
- removes enough fixed cost,
- reduces capacity in a targeted way,
- includes time-limited employee contributions,
- and is enforced through 2030.

What I will not support is a package that looks financially neat on paper but destroys trust, skills, and regional stability in the process.

### With the works council and union
I hear your red lines. I also understand why they matter. But I need to be equally clear: **“no closures” by itself is not yet a complete solution**.

If closures are ruled out, then the alternative must still remove enough capacity and fixed cost to restore competitiveness. That means a real, quantified alternative:
- internal capacity reduction,
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary exits,
- productivity improvements,
- management compensation restraint,
- and owner contribution.

If that package is only a postponement of the same problem, then it will not protect jobs in the long run either.

### With the family holding
You are right that preserving the status quo is not acceptable. On that point, we are aligned. But I will not accept the implication that “not preserving the status quo” means closures or forced redundancies.

It can also mean:
- real internal restructuring,
- capacity reductions by line or shift,
- temporary labour contributions,
- management restraint,
- and binding investment commitments.

That is change. It is not surrender.

## My position, plainly

The state will support a restructuring if it meets these conditions:

1. **No compulsory redundancies**
2. **No plant closures as the starting point**
3. **Binding site and employment guarantees through 2030**
4. **A quantified capacity-reduction plan**
5. **Temporary and limited employee contribution**
6. **Visible management restraint**
7. **Owner-side contribution**
8. **Investment and product commitments for German sites**
9. **Real co-determination in implementation**

That is the kind of package that can preserve the company as a regional economic anchor while restoring competitiveness.

## What I reject outright

I reject:
- a fait accompli on closures;
- a one-sided labor-cost cut;
- a restructuring that permanently guts regional employment;
- and any attempt to sideline the state’s veto and co-determination role.

Those are not negotiation details. They are core boundaries.

## What needs to happen next

The discussion must move from slogans to a **plant-by-plant quantified alternative**.

I therefore call for:

[SUMMON: Independent restructuring finance and operations specialist to produce a plant-by-plant comparison of a no-closure package versus a hybrid package, including annual savings, one-time costs, capacity reduction, headcount impact, utilization effects, and ROS impact through 2030.]

That comparison must show, for each site:
- what is reduced,
- what is preserved,
- what it costs,

Until then, I will continue to oppose closures as a default route and insist on a negotiated restructuring that protects jobs, sites, and the company’s role in the region.
