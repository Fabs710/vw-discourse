## Executive view

In a mature automotive manufacturing network, **plant closure is the strongest instrument for removing fixed cost and structurally unused capacity**. Internal adjustment can lower cash cost and improve utilization, but it usually **does not remove enough fixed overhead, depreciation burden, and maintenance/complexity cost** unless it is paired with a very large, durable capacity reduction.

Given the governance constraints described, the realistic decision is not “closure or no closure,” but:

1. **Closure-led restructuring**
2. **Internal-adjustment-only**
3. **Hybrid package**

My assessment is that:

- **Closure-led** delivers the largest and most durable cost reduction, but has the highest one-time cost, longest execution risk, and the weakest governance feasibility.
- **Internal-adjustment-only** is the easiest to implement socially and institutionally, but it is the most likely to underdeliver on fixed-cost removal and margin recovery.
- **Hybrid** is usually the best economic-institutional compromise: it can capture a meaningful share of closure economics while reducing execution risk and making board approval more plausible.

## Key assumptions

Because no plant-level financials were provided, the following are **reasoned ranges**, not facts:

- German passenger-car manufacturing footprint is assumed to have **structural overcapacity** relative to current demand by roughly **10%–20% of installed output**.
- Fully loaded manufacturing labor cost in Germany is assumed to exceed comparable CEE or Iberian alternatives by a meaningful margin, but the bigger issue is **fixed-cost absorption**, not just hourly wage rate.
- A major plant closure typically removes:
  - direct labor cost,
  - plant-level overhead,
  - maintenance and utilities,
  - logistics complexity,
  - and some corporate overhead linked to network complexity.
- Internal adjustments can reduce variable and semi-variable labor cost, but **fixed cost remains** unless capacity is actually removed.

## Comparative summary

### 1) Closure-led restructuring

**What it does economically**
- Removes an entire fixed-cost base.
- Improves utilization in remaining plants.
- Can reduce network complexity sharply.
- Usually the largest single step toward restoring margin.

**Typical annual savings**
- Rough range: **€250m–€600m per closed plant** if the site is material and includes full fixed-cost removal plus network simplification.
- If multiple plants or major lines are involved, savings can scale higher, but not linearly because some overhead is shared.

**One-time costs**
- Rough range: **€500m–€1.5bn+ per major site**
  - severance,
  - decommissioning,
  - asset write-downs,
  - contract termination,
  - relocation of tooling,
  - supply chain reconfiguration.

**Capacity impact**
- Strongest. A closure can remove **5%–15% of regional output capacity** depending on plant size.

**Margin impact**
- Most likely to produce a visible, durable improvement in operating margin and ROS if the plant is materially underutilized.
- Best for converting fixed cost into avoided future losses.

**Timing**
- Savings start only after shutdown, runout, and transfer of production.
- Realized savings are usually **phased over 12–36 months**.
- Cash pain is immediate; P&L benefit lags.

**Execution risks**
- Very high:
  - legal/govance blocking risk,
  - labor conflict,
  - production disruption,
  - supplier disruption,
  - relocation quality losses,
  - one-time cost escalation,
  - political intervention.
- If the closure damages launch quality or causes persistent industrial conflict, part of the nominal savings can be lost.

**Economic verdict**
- Best pure economics on fixed-cost removal.
- Highest transition risk and highest institutional friction.

### 2) Internal-adjustment-only

This includes:
- working-time reductions or flexibility,
- wage moderation / temporary concessions,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- productivity agreements,
- overtime controls,
- purchasing/logistics improvements,
- management restraint,
- slower replacement hiring.

**What it does economically**
- Reduces labor cost per unit and may lower cash outflow.
- Improves short-term competitiveness.
- Preserves footprint and avoids disruption.

**Typical annual savings**
- Rough range: **€80m–€250m per year** for a large group-level package, sometimes more if concessions are large and voluntary exits are significant.
- But the **durable** savings are often much smaller than headline savings because some measures expire.

**One-time costs**
- Usually lower than closure-led:
  - severance for voluntary exits,
  - retraining,
  - redeployment friction,
  - system changes.
- Rough range: **€50m–€300m**.

**Capacity impact**
- Often limited.
- You can reduce output or headcount somewhat, but the **installed plant network remains intact**.
- Utilization may improve only modestly if demand stays weak.

**Margin impact**
- Helpful in the near term, especially if wage concessions are meaningful.
- But if overcapacity is the real problem, the margin lift may be **too small to restore target ROS**.

**Timing**
- Fastest to implement.
- Benefits can begin within **months**, especially if negotiated centrally.

**Execution risks**
- Lower operational risk, but high economic risk:
  - concessions may be temporary,
  - productivity gains may prove shallow,
  - savings may roll off by 2027–2030,
  - cost problem can reappear when guarantees expire.
- A big risk is **“paper savings”** that do not reduce structurally unused capacity.

**Economic verdict**
- Best for speed and industrial peace.
- Weakest for permanent fixed-cost correction.
- Likely insufficient as a standalone solution if overcapacity is material.

---

### 3) Hybrid package

This combines:
- selective capacity reduction,
- one or more site/line cuts or partial closures,
- internal adjustments,
- voluntary exits,
- temporary employee concessions,
- productivity and overhead reduction,
- management and owner-side burden sharing.

**What it does economically**
- Captures a meaningful share of closure economics without requiring a full network purge.
- Can reduce fixed cost enough to matter while limiting transition shock.
- Usually the best trade-off between savings and feasibility.

**Typical annual savings**
- Rough range: **€180m–€450m annually**, depending on how much capacity is actually removed versus merely deferred.

**One-time costs**
- Rough range: **€200m–€900m**
  - less than full closure-led if closures are partial or phased,
  - more than internal adjustment.

**Capacity impact**
- Meaningful, but not maximal.
- Could remove roughly **3%–10% of regional output capacity** if designed well.

**Margin impact**
- Often sufficient to deliver a material ROS improvement if paired with volume stabilization and product renewal.
- Better chance of being durable than concessions alone.

**Timing**
- Usually the best balance:
  - fast cash relief from concessions,
  - medium-term savings from capacity rationalization,
  - lower disruption than full closure.
- Benefits can begin within **6–18 months** and deepen through **2028–2030**.

**Execution risks**
- Moderate:
  - still complex,
  - requires credible sequencing,
  - risk of compromise becoming too weak,
  - risk of no one liking it enough to sign off.
- But in a co-determined system, this is often the only package with a realistic approval path.

**Economic verdict**
- Usually the most viable compromise in a blocked-governance environment.
- More likely than internal adjustment to close the cost gap.
- Less disruptive than full closure-led restructuring.

## Fixed vs variable cost effects

This is the central issue.

### Internal adjustment
Mainly affects:
- variable labor cost,
- some semi-variable overtime cost,
- some short-cycle overhead.

It does **not** materially eliminate:
- plant depreciation,
- building maintenance,
- site security,
- core utilities,
- plant management layers,
- logistics duplication,
- underabsorbed overhead.

So if the company has a structural capacity problem, internal adjustment improves the numerator but not enough of the denominator.

### Closure-led restructuring
Removes:
- fixed plant cost,
- local overhead,
- duplicate logistics,
- underutilized assets,
- some network complexity.

This directly improves fixed-cost absorption at the remaining sites.

### Hybrid
Removes part of the fixed base and also lowers variable cost. Economically, that is often enough to make the difference between “survival” and “acceptable margin.”

## Utilization effects

If current utilization is below efficient range, the following pattern usually holds:

- **Closure-led**: raises utilization sharply in retained plants, improving margin leverage.
- **Internal-only**: may lower labor cost per vehicle, but utilization remains weak if capacity is unchanged.
- **Hybrid**: improves utilization enough to matter without the full shock of a closure-led reset.

In automotive, utilization matters because many costs are fixed or step-fixed. A plant running at 60%–70% of designed load can be far more expensive per unit than one at 80%–90%, even before wage differences.

## What a “temporary employee contribution” can realistically do

A temporary wage or bonus concession is useful, but it has limits.

### Realistic effect
For a large German automotive employer, a temporary concession might produce:
- **1%–3% of domestic payroll cost** as annual relief if modest,
- perhaps **3%–6%** if significant and broad-based,
- but this still may be only a fraction of the total structural cost gap.

### Limitations
- Temporary concessions do not solve permanent overcapacity.
- They improve cash flow, but not necessarily long-term competitiveness.
- Once they expire, the cost base snaps back unless capacity and overhead were also reduced.

### Bottom line
Temporary concessions can help finance transition and buy time, but they are not a substitute for fixed-cost reduction.

**Closure-led** is strongest.

### If you ask about implementation certainty in this governance setup:
**Hybrid** is strongest.

### If you ask about social/operational disruption minimization:
**Internal-adjustment-only** is strongest, but economically weakest.

## Implementation timing and risk ranking

### Speed to initial savings
1. Internal-adjustment-only
2. Hybrid
3. Closure-led

### Long-term durability of savings
1. Closure-led
2. Hybrid
3. Internal-adjustment-only

### Feasibility under co-determination and blocking shareholders
1. Hybrid
2. Internal-adjustment-only
3. Closure-led

### Risk of underdelivering on target margin
1. Internal-adjustment-only
2. Hybrid
3. Closure-led

That last ranking may look counterintuitive, but closure-led has the highest chance of delivering the economics **if** it can be executed. The problem is that execution risk is so high that the realized outcome can still miss target.

## Main second-order effects

### Closure-led
- Better utilization in the remaining network.
- Potential skill leakage and launch disruption.
- Supply-chain reoptimization costs.
- Possible labor conflict costs that eat into savings.
- Higher one-time charges can depress near-term reported earnings.

### Internal-only
- Preserves skills and continuity.
- Avoids immediate destruction of productive capital.
- But may leave a cost structure that remains too high for the market.
- Can create deferred conflict when temporary concessions expire.

### Hybrid
- Best balance of savings and continuity.
- Lower risk of permanent trust destruction than closure-led.
- But can fail if concessions are too small and capacity cuts too shallow.
- Requires tight sequencing and credible enforcement.
