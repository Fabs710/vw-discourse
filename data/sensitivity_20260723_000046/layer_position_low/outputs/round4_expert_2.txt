## Independent assessment: no-closure vs hybrid vs closure-led restructuring

Within industrial restructuring economics, the expert’s framework is sound. The core question is whether the company can remove enough **fixed cost and excess capacity** by means short of plant closure.

### Bottom line
- A **pure no-closure package** is usually the weakest instrument for a structurally overcapacitated carmaker.
- A **hybrid package** is the most plausible compromise economically and institutionally.
- A **closure-led package** produces the largest permanent fixed-cost reduction, but also the highest one-time cost, execution risk, and labor-relations disruption.

I’ll use the case facts and the expert ranges already on the record, and I’ll separate what is known from what is assumed.

---

## 1) Known facts from the case
- The company says German operations are structurally too expensive and too large for the market.
- Co-determination and the special governance framework make closures hard to impose.
- Employee-side stakeholders reject plant closures and compulsory redundancies.
- Management wants durable, not temporary, savings.
- Industrial peace matters, but so does permanent margin improvement through 2030.

## 3) Scenario comparison

### A. Pure no-closure package
This means:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- voluntary exits only,
- working-time adjustments,
- temporary employee contribution,
- productivity improvements,
- redeployment and qualification.

#### Economic profile
This can cut labor cost and some overhead, but it usually does **not** eliminate enough fixed cost if overcapacity is structural.

#### Indicative effect
- **Annual recurring savings:** about **€300m to €800m**
- **One-time restructuring cost:** about **€200m to €700m**
- **Headcount impact:** roughly **5% to 12%** over several years, mainly voluntary/natural attrition
- **Utilization improvement:** modest, perhaps **+5 to +12 percentage points**
- **ROS impact:** around **+0.5 to +1.5 percentage points**

#### Assessment
This is likely **insufficient alone** unless the actual competitiveness gap is relatively small. It risks becoming a temporary concession package rather than a structural fix.

---

### B. Hybrid package
This means:
- no immediate full plant closures,
- but real permanent capacity reduction by line, shift, function, or product allocation,
- voluntary exits only,
- temporary employee contribution,
- management restraint,
- owner contribution,
- targeted consolidation.

#### Economic profile
This is the best compromise if the aim is to get much of the fixed-cost benefit of closure without the binary shock of shutting plants outright.

#### Indicative effect
- **Annual recurring savings:** about **€700m to €1.6bn**
- **One-time restructuring cost:** about **€500m to €1.5bn**
- **Headcount impact:** roughly **8% to 18%**
- **Utilization improvement:** about **+8 to +18 percentage points**
- **ROS impact:** about **+1.0 to +2.5 percentage points**

#### Assessment
This is the **most plausible route** if the parties want something that is both economically meaningful and governable under German co-determination.

The key is that “hybrid” must mean **real permanent capacity takeout**, not a renamed delay strategy.

---

### C. Closure-led restructuring
This means:
- one or more German plants are closed,
- production is transferred or consolidated elsewhere,
- full site overhead is removed,
- associated jobs are eliminated.

#### Economic profile
This is the strongest lever for permanent fixed-cost reduction.

#### Indicative effect
- **Annual recurring savings:** about **€1.0bn to €2.0bn**
- **One-time restructuring cost:** about **€1.5bn to €3.5bn+**
- **Headcount impact:** roughly **15% to 25%**
- **Utilization improvement:** about **+10 to +25 percentage points**
- **ROS impact:** about **+1.5 to +3.5 percentage points**

#### Assessment
Economically, this is the strongest option. Operationally and institutionally, it is also the hardest to execute and most likely to trigger delay, conflict, and governance blockage.

---

## 4) Tradeoff summary

### No-closure
- Lowest social disruption
- Lowest one-time cost
- Weakest permanent savings
- Highest risk of underdelivery

### Hybrid
- Best balance of savings and feasibility
- Still politically difficult
- Requires real structural capacity reduction
- Most likely to be executable

### Closure-led
- Highest permanent savings
- Highest disruption and transition cost
- Greatest governance and labor-relations risk
- Best only if the savings gap is large and alternatives fail

---

## 5) Can a no-closure package close the competitiveness gap through 2030?
**Possibly, but only under strong assumptions.**

It would need:
- deep and durable labor-cost relief,
- meaningful permanent capacity reduction without formal closure,
- large overhead cuts,
- improved utilization,
- and significant management/owner contribution.

If those are not present, then a no-closure package is unlikely to close the gap.

---

## 6) Practical conclusion
From an industrial finance and operations perspective:

1. **Pure no-closure**: probably not enough by itself.
2. **Hybrid**: most plausible compromise.
3. **Closure-led**: strongest economically, but highest risk and hardest to implement.

If the objective is to reach a **durable 2030 cost structure** in this governance setting, the most realistic route is a **hybrid package with real permanent capacity reduction**, not a symbolic no-closure deal and not an immediately imposed closure plan.
