I will respond directly.

We are all agreed on one point: this company needs a **durable** solution, not a cosmetic one. ### To the Works Council
You keep saying management has not answered why the workforce should pay for a crisis it did not create. My answer is simple: because the company’s German footprint is **structurally too expensive and too large for the market we now face**. That is not a moral judgement on the workforce; it is an operating reality.

What I reject in your position is the attempt to make **“no closures, no compulsory redundancies”** the starting and ending point at the same time. If you rule out the main instruments of structural adjustment before the numbers are even discussed, then you are not negotiating a solution — you are defending the status quo.

You ask for a quantified alternative. Fair enough. But that alternative must be **credible at scale**. We need savings that are:
- large enough to close the cost gap,
- durable through 2030,
- and enforceable in practice.

If you want to avoid closures, then internal measures must deliver equivalent effect. That means not just “flexibility” in the abstract, but measurable reductions in capacity and labour cost. If your proposal cannot do that, then it does not solve the problem.

### To the Union
I agree with you on one point: this cannot be a one-sided wage cut dressed up as transformation. If employees are asked for a contribution, it must be part of a broader package.

But I do **not** agree that the collective framework can remain untouched while we somehow generate a structural turnaround. Temporary concessions are only acceptable if they are large enough to matter. A small, time-limited contribution that leaves the underlying cost base intact is not serious.

You say employees should not bear the crisis alone. Correct. But “burden-sharing” cannot become a veto on the very changes required to make the business viable. If the package lacks meaningful capacity reduction and labour-cost relief, then it fails. Full stop.

### On what management needs
I am not asking for a blank cheque. I am asking for a package that achieves all of the following:
- significant and durable cost reduction,
- lower German overcapacity,
- a sustainable labour-cost contribution,
- and freed-up capital for future products and technology.

I am open on the form:
- plant consolidation,
- closure where unavoidable,
- redeployment,
- voluntary exit schemes,
- working-time adjustments,
- productivity agreements,
- and a negotiated employee contribution.

But I am **not** open on a model that keeps all sites open, forbids forced reductions, and asks management to pretend that the numbers will fix themselves.

### To the State Government
I recognize your concern for regional stability and industrial value. But stability without competitiveness is just delay. If the company remains overburdened, then the jobs you want to protect will be at risk anyway — only later, and under worse conditions.

A responsible stakeholder does not defend every existing structure simply because it is politically difficult to change. If you want this company to remain a strong employer in the region, then it has to become financially fit. That requires real restructuring.

### To the Controlling Family Holding
I appreciate that you are focused on long-term enterprise value rather than short-term headlines. On that point, we are aligned. But long-term value is not protected by preserving excess capacity and legacy cost structures. It is protected by making the business competitive enough to invest and grow.

So when you say closures are not the default, I agree. They are not the goal. They are a tool. But if alternatives cannot deliver the necessary savings, then avoiding them becomes irresponsible. I will not sign up to a package that gives the appearance of balance while leaving the economics unresolved.

### To the Investors
You are right to demand measurable, durable savings. I agree. What I do not accept is the implication that any solution short of closures is automatically weak. The test is not the instrument; the test is the result.

If a negotiated package can materially restore margins through:
- capacity reduction,
- working-time flexibility,
- labour-cost contribution,
- and productivity gains,

then it is acceptable. If it cannot, then it is not.

- significant capacity adjustment,
- meaningful headcount reduction,
- removal of outdated guarantees,

I am prepared to discuss how this is achieved. I am **not** prepared to retreat from the need for a real structural correction.
