We are aligned on one thing: this company needs a credible, durable return to competitiveness. If management cannot restore margins and close the structural cost gap, then there is no investment case, and capital will move elsewhere.

But I need to be clear that from an institutional investor perspective, a settlement that protects every site and avoids compulsory redundancies is only acceptable if it still produces **real, measurable, durable savings**. Anything cosmetic, temporary, or too vague to move margins is not acceptable.

Where I agree with others:
- Management is right that the current German cost base is uncompetitive.
- The state, family, and workforce are right that a value-destructive confrontation would be costly.
- A negotiated package is preferable to prolonged strikes and uncertainty.

Where I disagree:
- With the Works Council and Union: “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” cannot be a blanket end-state unless alternative measures deliver equivalent savings at scale. If they do not, then the company is being asked to preserve an overgrown cost base and shareholders will continue to absorb the downside.
- With management, if it is treating closures as the first and only serious option. Plant closures may be necessary in the end, but they are not valuable in themselves; they are only justified if they are the best route to a durable earnings recovery. Management needs to show that all viable alternatives have been tested and that the savings case is specific, not rhetorical.
- With the state and family, if support for “stability” becomes a shield for underperformance. Protecting every job and every site at all costs is not a strategy; it is a way to keep returns structurally impaired.

What would move us:
1. A quantified restructuring plan showing how the company gets back to target margins.
2. Durable cost reduction, not one-off concessions.
3. Clear timeline and implementation certainty.
4. A package that does not merely defer the problem to the next cycle.

From our side, we can support:
- capacity reduction,
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment and voluntary exits,
- temporary employee contributions,
- and investment commitments where they are tied to genuine operational change.

But we will not support:
- an outcome that leaves the cost base uncompetitive,
- indefinite negotiation without execution,
- or a settlement that preserves legacy structures while asking capital markets to absorb the consequences.

So my position is simple: if there is a negotiated solution that materially restores competitiveness, we support it. If not, then management should not expect investors to back a compromise that merely delays the inevitable.
