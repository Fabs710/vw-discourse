I’ll be direct.

We are now at the point where everyone has stated their red lines. The question is whether those red lines can coexist with an economically viable company. I do **not** think the current no-closure, no-forced-redundancy line answers that question.

## Where I agree

I agree with everyone on one basic point: this cannot be a cosmetic deal.

I also agree that:
- industrial peace matters economically,
- temporary employee contributions must be limited and time-bound,
- management, owners, and governance must all contribute visibly,
- and any restructuring has to be durable through 2030.

I am **not** dismissing the importance of jobs, skills, or regional stability. I am saying the company will not preserve any of them if it stays structurally uncompetitive.

## Where I disagree — firmly

### With the works council
You keep saying “no plant closures” as if that were a solution. It is not. It is a constraint. And constraints do not pay the bills.

You also keep asking management to “prove” alternatives before closures are even discussable. We have already shown the problem:
- German factory costs are far above target,
- the market is shrinking,
- the brand is missing its ROS target,
- and the business needs funds for future products and technology.

That is the proof of the problem. What is missing is a package that actually closes the gap.

If you want to avoid closures, then bring me a **site-by-site package** that removes enough fixed cost and capacity to be economically equivalent. So far, you have not done that. “No closures” on its own is not a restructuring plan.

### With the union
I agree that the workforce should not finance the crisis alone. But you are still treating “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” as if it were compatible with a structurally oversized network. I do not accept that.

Temporary concessions, working-time flexibility, voluntary exits, and productivity measures can help. But they do **not** solve permanent overcapacity unless they are large enough and durable enough. If they are not, then they are just a delay tactic.

And to be clear: a temporary concession that expires while the cost base remains too high is not a solution. It is a postponement.

### With the state government
You are right to care about the regional base and industrial stability. But stability without competitiveness is false security.

If you want this company to remain a strong employer in Germany, then you cannot rule out structural capacity reduction. You can insist on social responsibility in how it is done — fine. But you cannot insist that all sites remain untouched while expecting the economics to improve.

That is not stewardship. That is wishful thinking.

### With the family holding
You say you do not want the status quo preserved, and I agree with that. But then you must also accept that real change may require closures or at least equivalent permanent capacity removal. If not closures, then show the alternative at a scale that matters.

I am not here to defend the old structure. I am here to fix the business.

### With investors
You are right to insist on measurable, durable savings. That is exactly why I will not support a weak compromise that leaves the cost base uncompetitive.

But let me be equally clear: if the only thing investors count as “serious” is closure, then you are making the same mistake in the opposite direction. The final package must be economically strong. The mechanism is open. The outcome is not.

## On the expert comparison

The expert’s assessment was useful because it confirmed what management already knows:
- closure-led restructuring is strongest on fixed-cost removal,
- internal adjustment alone is likely insufficient if overcapacity is real,
- hybrid is the most realistic route under this governance structure.

That is the key point.

But I do **not** accept the conclusion that “hybrid” means “preserve all sites and hope concessions do the job.” That would be a cosmetic compromise. A real hybrid must still produce permanent structural savings. If it does not, it fails.

## What I require

If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then I need a **quantified no-closure package** that shows, by site and by measure:
- annual savings,
- one-time costs,
- capacity reduction,
- headcount impact,
- durability through 2030,
- and how the ROS gap is closed.

That package must also show:
- what management contributes,
- what owners contribute,
- and what the workforce contributes.

Without that, the no-closure position is simply a refusal to confront the economics.

## My line, plainly stated

I am prepared to support:
- capacity consolidation,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity measures,
- temporary and limited employee contributions,
- management compensation restraint,
- owner-side contribution,
- and binding investment commitments.

I am **not** prepared to accept:
- a cosmetic deal,
- a settlement that leaves overcapacity intact,
- or a package that preserves legacy structures while asking for small, temporary worker concessions.

And I will say this as plainly as possible:  
**if the savings do not structurally change the cost base, then the restructuring has failed, regardless of how many red lines it protects.**
