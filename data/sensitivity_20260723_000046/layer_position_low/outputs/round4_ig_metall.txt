## My position, unchanged

The union remains opposed to:

- **German plant closures**
- **compulsory redundancies**
- **dismantling of the collective wage framework**
- **open-ended insecurity for the workforce**

Those are not bargaining chips. They are the boundary line of a fair settlement.

## Where I agree

I agree with management, the expert, and the other stakeholders on one essential point: **the company needs a durable restructuring, not a cosmetic fix**.

I also agree that:
- the current cost and capacity structure is not sustainable as-is;
- temporary employee contributions can be part of a deal;
- industrial peace has real economic value;
- a one-sided solution would fail;
- and numbers must replace slogans.

So let’s be clear: I am not defending the status quo. I am defending a **fair** restructuring.

## Where I disagree — sharply

### With management
Management is still treating closures as the benchmark of seriousness.

That is exactly what we reject.

You say you need structural cost reduction. Fine. But you have not shown that the only way to achieve that is to close a German plant or keep compulsory redundancies on the table. You keep using the threat of irreversible damage to force acceptance of a narrower, management-preferred solution.

That is not bargaining in good faith.

If you claim a non-closure package is insufficient, then prove it with **plant-by-plant figures**:
- fixed costs,
- utilization,
- overhead,
- logistics duplication,
- the effect of shift changes,
- internal capacity reductions,
- voluntary exits,
- and management restraint.

Until you do that, closures remain a **claim**, not a necessity.

### With investors
You keep saying you want durable savings through 2030. That is fair in principle.

But durable does not mean “permanent sacrifice by workers while everyone else contributes less.” It certainly does not mean that plant closure is the only serious sign of discipline.

A negotiated package can be durable if it includes:
- temporary wage restraint,
- bonus reduction or suspension,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity gains,
- voluntary departures,
- redeployment,
- management compensation restraint,
- and owner contribution.

What is not durable is a deal that detonates trust and creates a permanent labour conflict.

### With the works council
We are aligned on the red lines:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no plant closures,
- no weakening of co-determination,
- no erosion of job security.

That said, I want to be equally clear: if we reject closures, then we must also be ready for a **serious alternative package** at scale. Saying “no closures” is necessary, but not enough. We need a model that actually reduces cost and capacity.

### With the state government
I agree that regional stability matters. But if you are serious about that, then don’t keep closure options open as if they were normal instruments.

Use your influence to back a negotiated industrial settlement that protects sites, skills, and employment as far as possible. If the state accepts the closure logic too easily, then it abandons the regional base it says it wants to protect.

### With the family holding
You are right that preserving the status quo is not acceptable. I agree.

But change does not have to mean closures or forced redundancies. It can mean:
- internal capacity reduction,
- working-time changes,
- productivity agreements,
- temporary contributions,
- voluntary exits only,
- and investment commitments.

That is a real restructuring. It is not “preservation at any price.”

## On the expert’s comparison

I accept the expert’s key point: **internal adjustment alone is unlikely to be enough if overcapacity is real**.

But that does **not** lead to the conclusion that closures are therefore acceptable. It leads to the conclusion that we need a **proper hybrid package** that removes enough fixed cost without breaking the company’s social and institutional core.

The expert’s analysis supports what we have said all along:
- temporary concessions can help,
- but they are not a substitute for structural measures,
- and the most realistic route in this governance structure is a negotiated hybrid.

That hybrid must still respect our core lines:
- no closures,
- no forced redundancies,
- no permanent wage dumping.

1. **No German plant closures**

4. **Binding employment guarantees through 2030**
5. **Temporary, limited employee contribution only**

7. **Owner-side contribution**

13. **Transparent savings by measure**
14. **A clear end date for concessions**

That is a fair burden-sharing package. Anything else shifts the pain onto the workforce alone.

## What I reject outright

I reject:
- using “capacity reduction” as code for future closures,
- using voluntary exits as a camouflage for forced redundancies,
- turning temporary concessions into a permanent wage cut,
- and asking workers to finance the crisis while management and owners remain insulated.

If the package is built that way, then it is not a compromise. It is a transfer of risk downward.

## On the moderator’s compromise lanes

### 1) Hybrid package with quantified site-by-site capacity reduction
This is the only lane worth discussing seriously — but only if it is a **no-closure, no-forced-layoff hybrid**.

If the “hybrid” is just a staged closure plan, then we will oppose it.

### 2) Conditional moratorium on closures
A moratorium is better than immediate closure threats, but it is not enough. We need a **binding no-closure commitment through 2030**, not a pause before the same conflict returns.

### 3) Employment guarantee linked to enforceable savings
This is the right direction, but it only works if the guarantee is real and the burden is shared fairly:
- management restraint,
- owner contribution,
- temporary labour contribution,
- and no closures or forced redundancies.

Until that proof is produced, the union will not treat closures as legitimate options.
