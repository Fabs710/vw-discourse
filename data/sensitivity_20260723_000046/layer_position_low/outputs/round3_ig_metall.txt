I will be equally direct.

The works council is right on the core red lines, and management is still not answering the central fairness question.

## Where I agree
I agree that:
- the company needs a durable restructuring, not optics;
- the current cost and capacity structure is unsustainable as-is;
- a one-sided solution would fail economically and socially;
- temporary employee contributions are only possible if they are matched by binding guarantees and wider burden-sharing;
- numbers have to replace slogans.

I also agree with the works council that **the workforce must not be asked to finance the crisis through permanent job destruction**.

## Where I disagree with management
Management is still trying to make “competitiveness” sound like a justification for shifting the pain downward. It is not.

You say closures are only a tool, not the goal. But if you keep them on the table while refusing to rule out compulsory redundancies, you are using them as leverage to force the workforce into accepting structural loss. That is not a fair negotiation.

What I do **not** accept:
- plant closures as the baseline answer,
- compulsory redundancies,
- a permanent erosion of the collective wage framework,
- or employee “contribution” that becomes a euphemism for wage dumping.

If the business case requires action, then the burden must be shared across:
- labour,
- management,
- owners,
- and governance.

Not just the workforce.

## On the expert assessments
The experts were useful, and I accept the key point: if the problem is structural overcapacity, then labor concessions alone are not enough.

But the conclusion is **not** “therefore close plants.”  
The conclusion is: **prove that the alternative package cannot remove enough fixed cost and capacity before demanding closures or forced layoffs.**

That proof has not been delivered.

The expert also confirmed that a negotiated hybrid is institutionally the most realistic route in this governance setup. That matters. A strategy that is economically neat but institutionally impossible is not a serious strategy.

## What a fair package must include
If management wants labour to contribute, then the package must be all of the following:

- **temporary**
- **limited**
- **reversible**
- **binding**
- **matched by management and owner contributions**
- **tied to site and employment guarantees**
- **protected by clear end dates**

Possible elements:
- temporary wage restraint into a transformation fund,
- bonus suspension or reduction,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity agreements,
- redeployment and qualification,
- voluntary exit windows,
- management compensation restraint,
- owner support,
- investment and product commitments for German sites.

What I will not accept is a package that starts with worker sacrifice and ends with the company reserving the right to close plants later anyway.

## To the works council
I support your insistence on no compulsory redundancies and no plant closures as the starting point. That is the right line.

But I also need to say clearly: “no closures” can only hold if the alternative is **credible at scale**. We cannot defend a settlement that merely postpones the same conflict for two years.

So I want the works council and management to move from principles to a **site-by-site quantified alternative**:
- what capacity can be removed internally,
- what labor-cost relief each measure brings,
- how many jobs are preserved,
- what the duration is,
- and what binding guarantees are exchanged.

## To management
If you want labour cooperation, stop asking us to accept open-ended insecurity. Bring a package that shows:
- how much savings come from capacity adjustment without closure,
- how much from working-time and productivity measures,
- how much from management restraint,
- how much from owner-side support,
- and how this closes the gap through 2030.

If the only way your model works is by turning permanent job security into a disposable cost item, then the problem is not labour resistance. The problem is your restructuring design.

## To the state government and family holding
You are right to care about regional stability and long-term enterprise value. But those are not protected by sacrificing the workforce first.

If you want a durable industrial base, then you should support a package that keeps sites open, protects skills, and shares the burden fairly.

That means:
- visible management restraint,
- real owner contribution,
- no forced redundancies,
- and serious investment commitments.

## To investors
You are right to insist on measurable savings. But you are wrong if you treat plant closure as the only serious proof of discipline.

A negotiated package can be disciplined if it:
- reduces capacity,
- lowers labor cost temporarily and fairly,
- preserves skills,
- and is enforceable through 2030.

What you cannot reasonably demand is high savings from the workforce while rejecting any requirement for management, owners, or governance to contribute.

## My line, plainly stated
The union’s position is this:

- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No plant closures as the starting point or default answer.**
- **No dismantling of the collective wage framework.**
- **Yes to a temporary, limited, fair employee contribution.**
- **Yes only if management and owners also contribute.**
- **Yes only with binding site and employment guarantees.**

If management refuses that framework, then there is no fair settlement to be had. In that case, the conflict will continue, and we will use the means available to defend jobs and wage standards.

## What I want next
We now need a **quantified alternative package by site and by measure**:
- annual savings,
- one-time costs,
- employment impact,
- duration,
- and what each side contributes.

If that is not presented, then the claim that closures are unavoidable remains just that: a claim.
