We agree on the diagnosis, but not yet on the remedy.

From the capital-markets side, our standard is simple: **we need a credible, durable path back to competitive margins and return-on-sales targets**. If the final package does not materially lower the cost base and remove structural overcapacity, we will not support it — regardless of how elegant the compromise sounds.

## Where we agree
We agree that:
- the current structure is not sustainable as-is;
- cosmetic or short-lived fixes will destroy value;
- industrial conflict has a real economic cost;
- any employee contribution must be temporary, limited, and matched by broader burden-sharing;
- and management must show numbers, not slogans.

We also agree with the expert view that a **purely closure-led** approach is not automatically the only answer in this governance structure. It may be economically powerful, but it is institutionally fragile and likely to be expensive in execution.

## Where we disagree

### With the works council and union
Your insistence on **“no closures, no compulsory redundancies”** is understandable, but it is **not acceptable as a final outcome unless the alternative clearly delivers equivalent savings and capacity reduction through 2030**.

Right now, I have not heard a package that convinces me of that.

If “no closures” means:
- preserving too much fixed cost,
- leaving underutilized sites in place,
- and relying mainly on temporary concessions,

then that is not restructuring. It is deferral. We would reject it.

### With management
Management is right that the company needs structural correction. But you still have not proven that the closure option is necessary rather than just convenient in bargaining.

You need to stop speaking in generalities and show, side by side:
- what the closure-led route delivers in annual savings,
- what the hybrid route delivers,
- what the internal-adjustment route delivers,
- and what each does to margin, capacity, and one-time charges.

Until that is on the table, closures remain a claim, not a demonstrated necessity.

### With the state and family
We understand your concern for regional stability and long-term value. But stability is not value if it locks in a structurally weak business.

If the final package leaves the company:
- overcapacitated,
- under-margined,
- and dependent on repeated concessions,

then investors will continue to price in weak returns. That is not a sustainable outcome.

## Our position
We can support a negotiated restructuring only if it includes all of the following:

1. **Durable capacity reduction**
   - not just cosmetic efficiency measures.

2. **Meaningful cost reduction**
   - enough to restore competitive margins.

3. **A quantified labor contribution**
   - temporary, limited, and reversible, but real.

4. **Visible burden-sharing from management and owners**
   - compensation restraint and governance commitment.

5. **Implementation certainty**
   - a package that is enforceable, not aspirational.

6. **No open-ended delay**
   - the issue must be solved, not postponed.

## What we cannot support
We will not support:
- a “no closures” settlement that leaves the cost base uncompetitive;
- temporary concessions that merely roll the problem forward;
- a one-sided labor sacrifice;
- or a deal that protects optics while impairing returns.

## What would move us
A **side-by-side quantified model** is now essential. We need a credible comparison of:
- closure-led restructuring,
- hybrid restructuring,
- internal-adjustment-only.

For each:
- annual savings,
- one-time costs,
- capacity removed,
- headcount impact,
- ROS effect through 2030,
- and feasibility under the governance constraints.
