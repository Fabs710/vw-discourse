I will be direct.

We share the diagnosis that the company needs a durable competitiveness reset. We do **not** share the view that the answer must start with plant closures or a confrontational burden shift onto the workforce.

## Where I agree

I agree with management, investors, and the expert that:

- the cost gap is real,
- the current structure is not sustainable as-is,
- cosmetic measures will fail,
- and a settlement must produce **durable** savings, not just short-term relief.

I also agree with the state, works council, and union that:

- industrial peace matters,
- value destruction through prolonged conflict would be harmful,
- and any serious package should include investment and product commitments.

## Where I disagree

### With management
You are right that we need structural savings. You are **not** right to treat closures and forced reductions as the default answer before the alternatives have been fully quantified and exhausted.

The expert’s assessment confirms something important: closure is usually the stronger instrument for removing fixed cost, but it is also highly disruptive and not automatically the best governance outcome in this case. Given the special company statute, co-determination, and the blocking positions in the system, a closure-led strategy is not just harsh — it is institutionally fragile.

So I reject any attempt to present closure as the only serious path.

What I need from management is not a slogan about competitiveness, but a **quantified alternative package**:
- cost reduction by measure,
- capacity reduction by measure,
- timeline through 2030,
- one-time costs,
- and the effect on margin.

If internal adjustment cannot close the gap, then say that precisely. But do not hide behind closure as a threat before the numbers are tested.

### With the works council
I understand and respect the refusal of compulsory redundancies and the concern over historic guarantees. But I cannot support a blanket position that every German plant must remain untouched regardless of economics.

That is not stewardship. That is freezing the problem in place.

If the company’s footprint remains structurally oversized, then the answer must still include real capacity reduction. That may or may not mean closure. But it must mean something more than declarations of intent.

So I disagree with any framing that treats “no closures” as a complete solution. It is a red line only if nothing else can deliver the required savings. The burden is on the employee side to help produce a package that is economically credible at scale.

### With the union
You are right that the workforce did not create the problem and should not carry it alone. You are also right that any employee contribution must be temporary, limited, and matched by visible contributions from management and governance.

But I do **not** accept the implication that any substantial labour contribution is unacceptable simply because it affects established standards. If the company is fighting for competitiveness, then everyone with a claim on value must share in the adjustment.

That includes:
- labour,
- management,
- the owners,
- and, where appropriate, the broader governance structure.

A fair contribution from employees is acceptable only if it is paired with:
- binding site and employment guarantees,
- a credible investment plan,
- and a clear expiry/reversal mechanism.

Without those, I agree with your objection.

### With the state government
Your concern for the regional industrial base is legitimate. I share it. But I do not accept a position that implicitly turns regional protection into a veto on meaningful restructuring.

If the company remains uncompetitive, the regional base will be weakened anyway — only more slowly and with less certainty. Stability must come from a viable business model, not from preserving every existing structure unchanged.

### With the investors
You are right to demand measurable, durable savings. I agree that “delay” is not a strategy.

But I do not support a framework where the only acceptable proof of seriousness is plant closure. The test is whether the package produces the needed economic result. If a negotiated hybrid can do that, it is preferable to a value-destructive closure fight.

## My position

From the controlling family holding’s perspective, the priorities are:

1. **restore competitiveness and margins**
2. **avoid permanent damage to enterprise value**
3. **avoid open-ended industrial conflict**
4. **preserve governance control and strategic optionality**

That means I can support a serious restructuring, but only one that is:

- economically credible,
- durable through 2030,
- enforceable,
- and not needlessly destabilizing.

## What I would support

I am prepared to back a package built around:
- capacity reductions,
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment and qualification,
- voluntary exits and attrition,
- temporary and limited employee contributions,
- management restraint,
- and binding investment/product commitments.

I am **not** prepared to back:
- a one-sided wage cut,
- a symbolic compromise,
- or a package that preserves the old structure while postponing the hard decisions.

## What I need next

If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then bring a **credible quantified counterproposal** that shows:
- how much savings each measure delivers,
- how much capacity is removed,
- how durable the effects are,
- what burden management and owners take,
- and why this is enough to restore the brand’s return-on-sales target.

If management insists closures are unavoidable, it must also prove:
- that all less destructive options were tested,
- that the economic gain outweighs the conflict cost,
- and that the governance path is actually feasible.

Until then, I will not endorse a closure-first approach, and I will not endorse a preservation-at-all-costs approach either.

If a specialist is needed at this stage, I would summon a **governance and industrial-relations expert** to map which restructuring paths are actually passable under the supervisory-board veto structure and special statute.
