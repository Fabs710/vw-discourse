Management is still trying to frame this as if there are only two choices: accept closures and layoffs, or accept decline. That is a false choice, and I reject it.

Let me be absolutely clear again:

- **No German plant closures.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No dismantling of the job-security framework.**
- **No decision over the heads of the workforce.**

Those are not bargaining chips. They are the core of what we are here to defend.

## Where I agree with the other parties

I agree with the basic diagnosis that the company needs a durable solution, not a cosmetic one. I also agree that the current cost and capacity structure must be addressed. I am not denying the economic pressure.

I also agree with the state, the union, and even the family holding on one important point: a one-sided solution would be destructive. If the company wants a settlement, then burden-sharing must be real, visible, and fair.

And I accept the expert’s point that numbers matter. Fine. Then show them. But do not use “numbers” as a code word for making closures look inevitable before alternatives are properly tested.

## Where I disagree

### With management
You keep saying closures are a tool, not the goal. In practice, you are treating them as the starting point and the threat you want to hang over the negotiations.

That is exactly what we will not accept.

You say the German footprint is structurally too expensive. Then prove that you have exhausted the alternatives:
- capacity reduction **without** closure,
- working-time flexibility,
- internal transfers,
- qualification,
- voluntary exit windows,
- productivity improvements,
- temporary employee contributions,
- management restraint,
- owner-side contributions,
- and real investment commitments.

If those measures are not enough, then come back with transparent figures. But do not present closure as “the only serious route” when you have not yet demonstrated that the non-closure package cannot work.

### With investors
You say you need durable, quantified savings. That is fair in principle. But your position becomes unreasonable when you imply that only plant closures count as seriousness.

That is not a financial principle. That is a preference for easy accounting at the expense of real industrial stability.

A package that preserves sites, protects skills, and reduces costs through internal measures can absolutely be durable if it is properly designed and enforced through 2030. What is not durable is a settlement that blows up trust, destroys co-determination, and creates a permanent labour conflict.

### With the family holding
You say you oppose a closure-first approach but also reject preserving the status quo. On that, we are closer than on the others. But let me be clear: “not preserving the status quo” does **not** mean accepting closures or forced redundancies.

There is a middle path that is not a euphemism:
- lower capacity through internal adjustments,
- temporary reductions in working time,
- redeployment across sites,
- voluntary departures only,
- targeted productivity improvements,
- and binding guarantees to 2030.

That is the path we are prepared to discuss. Not closures, not coercion.

### With the state government
Your emphasis on regional stability and industrial base is right. But then you must be consistent: a policy that tolerates plant closures and forced redundancies is not protecting the region, it is undermining it.

If you are serious about the regional anchor role, then use your influence to block any restructuring that destroys German plants and skilled employment. We need you to stand with the workforce, not wave through management’s pressure campaign.

### With the union
We are aligned on the essentials:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- temporary and limited employee contribution only,
- no wage dumping,
- and no one-sided sacrifice.

But I need to be sharper than “shared sacrifice.” If the first and biggest sacrifice is permanent job loss and plant closure, then there is no fairness in the package at all. Labour cannot be asked to pay for a crisis by losing its industrial base.

If there is to be any temporary contribution from workers, it must be matched by:
- management compensation restraint,
- owner-side support,
- binding employment guarantees,
- and a clear end date.

## On the expert’s comparison

The expert said closure is usually the stronger instrument for fixed-cost removal. I do not dispute that in abstract. But “stronger” is not the same as “acceptable” or “necessary here.”

The real issue is not whether closure can cut costs. Of course it can. The real issue is whether the company has **proved** that the same savings cannot be achieved through a negotiated internal package that preserves every German plant.

Right now, that proof has not been provided.

And I am not prepared to let management convert a theoretical economic preference into an irreversible social decision.

## What a serious alternative would require

If management wants us to engage seriously, then put this on the table:

1. **No plant closures**
2. **No forced redundancies**
3. **Binding site guarantees through 2030**
4. **Binding employment guarantees through 2030**
5. **Temporary, limited employee contribution only**
6. **Management contribution and compensation restraint**
7. **Owner contribution**
8. **Investment and product commitments for German sites**
9. **Full quantification of savings by measure**
10. **Joint co-determination over implementation**

That is the baseline for real negotiation. Without that, you are not negotiating a solution. You are demanding surrender under pressure.

## What I reject outright

I reject any package that:
- closes a German plant,
- claims “equivalent capacity reduction” while keeping workers and sites in limbo,
- uses voluntary exits as a camouflage for forced redundancies,
- or strips away co-determination and job-security guarantees.

I also reject the notion that the workforce must first prove a no-closure solution is possible while management gets to keep closure on the table as an intimidation device.

The burden of proof lies with management, because management triggered this confrontation.

## My position going into this round

The works council remains fully opposed to closures and compulsory layoffs. We are willing to discuss restructuring only if it is built on:
- site protection,
- job protection,
- fairness,
- and real co-determination.

If management and investors want our cooperation, they need to stop talking as if the workforce is the variable that must be sacrificed to make the model work. It is not.

If needed, we should now see a **site-by-site alternative model** showing how capacity can be reduced without closing plants and without forced redundancies. If no one can present that, then the conversation is not about solutions — it is about which side blinks first.
