I want to be very clear from the state’s perspective: we recognize the need for a competitive restructuring. We are not defending the status quo as a matter of principle. But we are equally clear that a solution which destroys jobs, closes German plants, and weakens the company’s role as a regional economic anchor is not acceptable.

So there are two points where I agree with management:
- The company needs a durable improvement in competitiveness.
- A purely symbolic adjustment will not be enough.

But there is a major point where I disagree:
- Plant closures and mass job destruction in Germany must not be treated as the default answer.

This company is not just another balance-sheet exercise. It is a core industrial anchor in a region that depends on it. The state has a legitimate responsibility to protect employment, industrial capacity, and social stability. That is why we hold a blocking stake and why co-determination matters.

To management: if the proposal is really “closures first, workers pay most of the bill, and the rest of the stakeholders are expected to accept it,” then we will not support that. I do not think such a route is politically or economically sustainable. It may produce a short-term accounting effect, but it risks long-term damage:
- loss of skilled jobs,
- erosion of supplier networks,
- weakening of regional value creation,
- political backlash,
- and a company that is smaller but not necessarily healthier.

That said, I am not closing the door on restructuring. I would support a negotiated package if it meets a few clear conditions:
- no forced redundancies,
- no closure of German plants as the first or preferred instrument,
- a credible capacity adjustment through internal means, working-time arrangements, transfers, and product-line optimization,
- a fair and time-limited employee contribution,
- management and owners also taking visible responsibility,
- and binding investment commitments for the German sites.

To the works council and union: your insistence on no compulsory redundancies is understandable and, in my view, legitimate. But if the company is facing a structural cost gap, then we will need to see constructive alternatives that actually reduce costs without hollowing out the workforce. A refusal to engage with any capacity reduction at all would make a solution harder, not easier. The state will not back a position that denies the economic problem exists.

At the same time, I support your demand that any employee contribution must be matched by commitments from management. That means:
- no one-sided burden shifting,
- no stripping away of protections without a corresponding future plan,
- and no restructuring that sacrifices the workforce simply to preserve short-term flexibility for capital.

So my position is this:
- Yes to restructuring.
- No to a closure-led, job-destructive restructuring.
- Yes to a negotiated package that protects sites, preserves employment as far as possible, and restores competitiveness.
- No to being sidelined or presented with a fait accompli.

The state is ready to play a mediating role, but we will use our rights if necessary. If management wants our support, it needs to come back with a credible alternative that keeps German sites viable and treats the workforce as a partner, not a cost item to be cut away.
