I will respond plainly.

The Works Council is right on the core principle: **the workforce did not create this crisis and must not be made to bear it alone**. I also agree that management still owes us a quantified alternative before anyone can seriously talk about closures or forced layoffs.

But I need to sharpen the union’s line, because “no closures, no layoffs” is not enough by itself if management is allowed to translate that into a one-sided wage cut while keeping the structural problem intact.

### Where I agree with the Works Council
- No compulsory redundancies.
- No plant closures as the first or default answer.
- No decisions over workers’ heads.
- Any contribution from employees must be temporary, limited, and matched by binding guarantees.
- Co-determination must be real, not decorative.

That is all correct.

### Where I disagree with management
Management keeps saying “competitiveness” as if that automatically legitimizes deep workforce pain. It does not.

If you want labour to contribute, then the package must be:
1. **shared**, not one-sided;
2. **temporary**, not permanent erosion of wage standards;
3. **binding**, not vague promises;
4. **paired with guarantees**, not insecurity.

So let me say clearly:  
- I do **not** accept forced redundancies.  
- I do **not** accept plant closures as a bargaining threat to discipline the workforce.  
- I do **not** accept the dismantling of the collective wage framework.  
- I do **not** accept “employee contribution” if it is just a euphemism for wage dumping.

### On the savings question
Management and investors are demanding “quantified savings.” Fine. Then quantify the alternatives too.

A credible package can include:
- temporary wage contribution into a transformation fund,
- suspension or reduction of bonuses,
- shorter working time or flexibility corridors,
- productivity agreements,
- redeployment and qualification,
- voluntary exit windows,
- internal capacity adjustments,
- and management/owner contributions.

But if management wants these measures, it must also commit to:
- a binding site guarantee,
- a binding employment guarantee,
- no forced layoffs,
- no closure-led restructuring,
- investment commitments for German plants,
- and a clear timeline for return to normal wage conditions once the crisis package ends.

### On burden-sharing
This is where I draw the line most sharply.

If the proposal is:
- workers give up pay,
- workers accept more flexibility,
- workers accept reduced bonuses,
- while management keeps full freedom,
- owners contribute nothing,
- and the company still reserves the right to close plants later,

then that is **not** a settlement. That is shifting the pain downward and postponing the conflict.

If there is to be sacrifice, then it must be visible all the way up the chain:
- management compensation restraint,
- owner-level support,
- governance commitment,
- and an agreed transformation contribution from labour that is time-limited and reversible.

### My position for this round
The union is prepared to negotiate a serious alternative to closure and compulsory redundancies, but only if the framework is clear:

- no plant closures,
- no forced layoffs,
- no erosion of the collective agreement,
- fair employee contribution,
- management and owners contribute too,
- binding guarantees through 2030,
- and measurable investment and product commitments.

If management is serious, it should stop speaking in generalities and put numbers on the table:
- what savings from working-time measures,
- what from bonus restraint,
- what from redeployment,
- what from management contribution,
- what from owner support,
- and how that compares with closure scenarios.

Until then, the union will continue to back resistance, including industrial action if needed. We are not prepared to let the company solve its competitiveness problem by turning permanent job security into a disposable luxury.
