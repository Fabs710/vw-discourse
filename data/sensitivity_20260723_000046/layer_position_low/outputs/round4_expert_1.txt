## Independent restructuring assessment: no-closure vs hybrid vs closure-led

I’ll stay within industrial restructuring economics: cost, capacity, utilization, margin, fixed/variable effects, timing, and execution risk.

### Bottom line
Based on the facts given, **a pure no-closure package is unlikely to close the competitiveness gap by 2030 unless it includes unusually deep and durable internal capacity reduction plus meaningful labor-cost relief**.  
A **hybrid package** is the most plausible economically and institutionally: it can capture most of the savings of closure-led restructuring **if** it includes real permanent capacity cuts, not just temporary concessions.  
A **closure-led package** gives the strongest fixed-cost reduction, but it also carries the highest transition cost, highest industrial-relations risk, and the biggest execution uncertainty.

The key issue is not whether savings are possible in principle; it is whether they are **large enough, durable enough, and fast enough** relative to the cost gap.

## 1) What is missing from the case

- target margin gap in euros, not just “ROS target missing”

Without that, only ranges are defensible.

## 2) Economic logic by scenario

### A. Pure no-closure internal adjustment
This means:
- no plant closures
- no compulsory redundancies
- voluntary exits only
- working-time changes
- wage restraint / temporary contribution
- productivity improvement
- redeployment and qualification
- maybe shift elimination or partial line shutdowns

### Economic effect
This can reduce **variable labor cost** and some overhead, but it struggles to remove:
- plant-level fixed cost
- duplicate overhead
- underutilized depreciation
- energy/maintenance/security costs on excess footprint
- logistics inefficiency from keeping too many sites open

So the savings are real, but often **insufficient if overcapacity is structural**.

### Likely range
If executed aggressively and credibly, I would expect:

- **Annual recurring savings:** roughly **€300m to €800m**
- **One-time restructuring cost:** roughly **€200m to €700m**
- **Utilization improvement:** maybe **+5 to +12 percentage points** at affected plants, but not enough if the group is broadly overbuilt
- **Headcount reduction:** mostly voluntary, perhaps **5% to 12%** of domestic workforce over several years
- **ROS impact:** typically **modest**, maybe **+0.5 to +1.5 percentage points**, depending on volume recovery and pricing

### Main limitation
If the structural gap is larger than about **€1bn per year**, this scenario usually underdelivers unless:
- labor concessions are unusually deep,
- and capacity reduction is large enough to mimic closure economics.

That is rare without some form of site/line consolidation.

---

### B. Hybrid package
This means:
- no immediate full plant closure
- but real permanent capacity reduction by line, shift, product allocation, or function
- voluntary exits only
- temporary employee contribution
- management restraint
- owner contribution
- targeted consolidation of production and overhead
- possible mothballing, conversion, or site specialization

### Economic effect
This is the most important distinction: a hybrid can capture **most of the fixed-cost benefit of closure** if it removes actual capacity and overhead, even without shutting a plant outright.

The savings come from:
- fewer shifts
- fewer active lines
- higher utilization on the retained lines
- elimination of duplicate functions
- lower indirect labor and support cost
- better plant specialization

### Likely range
- **Annual recurring savings:** roughly **€700m to €1.6bn**
- **One-time restructuring cost:** roughly **€500m to €1.5bn**
- **Utilization improvement:** **+8 to +18 percentage points** at retained sites, if volumes are consolidated properly
- **Headcount reduction:** roughly **8% to 18%** over time, still mostly via voluntary exits, transfers, natural attrition, and retirement bridges if available
- **ROS impact:** roughly **+1.0 to +2.5 percentage points**

### Why this is the most plausible route
It can deliver a meaningful part of the fixed-cost takeout without the binary political and operational shock of immediate closures.  
It also gives management a better chance of getting board approval and labor cooperation.

### Risk
If “hybrid” is only a softer word for delay, then it fails. The package must include:
- hard line closures
- shift eliminations
- permanent product reallocations
- measurable overhead cuts

Without those, the savings are mostly temporary.

---

### C. Closure-led restructuring
This means:
- one or more plants shut
- production reallocated elsewhere
- associated headcount eliminated
- fixed cost removed decisively

### Economic effect
This is the strongest lever for permanent fixed-cost reduction. It can remove:
- full site overhead
- maintenance/security/utilities on closed capacity
- plant management and support cost
- logistics duplication
- some depreciation burden, depending on accounting treatment and asset disposition

### Likely range
- **Annual recurring savings:** roughly **€1.0bn to €2.0bn**
- **One-time restructuring cost:** roughly **€1.5bn to €3.5bn+**
- **Utilization improvement:** **+10 to +25 percentage points** for the surviving network, depending on which plant closes and how much volume is transferred
- **Headcount reduction:** roughly **15% to 25%** of domestic workforce over time, if closures are substantial
- **ROS impact:** potentially **+1.5 to +3.5 percentage points**, with the caveat that transition disruption can delay realization

### Why it is economically strongest
Because it directly removes fixed cost rather than trying to squeeze it through behavior change.

### Risk
This is where the industrial-economics trade-off is clearest:
- highest savings
- highest one-time cost
- highest execution risk
- highest disruption to supply chain and ramp-up
- biggest chance of labor conflict and delay

If implementation slips, the financial benefit is pushed out, while the one-time cost hits early.

---

## 3) Comparative assessment

### If the goal is to fix the cost gap quickly
**Closure-led** is strongest on paper.

### If the goal is to maximize probability of implementation
**Hybrid** is strongest overall.

### If the goal is to avoid major transition shock
**No-closure internal adjustment** is easiest to start, but least likely to be enough.

---

## 4) Fixed vs variable cost implications

### No-closure
- Mainly variable or semi-variable savings
- Some overhead reduction
- Limited fixed-cost relief
- High risk of “saving effort without saving structure”

### Hybrid
- Can remove substantial fixed cost if capacity is truly taken out
- Better balance of recurring savings and feasibility
- Most of the upside of closure with less binary disruption

### Closure-led
- Maximum fixed-cost removal
- Best for long-run unit cost if volumes are stable
- But transition cost is front-loaded and large

---

## 5) Timing through 2030

### No-closure
- Savings begin quickly if wage/working-time measures start in 2025–2026
- But full benefit may fade if concessions expire
- Needs recurring renegotiation to stay durable

### Hybrid
- Savings can start in 2025–2026 and become durable by 2027–2028
- Best balance between speed and permanence
- Requires disciplined implementation milestones

### Closure-led
- Usually slowest to realize full benefit
- Planning, labor conflict, tooling transfer, and ramp-up can consume 18–36 months
- Full savings often arrive late, though accounting improvements can appear earlier

---

## 6) Implementation risks

### No-closure risks
- insufficient savings
- concession fatigue
- savings evaporate when temporary measures expire
- no real resolution of structural overcapacity

### Hybrid risks
- partial measures produce partial savings
- internal compromise may not be deep enough
- complexity in plant-by-plant execution
- risk of “preserved capacity” still dragging margins

### Closure-led risks
- legal/governance blockage
- long industrial conflict
- production loss during transition
- supplier disruption
- reputational and retention damage
- higher upfront cash drain

---

## 7) My judgment on whether a no-closure scenario can close the gap through 2030

### Short answer
**Only under a strong set of assumptions.** Specifically:
1. labor-cost relief is deep enough and durable enough
2. capacity is reduced materially without formal closures
3. overhead is cut structurally, not just temporarily
4. product allocation improves utilization meaningfully
5. management and owners contribute enough to broaden burden-sharing
6. volume does not deteriorate further

### If those assumptions do not hold
Then **no-closure alone is unlikely to be enough**.

### More likely outcome
A **hybrid package** can probably do most of the needed work, and in many real industrial cases it is the only package that is both:
- economically meaningful
- and institutionally executable

---

## 8) Practical quantified conclusion

If I had to frame this in decision terms:

- **No-closure internal adjustment:** likely **insufficient alone**, unless the required gap is relatively small.
- **Hybrid package:** **most plausible** path to a credible 2030 outcome.
- **Closure-led:** **strongest cost solution**, but with the highest execution risk and transition cost.

---

## 9) What would make a no-closure package credible
A no-closure plan would need to show, by site:
- at least **10% to 15% permanent cost reduction** at the network level, not mostly temporary
- **utilization gains of several points** at multiple plants
- hard reduction in support functions and overhead
- a clear post-2030 cost structure
- and a one-time cost that is not so high it consumes most of the benefit

If it cannot show that, then it is not a true alternative to closures.
