We share the core line that the burden cannot fall on the workforce alone.

I agree with the Works Council on the non-negotiables:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no plant closures in Germany,
- no dismantling of the collective wage framework,
- no decisions taken over the heads of employees.

That said, I want to be very clear: if management is coming with a restructuring that is effectively “jobs and sites on the chopping block, workers pay for it, owners and management remain untouched,” then we will oppose it with full industrial force. That is not a fair transformation; it is shifting the crisis downward.

Where I am prepared to engage is on a genuine shared-sacrifice package, not on a one-sided cut program. For us, that means:

- a binding site and employment guarantee,
- no forced layoffs,
- capacity reductions through negotiated tools rather than closures,
- temporary labour-cost concessions only if they are clearly limited in time and scope,
- management, board, and owners also contributing visibly,
- investment and product commitments for the German sites.

Possible worker-side measures can include:
- suspending or reducing certain bonuses,
- a temporary wage contribution into a transformation fund,
- shorter working hours or flexible capacity tools,
- qualification and redeployment arrangements,
- voluntary exit options only, with strong safeguards.

But I need to state the limit plainly: if the company insists on plant closures or open-ended job destruction, there is no basis for agreement. And if the “solution” is simply wage cuts for the workforce while management keeps the strategic freedom and shareholders are spared, then we will not accept that either.

The union is ready to negotiate seriously, but only on a settlement that protects jobs and sites and distributes the pain fairly. If management wants industrial peace, it needs to put something substantial on the table first.
