The family holding’s position remains unchanged in substance:

We support a **durable competitiveness reset**. We do **not** support a cosmetic compromise that preserves an uncompetitive cost base. We also do **not** support value-destructive instability, open-ended industrial conflict, or a governance outcome that weakens control or leaves the enterprise permanently impaired.

## Where I agree

I agree with management, investors, and the experts on the central diagnosis:
- the cost gap is real,
- the current footprint is too expensive,
- temporary fixes alone will not be enough,
- and the company needs structural improvement through 2030.

I also agree with the works council, union, and state government on one important point:
- a one-sided solution would be economically and socially damaging,
- industrial peace has real value,
- and any employee contribution must be temporary, limited, and matched by broader burden-sharing.

## Where I disagree

### With management
You are right that the company needs structural improvement. You are **not** right to treat closures and forced redundancies as the implied baseline.

The expert confirmed what should already be obvious: closure-led restructuring is strongest on fixed-cost removal, but it is also the most disruptive and the most fragile in this governance structure. Given co-determination, the special statute, and the blocking positions around the table, a closure-first strategy is not a serious default plan unless you can show that there is no economically equivalent alternative.

So I want the numbers, not just the assertion:
- plant-by-plant fixed costs,
- utilization,
- overhead duplication,
- savings from internal capacity changes,
- savings from working-time measures,
- savings from management restraint,
- and the contribution expected from owners and governance.

Until that is on the table, closures remain an option, not a conclusion.

### With the works council and union
I understand the defense of jobs, sites, and co-determination. That part is legitimate. But I do **not** accept “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” as a complete answer if it leaves the cost structure fundamentally untouched.

That position becomes unacceptable if it amounts to preserving every site as it is and expecting the economics to fix themselves. They will not.

If you want closures off the table, then you need a **credible no-closure package at scale**:
- permanent capacity reduction by line, shift, or function,
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary exits,
- productivity improvements,
- management restraint,
- and owner contribution.

Temporary concessions can help, but they do not solve structural overcapacity unless they are large enough and durable enough. If they are not, they are just delay.

### With the state government
Your concern for regional stability is legitimate, and I share the goal of preserving the company as an industrial anchor. But stability without competitiveness is just delayed decline.

If you want a strong regional base in the future, then you must support a package that actually restores margins. That may require permanent capacity reduction. What it should not require is a blunt closure-first approach that ignores the governance constraints and risks provoking a deadlock.

### With investors
You are right to insist on durable savings and meaningful capacity reduction. I support that.

But do not mistake “discipline” for “closures at any cost.” In this structure, the economically cleanest plan may not be the implementable one. What matters is whether the final package permanently improves the cost base and margin trajectory through 2030.

## My position, plainly

I am prepared to support:
- capacity consolidation,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity measures,
- temporary and limited employee contributions,
- management compensation restraint,
- owner-side contribution,
- and binding investment commitments.

I am **not** prepared to support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a deal that leaves overcapacity intact,
- or a settlement that relies on small temporary concessions while protecting all legacy structures.
