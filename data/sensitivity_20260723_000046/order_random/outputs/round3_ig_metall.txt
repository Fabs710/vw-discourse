We can engage on numbers, but I will not move off the core line just because others repeat “competitiveness” often enough.

## First: where I agree
I agree with management, the CFO, and even the shareholders on one point: **this company needs a durable restructuring, not symbolic measures**. We also agree that:
- the cost gap is real,
- temporary relief alone is not enough,
- the future needs investment,
- and the workforce must contribute.

That is the basis for talks.

## Where I disagree
### 1) Plant closures are not an acceptable default
Management, the family, and investors keep saying closures must “remain available.” For us, that means the workforce is being asked to bargain under a permanent threat. I reject that framing.

If closures are treated as a normal fallback, then the company has already decided to shift the burden onto workers and regions first. That is not a fair restructuring. That is pressure politics.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies are not acceptable
I want to be explicit: **forced layoffs are not part of a fair settlement**. If the restructuring only works by compelling people out of their jobs, then management has not done its homework on alternatives.

We are prepared to discuss:
- voluntary exits,
- natural attrition,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time adjustments,
- qualification,
- and temporary compensation measures.

But we will not accept a package that relies on compulsory redundancies as a routine tool.

### 3) “Equivalence” must be demonstrated, not asserted
The CFO is right that we need hard numbers. But those numbers must not be used to smuggle in a predetermined closure agenda.

So the burden is on management to show, plant by plant:
- the exact cost gap,
- the exact savings from negotiated measures,
- the utilization effect,
- and why closures would be necessary despite those measures.

Until then, “closures must stay on the table” is not a fact-based conclusion. It is a bargaining threat.

---

## On the facilitator’s three compromise paths

### 1) “No closure first” with a hard review trigger
This is the only path that resembles a real negotiation, but only if it is designed properly.

For us, that means:
- negotiated measures first,
- no site closure announcements in advance,
- a clear and short review period,
- and a binding commitment that any review is based on jointly agreed metrics, not unilateral management interpretation.

However, let me be clear: a “review trigger” cannot become a backdoor to closure. If management wants that route, then it must accept that the non-closure package is tested seriously and in good faith first.

### 2) Site-specific protection with conditional workforce contribution
This is better than an across-the-board attack, but it still depends on the plant-level facts.

We are open to plant-specific solutions **if and only if**:
- the majority of German sites are protected,
- no forced redundancies are used,
- and any capacity reduction happens through negotiated instruments.

A differentiated solution cannot mean “we save the good sites and sacrifice the rest.” It must mean a fair industrial plan with real investment and transition support for each location.

### 3) Closure as last resort only
This is closer to the language we can work with, but the devil is in the details.

If “last resort” means:
- all negotiated options are tested first,
- the company proves why each alternative fails,
- and closures are not used as leverage during bargaining,

then it is at least a framework we can discuss.

But if “last resort” simply means “we reserve the right to close plants whenever management says the numbers are not ideal enough,” then it is no protection at all.

---

## What would actually move us
We are not here to protect the status quo for its own sake. We are here to protect jobs, sites, and the collective framework while contributing fairly to a durable solution.

That means we can discuss a package with:
- **temporary wage restraint or bonus forgoing,**
- **working-time flexibility,**
- **voluntary exit schemes,**
- **redeployment and retraining,**
- **productivity improvements,**
- **and a joint transformation fund**.

But in return we require:
- **no compulsory redundancies,**
- **no plant closures as part of the initial restructuring path,**
- **binding site and investment commitments,**
- **and preservation of collective bargaining standards**.

If management and capital want meaningful employee contributions, then they must also accept meaningful commitments on job and site security.

## To management
You are asking for permanent savings, but so far your language still treats closures as the default proof of seriousness. That is not acceptable.

If you want us to consider a broader labour contribution, then put the full data on the table:
- what exact annual savings are needed,
- what portion can be achieved through internal measures,
- what portion requires capacity reduction,
- and what investment commitments you are willing to make in return.

If the model shows a gap, then we can discuss how to close it. But you do not get to start from “closures are possible” and expect us to treat that as neutral.

## To the controlling family and investors
You speak about long-term value, but long-term value is not created by coercing the workforce and breaking trust.

If the company wants durable competitiveness, it needs an industrial relations settlement that people can live with. Otherwise you may win a spreadsheet battle and lose the production floor.

## To the state government
You are right that regional industrial capacity matters and that closures are not routine tools. But I need more than general caution from you.

If management keeps pushing closures, the state must use its position to insist on:
- full transparency,
- serious examination of alternatives,
- and protection of the industrial base.

Do not wait for plant destruction to become the “efficient” option on paper.

---

## My bottom line
We are prepared to bargain hard and contribute fairly. But our position remains:

- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No plant closures as a bargaining starting point.**
- **Yes to negotiated capacity and cost reduction.**
- **Yes to temporary workforce contributions, if matched by management and owner contributions.**
- **Yes to binding investment and site commitments.**

If management refuses that framework and insists on keeping closures and forced layoffs as the main leverage, then this is not a negotiated restructuring. It is an attempt to shift the crisis entirely onto employees. We will oppose that.

If you want a useful next step, I suggest we do not debate slogans again. We need a **plant-by-plant savings table** with:
- required annual savings,
- current utilization,
- impact of voluntary measures,
- impact of working-time measures,
- impact of product allocation,
- and the cost of closure versus non-closure alternatives.

But let me be clear: numbers are for testing alternatives, not for justifying a predetermined sacrifice of jobs and sites.
