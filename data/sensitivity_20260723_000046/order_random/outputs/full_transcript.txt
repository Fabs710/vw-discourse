## Typical economics of the options

### 1) Closures
From a financial standpoint, closures are the **largest and most durable** lever if a plant is genuinely structurally uneconomic.

**Advantages**
- Removes fixed costs permanently
- Eliminates excess capacity in one step
- Avoids “half measures” that leave overhead inflated
- Can materially improve utilization at remaining sites

**Costs / risks**
- High restructuring cost: severance, remediation, transfer, logistics, write-downs
- Long implementation lead time
- Labour disruption risk
- Potential loss of product flexibility and supply resilience
- If the plant also carries critical know-how or supplier adjacency, replacement cost can be underestimated

**Important point:**  
A closure is only economically justified if the **present value of avoided future losses and fixed costs** exceeds the one-time exit cost and the strategic loss of the site.

---

### 2) No closure, but capacity reduction through negotiated instruments
This is often the preferred compromise, but it only works if the problem is **moderate overcapacity**, not deep structural overhang.

Tools include:
- voluntary exits
- early retirement
- internal redeployment
- reduced working hours
- temporary pay restraint
- lower shift count
- product allocation shifts
- greater flexibility in staffing

**Advantages**
- Lower social and political friction
- Faster agreement is possible
- Preserves optionality and know-how
- Often cheaper upfront than closure

**Limits**
- Many measures are **temporary**
- Savings may be too small relative to the gap
- Utilization may remain low, so overhead per unit stays high
- If product allocation is not secured, the “saved” capacity just becomes idle capacity elsewhere

**Economic truth:**  
This approach works only if the company can reduce output and overhead enough that the remaining footprint runs at **sufficiently high utilization**. If not, it becomes a deferred problem.

## Labour-cost scenarios: what matters economically
The relevant question is not “Can employees contribute?” but “How much of the gap can labour realistically close?”

A useful planning framework is:

### A. Temporary labour contribution
Examples:
- bonus deferral
- temporary wage restraint
- reduced special payments
- suspended allowance increases

**Effect:**  
Good for short-term cash and P&L relief, but weak as a permanent competitiveness fix.

### B. Structural labour-cost reduction
Examples:
- longer working time for same pay
- lower entry pay for new cohorts
- removal of legacy premiums
- headcount reduction
- changes to overtime rules

**Effect:**  
More durable, but harder to agree and usually slower to realize.

### C. Productivity-led labour saving
Examples:
- fewer indirect staff
- higher line efficiency
- lower scrap/rework
- better scheduling
- less complexity in variants

**Effect:**  
This is often the best long-term lever, but it requires operational discipline and volume stability.

**Rule of thumb:**  
If the company needs a **multi-hundred-million-euro annual improvement**, then temporary concessions alone rarely suffice. You usually need:
- a material headcount reduction,
- simplification of the footprint,
- and a lasting productivity step-up.

## Why “no closures” can be economically feasible sometimes
It is feasible if all of the following are true:

- the cost gap is **manageable**, not extreme
- there is enough volume to keep all sites above efficient utilization
- a negotiated labour package can permanently reduce conversion cost
- product allocation can be rebalanced across plants
- one or more plants can be converted to higher-value work rather than closed
- management accepts a longer payback period

In that case, a combination of:
- shift reductions,
- voluntary exits,
- internal transfers,
- selective capex,
- and targeted wage restraint  
can produce a credible restructuring.

---

## Why “no closures” may fail
It likely fails if:

- one or more plants are fundamentally underutilized with no realistic product assignment
- the fixed-cost base is too high relative to expected volume
- labour concessions are temporary or modest
- management needs a fast and permanent ROS step-up
- the company is carrying duplicated overhead, logistics, or model complexity
- investments in EV and software require cash that cannot be freed otherwise

In that case, keeping every site open risks a **slow erosion of margin**, which is often worse than a hard restructuring because it consumes cash for years.

---

## What would count as a credible alternative to closures
A credible alternative would need all of the following:

1. **Quantified savings at or near the closure scenario**
   - not “some savings,” but comparable annualized cost reduction

2. **Permanent capacity correction**
   - fewer shifts, fewer lines, lower fixed overhead, lower complexity

3. **Headcount reduction of meaningful scale**
   - mostly via voluntary and attritional means, but large enough to matter

4. **Durable labour contribution**
   - not only a one-year concession

5. **Investment and product commitments**
   - otherwise the remaining footprint may still be underloaded

6. **A credible implementation timetable**
   - savings visible within 12–24 months, not only after several model cycles

[Expert: independent plant-level industrial plann — Round 3]
As an independent plant-level industrial planning assessment, the core question is:

**What would make a negotiated non-closure restructuring economically credible, versus requiring closure/downsizing as the only durable solution?**

## Expert assessment

A non-closure solution is economically credible only if it can deliver, at plant level and within a defined time window, **durable annual cost reduction roughly equivalent to the savings from the capacity removal that closure would achieve**, while preserving acceptable utilization and operational stability.

In practical terms, that means management would need to show all of the following:

### 1) A quantified structural gap
- A transparent plant-by-plant cost and capacity model.
- Clear identification of:
  - fixed-cost burden,
  - variable labor cost gap,
  - underutilization,
  - and product-mix disadvantages.
- The gap must be measured against a realistic benchmark, not a theoretical ideal.

### 2) Durable savings, not temporary deferral
Negotiated measures are credible only if they create savings that persist after the first contract cycle. Useful instruments include:
- headcount reduction through voluntary exits and attrition,
- redeployment to growing lines or functions,
- working-time reduction with flexible deployment,
- bonus/allowance moderation,
- productivity and line-balance improvements,
- product allocation changes that raise utilization,
- and simplification of shift and staffing patterns.

Measures that are **not** sufficient on their own:
- one-off wage freezes,
- short-term bonus waivers,
- temporary state or company subsidies,
- or deferred decisions without footprint change.

### 3) Utilization must remain structurally viable
A plant can only stay open if post-restructuring load factors remain high enough to support:
- efficient labor deployment,
- acceptable overhead absorption,
- and stable logistics.

If a plant remains chronically underloaded after all realistic product allocation options are applied, then a non-closure solution becomes weak. At that point, partial downsizing or conversion may be more credible than maintaining the full footprint.

### 4) The savings target must match the footprint problem
If the company’s issue is true overcapacity, then non-closure savings must do one of two things:
- either **remove equivalent capacity through internal means** such as line consolidation, shift elimination, or site conversion,
- or **raise output sufficiently** through product allocation to restore utilization.

If neither is feasible, then a non-closure deal will usually only postpone the decision.

## What I would regard as a credible test
A negotiated alternative should be judged credible only if it includes:

- **plant-level baseline model**
- **3–5 year cash and margin impact**
- **verified annual savings by measure**
- **headcount trajectory by site**
- **capacity/utilization target by site**
- **implementation milestones**
- **fallback trigger if savings are missed**

A serious review trigger should not be vague. It should specify:
- the KPI set,
- the measurement date,
- the minimum acceptable utilization,
- the minimum annual savings,
- and what happens if targets are missed.

## Practical threshold logic
Without the company’s actual data, I cannot give a numeric target with confidence. But analytically:

- If negotiated measures can close **most of the structural gap** and leave plants at sustainable utilization, non-closure restructuring is plausible.
- If negotiated measures close only **a minority of the gap**, or utilization remains weak, then the company is likely just buying time.
- If a site cannot reach a viable cost position even after realistic redeployment, worktime changes, and product allocation, then **partial downsizing or closure becomes the economically rational outcome**, regardless of governance preferences.

## Bottom line
A non-closure solution is credible only if it is **functionally equivalent** to the cost and capacity effect of closure, not merely politically preferable. The test is not whether the package feels balanced; the test is whether it produces:

- durable annual savings,
- viable plant utilization,
- and a footprint that can compete without recurring crisis measures.

If that cannot be shown at plant level, then the non-closure alternative is not industrially robust.

If you want, I can next convert this into a **simple decision matrix** for the round-3 negotiation: “credible non-closure,” “partial downsizing,” or “closure/exit required,” with the plant-level indicators under each.

## Minimum thresholds for a negotiated alternative to be economically credible

A non-closure restructuring is economically credible only if it delivers, **at plant level**, enough **durable annual savings** and **capacity correction** to bring the remaining footprint onto a sustainable margin path **without relying on repeated crisis measures**.

### 1) Savings threshold
As a rule of thumb, the negotiated package should close **the majority of the structural gap** — not just a temporary slice of it.

In practical terms, I would want to see:
- **at least 70–80% of the identified structural cost gap** closed through durable measures, or
- a package that leaves only a **small residual gap** that is clearly offset by product allocation, utilization gains, and overhead reduction.

If the package only covers, say, **30–50%** of the gap, that is usually not a restructuring; it is time-buying.

### 2) Utilization threshold
A plant remains credible if post-restructuring utilization is high enough to absorb fixed costs efficiently.

Indicative planning logic:
- **comfortably viable:** roughly **80%+ utilization** on a sustained basis,
- **borderline:** roughly **70–80%**,
- **structurally weak:** **below ~70%** unless there is a special strategic role, conversion path, or exceptional product logic.

Below that range, the overhead dilution usually becomes hard to fix with labor measures alone.

### 3) Timing threshold
Savings must arrive fast enough to matter for the company’s investment cycle.

A credible non-closure deal should:
- deliver most savings within **12–24 months**,
- and show a clear path to full effect within **no more than 2–3 years**.

If the payoff is pushed beyond that, the company risks funding the transition without seeing the operational relief in time.

### 4) Durability threshold
The savings must survive beyond the first labor agreement cycle.

That means:
- not just one-off bonuses forgone,
- not just a temporary wage freeze,
- but structural changes in headcount, staffing patterns, shift systems, product allocation, and overhead base.

### 5) Financial outcome threshold
At group or brand level, the package should plausibly move the business back toward its target return on sales through the planning horizon.

A realistic test is:
- does the package materially improve the operating margin trajectory over **3–5 years**,
- and does it reduce the probability of another restructuring round?

If not, then the “solution” is probably incomplete.

## What I would call a credible non-closure package
A package becomes credible if it combines:
- voluntary exits and natural attrition,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity and line-balance measures,
- product allocation that lifts utilization,
- and some labor-cost contribution,

**and** the resulting footprint shows:
- sustainable utilization,
- a durable annual savings level close to the structural gap,
- and a plausible return to target margins.

## What I would *not* consider credible
Not credible would be:
- temporary wage restraint only,
- symbolic headcount measures,
- a “review later” approach without hard metrics,
- or a site structure that remains chronically underloaded.

That would simply postpone closure or downsizing rather than replace it.

## 4. Genuine conflicts (structurally incompatible positions)
1. **Closure legitimacy**
   - Labor and the state reject closures as a starting point.
   - Management, the family, and investors insist closures must remain available.

2. **Compulsory redundancies**
   - Labor says no forced layoffs.
   - Capital-side actors will not rule them out in advance.

3. **Bargaining sequence**
   - Labor wants protection first, then numbers.
   - Management/capital want numbers first, then no guarantees.

4. **Scope of guarantees**
   - Labor wants binding job/site guarantees through 2030.
   - Management and owners view broad guarantees as potentially incompatible with competitiveness.

5. **Governance vs. flexibility**
   - The state and works council stress co-determination and statutory veto rights.
   - Management and investors want enough freedom to execute restructuring.

## 5. Areas of convergence
- All parties accept that the company has a real competitiveness and cost problem.
- Purely symbolic or short-term fixes are rejected.
- Most parties accept that some form of shared sacrifice is necessary.
- There is broad openness to voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, work-time adjustments, productivity improvements, and investment commitments.
- All sides agree that plant-level transparency is essential.
- There is recognition that future investment and product allocation must be part of any durable settlement.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker
- **Social stability vs. economic sufficiency:** preserving jobs and sites reduces conflict, but may leave the cost gap unresolved.
- **Credibility of restructuring vs. labor trust:** keeping closures and redundancies on the table strengthens management’s leverage, but undermines trust and may trigger conflict.
- **Speed vs. legitimacy:** faster action helps cash flow and investor confidence, but a rushed package may fail politically and industrially.
- **Uniform treatment vs. site differentiation:** a single rule is simpler, but site-by-site treatment better matches plant economics.
- **Guarantees vs. flexibility:** binding protections help secure labor buy-in, but may limit management’s ability to adjust if the business case deteriorates.

## 7. Integrative pathways
### A. Sequenced restructuring with a binding review gate
Implement a substantial first phase based only on negotiated measures, with no closure announcements or forced redundancies before a short review date. At the review, use jointly agreed KPIs to determine whether additional measures are needed.

### B. Site-tiered settlement
Classify plants by viability:
- viable sites get strong investment and job commitments,
- borderline sites get intensive restructuring support,
- structurally weak sites enter a protected transition process before any closure discussion.

### C. “No forced redundancies first” hierarchy
Adopt a formal sequence:
1. voluntary exits
2. attrition
3. redeployment/retraining
4. work-time flexibility
5. productivity gains
6. only then, if the quantified gap remains, renewed discussion of deeper capacity measures

### D. Conditional labor contribution with reciprocal commitments
Labor contributes through temporary restraint, flexibility, and productivity measures, while management/owners provide binding investment, transparency, and limited site guarantees. This can work only if the savings are quantified and durable.

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction
- The real impasse is not the diagnosis but the **sequence and enforceability** of restructuring: “protection first, then numbers” versus “numbers first, then no guarantees.”
- The expert clarified that the decisive question is **not whether closures are politically acceptable**, but whether a negotiated alternative is **economically equivalent** at plant level.
- The discussion revealed that closures are doing two different jobs: as a **real economic fallback** for capital-side actors and as a **symbolic coercion threat** for labor, which is why the same word triggers such different reactions.
- A potentially workable compromise exists only if it is **site-specific**, not company-wide; blanket rules are what make the positions mutually exclusive.
- Labor’s position became more nuanced over the rounds: it moved from general refusal to a willingness to accept a **technical test of durability**, provided closures are not pre-announced and management’s assumptions are transparent.
- The state’s role is not merely political support for workers; it is a **governance actor with a blocking minority**, which means any deal must be robust enough to survive statutory and shareholder constraints.
- Investors’ core concern is less the social form of restructuring than **credible margin restoration**; they will tolerate higher upfront concessions if the outcome is durable and quantified.
- The key practical bridge is a **hard review gate with measurable KPIs**, which emerged as the only mechanism that simultaneously preserves labor’s chance to prove alternatives and capital’s need for an enforceable fallback.
