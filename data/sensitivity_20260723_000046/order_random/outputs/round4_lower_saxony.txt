I will be direct.

## My position
I support a **durable restructuring**. I do **not** support a restructuring that normalizes plant closures or compulsory redundancies as the default answer, and I will not accept any package that sidelines the state’s co-determination and veto role.

The company needs to become competitive. But it must do so **without gutting regional employment** and without turning the state into a spectator.

---

## Where I agree

### With management, the family, investors, and the CFO
You are right that:
- the cost gap is real,
- the current structure is not sustainable as-is,
- temporary fixes alone are not enough,
- and this cannot be a symbolic peace deal.

I also agree that the discussion must be **plant-by-plant** and based on hard numbers. That is the only serious basis for any decision.

### With the union and works council
You are right that:
- workers cannot be asked to carry the entire burden,
- trust matters,
- and a settlement that destroys industrial relations will damage the company long term.

I also agree that voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time measures, productivity gains, qualification, and targeted labor contributions are all legitimate instruments.

---

## Where I disagree

### 1) I do not accept closures as a normal fallback
To management, the family, and investors: saying closures must “remain available” may sound like prudent realism, but in this company it is not neutral. It means the bargaining framework is built around the threat of plant loss.

That is not a starting point I can accept.

If a site is already being treated as expendable before negotiated alternatives are fully tested, then the burden has been shifted too far onto workers and the region. As anchor shareholder and political actor, I cannot support that.

### 2) I do not accept compulsory redundancies as the baseline
I understand the arithmetic argument. But arithmetic does not justify making forced layoffs the normal instrument of adjustment.

If the only credible path requires compulsory redundancies, then management has not yet demonstrated that it has exhausted:
- voluntary exits,
- internal mobility,
- redeployment,
- working-time arrangements,
- skill conversion,
- product allocation changes,
- and productivity improvements.

Those must come first.

### 3) I do not accept a deal that weakens co-determination
This is not a side issue. The state’s blocking minority and board role are there for a reason: to protect the long-term public and regional interest.

Any attempt to treat the state as a formality, or to push through irreversible plant decisions without genuine participation, would meet resistance. That is not an abstract principle; it is part of the governance structure of this company.

---

## On the expert and CFO thresholds
The expert and CFO are right that a non-closure solution must be **economically credible**, not just politically convenient.

I accept the logic that any negotiated package must:
- close a substantial part of the structural gap,
- restore viable utilization,
- and deliver durable savings within a reasonable timeframe.

But I will add this: the model must also show the **regional and social cost of closure**, not just the accounting effect. A decision that looks efficient in a spreadsheet but weakens the company’s industrial base, supplier network, and public legitimacy is not a sustainable solution.

## On the compromise paths

### 1) Sequenced restructuring with a binding review gate
This is the only route that is worth serious discussion, but only if the first phase is genuinely substantial and closures are not pre-announced.

For me, that means:
- negotiated measures first,
- no irreversible closure decisions at the outset,
- a fixed and short review period,
- jointly agreed metrics,
- and a real obligation to test whether the non-closure package works.

A review gate is acceptable only if it is a **test of alternatives**, not a delayed threat.

### 2) Site-tiered settlement
This is more plausible than an across-the-board attack, because not every site has the same economics.

But it must not become a “save the strong sites and sacrifice the weak ones” exercise. The weaker sites need:
- a credible industrial future,
- investment where viable,
- conversion options where needed,
- and serious transition support before anyone talks about closure.

### 3) “No forced redundancies first”
This is the right order of preference. I can support that as a hierarchy.

What I cannot accept is a structure where “first” simply means “until management decides it has waited long enough.” The hierarchy must be meaningful and enforceable.

---

## What would move me
I could support a tough negotiated package if it contains:
- a quantified and durable cost reduction,
- meaningful capacity adjustment through negotiated means first,
- voluntary exits and redeployment,
- working-time and compensation adjustments,
- a fair employee contribution,
- binding investment commitments,
- and a clear product strategy for the remaining German footprint.

That is a serious restructuring.

But it must preserve the company as a regional anchor, not hollow it out.

---

## What I will not support
I will not support:
- a blanket exclusion of closures if that prevents any real restructuring,
- a package that normalizes compulsory redundancies,
- a settlement that protects every site unchanged while leaving the company uncompetitive,
- or a process that marginalizes the state’s statutory role.

---

## To management
You want flexibility. I understand that. But flexibility is not the same as unilateral leverage.

If you want my support, bring:
- transparent plant-level data,
- credible non-closure savings,
- a serious investment plan,
- and proof that closures are not being used as the first instrument of pressure.

---

## To the union and works council
You are right to insist on fairness and on protecting jobs. But a refusal to engage with plant-level economics will not help your case.

If you want closures and forced layoffs off the table, then the non-closure package must be strong enough to stand on its own. I will support that if the numbers justify it.

---

## Bottom line
My position is unchanged:
- **I support restructuring.**
- **I support shared sacrifice.**
- **I do not accept plant closures or compulsory redundancies as a default answer.**
- **I will support a non-closure solution only if it is economically credible, durable, and consistent with the state’s regional and governance responsibilities.**

That is the standard I will apply.
