We can engage on a structured package, but let me be equally clear: **I will not support a settlement that rules out closures or compulsory redundancies in all circumstances.** That would not be stewardship; it would be a promise to preserve an uncompetitive footprint even if the numbers say otherwise.

## Where I agree
I agree with the union, the works council, the state, management, and the CFO on the core diagnosis:
- the company has a real competitiveness problem,
- the cost gap is structural,
- temporary fixes alone are insufficient,
- and durable investment in future products matters.

I also agree that a **pure shock approach** would be value-destructive if it triggers prolonged conflict, brand damage, or execution chaos. That is not in the interest of the family holding either.

## Where I disagree
### 1) “No closures” cannot be an absolute
The union and works council are asking for a framework in which plant closures are excluded from the outset. I cannot accept that.

If the footprint is structurally too large for the market and the cost base remains above benchmark after serious negotiation, then refusing to adjust capacity is simply postponing the problem. That destroys value, weakens margins, and reduces the company’s ability to invest in the future.

So my position remains:
- **closures should not be the first choice,**
- but **they must remain available if negotiated alternatives do not close the gap.**

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be unconditional
I understand the workforce’s concern. But if we make forced redundancies impossible in all cases, we may be promising something the economics cannot support.

I prefer voluntary exits, redeployment, attrition, working-time changes, and productivity gains. But if those measures do not deliver the required structural effect, then management cannot be trapped by an absolute promise that preserves every role regardless of business reality.

### 3) Shared sacrifice must be real, not rhetorical
I agree with the expert and the CFO that the analysis must be plant-by-plant. That is exactly right.

But I will not accept a model where:
- workers are asked for permanent concessions,
- management is asked for only symbolic restraint,
- and owners/investors are expected to absorb the value loss without a credible restructuring path.

A real deal must produce:
- durable cost reduction,
- meaningful capacity adjustment,
- and a credible path back to target margins.

## On the union’s position
I respect the fact that the union is engaging on numbers rather than slogans. That is better than denial.

But I do not accept the claim that closure is merely a bargaining threat. If a site is structurally uneconomic, then the possibility of closure is not intimidation; it is the consequence of arithmetic. The burden is on the non-closure proposal to prove it can match the economic effect, not on us to pretend the issue does not exist.

And I should say plainly: strikes and prolonged confrontation may create pressure, but they do not create competitiveness. They can easily deepen the damage they claim to resist.

## On the works council
The works council is right to insist on transparency, site-level data, and industrial logic. That is fair.

But transparency does not imply immunity. A site-by-site model may well show that different plants require different treatment. If so, then differentiated outcomes are not betrayal; they are responsible governance.

What I cannot support is a settlement that protects all sites equally regardless of economics. That is not a durable industrial policy; it is a freeze.

## On the regional state government
I respect the state’s role and its blocking position under the statute. But that role is not to prevent all structural adaptation. It is to protect a viable industrial base.

If a plant remains uneconomic after all realistic alternatives, then the state should not force the company into permanent underperformance simply to avoid difficult regional consequences. The region’s long-term interest is a strong company, not a preserved appearance of stability.

## On management
I support management’s insistence on hard numbers and plant-level analysis. That is essential.

But I also want to be clear: management should not treat this as an opportunity for a confrontational clean sweep. If the company wants the backing of the controlling shareholder, it must show:
- a disciplined, quantified restructuring case,
- a credible labour contribution,
- protection against uncontrolled instability,
- and a serious plan for future investment.

You do not get a blank cheque. You do get support for a fact-based, legally robust, operationally executable restructuring.

## On the CFO / expert thresholds
The threshold logic is useful:
- close most of the structural gap,
- restore sustainable utilization,
- and deliver savings quickly enough to matter.

That is the right test.

What I need to see is not just a generic margin ambition, but a plant-by-plant plan showing:
- current utilization,
- current cost position,
- the savings from negotiated measures,
- the savings from downsizing or closure,
- the one-time costs,
- and the 3–5 year margin effect.

Without that, nobody can honestly claim that a non-closure solution is equivalent.

## What I would support
I would support a settlement if it includes:
- a quantified and durable cost reduction,
- meaningful capacity adjustment,
- voluntary exits and redeployment first,
- working-time and compensation measures,
- a fair employee contribution,
- and binding investment commitments for future products.

That could be a non-closure solution **only if** it is truly equivalent in effect.

## What I will not support
I will not support:
- a blanket exclusion of closures,
- an unconditional ban on compulsory redundancies,
- a package that keeps every site unchanged while leaving the company uncompetitive,
- or a short-term truce that merely postpones the crisis.

## My boundary
I will be explicit: I will not support any outcome that permanently impairs the enterprise’s value or traps the owners in a governance position where they cannot allow necessary restructuring. That is not a hidden threat; it is the line between stewardship and self-damage.

## [SUMMON: independent plant-level industrial planning model]
We need the plant-level model on the table, with:
- utilization by site,
- direct and indirect cost per vehicle,
- annual savings from each negotiated measure,
- one-time restructuring costs,
- and margin impact over 3–5 years.

That is the only sensible basis for deciding whether the non-closure alternative is truly credible or whether capacity reduction must be part of the package.

## Bottom line
- **I support a negotiated restructuring.**
- **I support shared sacrifice.**
- **I do not accept categorical bans on closures or compulsory redundancies.**
- **I will support a non-closure solution only if it is financially equivalent, durable, and transparent at plant level.**

That is the standard I will apply.
