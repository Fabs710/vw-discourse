The key question for my remit is the CFO / industrial-planning one: **when is a negotiated non-closure solution economically credible?**

## Short answer
A non-closure package is only credible if, at plant level, it can deliver:

1. **a large share of the required structural savings**  
2. **within a short enough horizon to matter for the business case**  
3. **without leaving residual overcapacity so large that the cost gap simply reappears**  
4. **and with utilization high enough that the remaining footprint is actually sustainable**

If those conditions are not met, then a negotiated alternative may be socially desirable, but it is **not economically equivalent** to closure / downsizing.

---

## Practical threshold logic

I would frame credibility around four tests:

### 1) Savings coverage
A negotiated package should close **most** of the structural gap, not just a small fraction.

- As a rule of thumb, I would want to see **at least ~70–80% of the recurring annual gap** covered by measures that are durable, not one-off.
- If the package covers only, say, 30–50% of the gap, then the company has merely deferred the problem.

### 2) Time to cash effect
The measures must reduce cost quickly enough to support the turnaround.

- I would expect meaningful effect within **12–24 months**.
- If savings are back-end loaded beyond that, the company risks burning cash before the restructuring lands.

### 3) Utilization / footprint adequacy
The post-deal footprint must be sized to realistic demand.

- For auto assembly and core component capacity, a site running **well below roughly 70% utilization** for an extended period is usually a warning sign.
- Exact thresholds vary by plant type, mix, and fixed-cost structure, but chronically weak utilization means the overhead absorption problem persists.
- In simple terms: if the footprint is still too big after the negotiated measures, the cost base will remain structurally too high.

### 4) Unit economics after measures
The remaining plants must be competitive on a unit-cost basis.

You need to look at:
- direct labour cost per vehicle
- indirect overhead per vehicle
- logistics cost
- changeover / mix loss
- energy and maintenance burden
- depreciation from any new investment

If the post-package cost per unit is still materially above internal target and benchmark peers, then the solution is not durable.

---

## What I would want to see in the model
For each plant, the model should include:

- current annual output
- installed capacity
- utilization rate
- direct and indirect cost per vehicle
- fixed cost base
- savings from:
  - voluntary exits
  - attrition
  - redeployment
  - working-time changes
  - wage / bonus restraint
  - productivity measures
  - product allocation changes
- one-time restructuring costs
- payback period
- 3–5 year EBIT / margin effect

That is the only way to judge whether non-closure is really equivalent.

---

## My rule of thumb
A negotiated alternative becomes economically credible when it can show all of the following:

- **durable recurring savings close to the majority of the gap**
- **payback in roughly 2–3 years or less**
- **post-restructuring utilization at a sustainable level**
- **no major site remaining structurally underabsorbed**
- **no hidden dependence on repeated future concessions**

If a package needs repeated renegotiation every few years to stay afloat, it is not a solution; it is a pause.

---

## Important caveat
These are **industrial-planning thresholds**, not legal or labor-relations judgments. I am not opining on whether closures or layoffs are permissible, only on whether a negotiated alternative is **economically sufficient**.
