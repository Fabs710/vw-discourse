Yes. On the industrial-planning question, the current discussion can be translated into a concrete test for whether a negotiated non-closure solution is economically credible.

## My assessment

A non-closure package is credible only if it does **all four** of the following at plant level:

1. **Closes most of the structural recurring cost gap**
2. **Does so within a short enough horizon to matter**
3. **Leaves a footprint that is actually utilized at sustainable levels**
4. **Avoids depending on repeated future concessions**

If those conditions are not met, the package may be socially preferable to some parties, but it is **not equivalent** to a deep structural restructuring.

## Practical threshold framework

### 1) Recurring savings coverage
For a serious negotiated alternative, I would expect it to cover roughly **70–80% of the recurring annual gap** through durable measures.

Durable means things like:
- permanent or multi-year workforce reduction through voluntary exits and attrition
- lasting working-time or shift-system changes
- structural productivity gains
- product allocation improvements
- permanent overhead reductions

Measures that are only temporary — one-year wage restraint, deferred bonuses, short-term freezes — do not count fully toward durability.

If the package only covers, say, **30–50%** of the gap, it is not enough.

### 2) Timing
The savings need to arrive fast enough to matter.

A workable target is:
- **visible effect within 12–24 months**
- not back-loaded so far that the company burns cash before the benefits arrive

If the cost relief comes too late, the restructuring is not operationally credible.

### 3) Utilization after restructuring
A plant that remains chronically underloaded is still a structural problem.

As a rule of thumb:
- **below about 70% utilization** for a core assembly or major production site is usually a warning sign
- the exact threshold depends on plant type, mix, automation, and fixed-cost structure

The key question is whether the remaining footprint can absorb overheads without constant distress. If not, the footprint is still too large.

### 4) Unit economics after measures
You would want the post-deal plant economics to show:
- lower direct labour cost per vehicle
- lower indirect cost per vehicle
- better fixed-cost absorption
- lower logistics and complexity burden
- a credible path to target margin

If the residual cost per vehicle remains materially above benchmark, the plan is not durable.

## What the plant-level model should contain

For each site, I would want:

- annual output
- installed capacity
- utilization rate
- direct labour cost per vehicle
- indirect overhead per vehicle
- fixed cost base
- energy and maintenance burden
- savings from:
  - voluntary exits
  - natural attrition
  - redeployment
  - working-time changes
  - wage/bonus restraint
  - productivity measures
  - product allocation changes
- one-time restructuring costs
- payback period
- 3–5 year EBIT / margin trajectory

That is the minimum basis for deciding whether a non-closure package is economically strong enough.

## My rule of thumb

A negotiated alternative is economically credible if it can show:

- **most of the recurring gap closed**
- **payback in roughly 2–3 years or less**
- **post-restructuring utilization at a sustainable level**
- **no major site left structurally underabsorbed**
- **no reliance on repeated future concessions**

If those are not true, then the package is only a delay mechanism.

## Bottom line

From a plant-level industrial-planning perspective:

- **Negotiated alternatives can be credible**
- but only if they deliver **durable cost reduction and capacity correction at scale**
- and only if the resulting footprint is **sustainably utilized**
