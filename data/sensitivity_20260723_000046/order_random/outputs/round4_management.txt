We have now had four rounds, and the positions are clear. I will be equally clear.

## Where I agree

I agree with the union, the works council, the state, the family, the investors, and the expert on one central point:

**This company has a real structural competitiveness problem, and it cannot be fixed with symbolism or a temporary truce.**

I also agree that:
- the cost gap is real,
- the solution must be durable,
- plant-level transparency is necessary,
- future investment and product allocation matter,
- and a disorderly conflict would damage value.

So yes: we are aligned on the diagnosis.

## Where I disagree

### 1) “No closures” cannot be an absolute
To the union, works council, and state: I understand the social and political objection to closures. But I do **not** accept a blanket rule that German plant closures are excluded under all circumstances.

If the footprint remains structurally too large for the market and the cost base remains above benchmark, then refusing to adjust capacity is not protection. It is delay. And delay burns cash, weakens margins, and reduces our ability to invest in the future.

Let me be precise:
- **I do not want closures as the first choice.**
- **I do insist that closures remain available if negotiated alternatives do not close the gap.**

That is not brinkmanship. It is responsible restructuring.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” also cannot be unconditional
I understand why labor wants to avoid forced layoffs. But if we make that an absolute rule, then we may be promising something the economics cannot sustain.

If voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time changes, and productivity gains are enough, then I prefer them. If they are not enough, then the company cannot be trapped by a promise that preserves every position regardless of economic reality.

### 3) Shared sacrifice must be measurable
I do not accept a deal where workforce contribution is defined vaguely, while the company is expected to absorb the full burden of future investment and competitiveness.

A credible settlement must contain:
- durable cost reduction,
- real capacity adjustment,
- measurable labour-cost contribution,
- and clear investment commitments.

Without that, this is not restructuring. It is postponement.

---

## To the Industrial Union

You keep saying closures and compulsory redundancies are red lines. I hear that. But red lines do not change arithmetic.

If you want those tools off the table, then bring a package that is **economically equivalent** in durable annual savings and capacity correction. Not temporary relief. Not a pause. Equivalent structural effect.

That means:
- meaningful voluntary exits,
- substantial productivity gains,
- working-time flexibility,
- a real labour-cost contribution,
- and a footprint that becomes competitive without recurring crisis measures.

Right now, I do **not** see evidence that your package achieves that at sufficient scale. “No closures” is not a plan. It is a demand.

And one more thing: warning of strikes is not a substitute for a viable restructuring proposal. Prolonged industrial action hurts output, customers, and the brand. That does not strengthen your position; it weakens the company we all depend on.

---

## To the Works Council

I respect that you are engaging on plant-level economics rather than slogans. That is the right basis for discussion.

But transparency does not create immunity. A plant-level model may well show that different sites require different treatment. If so, differentiated outcomes are not betrayal; they are responsible governance.

What I cannot support is a settlement that protects all sites equally regardless of economics. That is not a durable industrial policy; it is a freeze.

And let me be explicit: co-determination gives the workforce a strong say. It does not give anyone a veto over arithmetic.

---

## To the Regional State Government

I respect the state’s role and the blocking-minority position under the statute. But that role is not to prevent all structural adaptation. It is to protect a viable industrial base.

If a plant remains uneconomic after all realistic alternatives, then the state should not force the company into permanent underperformance simply to avoid difficult regional consequences. The region’s long-term interest is a strong company, not a preserved appearance of stability.

---

## To the Controlling Family and Investors

I agree with you more than the labor side would like to admit: a cosmetic fix is unacceptable.

But I also need your support for an outcome that is operationally executable, not just theoretically severe. If we are serious about restoring returns, then the company needs:
- durable capacity reduction,
- durable labour-cost reduction,
- and room to fund future products and technology to 2030.

I will not support a compromise that looks prudent on paper but leaves the business structurally underpowered.

---

## On the expert’s threshold logic

The expert is right: a negotiated alternative is only credible if it closes most of the structural gap, restores sustainable utilization, and delivers savings fast enough to matter.

I accept that logic.

But I also need to state the reverse: if the plant-level model shows that negotiated measures cannot close the gap to a sustainable level, then refusing closures and stronger workforce adjustment would simply institutionalize underperformance.

So the test is not whether a non-closure package sounds fair. The test is whether it actually fixes the cost base.

---

## What I would support

I would support a settlement if it contains:
- a quantified and durable cost reduction,
- meaningful capacity adjustment,
- voluntary exits and redeployment first,
- working-time and compensation adjustments,
- a fair but real employee contribution,
- and binding investment commitments for future products.

That package can be non-closure **only if** it is truly equivalent in effect.

---

## What I will not support

I will not support:
- a deal that rules out closures in all circumstances,
- a deal that rules out compulsory redundancies regardless of the numbers,
- a package that protects every site unchanged while leaving the company uncompetitive,
- or a short-term truce that merely postpones the crisis.

---

## [SUMMON: independent plant-level industrial planning model]

We need an independent, plant-by-plant model comparing:
- annual savings from negotiated measures,
- annual savings from closure or partial downsizing,
- one-time restructuring costs,
- and margin impact over 3–5 years.

That model needs to answer, site by site:
1. What is the cost gap?
2. What is the utilization level?
3. What is the durable savings potential of non-closure measures?
4. What is the economics of closure or downsizing?
5. What mix gets us back to target margins fast enough to matter?

Without that, this discussion remains too political and too vague.

---

## Bottom line

My position is unchanged:

- **I support a negotiated restructuring.**
- **I support shared sacrifice.**
- **I do not accept categorical bans on closures or compulsory redundancies.**
- **I will support a non-closure solution only if it is financially equivalent and durable.**

That is the standard I will apply. Not slogans, not pressure, not wishful thinking. If the alternative can meet that bar, I will back it. If it cannot, then we must use the tools necessary to restore competitiveness.
