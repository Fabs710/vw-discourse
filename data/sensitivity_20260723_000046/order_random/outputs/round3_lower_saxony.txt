I will speak plainly.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family, investors, and the CFO on one essential point: **the company needs a durable restructuring, not a cosmetic truce**. The cost and capacity problem is real, and I will not defend a status quo that leaves the company uncompetitive.

I also agree that:
- temporary concessions alone are not enough if the gap is structural,
- investment and product allocation matter,
- and any serious package must be quantified plant by plant.

## Where I disagree
### 1) I do not accept plant closures as a normal restructuring instrument
To management, the family, and investors: I understand why you keep saying closures must “remain available.” But for me, that is not a neutral technical position. In this company and in this region, closures are a **last resort** only.

If closures become the default fallback, then the restructuring has already shifted too much of the burden onto the region, the workforce, and the industrial base. That is not acceptable to me.

### 2) I do not accept compulsory redundancies as the baseline
I understand the CFO’s point that structural adjustment may require headcount reduction. But there is a difference between reduction and forced layoffs.

I will support:
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time flexibility,
- and temporary compensation measures.

I will **not** support a package that makes compulsory redundancies the normal instrument of adjustment. That would be socially destructive and politically irresponsible.

### 3) I do not accept a restructuring that sidelines the state’s veto role
Let me be direct: the state’s blocking-minority stake and co-determination role are not symbolic. They exist to protect regional interests, jobs, and long-term industrial stability.

Any attempt to push through a package by treating the state as a marginal actor would meet resistance. That is not a threat; it is the reality of governance here.

## On the arguments made by others

### To management
You are right that the company needs structural cost reduction. But you are still not entitled to assume that closures and forced layoffs are the first serious answer.

Before you demand irreversible measures, you need to show:
1. the exact cost gap,
2. the exact capacity problem,
3. what savings are achievable through negotiated means,
4. and why a non-closure path is insufficient.

Until then, “closures must remain on the table” is a bargaining stance, not a demonstrated necessity.

### To the controlling family
I hear your insistence that the footprint must not be frozen regardless of economics. I accept that in principle. But long-term stewardship also means preserving the company’s industrial base, not just optimizing the next margin target.

A hard line on flexibility is one thing. A willingness to sacrifice sites and regional employment too quickly is another. That I will not support.

### To the institutional investors
You are right to demand durability and implementation certainty. I share that concern. But I will not call a deal durable if it is only durable because it breaks trust and destabilizes industrial relations. That is not value creation; it is delayed conflict.

### To the union and works council
You are right to insist that this cannot be a one-sided burden shift onto workers. I support that.

But I also need to be frank: a simple declaration of “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” is not yet a restructuring plan. If you want those tools off the table, you must show a package that is economically serious enough to preserve competitiveness without them.

---

## What would move me
I could support a tough negotiated package if it has all of the following:
- a **plant-level quantified cost and capacity model**,
- a **durable labour-cost contribution**,
- **voluntary and reversible workforce instruments first**,
- **binding investment and product commitments**,
- and a **credible path back to target margins**.

That is the standard.

## What I will not support
I will not support:
- a package that preserves every site unchanged,
- a blanket exclusion of closures under all circumstances,
- an unconditional ban on compulsory redundancies regardless of the numbers,
- or a settlement that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive while claiming success because industrial peace was preserved.

---

## My position on the compromise ideas
### “No closure first” with review trigger
This is the only idea on the table that is worth serious discussion, but only if the review is real, time-bound, and based on jointly agreed metrics. It must not become a backdoor to endless delay.

### Site-specific protection
This is more promising than an across-the-board defense of the entire footprint. Different plants have different economics. I would consider differentiated treatment **if** it preserves the company as a regional anchor and produces durable savings.

### Closure as last resort
That is essentially my position already. But “last resort” must mean the negotiated alternatives have been fully tested and shown insufficient, not merely inconvenient.

---

## My boundary
I will **not** support any outcome that:
- guts regional employment,
- closes plants in the state without exhausting alternatives,
- weakens the company’s role as a regional economic anchor,
- or removes the state’s co-determination and veto role from the process.

That is my line.

## Practical next step
We need a **plant-by-plant model**, not more slogans. It should show:
- current utilization,
- direct and indirect cost per vehicle,
- the savings from voluntary measures,
- the savings from working-time and productivity measures,
- the effect of product allocation,
- and the closure versus non-closure economics.

## Bottom line
I support restructuring.  
I support shared sacrifice.  
I support tough negotiations.

But I do **not** accept closures and forced redundancies as the starting answer, and I will not be pushed into that position by repeated assertions alone. If the non-closure alternative cannot be shown to be economically credible, then we must revisit the diagnosis — not immediately jump to dismantling plants and jobs.
