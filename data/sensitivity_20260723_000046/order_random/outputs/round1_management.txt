I’ll respond directly and clearly.

We all agree on one thing: **the company needs a durable restructuring, not a cosmetic fix**. On that point, I welcome the acknowledgement from the family and the state that the cost base, capacity, and future investment capacity all have to be addressed. That is the right diagnosis.

But I need to be equally clear on the core issue: **the current German footprint is structurally too expensive and too large for the market we are operating in**. If we refuse to deal with that reality, then we do not protect jobs in the long run — we erode the company’s competitiveness, investment power, and ultimately its future in Germany.

## To the industrial union
I understand your demand for no compulsory redundancies and no plant closures. I also understand why you want guarantees. But I cannot accept those as absolute red lines.

Why not?

- **If we rule out closures and forced reductions in advance, we remove the only credible pressure that can produce a structural cost reduction.**
- **“Shared sacrifice” cannot mean only temporary measures and internal redeployment while the cost base remains above target.**
- **Preserving every site and every legacy arrangement unchanged is not a restructuring; it is delay.**

We are not talking about punishing the workforce. We are talking about securing a viable industrial base for 2030. That requires hard choices.

That said, I am not insisting that closures are the preferred first choice. I am saying they must remain a real option unless an alternative delivers **equivalent, durable savings**. If the union can bring a package that genuinely reduces capacity and labour costs on a permanent basis, we can talk. But it must be measurable, binding, and sufficient.

## To the regional state government
I take seriously your point that closures are a last resort, not a casual lever. I do not dismiss the regional responsibility or the company’s role as an anchor employer. But I also have to say: **a veto in practice cannot mean a veto over adjustment**.

If every meaningful capacity correction is blocked because it has social consequences, then the company will lose competitiveness faster and the consequences will be worse. The regional interest is not served by preserving every structure until the business deteriorates further.

I am open to negotiated alternatives to closures. In fact, I would prefer them if they are real. But they must meet three conditions:

1. **They must cut capacity materially.**
2. **They must lower labour costs durably.**
3. **They must restore margin, not just postpone the problem.**

If those conditions are not met, then insisting on “no closures” is not a solution — it is denial.

## What management needs
Let me be concrete. A package that can move us must include:

- a **substantial and durable labour-cost contribution**;
- **capacity reduction** sufficient to remove structural overhang;
- **workforce adjustment** that is real, not symbolic;
- **investment commitments** tied to the future product cycle;
- and a settlement that gives the company room to restore return on sales.

That means I am not looking for a short-term freeze, a staged delay, or a deal that preserves today’s structure and hopes for a better market tomorrow. The market may not deliver that.

## What I will not accept
I will not support a package that:

- keeps all sites untouched,
- eliminates compulsory redundancies in name while leaving overcapacity in place,
- offers only temporary savings,
- or shifts the full burden onto management and owners without a material workforce contribution.

That would not be a restructuring. It would be a postponement of the crisis.

## Where I am open
I remain open on the **method**, not on the **outcome**.

So yes:
- negotiated capacity reduction,
- working-time instruments,
- redeployment,
- voluntary exit schemes,
- qualification measures,
- and a serious labour contribution

are all possible routes.

But they must add up to a package that is structurally equivalent to the scale of the problem. If they do not, then we are back to the need for closures and deeper measures.

## Bottom line
My position is straightforward:

- **We need a durable reduction in cost and capacity.**
- **Plant closures are not my first preference, but they cannot be excluded if no equivalent alternative exists.**
- **No compulsory redundancies is not a viable absolute if the company’s structural competitiveness is at stake.**
- **We need real contributions from all sides, including the workforce.**

If the other stakeholders want to avoid closures and forced job cuts, they need to bring forward a package that actually solves the structural problem — not one that merely softens it.
