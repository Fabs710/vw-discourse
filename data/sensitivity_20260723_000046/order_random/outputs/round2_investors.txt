We appreciate the CFO’s attempt to put this on an economic footing. That is exactly where this discussion needs to be. From our perspective, the question is not whether the restructuring is painful — it is whether it restores **durable, risk-adjusted value** or merely postpones deterioration.

## Where we agree
We agree with management, the family, and the CFO on the core diagnosis:

- the current German cost base is too high,
- the company’s footprint likely includes structural overcapacity,
- and a cosmetic settlement would be value-destructive.

We also agree with the CFO that **temporary concessions alone are not enough** if the cost gap is large. If the savings are not durable, shareholders just end up financing the same problem again in a few years.

## Where we disagree
### 1) “No closures” cannot be an absolute
We do **not** accept the union’s and works council’s position that plant closures are categorically off the table.

From a shareholder perspective, that is precisely the kind of outcome that would leave the cost base structurally uncompetitive and margins impaired. If the plant network remains too large for the demand base, then forcing all adjustment into temporary work-time measures, voluntary exits, and soft concessions is not enough.

If there is a credible alternative that produces equivalent permanent savings, we are open to it. But we do not accept a settlement that protects every site first and asks the balance sheet to absorb the consequences later.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” also cannot be absolute
We understand the social and industrial-relations logic behind avoiding forced layoffs. But again, as an absolute rule this is not acceptable if it prevents the company from achieving the necessary structural reset.

We prefer voluntary exits, redeployment, and attrition where they are sufficient. But if they are not sufficient, then the company cannot be trapped by a commitment that preserves every job regardless of economic reality.

### 3) We do not support prolonged conflict
We also do not support a drawn-out confrontation that destroys value through strikes, uncertainty, and delayed implementation.

A bad compromise is not better than a hard decision if the bad compromise leaves the business underpowered and drags out the pain. But an open-ended labor conflict is also value-destructive. Deal certainty matters.

## What would move us
The CFO’s framework is the right one. We would support the solution that credibly delivers:

1. **durable cost reduction**,  
2. **meaningful capacity adjustment**,  
3. **a clear improvement in margins / return on sales**, and  
4. **implementation certainty**.

That means we need a package that is not just politically defensible, but economically equivalent to the problem.

## On the concrete package options
### To the union and works council
Your package may be a starting point, but right now it still sounds too heavily weighted toward preserving the footprint.

We would need to see:
- the exact cost gap,
- the amount of capacity you are actually willing to remove,
- the size and duration of the labour contribution,
- and whether the remaining structure is truly competitive.

If the answer is “we can preserve all sites with enough flexibility and goodwill,” we are skeptical. That often proves temporary, not structural.

### To management
We are not asking for shock therapy. We are asking for a **credible, quantified restructuring case**.

The next step should be a plant-by-plant model, as the CFO suggested, showing:
- current utilization,
- cost per vehicle,
- closure economics,
- and the margin impact of negotiated alternatives.

Without that, the debate is still too abstract. We do not need rhetoric; we need a defensible capital-allocation case.

### To the state
We respect the regional role, but regional protection cannot be allowed to hard-cap strategic adjustment. If the evidence shows that one or more sites are structurally uneconomic, then refusing to confront that reality just shifts the loss into future value destruction.

The regional interest is not served by preserving every plant if the result is a weaker company and less investment capacity.

## Our limit
There is one outcome we will not support: a “settlement” that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive while claiming success because industrial peace was preserved.

That would mean:
- margins remain below target,
- the share price continues to reflect chronic underperformance,
- and management has simply bought time rather than fixed the business.

That is not acceptable to us as shareholders.

## What we would back
We would back a tough, negotiated settlement if it includes:

- a quantified and durable reduction in costs,
- a material capacity correction,
- workforce contributions that are real, not symbolic,
- and a credible path back to target margins within a reasonable timeframe.

If that requires closures, then closures must stay available. If a non-closure solution truly matches the economics, we are open to it. But the burden of proof is on the proponents of the alternative, not on shareholders to accept optimism.

## Bottom line
We are prepared to support decisive restructuring. We are **not** prepared to support a cosmetic compromise, nor a settlement that rules out the very tools needed to restore competitiveness.

If the final package does not credibly close the cost and capacity gap, we will regard it as value-destructive.
