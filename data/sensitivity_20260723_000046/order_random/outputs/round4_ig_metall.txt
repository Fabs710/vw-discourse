We can engage on a structured package, but let me be clear: **I am not moving off the core line because management, owners, or investors repeat “competitiveness” and “durability” often enough.**

## Where I agree
I agree with the expert, the CFO, and even parts of management on the basic diagnosis:
- the cost gap is real,
- the restructuring must be durable,
- temporary stopgaps alone are not enough,
- and the workforce must contribute.

That is exactly why we submitted a counter-proposal in the first place. We are not defending the status quo.

## Where I disagree

### 1) Plant closures are not an acceptable default
To management, the family, and investors: saying closures must “remain available” sounds technical, but in practice it means the workforce is bargaining under a permanent threat of destruction. I reject that framing.

If closures are treated as a normal fallback, then the burden is being shifted onto workers and regions first. That is not a fair restructuring. That is coercion dressed up as prudence.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies are not acceptable
I will say it again plainly: **forced layoffs are not part of a fair settlement**.

We are prepared to discuss:
- voluntary exits,
- natural attrition,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time changes,
- productivity improvements,
- and temporary employee contributions.

But we will not accept a package that relies on compulsory redundancies as a routine tool.

### 3) “Equivalent savings” must be demonstrated, not asserted
The expert and CFO are right on one point: we need plant-level numbers. Fine. Put the full model on the table.

But those numbers cannot be used to pre-decide closure. They must be used to test whether a socially responsible alternative works. Until then, “closures must remain available” is not a fact, it is a bargaining threat.

---

## On the expert and CFO thresholds
The expert says a non-closure solution is only credible if it closes most of the structural gap and keeps utilization viable. I can work with that as a test criterion.

But here is the key point: **if management wants non-closure alternatives to be judged on equivalence, then management must first show its own assumptions transparently**:
- exact cost gap by plant,
- exact savings from internal measures,
- exact impact of product allocation,
- exact one-time costs of closure/downsizing,
- and the margin effect over 3–5 years.

Without that, no one can honestly claim that closures are necessary.

So yes, we accept the need for numbers. We do not accept numbers being used as a one-way ratchet toward plant exit.

---

## On the facilitator’s compromise options

### 1) Sequenced restructuring with a binding review gate
This is the only option that is even in the direction of a real negotiation — **if** the initial phase is substantial and closure is not announced in advance.

For us, that means:
- negotiated measures first,
- no irreversible closure announcements before the review,
- a short, fixed review period,
- jointly agreed KPIs,
- and a real obligation to test the non-closure package in good faith.

What it cannot be is a delayed closure threat. If management wants a review, then management must also accept that the non-closure alternative gets a genuine chance.

### 2) Site-tiered settlement
This is better than a blanket attack, but I am very cautious.

Differentiation cannot mean “save the attractive sites and sacrifice the rest.” It must mean a fair industrial plan:
- viable sites get investment and product allocation,
- weaker sites get a transition path,
- and all German plants remain part of the strategy unless management can prove otherwise.

### 3) “No forced redundancies first”
This is the least bad formulation, but only if “first” is not a euphemism for “until management gets annoyed enough.” We can discuss a hierarchy of measures, but the point must be that forced redundancies are avoided, not normalized.

---

## What would actually move us
We are prepared to bargain hard and contribute fairly. But the package must be balanced.

That means we can discuss:
- temporary wage restraint or bonus forgoing,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exit schemes,
- redeployment and retraining,
- productivity improvements,
- and a joint transformation fund.

But in return we require:
- **no compulsory redundancies,**
- **no plant closures as part of the initial restructuring path,**
- **binding site and investment commitments,**
- **preservation of the collective wage framework,**
- and a fair contribution from management and owners as well.

If you want a real deal, then employees cannot be the only side made to carry pain.

---

## To management
You say you need flexibility to act. You already have room through co-determination, negotiation, redeployment, and voluntary instruments. What you are really demanding is a reserve threat of plant closure and forced layoffs. That is not “flexibility”; that is leverage against the workforce.

If you want a serious discussion, bring the plant-by-plant model and show:
- what savings are needed,
- what can be achieved without closures,
- what investment you are willing to commit,
- and what management and owners will contribute.

## To the controlling family and investors
You keep talking about long-term value. Fine. Then understand that long-term value is not created by breaking trust, tearing up guarantees, and turning industrial relations into an arms race.

A company that forces through restructuring by coercion may get a short-term accounting improvement and a long-term production problem.

## To the regional state government
You are right to emphasize regional responsibility and the industrial base. But that only matters if you use your blocking position to insist that closures are not the default answer.

Do not wait until plants are sacrificed and then call that “necessary adjustment.” Use your power now to insist on a fair, negotiated industrial plan.

---

## My boundary
I will not support any package that:
- makes compulsory redundancies a normal tool,
- starts from the assumption that plant closures are legitimate pressure points,
- or shifts the restructuring burden almost entirely onto workers.

That is my line.

## What I can support
I can support a package that includes:
- measurable employee contributions,
- working-time and flexibility arrangements,
- voluntary exits,
- qualification and redeployment,
- productivity improvements,
- and binding commitments on jobs, sites, and future investment.

But that package must be designed to protect employment and the collective agreement framework — not to dismantle them.

## [SUMMON: independent plant-level industrial planning model]
We now need a genuine plant-level model, not more slogans.

It should show:
- current utilization,
- direct and indirect cost per vehicle,
- the savings from each negotiated measure,
- the one-time and long-term costs of closure or downsizing,
- and the 3–5 year margin trajectory.

Without that, “closures remain available” is just a political preference, not an evidenced necessity.

## Bottom line
My position remains:
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No plant closures as the initial restructuring path.**
- **Yes to negotiated cost and capacity reduction.**
- **Yes to fair employee contribution, if matched by management and owner contribution.**
- **Yes to a durable settlement that protects jobs, sites, and the collective framework.**

If the numbers can support a non-closure solution, we will engage seriously. If they cannot, then management must still not assume that workers will quietly accept being made the sole shock absorber of the crisis.
