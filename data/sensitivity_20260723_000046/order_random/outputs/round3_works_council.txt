We have now heard the same message repeated from management, the family, and investors: “closures must remain available,” “forced layoffs may be needed,” “prove equivalence.” Let me answer just as clearly:

**I do not accept plant closures or compulsory redundancies as the basis of this restructuring.**  
Not as a starting point, not as a bargaining threat, and not as a “normal tool.”

## Where I agree
I agree with the CFO, and to a limited extent even with management, on one point: **the company needs a durable solution, not a cosmetic one**. We also agree that:
- the cost problem is real,
- temporary concessions alone are not enough,
- investment and product allocation matter,
- and the company must remain viable long term.

I do not dispute the need for serious restructuring. I dispute the claim that it must come through destroying plants and forcing people out.

## Where I disagree

### 1) Plant closures are not an acceptable fallback
Management, the family, and investors keep saying closures must remain “available.” From the workforce side, that is not a neutral phrase. It means we are being asked to negotiate under the threat of permanent destruction of jobs, skills, supplier networks, and regions.

That is unacceptable.

If closures are treated as a normal fallback, then the burden of adjustment has already been loaded onto workers and sites first. That is not a fair restructuring. It is coercion by another name.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies are not acceptable
I also reject the idea that structural adjustment requires forced layoffs. That is exactly the one-sided burden shifting the works council has been fighting against.

We are prepared to discuss:
- voluntary exits,
- natural attrition,
- internal redeployment,
- retraining and qualification,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity improvements,
- and temporary, negotiated contributions.

But **forced redundancies are a red line**.

### 3) “Equivalent savings” cannot be used as a blank cheque
The CFO is right that we need hard numbers. Fine. Then let management put the full plant-by-plant data on the table.

But those numbers cannot be used to predetermine closure. They must be used to test whether a socially responsible alternative is possible. That means:
- exact cost gap,
- exact savings from each negotiated instrument,
- utilization by site,
- one-time restructuring costs,
- and the 3–5 year margin impact.

Until that is shown transparently, “closures must remain on the table” is just pressure, not proof.

## On the proposed compromise paths

### 1) “No closure first” with a hard review trigger
This is only acceptable if it starts from the right premise: **no closures in the initial restructuring** and no irreversible announcements beforehand.

A review trigger can only work if:
- the negotiated package is implemented in good faith,
- the metrics are jointly agreed,
- and management does not use the review as a backdoor to force closure after the workforce has already given up concessions.

If the review is just a delayed threat, it is not a compromise.

### 2) Site-specific protection
This is better than an across-the-board attack, but it must not become “save the attractive sites and sacrifice the rest.”  
If the company wants differentiated treatment, then it must show how **all German plants** remain part of a viable industrial strategy, with investment and product allocation, not abandonment.

### 3) Closure as last resort
For us, closure is not just a “last resort.” It is the wrong tool in this company unless every alternative has genuinely been exhausted and proven impossible. So far, I do **not** see that proof.

## What would actually move us
We are not refusing change. We are refusing destruction.

We can discuss a serious package involving:
- temporary wage restraint or bonus forgoing,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exit schemes,
- redeployment and retraining,
- productivity measures,
- and a joint transformation fund.

But in return we require:
- **no German plant closures,**
- **no compulsory redundancies,**
- **binding site and investment commitments through 2030,**
- **and preservation of the collective bargaining framework and co-determination rights.**

That is the basis of any serious discussion.

## To management
You say you need room to act. You already have room — but co-determination is not an obstacle to be bypassed, it is the legal framework that prevents exactly the kind of unilateral damage you are proposing.

If your restructuring only works by threatening plant closures and forced layoffs, then it is not a viable plan. It is a confrontation strategy.

And let me be clear: warning strikes and further industrial action remain on the table if this remains your direction of travel.

## To the controlling family
You speak about long-term value. Then stop treating the workforce as the variable cost to be cut first. There is no long-term value in breaking trust, tearing up guarantees, and making the company’s industrial relations model unusable.

A family owner should understand that this company is not just a financial asset. It is an industrial responsibility.

## To the regional state government
I support your insistence that closures are not routine tools and that regional responsibility matters. But that only matters if you actually use your blocking role to stop the normalization of closures and forced layoffs.

Do not talk about protecting the region while accepting a process that dismantles its industrial base.

## To institutional investors
You want deal certainty and margins. We understand that. But certainty built on coercion is not durable certainty. It is the road to the next conflict.

A workforce that has been forced into concessions will not deliver trust, flexibility, or stability. If you want durable value, back a solution that preserves jobs and sites instead of writing them off.

## Bottom line
Our position remains unchanged and non-negotiable on the essentials:

- **No German plant closures.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No dismantling of the job-security framework.**
- **Yes to negotiated transformation, if and only if jobs, sites, and co-determination are protected.**

If management wants a serious technical discussion, then bring the plant-by-plant model:
- current utilization,
- cost per vehicle,
- savings from voluntary instruments,
- savings from working-time measures,
- effect of product allocation,
- and closure versus non-closure economics.

We will engage on the numbers. But not on the premise that closures and forced layoffs are already legitimate.
