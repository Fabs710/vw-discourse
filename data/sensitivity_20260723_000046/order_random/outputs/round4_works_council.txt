We have now heard the same pressure from management, the family, and investors once again: “closures must remain available,” “forced layoffs may be needed,” “prove equivalence.” My answer is unchanged:

**German plant closures are not acceptable as part of this restructuring.**  
**Compulsory redundancies are not acceptable.**  
**Co-determination and the job-security framework are not bargaining chips.**

I will engage on numbers and on serious alternatives. I will not engage on a premise that treats plant destruction as a normal management tool.

## Where I agree

I agree with the expert, the CFO, and even parts of management on the basic diagnosis:
- the cost gap is real,
- the restructuring must be durable,
- temporary stopgaps alone are not enough,
- and the workforce must contribute.

I also agree that a vague, symbolic peace would be irresponsible. Nobody here is defending the status quo for its own sake.

But agreement on the diagnosis does **not** mean agreement on the remedy.

## Where I disagree

### 1) Plant closures are not a legitimate fallback
Management, the family, and investors keep saying closures must “remain available.” In practice, that means the workforce is asked to bargain under permanent threat.

That is not a neutral technical position. It is pressure politics.

If closures are treated as a normal fallback, then the burden of adjustment is being shifted onto workers, regions, and suppliers first. That is not fair restructuring. It is coercion.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies are not acceptable
I will say it again plainly: **forced layoffs are not part of a fair settlement**.

We are prepared to discuss:
- voluntary exits,
- natural attrition,
- internal redeployment,
- retraining and qualification,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity improvements,
- and temporary negotiated contributions.

But we will not accept a package that relies on compulsory redundancies as a routine instrument.

### 3) “Equivalent savings” cannot be used as a blank cheque
The CFO and expert are right that we need plant-level numbers. Fine. Put the full model on the table.

But those numbers cannot be used to predetermine closure. They must be used to test whether a socially responsible alternative works. Until then, “closures must remain available” is not proof of necessity. It is a bargaining threat.

## On the expert’s thresholds

The expert says a non-closure solution is only credible if it closes most of the structural gap and keeps utilization viable. I can work with that as a test criterion.

But the burden is not on the workforce to prove a negative while management withholds the real assumptions. The burden is on management to show, plant by plant:
- the exact cost gap,
- the exact savings from internal measures,
- the exact impact of product allocation,
- the exact one-time costs of closure or downsizing,
- and the 3–5 year margin effect.

Without that, nobody can honestly claim that closures are necessary.

## On management’s argument

You say you need flexibility to act. You already have room through co-determination, negotiation, redeployment, and voluntary instruments. What you are really demanding is a reserve threat of plant closure and forced layoffs. That is not flexibility; that is leverage against the workforce.

If you want a serious discussion, bring the plant-by-plant model and show:
- what savings are needed,
- what can be achieved without closures,
- what investment you are willing to commit,
- and what management and owners will contribute.

## On the controlling family and investors

You keep talking about long-term value. Fine. Then understand that long-term value is not created by breaking trust, tearing up guarantees, and turning industrial relations into an arms race.

A company that forces through restructuring by coercion may get a short-term accounting improvement and a long-term production problem.

You can call that discipline if you want. I call it a recipe for the next conflict.

## On the regional state government

You are right to emphasize regional responsibility and the industrial base. But that only matters if you use your blocking position to insist that closures are not the default answer.

Do not wait until plants are sacrificed and then call that “necessary adjustment.” Use your power now to insist on a fair, negotiated industrial plan.

## On the compromise ideas

### 1) Sequenced restructuring with a binding review gate
This is the only option that is even in the direction of a real negotiation — **if** the initial phase is substantial and closure is not announced in advance.

For us, that means:
- negotiated measures first,
- no irreversible closure announcements before the review,
- a short, fixed review period,
- jointly agreed KPIs,
- and a real obligation to test the non-closure package in good faith.

What it cannot be is a delayed closure threat. If management wants a review, then management must also accept that the non-closure alternative gets a genuine chance.

### 2) Site-tiered settlement
This is better than a blanket attack, but I am very cautious.

Differentiation cannot mean “save the attractive sites and sacrifice the rest.” It must mean a fair industrial plan:
- viable sites get investment and product allocation,
- weaker sites get a transition path,
- and all German plants remain part of the strategy unless management can prove otherwise.

### 3) “No forced redundancies first”
This is the least bad formulation, but only if “first” is not a euphemism for “until management decides it has waited long enough.” We can discuss a hierarchy of measures, but the point must be that forced redundancies are avoided, not normalized.

## What would actually move us

We are prepared to bargain hard and contribute fairly. But the package must be balanced.

That means we can discuss:
- temporary wage restraint or bonus forgoing,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exit schemes,
- redeployment and retraining,
- productivity improvements,
- and a joint transformation fund.

But in return we require:
- **no compulsory redundancies,**
- **no plant closures as part of the initial restructuring path,**
- **binding site and investment commitments,**
- **preservation of the collective wage framework,**
- and a fair contribution from management and owners as well.

If you want a real deal, then employees cannot be the only side made to carry pain.

## To management

You say you need room to act. You already have room — but co-determination is not an obstacle to be bypassed, it is the legal framework that prevents exactly the kind of unilateral damage you are proposing.

If your restructuring only works by threatening plant closures and forced layoffs, then it is not a viable plan. It is a confrontation strategy.

And let me be clear: warning strikes and further industrial action remain on the table if this remains your direction of travel.

## To the controlling family

You speak about stewardship and long-term value. Then stop treating the workforce as the variable cost to be cut first.

There is no long-term value in breaking trust, tearing up guarantees, and making the company’s industrial relations model unusable.

## To institutional investors

You want deal certainty and margins. We understand that. But certainty built on coercion is not durable certainty. It is the road to the next conflict.

A workforce that has been forced into concessions will not deliver trust, flexibility, or stability. If you want durable value, back a solution that preserves jobs and sites instead of writing them off.

## Bottom line

My position remains non-negotiable on the essentials:

- **No German plant closures.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No dismantling of the job-security framework.**
- **Yes to negotiated transformation, if and only if jobs, sites, and co-determination are protected.**

If management wants a serious technical discussion, then bring the plant-by-plant model:
- current utilization,
- cost per vehicle,
- savings from voluntary instruments,
- savings from working-time measures,
- effect of product allocation,
- and closure versus non-closure economics.

We will engage on the numbers. But not on the premise that closures and forced layoffs are already legitimate.
