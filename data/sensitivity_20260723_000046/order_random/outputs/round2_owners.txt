We have now moved from principles to a real package discussion, so I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the union on one point: **a purely punitive restructuring would be value-destructive**. If the process destroys labour peace, delivery reliability, and the company’s reputation, then the cure can be worse than the disease.

I also agree that:
- **temporary measures alone are not enough**,
- **investment and product allocation matter**,
- and **all sides must contribute**.

That is not in dispute.

## Where I disagree
### 1) “No closures” cannot be an absolute
I cannot accept the union’s position that **German plant closures are off the table in all circumstances**. That is not a credible response to structural overcapacity.

If the company’s footprint remains too large for the market, then refusing to adjust capacity at the site level is not protection — it is postponement. And postponement has a cost: weaker margins, reduced investment capacity, and eventually a worse outcome for jobs and sites.

So to be explicit:
- **I do not support closures as a first choice.**
- **I do support keeping them available if no equivalent negotiated alternative exists.**

That is a serious distinction, and it matters.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be an unconditional red line
I understand why labor wants to avoid forced layoffs. But as a governing shareholder, I cannot endorse an absolute that would make structural adjustment impossible if the numbers do not work otherwise.

If voluntary instruments, redeployment, working-time changes, and internal transfers can achieve the required savings, then I prefer them. But if they do not, then the company cannot be trapped by a promise that preserves every job regardless of economic reality.

### 3) Shared sacrifice must be measurable
I do not accept a package that asks owners and management to contribute while the workforce contribution is defined only in soft terms.

A credible settlement must contain:
- **durable cost reduction**,
- **real capacity adjustment**,
- **measurable labour-cost contribution**,
- and **binding investment commitments**.

Without that, the deal is not a restructuring; it is a delay mechanism.

## Where I agree with the state
I do agree with the state government that closures are a serious matter and should not be treated casually. They are not the default tool. I also agree that regional consequences matter and that future investment is essential.

But I do not agree that this means closures should be ruled out in practice. If a negotiated alternative can produce equivalent savings, fine. If not, then insisting on “no closures” simply shifts the burden into continuing decline.

## On the union’s concrete package
You offered instruments such as:
- capacity reduction through negotiated means,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary wage restraint,
- voluntary departure schemes,
- redeployment,
- and an industrial transformation fund.

Those are all useful tools. But the key question is whether they are sufficient in scale and durability.

At this stage, I do **not** see enough evidence that they can fully solve the structural problem on their own. If you want closures off the table, you need to show:
1. the exact cost gap,
2. the exact savings from your package,
3. how those savings are durable and not temporary,
4. and how the remaining footprint becomes competitive.

Until then, “no closures” is not a package; it is a demand.

## To management and investors
I also want to be clear that I am not giving management a blank cheque for shock therapy.

A disorderly confrontation that destroys trust and triggers prolonged industrial action would be bad governance and bad ownership. If restructuring is needed, it must be pursued in a way that is orderly, legally robust, and economically credible.

So I am not saying “take the hardest option no matter what.”  
I am saying: **do not rule out the measures needed to restore competitiveness.**

## What I would support
I would support a settlement if it includes:
- **a quantified and durable cost reduction**,
- **meaningful capacity adjustment**,
- **voluntary exits and redeployment first**,
- **working-time and compensation adjustments**,
- **clear investment commitments for future products**,
- and **a credible path back to target margins**.

If that package can be shown to work without closures, then I can support it. But the burden is on the proponents of that alternative to prove it is financially equivalent.

## What I will not support
I will not support:
- a deal that preserves all sites unchanged,
- a deal that eliminates forced measures in advance regardless of economics,
- a deal that produces only temporary relief,
- or a deal that protects current guarantees while leaving the company structurally uncompetitive.

## Bottom line
My position remains firm:

- **I support a negotiated restructuring.**
- **I support shared sacrifice.**
- **I support avoiding closures and compulsory redundancies if, and only if, the alternative is genuinely equivalent.**
- **I do not accept categorical vetoes that would prevent the company from restoring competitiveness.**

If the workforce side wants to keep closures and forced layoffs off the table, then it must bring a package that actually closes the competitiveness gap in a durable way. If it cannot, then those options remain necessary tools, however unwelcome.
