We hear the pressure from management, the family, and capital markets. We also hear the state’s concern about the regional impact. But let me be clear: **a restructuring that is built on plant closures and compulsory redundancies is not a solution we can support.** It is exactly the kind of one-sided adjustment that would push the conflict into escalation.

## Where we agree
We agree that:
- the company has a **real competitiveness problem**,
- a **cosmetic fix is not enough**,
- the future must include **investment, product security, and durable savings**,
- and the workforce will need to make a **fair contribution**.

We are not denying the need for change. We are denying the idea that change must come through **closures and forced layoffs**.

## Where we disagree
### 1) Plant closures cannot be the default tool
To management, the family, and investors: you keep saying closures must “remain on the table.” For us, that is not a neutral phrase. It means the workforce is being asked to bargain under threat of plant destruction.

That is unacceptable.

If a site is closed, jobs, skills, supplier networks, and regional economic stability are lost permanently. That is not a reversible adjustment. So no, we do not accept closures as a normal restructuring instrument.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies are not acceptable
We are not prepared to let the burden of adjustment fall primarily on the workforce through forced dismissals. If the company needs reductions, then it must use:
- voluntary exit schemes,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time reductions,
- qualification and retraining,
- natural attrition,
- and negotiated temporary instruments.

If management’s answer is “we need forced layoffs,” then it has not yet produced a credible social alternative.

### 3) A “durable” deal must be fair, not just cheap
The investor and management arguments repeatedly equate durability with deeper labour cost cuts. That is too narrow. A durable settlement has to preserve:
- jobs,
- sites,
- collective bargaining standards,
- and future investment.

If the company wants employees to contribute, then owners and management must contribute in a visible and meaningful way too.

## Our concrete package
We are prepared to discuss a serious package, but it must be based on **preserving the footprint** rather than shrinking it through destruction.

### What we can discuss
- **Capacity reduction through negotiated instruments**, not closures.
- **Working-time reductions or flexibility** where needed and time-limited.
- **Temporary wage restraint or forgone bonuses** as part of a broader package.
- **Voluntary departure programs** and early retirement.
- **Redeployment and qualification** across the group.
- **Investment-linked commitments** for future platforms and models.
- **A joint industrial transformation fund** financed by company, owners, and, if needed, a moderate employee contribution.

### What we will not accept
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No German plant closures.**
- **No unilateral wage dumping or dismantling of the collective agreement.**
- **No settlement where workers carry the bulk of the pain while others merely demand “competitiveness.”**

## On the savings question
Management keeps asking for “structural” savings. Fine. Then let’s speak structurally.

If the company needs a durable cost improvement, then it should put numbers on the table for:
1. the exact cost gap,
2. the required capacity adjustment,
3. what savings can be achieved through internal measures,
4. what management and owners are prepared to contribute,
5. and what investment guarantees are offered in return.

Without that, “closures must remain possible” is just a bargaining threat, not a plan.

## To the state government
We appreciate the state’s position that closures are a last resort and that regional responsibility matters. We would go further: in this case, closures are not an acceptable route unless every negotiated alternative has been genuinely exhausted and the company has demonstrated that no equivalent solution exists.

We are ready to work on negotiated alternatives, but we need the state to insist that management actually develops them seriously instead of using closure pressure as leverage from the start.

## To the controlling family and investors
If you want to defend long-term value, then do not destroy the company’s industrial base in the name of short-term margin repair.

A carmaker does not become stronger by cutting away the trust of its workforce and threatening core sites. That weakens industrial relations, damages delivery confidence, and makes transformation harder. If you want durable competitiveness, then you need a **socially viable restructuring**, not a punitive one.

## Our bottom line
We remain open to a negotiated settlement, but only if it meets these conditions:
- **no plant closures,**
- **no compulsory redundancies,**
- **shared sacrifice from all sides,**
- **binding site and investment commitments,**
- **and preservation of the collective bargaining framework.**

If management refuses to move off closures and forced layoffs, then we will treat that as a rejection of a fair settlement and respond accordingly, including further industrial action.

If you want, we can now move one step further and discuss a **concrete union counter-package** with numbers and instruments — but only on the basis that jobs and sites are genuinely protected.
