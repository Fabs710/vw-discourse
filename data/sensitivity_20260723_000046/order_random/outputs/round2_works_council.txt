I will be equally direct: **there will be no acceptance of plant closures or compulsory redundancies as the basis of this restructuring.** Not after the historic job-security pledge was broken, and not under the pretext that workers must simply absorb the cost of management’s strategic failures.

We are prepared to negotiate a serious transformation. We are **not** prepared to negotiate away the industrial base.

## Where I agree
I agree with the CFO, and to a limited extent even with management, on one point: **the company needs a durable solution, not a cosmetic one.** If the cost base and capacity are genuinely misaligned, then the response must be real, measurable, and future-oriented.

I also agree that:
- temporary measures alone are not enough,
- the company must remain investable,
- and future products and platforms matter.

But that is where my agreement stops.

## Where I disagree

### 1) Plant closures are not a legitimate “tool” in this company’s normal restructuring
Management, family, and investors keep repeating that closures must “remain available.” Let me be clear: from the workforce’s point of view, that means negotiating under a threat to destroy jobs, communities, and skills that took decades to build.

That is not a neutral economic instrument. It is a line we will not cross.

A plant closure is not a technical correction on a spreadsheet. It is the permanent dismantling of a site, its supplier network, and the social fabric around it. If you insist on keeping closures on the table, you are not asking for partnership — you are asking for surrender.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies are unacceptable
I do not accept the argument that “structural adjustment” requires forcing people out of their jobs. That is exactly the kind of one-sided burden shifting we said would not be accepted.

We are prepared to discuss:
- voluntary departures,
- natural attrition,
- early retirement,
- internal transfers,
- retraining,
- working-time flexibility,
- and negotiated productivity measures.

But **forced layoffs are a red line**. If the company cannot adjust without compulsory redundancies, then management has not yet exhausted the socially responsible alternatives.

### 3) “Equivalent savings” is being used too loosely
The controlling shareholder, investors, and management all say alternatives are acceptable only if they are “financially equivalent.” Fine — then put the full plant-by-plant numbers on the table.

Right now, the demand sounds like this:
- workers must give up guarantees,
- workers must accept uncertainty,
- workers must accept pressure,
- and only then will you decide whether closures were necessary.

That is backwards.

If you want us to consider a negotiated alternative, then prove:
1. the exact cost gap,
2. the actual savings from each measure,
3. the permanence of those savings,
4. and the impact on each plant and region.

Until then, “equivalence” is just a slogan for pre-decided cuts.

## What would actually move us
The works council’s position remains unchanged, and for good reason:

1. **Binding site guarantees for all German plants through 2030.**  
2. **No compulsory redundancies through 2030.**  
3. **No plant closures.**  
4. **A genuine co-determination role in shaping the restructuring.**  
5. **Real investment commitments in future products and technologies.**  
6. **A fair, shared contribution from management and owners, not a one-sided workforce bill.**

That is the basis for any serious discussion.

## To management
You say you need room to act. You already have room — but co-determination is not a nuisance, it is the legal and moral framework that prevents exactly the kind of destructive unilateralism you are proposing.

If your restructuring plan can only work by tearing up job security and using closure threats as leverage, then it is not a viable plan. It is a confrontation strategy.

And let me be equally clear: warning strikes and escalation are not rhetorical. We are prepared to use them again if this remains the direction of travel.

## To the controlling family
You speak about long-term value, but there is no long-term value in breaking trust with the workforce while demanding loyalty from it. If the company destroys the job-security framework, it will destroy the very industrial relations model that has supported stability and quality for years.

A family owner should understand that a carmaker is not just a financial asset. It is an industrial responsibility. That responsibility includes preserving sites and skills, not just optimizing the next margin target.

## To the regional state government
I support your insistence that closures are not routine tools and that regional responsibility matters. But I need you to be more than cautious rhetoric. Your blocking role is only meaningful if it is used to prevent the very outcome management is trying to normalize.

So I ask you plainly: will you stand with the workforce against closures and forced redundancies, or will you allow “competitiveness” to become the excuse for dismantling the German industrial base?

## To institutional investors
You want “deal certainty” and a return to margin. We understand that. But certainty built on social destruction is not durable certainty. It is a false calm before the next conflict.

A workforce that has been coerced will not deliver trust, flexibility, or stability. It will deliver resistance. If you want durable value, then back a solution that preserves jobs and sites, not one that merely writes them down.

## On the CFO’s model request
I welcome the call for numbers. But the numbers must not be used as a pretext to declare closures inevitable before alternatives are seriously tested.

So here is the condition for any further technical discussion:

- We will engage on a plant-by-plant model.
- We will discuss savings instruments.
- We will discuss capacity adjustment.
- But **the starting assumption must be no closures and no compulsory redundancies**.

If management wants to prove otherwise, it must do so transparently and with full disclosure, not by presenting the workforce with a fait accompli.

## Bottom line
Our position is unchanged and non-negotiable on the essentials:

- **No German plant closures.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No dismantling of the job-security framework.**
- **Yes to negotiated transformation, if and only if jobs, sites, and co-determination are protected.**

If that is not acceptable to management and capital, then they should not mistake conflict for surprise. The workforce will defend its interests with every tool available.
