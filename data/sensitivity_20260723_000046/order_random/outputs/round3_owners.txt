We are now at the point where everyone has stated their red lines. I will do the same, clearly.

## Where I agree
I agree with the union, works council, state, CFO, management, and investors on one fundamental point: **the company needs a durable restructuring, not a symbolic one**.

I also agree that:
- temporary measures alone are insufficient if the cost gap is structural,
- investment and future product allocation matter,
- and a settlement that destroys labour peace or delivery reliability would be value-destructive.

That said, agreement on the diagnosis does **not** mean agreement on the remedy.

## Where I disagree
### 1) I do not accept “no closures” as an absolute
The union and works council keep asking everyone else to accept that closures are excluded from the outset. I cannot accept that.

If the footprint remains structurally too large for the market and the cost base remains above benchmark, then refusing to adjust capacity at the plant level is not protection — it is delay. Delay burns cash, weakens margins, and reduces the company’s ability to invest in the future.

So my position remains:
- **closures should not be the first choice,**
- **but they must remain available if negotiated alternatives do not close the gap.**

That is not hostility to labor; it is basic stewardship.

### 2) I do not accept “no compulsory redundancies” as an unconditional rule
I understand why the workforce wants to avoid forced layoffs. But if we make that an absolute, then we may be promising what the economics cannot sustain.

If voluntary exits, redeployment, working-time changes, and productivity gains are enough, then I prefer them. If they are not enough, then the company cannot be trapped by a promise that preserves every position regardless of economic reality.

### 3) I do not accept a deal that preserves the footprint first and tests competitiveness later
The union’s language still implies that the company should guarantee sites and jobs first, and only then see whether the savings are enough. That is backwards.

A serious restructuring must answer:
- what is the cost gap,
- what savings are durable,
- what capacity reduction is actually needed,
- and what investment is required to make the remaining footprint competitive.

If the answer is “we can keep all sites and all guarantees intact,” I am skeptical unless that is proven with numbers.

## On the facilitator’s compromise paths
### “No closure first” with a hard review trigger
This is only acceptable if the review is real, time-bound, and based on agreed metrics. It cannot become a mechanism to postpone the problem indefinitely.

I could support such a path **only if**:
- the initial package is substantial,
- savings are measurable,
- and the review point has consequences if the targets are not met.

### Site-specific protection with conditional workforce contribution
This is the most plausible direction so far, because it recognizes that not every site has the same economics. But it only works if:
- the viable sites get investment and product allocation,
- the weaker sites get a credible transition path,
- and the overall package produces real, durable savings.

### Closure as last resort
That is already my position. But “last resort” must mean something concrete:
- all serious alternatives tested,
- plant-level evidence reviewed,
- and no equivalent non-closure solution available.

If those conditions are met, then closure remains legitimate. If they are not met, then closure should not be used.

## To the union and works council
You keep saying that closures and compulsory redundancies are red lines. I hear that. But red lines do not alter arithmetic.

If you want those tools kept off the table, then you must bring a package that is **economically equivalent** in durable annual savings and capacity correction. Not temporary relief. Not a pause. Equivalent structural effect.

That means:
- meaningful voluntary exits,
- substantial productivity gains,
- working-time flexibility,
- labor-cost contribution,
- and a real shift in plant utilization.

If you can show that, I will take it seriously. If you cannot, then insisting on absolute exclusions is not a solution.

## To management
I support your insistence that we need hard numbers, and I support the CFO’s call for a plant-by-plant model.

But I will not support a shock-treatment approach that provokes unnecessary instability. The restructuring must be:
- legally robust,
- economically credible,
- and implemented in an orderly way.

You do not get a blank cheque. You do get support for a disciplined and fact-based process.

## To the regional state government
I understand your mandate and I respect the state’s blocking role. But your role is not to freeze industrial adaptation. It is to protect a viable industrial base.

That means you should help test alternatives seriously, but not pre-commit to preserving every site regardless of economics. If a plant is structurally uneconomic, you cannot solve that by political language alone.

## To institutional investors
I share your concern about prolonged conflict and value erosion. A deal that preserves appearances but not competitiveness would be unacceptable.

But I also reject the idea that value is best served by maximal pressure at all costs. Industrial peace matters too — not as a slogan, but because this company needs stable execution.

## What I would support
I would support a settlement if it contains:
- a quantified and durable cost reduction,
- meaningful capacity adjustment,
- voluntary exits and redeployment first,
- working-time and compensation adjustments,
- a fair but real employee contribution,
- and binding investment commitments for future products.

That package can be non-closure if — and only if — it is truly equivalent in effect.

## What I will not support
I will not support:
- a deal that rules out closures in all circumstances,
- a deal that rules out compulsory redundancies regardless of the numbers,
- a package that protects every site unchanged while leaving the company uncompetitive,
- or a short-term truce that merely postpones the crisis.

## [SUMMON: independent plant-level industrial planning model]
We need an independent, plant-by-plant model comparing:
- the annual savings from negotiated measures,
- the annual savings from closure or partial downsizing,
- the one-time restructuring costs,
- and the margin impact over 3–5 years.

Without that, this discussion remains too political and too vague.

## Bottom line
My position is unchanged:
- **I support a negotiated restructuring.**
- **I support shared sacrifice.**
- **I do not accept categorical bans on closures or compulsory redundancies.**
- **I will support a non-closure solution only if it is financially equivalent and durable.**

That is the standard I will apply.
