I agree with one important point from the controlling family: **the company does need a credible restructuring** and we cannot pretend that competitiveness will recover through symbolism alone. If the business model is under pressure, then the response must be durable and economically serious.

But I have to draw a clear line on the method.

## Where I agree
- **A shared-sacrifice solution is necessary.**  
  The burden cannot fall only on shareholders, the workforce, or one region.
- **Productivity, capacity, and cost must be addressed.**  
  We are not defending inefficiency for its own sake.
- **Future investment matters.**  
  Any restructuring that cuts today but undermines the company’s next product cycle is self-defeating.

## Where I disagree
### 1) Plant closures must not be the default
I do **not** accept that closures should simply remain “on the table” as a normal option. In a company of this significance, with a special statutory stake, co-determination, and a regional responsibility to the economic base, closures in Germany are not a routine efficiency lever. They are a last resort.

If management wants support for deep restructuring, it must first demonstrate that:
- every alternative to closure has been exhausted,
- the savings cannot be achieved through negotiated capacity reduction, working-time adjustments, redeployment, or targeted efficiency measures,
- and the regional economic consequences have been fully addressed.

Until then, I will not treat closures as politically or economically acceptable.

### 2) No compulsory redundancies is not a slogan
The workforce position is not an “absolute too rigid” demand; it is a legitimate protection against a restructuring being loaded onto employees alone. If the company wants social peace and durable change, it cannot start by threatening mass dismissals.

I am prepared to discuss:
- voluntary departures,
- early retirement,
- internal transfers,
- qualification and redeployment,
- temporary working-time sacrifices,
- and negotiated cost measures.

But **forced layoffs** would be a red line for me unless all other routes have genuinely failed and there is no alternative preserving the company’s regional footprint.

### 3) The state will not be sidelined
As a blocking-minority shareholder and co-governance actor, we are not decoration in this process. Any plan that tries to force through deep cuts while bypassing the state’s role, or while ignoring the long-term regional consequences, will meet resistance.

## What a workable path would need
If there is to be support from my side, then the package must include:

1. **Binding site guarantees** for the German plants.  
2. **No compulsory redundancies** as the governing principle.  
3. **Capacity adjustment through negotiated means**, not closures.  
4. **Real contributions from management and owners**, not just pressure on labor.  
5. **Clear investment commitments** in future products, especially for the transition period.  
6. **A jointly negotiated social plan** that protects the regional economy and preserves skills.

## Bottom line
I am not willing to endorse a restructuring that treats plant closures and mass layoffs as acceptable baseline tools. That would damage the company’s role as a regional anchor and undermine the co-determination balance that has long been part of its stability.

If management and the controlling shareholder want a durable solution, they should come to the table with a plan that **preserves sites, protects jobs as far as possible, and restores competitiveness through negotiated transformation**. Without that, I will not support it.
