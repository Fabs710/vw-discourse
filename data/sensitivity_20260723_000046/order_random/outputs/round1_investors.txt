We are watching this very closely, and from a capital-markets perspective the key question is not who “wins” the symbolism of the dispute, but whether the company gets back to **competitive, durable margins**.

## Where we agree
I agree with management, the family, and the state on one essential point: **this cannot be a cosmetic restructuring**. The current cost base and capacity profile are clearly not acceptable if the goal is to restore returns. If the company only gets temporary labour peace without a real productivity and cost reset, then shareholders are left with continued margin pressure and further value erosion.

I also agree that **some form of workforce contribution is likely necessary**. A deal where capital absorbs the pain while labour costs and structural overcapacity stay basically intact would not be credible.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** agree with the position, from either the union or the state, that **plant closures and compulsory redundancies should effectively be taken off the table in advance**.

From our point of view, that is exactly the kind of arrangement that would be fundamentally unacceptable: it would likely leave the cost base structurally uncompetitive and margins impaired for years. If the company cannot materially reduce capacity and labour costs, then preserving every site and every guarantee becomes value-destructive, not protective.

At the same time, I also do **not** support a blunt, chaotic approach to restructuring. If closures or layoffs become the only route, they must be part of a **credible, orderly, and negotiated plan**, not a prolonged conflict that destroys more value through strikes, delay, and uncertainty.

## Our position
We would support the path that most credibly delivers:

- a **durable reduction in structural costs**,
- a **clear capacity adjustment** consistent with demand,
- a **measurable improvement in return on sales**,
- and **deal certainty** without endless confrontation.

That could be through negotiated alternatives to closures, but only if they are **financially equivalent** to the required savings and are durable, not temporary.

What we will not support is:
- a settlement that preserves the existing footprint almost unchanged,
- promises future competitiveness without hard near-term cost action,
- or a prolonged strike pattern that delays restructuring while margins keep deteriorating.

## On the specific proposals
### To the union
Your insistence on no closures and no compulsory redundancies is understandable from your side, but as an absolute position it does not solve the shareholder problem. If the end result is still an uncompetitive cost base, then the burden has simply been shifted into lower future value, weaker investment capacity, and likely more painful action later.

We are open to:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- working-time measures,
- targeted concessions,
- and investment-linked guarantees,

but only if they add up to a **real and lasting** adjustment.

### To the state
We respect the regional and political responsibilities, but the company cannot be managed as if preserving every site is the primary objective. Regional protection cannot come at the expense of long-term competitiveness. If the business remains structurally impaired, the local economy ultimately suffers anyway.

### To the family and management
We support the insistence on structural action, but the package must be judged by outcomes, not rhetoric. If the plan ends up being too weak, too slow, or too compromised, it will not rebuild confidence. Capital will continue to discount the business.

## Bottom line
Our support depends on whether the final settlement delivers **credible, durable competitiveness**.

If the result is:
- real capacity reduction,
- real labour-cost reduction,
- and a believable return to competitive margins,

then we can back it.

If the result is instead:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- no meaningful footprint adjustment,
- and continued margin erosion,

then we will not support it, and we will view that as value-destructive.
