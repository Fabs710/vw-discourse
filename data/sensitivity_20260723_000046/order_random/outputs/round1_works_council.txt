I will be equally direct: **management’s move to keep plant closures and compulsory redundancies on the table as a starting point is exactly what destroys trust and triggers resistance.** After the historic job-security pledge has already been broken once, you cannot now ask the workforce to accept a fait accompli and call that a “credible restructuring.”

## Where I agree
I agree with one thing from almost everyone here: **the company needs a durable solution, not symbolism**.  
Yes, there is a real competitiveness problem. Yes, the business needs to be fit for the future. And yes, we all have to accept that this will require change.

I also agree that:
- temporary measures alone are not enough,
- future investment matters,
- and a deal has to be economically serious.

But that is where the agreement ends.

## Where I disagree sharply

### 1) Plant closures are not an acceptable baseline option
To management, the family, and capital markets: you keep saying closures “must remain on the table.” For us, that means one thing: **workers are expected to negotiate under the threat of plant destruction**. That is not shared responsibility; that is coercion.

For the works council, this is not a tactical preference. It is a core limit.  
**No German plant closure.**  
Not as the preferred option, not as leverage, not as a bargaining chip.

If there is a capacity problem, it must be solved through:
- negotiated capacity reductions,
- working-time flexibility,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary departures,
- and a fair contribution from management, owners, and shareholders.

But **not** by closing sites.

### 2) Forced redundancies are unacceptable
The same applies to compulsory layoffs.  
If the company wants to claim social responsibility, then it cannot start by threatening thousands of families with involuntary job loss.

So let me be unambiguous:

- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No mass layoffs disguised as “adjustment.”**
- **No restructuring that loads the risk onto the workforce while protecting ownership and executive control.**

If management believes it needs reductions, then it must use **voluntary, negotiated, reversible instruments** only.

### 3) “Real contributions” must apply to all sides
To the controlling family and institutional investors: you repeatedly speak about shareholder value, but you are asking workers to carry the hardest part of the adjustment while insisting on the right to close plants if your savings targets are not met.

That is not an equitable baseline.

If there is a crisis, then the contribution must be shared through:
- owner-side restraint,
- management responsibility,
- investment commitments,
- and a fair burden-sharing package.

The workforce is not the only balance sheet in this company.

### 4) The state is right to insist on regional responsibility
I agree with the regional state government that closures are not routine efficiency tools. They are a last resort, and in practice I do not see evidence that all alternatives have been exhausted — because those alternatives are barely being seriously developed before the closure threat is raised.

## What would actually move us
You ask what could move the works council. The answer is clear and not complicated:

1. **Binding site guarantees for all German plants to 2030.**  
2. **No compulsory redundancies through 2030.**  
3. **No plant closures.**  
4. **A genuine co-determination role in shaping the restructuring, not a take-it-or-leave-it package.**  
5. **A negotiated workforce contribution only if it is matched by real contributions from management and owners.**  
6. **Concrete investment commitments in future products and technologies.**

That is the minimum basis for any serious discussion.

## What will not move us
What will not move us is:
- threats of closure,
- pressure tactics,
- “either you accept this or we shut a plant,”
- or the idea that historic guarantees can simply be removed because markets are difficult.

That approach will not produce trust. It will produce resistance, including industrial action. And we are already prepared for that.

## Bottom line
So my position is straightforward:

- **We are open to restructuring.**
- **We are not open to plant closures.**
- **We are not open to compulsory redundancies.**
- **We are open to negotiated, fair, reversible adjustments that protect jobs and sites.**

If management, owners, and capital want a durable solution, then bring a plan that secures the German plants and the workforce through 2030.  
If not, then they should not be surprised that the workforce uses every tool available to defend its interests.
