I appreciate that all sides recognise the same hard reality: the company needs a durable industrial reset, not slogans. On that point, I agree with management, the union, the works council, and the state.

But I want to be equally clear: **a solution that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive is not acceptable**. As controlling owners, our duty is to protect the long-term value of the enterprise. That means we cannot endorse a package that merely postpones the adjustment while margins continue to erode.

### Where I agree
- The company needs future investment in products, technology, and industrial capability.
- A negotiated settlement is preferable to open-ended confrontation.
- Workforce contributions can be part of the solution.
- Labour peace and stability matter, because prolonged conflict destroys value.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the implicit premise from the works council, union, and state that every plant must remain open and that the necessary adjustment can be achieved without hard structural change.

That position may be understandable politically, but it does not answer the economics.

- If there is excess capacity, it has to be reduced.
- If the cost base is too high, it has to come down materially.
- If the company misses its return-on-sales target, that cannot be solved by goodwill alone.
- If German operations are uncompetitive relative to global peers, then the burden cannot be shifted entirely onto owners and future investment.

### On plant closures and job cuts
I am **not** saying plant closures are desirable. They are obviously serious and should not be used lightly. But I am also not prepared to rule them out in advance if they are necessary to restore competitiveness.

Similarly, I am **not** prepared to accept a blanket refusal of compulsory redundancies if that would prevent a real restructuring. A promise of “no layoffs, ever” may sound protective, but if it blocks the savings needed to secure the business, it ultimately endangers more jobs and more value.

### On guarantees
I also do **not** support unconditional long-dated guarantees that lock in the current structure regardless of performance. That would be governance without discipline.

However, I am open to **conditional security commitments** if they are tied to:
- measurable cost reductions,
- real capacity alignment,
- productivity improvements,
- and a credible path to restored margins.

That is the key point. Security cannot be demanded as a substitute for restructuring. It can only be granted as part of a settlement that actually works.

### On the role of owners
The suggestion that owners should “contribute” is reasonable in principle, but it must be understood correctly. The controlling family does not solve operational inefficiency by writing slogans or preserving every legacy promise. Our contribution is to ensure disciplined governance, strategic continuity, and the willingness to support a serious plan. We will not back a solution that destroys enterprise value.

### Bottom line
My position is this:

- **No cosmetic compromise.**
- **No settlement that preserves excess capacity and a structurally uncompetitive cost base.**
- **No unconditional site guarantees detached from performance and economics.**
- **Yes to a negotiated package if it delivers durable savings, restores margins, and secures the company’s future.**

If the other stakeholders want to avoid closures and forced layoffs, then they need to put forward something economically equivalent, not just politically preferable. Otherwise, the hard restructuring remains on the table.
