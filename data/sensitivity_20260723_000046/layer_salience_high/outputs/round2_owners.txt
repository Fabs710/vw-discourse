As the controlling shareholder, I’ll be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, investors, the state, and even the employee side on one essential point: **this cannot be a cosmetic settlement**.  
If the outcome leaves the company with the same structural cost problem, it will fail. That would damage long-term value, weaken investment capacity, and ultimately put more jobs at risk later.

I also agree that:
- the company needs **future product and technology investment**,
- the package must be **durable**, not a short truce,
- and some form of **workforce contribution** will likely be necessary.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the employee-side position that the company can be made competitive while treating all German sites as untouchable and excluding compulsory redundancies under all circumstances.

That is not a restructuring plan; it is a veto on restructuring.  
If the savings do not come from somewhere real and lasting, then the burden simply gets pushed into the next cycle, where the problem will be worse.

At the same time, I also do **not** accept management’s implication that closures and layoffs are automatically the best or only credible answer. They are not. They are severe measures with permanent consequences, and if they trigger prolonged conflict, production disruption, and reputational damage, they may destroy more value than they create.

So I am not siding with either extreme.

## On the plant-closure question
Let me be explicit: **I am not endorsing closures as a first choice**.  
But I am also **not ruling them out in principle** if they are the only way to restore a viable cost base and long-term competitiveness.

That is the hard truth. A controlling shareholder cannot support a solution that preserves every legacy promise while the business weakens.

## On compulsory redundancies
Same logic applies. I prefer solutions based on:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- working-time instruments,
- and qualification.

But I will not accept a blanket prohibition on compulsory redundancies if it makes a real restructuring impossible. A promise that sounds humane but prevents adaptation is not stewardship.

## To the Works Council and Union
You asked for binding guarantees through 2030. I understand why. But unconditional guarantees are not available if they lock in the current structure regardless of performance.

If you want stronger protection, then it must be tied to a package that actually delivers:
- durable cost reductions,
- real capacity alignment,
- productivity gains,
- and a credible path back to margin targets.

If you can produce an economically equivalent alternative, I will take it seriously.  
So far, I hear instruments, but not yet a full solution.

### What I need from you
I need you to show:
- how much cost is removed **permanently**,
- how capacity is actually reduced,
- how the savings survive beyond the next bargaining round,
- and how the company gets back to competitiveness without loading the entire burden onto capital and future investment.

If you cannot show that, then insisting on “no closures, no layoffs, no framework change” becomes a refusal to solve the problem.

## To management
I also need to challenge you.

You are right to demand structural savings, but you still have not made a sufficiently convincing case that every hard option must be on the table immediately. You need to be more precise:
- What is the exact savings gap?
- What is the plant-by-plant economics?
- What is the cost of conflict versus the cost of restructuring?
- Which measures are truly structural, and which are just accounting relief?

If you want ownership and the board to back tough decisions, you need a tighter, quantified case than “the arithmetic is bad.”

## To the regional state
I value the state’s stabilizing role, but I will not support turning the company into a protected industrial museum.  
If the state blocks every meaningful adjustment, it may preserve sites for a while but erode the enterprise that sustains them.

Co-determination and regional stability matter. But they do not override the need for a viable business.

## My position on a settlement
I am prepared to support a package if it meets these conditions:

1. **Verified, quantified savings**
2. **Durable capacity reduction**
3. **Meaningful labour-cost contribution**
4. **Investment commitments for future products**
5. **Protection against open-ended conflict**
6. **A credible path to restored margins**

## What would move me
A credible alternative package could include:
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- temporary employee contributions,
- productivity measures,
- overhead reductions,
- and conditional site security.

But it must be shown, not asserted, that this reaches the same economic outcome as harder measures.

## My red line
What I cannot accept is:
- preserving all sites regardless of economics,
- excluding compulsory redundancies categorically,
- and leaving the cost base structurally uncompetitive.

That would be value-destructive and would fail my duty as controlling owner.

## Bottom line
I am open on the **form** of the restructuring.  
I am not open on avoiding **real** restructuring.

If the employee side wants to avoid closures and forced layoffs, it needs to bring forward a package that is economically equivalent, measurable, and durable. If management wants support for hard measures, it must justify them with plant-level numbers and a clearer savings case.

Until then, I will not endorse a settlement that simply delays the crisis or trades long-term value for short-term calm.
