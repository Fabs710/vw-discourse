I’ll be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with one thing you all keep repeating: this cannot be a cosmetic truce.  
A deal that leaves the company with the same overcapacity, the same cost base, and the same structural weakness would be a failure. We do need a durable restructuring, and we do need future investment.

I also agree that workforce measures can be part of the solution. I am not insisting on closures for their own sake. I am insisting on **credible, durable savings**.

## Where I disagree
What I cannot accept is the repeated attempt to turn **“no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no changes to guarantees”** into the starting premise.

That is not a restructuring framework. That is a refusal to confront reality.

You say this is not a law of economics, but it is arithmetic:
- demand in Europe is weaker,
- German factory costs are too high,
- capacity is too large,
- and the brand is missing its return-on-sales target.

If we preserve every site, reject compulsory redundancies outright, and rely only on working-time cuts, voluntary exits, and redeployment, we do **not** get the scale or durability of savings needed. We get postponement. And postponement is exactly what this company cannot afford.

## To the Works Council
You asked for numbers and transparency. Fair enough.

But your “minimum acceptable package” is not a restructuring proposal; it is a demand list:
- binding site guarantees through 2030,
- no compulsory redundancies through 2030,
- preservation of the existing framework,
- and only voluntary, internal adjustments.

That may be socially comfortable, but it does not solve the structural problem.

You also keep saying management should “explain why” alternatives are insufficient. We have explained it: because the savings gap is too large for soft measures alone. If you want me to treat your counter-plan as economically serious, it must show:
- how much capacity is actually removed,
- how much labour cost falls structurally,
- how the savings are sustained beyond the next bargaining round,
- and how this gets us back to competitive margins.

At the moment, you have not done that.

## To the Trade Union
I appreciate that you at least acknowledge the need to table something with numbers. That is more constructive than a blanket refusal.

But your fallback still misses the point.  
More working-time reduction, deeper bonus restraint, and temporary wage moderation may help at the margin. They do **not** substitute for a real structural reset if the footprint remains too large.

And let me be clear: a time-limited solidarity contribution is only meaningful if it is tied to a package that materially improves the economics. A contribution that merely buys time without fixing capacity is not acceptable.

You also ask management to quantify the cost of strikes and conflict. I will do that plainly: prolonged strikes damage output, customer trust, delivery reliability, and the company’s reputation with investors and future talent. But that is precisely why I am saying we need a structural settlement now, not a soft compromise that guarantees another round later.

## To the Regional State
I understand your political role, but your position still does not answer the economic problem.

“Do everything else first” sounds reasonable until “everything else” still leaves us with an uncompetitive cost base. Then what?

If you are asking me to rule out closures and forced job reductions in advance, then you are asking me to accept a settlement that may not restore the business. That would be irresponsible.

I am open to alternatives, but not to vetoes that make restructuring impossible.

## To the Controlling Family and Investors
I am aligned with you on the core point: no cosmetic compromise, no structural impairment of margins, no endless conflict.

Where I want to push further is this: if the board and ownership bloc want a credible turnaround, then they have to support the hard decisions needed to make it real. That means not just saying “discipline,” but accepting that some sites, some capacity, and some jobs may have to go if that is what the economics require.

If the final package does not produce a **material and durable** reduction in the cost base, then it will not restore competitiveness. And if it does not restore competitiveness, it does not protect long-term value.

## The actual issue: economically equivalent alternatives
Everyone keeps using that phrase, so let me define it.

An economically equivalent alternative would have to deliver, at minimum:
- durable capacity reduction,
- substantial and recurring labour-cost savings,
- a measurable improvement in margins,
- and a credible implementation path that does not unravel after one bargaining cycle.

So far, I have heard proposals for:
- working-time reductions,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- and investment commitments.

These may be components. They are **not yet a full answer**.

## My minimum acceptable package
If we are to avoid the most disruptive measures, I need a package that contains all of the following:

1. **A verified, quantified savings plan** that closes the competitiveness gap  
2. **Real capacity reduction**, not just reshuffling production  
3. **A meaningful labour-cost contribution** that is durable, not symbolic  
4. **Binding productivity and process improvements**  
5. **A clear investment path to 2030** for future products and technology  
6. **A mechanism that prevents the savings from evaporating in the next cycle**

## My fallback
If the employee side will not accept a package with structural effects, then the fallback remains on the table:
- plant rationalisation,
- job reductions,
- and, where necessary, closures.

I am not saying this lightly. But I am saying it clearly: if the alternatives do not match the economics, then the hard options remain necessary.

## My red line
What I will not accept is a settlement that:
- preserves all German sites regardless of economics,
- excludes compulsory redundancies under all circumstances,
- and leaves the cost base fundamentally unchanged.

That would be a cosmetic agreement, and I will not sign off on one.

## One practical point
If you want to prove that alternatives can work, then bring forward a **plant-by-plant savings map**:
- current cost position,
- required cost position,
- achievable savings by instrument,
- timing,
- and residual gap.

Without that, we are debating slogans, not restructuring.

## Bottom line
I am prepared to negotiate hard on the **form** of restructuring.  
I am **not** prepared to compromise on the **need** for deep, durable structural savings.

If the workforce and the other stakeholders want to avoid closures and forced redundancies, then they need to help deliver an alternative that is economically equivalent — not merely politically more comfortable.

That is the only basis on which this company can recover competitiveness and fund its future.
