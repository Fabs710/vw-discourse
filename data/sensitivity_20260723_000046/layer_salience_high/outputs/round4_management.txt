We have heard a lot of principled language. I’ll keep this grounded in the economics.

## Where I agree

I agree with everyone on three points:

1. **This cannot be cosmetic.**  
   A temporary truce that leaves the cost base untouched would fail the company.

2. **Plant-by-plant transparency is essential.**  
   We need the actual numbers, not general assertions.

3. **Conflict costs matter.**  
   A prolonged strike would damage output, reputation, and trust — and yes, that has to be priced in.

So far, that is the common ground.

## Where I strongly disagree

### To the Works Council
Your position is still essentially:  
- no closures,  
- no compulsory redundancies,  
- no framework change,  
- and no staged process that could later lead to structural cuts.

That is not a restructuring offer. That is a veto on restructuring.

You keep saying management has not proven closures or forced layoffs are necessary. Fair enough — that is exactly why I asked for the plant-level numbers. But you then jump to the conclusion that because the case is not yet proven, your preferred outcome must therefore be accepted. It does not work that way.

A serious alternative must show:
- how much fixed cost is actually removed,
- how much capacity is actually taken out,
- how the savings persist through 2030,
- and how the brand gets back to target returns.

So far, you have proposed instruments, not a verified savings bridge.

### To the Trade Union
You are more constructive than the works council in tone, but not yet in substance.

You acknowledge that temporary concessions alone may not be enough. Correct. That is precisely the problem. If the business has a structural fixed-cost and under-utilization problem, then a time-limited wage contribution, bonus restraint, working-time flexibility, and voluntary exits may help — but they do not automatically solve the gap.

I also reject the idea that binding job and site guarantees through 2030 can be unconditional if they freeze today’s structure regardless of performance. If you want stronger protection, it must be tied to a package that actually restores competitiveness.

### To the Regional State
You say “no closures in the first phase.” That is not a solution. It is a delay mechanism.

If the state uses its blocking role only to prevent any serious adjustment, it may preserve the appearance of stability while making the eventual adjustment worse. That would not be responsible regional stewardship.

I am open to a staged process, but only if it is tied to measurable outcomes and real restructuring. A blanket no-closure stance is not enough.

### To the Controlling Family
You are right about one thing: a cosmetic compromise would be value-destructive. On that, we agree.

But you also cannot hide behind “long-term value” while refusing the hard question: if the company has excess capacity, some sites, some jobs, and some legacy structures may have to go. That is not ideological. That is governance.

You say you are open to a mixed package. Fine. Then the mixed package has to produce:
- real capacity reduction,
- recurring labour-cost savings,
- and a durable margin improvement.

Not just a nicer headline.

### To Institutional Investors
You are right to insist on a risk-adjusted NPV case. That is the correct standard.

But I will say this bluntly: you cannot ask for “proof” and then simultaneously support a framework that bars the measures that might actually create the proof. If the employee side insists on no closures and no compulsory redundancies as absolute conditions, then the economics of any package are being constrained before the analysis is complete.

Your position should not be “avoid pain at any cost.” It should be “deliver durable value.” On that basis, hard options must stay available until the numbers say otherwise.

## What the expert clarified — and what they did not

The expert was helpful. The key point is this:

- if the problem is mainly variable cost, softer measures may be enough;
- if the problem is mainly fixed-cost under-absorption, then the company needs real capacity reduction.

That is exactly why I keep saying the current footprint cannot be treated as sacrosanct.

The expert did **not** prove that closures are mandatory.  
But they also did **not** prove that your preferred no-closure package is sufficient.

So the conclusion is not “therefore nothing hard can happen.”  
The conclusion is: **show the plant-level bridge, or accept that closures and redundancies remain on the table.**

## My position, clearly

I am not asking for closures for their own sake.  
I am saying:

- if the economics require closures, they remain on the table;
- if the economics require compulsory redundancies, they remain on the table;
- if alternatives can truly match the economics, I will consider them.

That is the real position.

## What would move me

I could move if I saw a package that demonstrably delivers all of the following:

1. **Verified plant-level savings**
2. **Material and durable capacity reduction**
3. **Recurring labor-cost improvement**
4. **A credible path back to target returns**
5. **A workforce contribution that is real, not symbolic**
6. **Implementation certainty through 2030**

If you can deliver that without closures, I’m open.  
If you cannot, then the hard measures remain available.

## What I will not accept

I will not accept:
- preserving every German site regardless of economics,
- excluding compulsory redundancies categorically,
- and leaving the cost base structurally uncompetitive.

That would fail my duty to restore competitiveness, protect long-term investment, and secure the company’s future to 2030.
