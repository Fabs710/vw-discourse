## Expert response: how to judge the three paths

I’ll stay within restructuring finance and operations.

### Bottom line
Based on the facts provided, **none of the parties has yet proven** which path is value-optimal. The right decision depends on plant-level economics that are still missing.

What can be said now:

- **Status quo** is the weakest option if there is real structural overcapacity.
- **Negotiated restructuring without closures** can work only if it produces **durable, recurring fixed-cost reduction** and real capacity/utilization improvement.
- **Closure/consolidation** is usually the strongest lever for permanent fixed-cost removal, but it carries the highest one-off cost, disruption risk, and implementation uncertainty.

A **mixed package** is most plausible economically, but only if it is quantified and enforceable.

## What must be measured, plant by plant

For each German site, management should provide:

1. **Output volume**
2. **Utilization rate**
3. **Direct labor cost per unit**
4. **Fixed manufacturing overhead**
5. **Variable cost per unit**
6. **Product mix and margin contribution**
7. **Logistics cost**
8. **One-off closure / restructuring cost**

10. **Investment required to remain competitive**

Without this, claims like “closures are necessary” or “alternatives are enough” are not evidence-based.

## How to compare the three scenarios

### 1) Status quo
**Pros**
- No immediate disruption
- No closure cost
- No major labor conflict upfront

**Cons**
- Structural overcapacity remains
- Fixed costs stay under-absorbed
- Margin pressure persists
- Likely delays the eventual adjustment

**Conclusion**
Usually the worst long-run outcome if the current cost base is truly uncompetitive.

---

### 2) Negotiated restructuring without closures
This is economically credible only if it includes:

- voluntary exits and attrition
- working-time reduction/flexibility
- redeployment and qualification
- overhead cuts
- product allocation / utilization improvement
- some durable employee contribution
- management overhead reduction

**What it can solve**
- part of the labor-cost gap
- some fixed-cost reduction through headcount and overhead cuts
- some utilization improvement

**What it cannot automatically solve**
- subscale plant economics
- excess footprint fixed costs
- duplicated site infrastructure
- chronic low utilization

**Conclusion**
This can be the best NPV option if the cost gap is moderate and volume can be reallocated efficiently. It fails if it is only a temporary wage concession.

---

### 3) Closure / consolidation
**Pros**
- strongest permanent fixed-cost reduction
- removes duplicated overhead
- can reset utilization at remaining plants

**Cons**
- very large one-off cost
- strike and disruption risk
- logistics transfer cost
- quality/ramp-up risk at receiving plants
- regional and supplier knock-on effects

**Conclusion**
Economically rational if one or more plants are clearly subscale and alternatives cannot close the gap. But the NPV benefit can be eroded sharply by conflict and delay.

---

## The key technical issue: fixed vs variable cost

This is the central question.

If the problem is mainly:
- **variable cost**
then labor moderation and productivity may be enough.

If the problem is mainly:
- **fixed cost under-absorption**
then the company needs real capacity reduction, not just temporary concessions.

That is why “working-time tools” alone are not enough to claim equivalence to closures.

---

## What conflict costs must be included

Any scenario should include:

- lost production during strikes
- restart inefficiency
- overtime and expediting
- supplier disruption
- quality risk after restart
- delay to product launches
- board delay / implementation slippage

These are not side issues. In a large automotive plant, they can materially change the ranking of scenarios.

---

## Regional impact: how to treat it analytically

From a finance perspective, regional impact is not just political noise. It affects economics through:

- supplier concentration risk
- labor availability
- local logistics
- delay risk
- reputational and execution friction

A closure with strong regional backlash may have a lower risk-adjusted NPV than a softer negotiated package, even if its gross savings are higher.

---

## What a decision matrix should show

For each plant and each scenario:

- recurring annual savings
- one-off restructuring costs
- implementation timeline
- conflict-cost estimate
- probability of delay
- 3–5 year NPV
- sensitivity to utilization and strike duration

That is the only rigorous way to decide.
