I’ll respond plainly.

## Where I agree with the works council

I agree that:
- the company needs a **durable restructuring**, not a cosmetic pause
- **plant-by-plant transparency** is essential
- **conflict costs** must be included in the economics
- temporary measures alone are not enough if they do not change the structure
- workers should not be the only ones asked to contribute

That part is correct, and it matters.

## Where I disagree with the works council

I do **not** think it is enough to simply declare:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no staged process,
- and no examination of hard alternatives.

That posture may be understandable politically, but by itself it does **not** answer the economic problem. If the company truly has a fixed-cost and capacity problem, then we need a **verified alternative** that closes the gap. Saying “show me the numbers” is right. But refusing every adjustment mechanism in advance makes that proof impossible to translate into a deal.

So I do not support a blanket refusal of any sequencing or conditionality. What I do support is this: **no irreversible step without a proved case and a fair bargain**.

## Where I agree with management

Management is right on one point: a package that merely buys time will fail.  
If the structure remains uncompetitive, we are back here in 12 to 24 months, and everyone loses.

I also agree that the final settlement must deliver:
- recurring savings,
- better utilization,
- overhead reduction,
- and a credible path to margin improvement.

## Where I disagree with management

What I do **not** accept is management turning “durable restructuring” into a code word for:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- and tearing up the collective framework first, then asking for cooperation later.

That is not a serious bargaining stance. It is an ultimatum.

Management keeps saying the alternatives are not economically equivalent. Fine — but that is still a claim. It has not been proven at the level of:
- each plant,
- each cost block,
- each capacity effect,
- each one-off cost,
- and each conflict-cost scenario.

Until that is shown, closures and forced layoffs remain **options**, not necessities.

## Where I agree with the expert

The expert has clarified the core issue well:
- the company likely has a **fixed-cost absorption problem**
- temporary concessions alone may not be enough
- closure/consolidation can be economically strong, but comes with high one-off and conflict costs
- a **mixed package** is the most plausible route

I agree with that diagnosis.

## Where I disagree with how some are using the expert

The expert’s analysis does **not** justify saying:
- therefore closures must happen,
- or therefore all sites are protected forever.

It says something narrower:
- the answer depends on the plant-level numbers
- a mixed package may be best
- conflict costs and implementation risk can change the result materially

That means the next step is not ideological repetition. It is a **hard, quantified bargaining package**.

## My position on the red lines

Let me be very clear:

- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No plant closures**
- **No dismantling of the collective wage framework**
- **No one-sided pain for workers**

Those remain my lines.

If management, owners, or investors want movement, they need to come with a package that:
- preserves jobs and sites,
- includes a meaningful employee contribution,
- and also forces management and owners to contribute.

That is the only basis on which I can justify a settlement to the membership.

## What I would consider acceptable

I can support a strong, durable package if it includes:

1. **Binding job and site protection**
2. **Time-limited employee contribution**
3. **Working-time flexibility**
4. **Voluntary exits and redeployment**
5. **Qualification and retraining**
6. **Bonus restraint**
7. **Management overhead cuts**
8. **Owner contribution**
9. **Investment commitments for future products**
10. **A transparent savings bridge by plant**

That is fair burden-sharing. It is not a giveaway.

## What I will not accept

I will not accept:
- a “staged” process that is just a delayed closure path
- a temporary concession that becomes permanent insecurity
- a package where workers pay now and management keeps closure power later
- or a deal where labor carries the pain while owners and management merely observe

If the state or others want to preserve trust, they should not sign up to that either.

## To the regional state

Your role as anchor shareholder matters. But “no closures in the first phase” is not strong enough if it leaves the workforce exposed to the same outcome later.

If you want credibility with employees, then use your position to secure:
- no forced layoffs,
- no immediate closures,
- binding investment,
- and a real negotiated transition.

Not a polite delay.

## To the controlling family and investors

If you want long-term value, then show long-term responsibility.

That means:
- management also cuts overhead,
- owners also contribute,
- and the burden is not dumped on the workforce.

I understand the need for competitiveness. But you will not get sustainable competitiveness by breaking the collective framework and expecting loyalty in return.

## My current line

I remain open to a fair, quantified, shared-sacrifice settlement.  
I remain closed to closures and forced layoffs.

If management wants to test whether alternatives can work, then bring the plant-level figures and a concrete burden-sharing package. If they refuse, then they should not be surprised when the workforce uses the instruments it has.
