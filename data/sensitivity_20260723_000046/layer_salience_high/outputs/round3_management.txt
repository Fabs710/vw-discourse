We have now had three rounds, and I will be equally clear.

## Where I agree

I agree with everyone on one point: this cannot be a cosmetic deal.

A settlement that merely postpones the problem, preserves structural overcapacity, and leaves the cost base fundamentally uncompetitive would fail the company. It would not restore margins, would not fund future products, and would not be responsible stewardship.

I also agree that:
- plant-by-plant transparency is necessary,
- conflict costs matter,
- future investment is essential,
- and a durable solution is better than endless confrontation.

So far, that is the only genuine common ground.

## Where I disagree

I do **not** accept the repeated attempt to turn “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no framework change” into the baseline.

That is not a restructuring position. It is a veto on restructuring.

The expert has already made the key economic point: the issue is fixed-cost absorption and durable savings. If German capacity remains too large, then working-time tools, voluntary exits, redeployment, and temporary concessions may help, but they do not automatically solve the structural problem.

So I reject the idea that the current footprint is sacrosanct.

## To the Works Council

You say management has not proven closures or compulsory redundancies are necessary. Fair enough: that is exactly why we asked for plant-level transparency.

But let me be blunt: you still have not shown that your preferred package closes the structural gap.

What you have offered is a list of instruments:
- working-time reductions,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary departures,
- productivity gains,
- temporary employee contributions,
- bonus restraint.

Those are all potentially useful. But they are not yet an economically equivalent alternative.

Why not? Because you have not shown:
- how much fixed cost is actually removed,
- how much capacity is truly taken out,
- how the savings persist through 2030,
- and how the business gets back to target returns without a meaningful footprint adjustment.

You keep saying “no closures.” I am saying: then show me the durable savings bridge. Not slogans. Not moral appeals. A bridge.

Your red line is understandable politically, but it does not answer the economics.

## To the Trade Union

You are more constructive than the works council in one respect: you at least acknowledge the need for a real alternative with numbers.

But your fallback still assumes that softer instruments can do the job of a structural reset. I do not think that is credible unless the numbers prove it.

Temporary wage moderation, bonus restraint, and working-time flexibility may buy time. They do not, by themselves, fix underabsorbed fixed costs at subscale plants.

And I need to say something directly about your stance on guarantees: unconditional job and site guarantees through 2030 are not available if they lock in today’s structure regardless of business performance.

If you want stronger protection, it must be conditional on a package that actually restores competitiveness. Otherwise we are just freezing the problem in place.

## To the Regional State

I respect your political role and your concern for the industrial base. But “no closures in the first phase” is not enough if it becomes a way to block any serious adjustment indefinitely.

The question is not whether closures are nice. They are not. The question is whether the business can be made competitive without them.

If you want me to avoid closures, then help deliver an alternative that is:
- quantified,
- durable,
- and economically equivalent.

If you cannot, then a blanket blocking posture simply protects the status quo, not the future.

## To the Controlling Family

You are right to insist on long-term value and not to support a cosmetic compromise. On that point, we are aligned.

Where I push harder is this: if you want long-term value, then you must accept that some sites, some capacity, and some jobs may need to go if that is what the economics require.

That is not cruelty. It is governance.

But I also note your useful point: a mixed package may be the most plausible path. I agree with that in principle. The mixed package, however, must still deliver:
- a real capacity reduction,
- recurring labor-cost savings,
- and a durable margin improvement.

A mixed package that preserves the cost base and postpones hard choices is not acceptable.

## What would genuinely move management

Let me be precise.

I could move if I saw a package that demonstrably delivers all of the following:

1. **A verified, plant-level savings bridge**
2. **A material and durable reduction in capacity**
3. **Recurring labor-cost savings, not one-off relief**
4. **A credible path back to the return-on-sales target**
5. **A workforce contribution that is real, not symbolic**
6. **Implementation certainty through 2030**

What I cannot accept is a package that:
- leaves every site untouched,
- excludes compulsory redundancies under all circumstances,
- and hopes productivity gains will somehow absorb the gap.

That is not a solution.

## My position on closures and compulsory redundancies

Let me be explicit because there is still too much evasion.

I am not demanding closures for their own sake.

I am saying:
- if the economics require closures, they remain on the table;
- if the economics require compulsory redundancies, they remain on the table;
- if alternatives can truly match the economics, then I am prepared to consider them.

That is the actual position. Not ideology. Not symbolism. Economics.

## My minimum acceptable package

At minimum, I need a package that contains:

- measurable, permanent cost reductions
- meaningful capacity reduction
- a substantial labor-cost contribution
- investment commitments to 2030
- and a mechanism that prevents the savings from evaporating after one bargaining cycle

That means the following instruments can be part of the answer:
- working-time flexibility
- voluntary exits
- redeployment
- qualification
- bonus restraint
- temporary labor-cost contributions
- overhead reductions
- product and investment sequencing

But only if they produce the hard outcomes above.

## My red line

What I will not accept is:
- preserving all German sites regardless of economics,
- ruling out compulsory redundancies categorically,
- and leaving the cost base structurally uncompetitive.

That would fail my duty to restore competitiveness and protect the future of the company.

## Final point

Everyone here is asking for trust. Trust is earned by facing the economics honestly.

So here is the next step I expect:
- a plant-by-plant decision matrix,
- current and target cost positions,
- fixed versus variable cost split,
- savings from each alternative,
- one-off restructuring costs,
- conflict-cost scenarios,
- and the NPV outcome under each route.

Until that exists, I will continue to hold the hard options open.

We need a durable restructuring that restores competitiveness, funds future products, and gets this company back on a sustainable path to 2030. Anything less is not enough.
