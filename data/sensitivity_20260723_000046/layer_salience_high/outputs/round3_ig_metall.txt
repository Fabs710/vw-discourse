I’ll be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the works council, the state, and the expert on one key point: this cannot be a cosmetic deal.  
If the company remains structurally uncompetitive, then we will be back at the table in a year or two, and nobody wants that.

I also agree that we need:
- plant-by-plant transparency,
- a durable restructuring,
- future investment,
- and some form of shared burden.

And yes: conflict costs matter. A long strike would hurt everyone.

## Where I disagree
What I do **not** accept is management’s attempt to turn “durable restructuring” into a code word for:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- and a one-sided wage cut program.

That is not an “economic truth.” It is a bargaining position.

The expert confirmed something important: **no one has yet proven** that closures and forced layoffs are economically necessary. That matters. Until management shows the plant-level numbers, it cannot demand irreversible sacrifices from the workforce as if they were already justified.

## To management
You say soft measures are not enough. Maybe they are not. But you have not proven that.

If you want us to believe that alternatives fail, then show:
- the exact savings gap,
- the fixed vs variable cost split,
- the savings from working-time tools and voluntary exits,
- the one-off costs of closure and redundancy,
- and the cost of conflict.

Because conflict is not a footnote. It is part of the economics.  
A settlement that tears up trust and triggers prolonged industrial action may look “clean” on paper and fail in reality.

## To the works council
I support your refusal of closures and forced layoffs. That is the correct red line.

But I want to be careful about one thing: we should not make the mistake of sounding like we are only saying “no.” We have already signaled a real alternative:
- temporary labour-cost contributions,
- working-time reductions,
- bonus restraint,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- productivity measures,
- and a fair contribution from management and owners.

That is not weakness. It is a serious bargaining offer.

## To the state
Your role is important, and I appreciate that you recognize the need for adjustment without irreversible damage.

But let me be clear: “no closures in the first phase” is not enough if it leaves the door open to the same outcome after the workforce has already given concessions. If you want credibility with employees, you need to help secure:
- binding job protection,
- binding site protection,
- and a negotiated package that cannot simply be undone later.

The workforce will not sign up for a temporary sacrifice that becomes a permanent loss of leverage.

## To the controlling family and investors
You keep saying you want long-term value. Fine. Then show long-term responsibility.

Long-term value is not protected by forcing through the largest possible cut at the expense of trust, skills, and industrial peace. If you break the wage framework and force insecurity across the plants, you may get a near-term headline and long-term damage.

That said, I will also be honest with you: the workforce contribution cannot be symbolic. If we want to avoid closures and forced redundancies, then there must be a meaningful and durable contribution from employees too.

But that contribution must be matched by:
- management restraint,
- owner responsibility,
- and investment commitments.

## What would move us
We can move on:
- the scale of a time-limited employee contribution,
- the mix of working-time reductions and voluntary exits,
- the duration of bonus restraint,
- the sequencing of measures,
- and the structure of a productivity program.

We will **not** move on:
- closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or a settlement where only workers carry the pain.

## Our position on the expert’s analysis
The expert said the rational answer is likely mixed, not binary. I agree.

But “mixed” must mean:
- negotiated restructuring,
- capacity adjustment through social instruments,
- and durable cost reduction without destroying sites.

It does **not** mean:
- half a compromise now,
- then closures later once the workforce has already conceded.

If management wants a mixed package, it must be one that protects jobs and sites, while still delivering real savings through shared sacrifice.

## Minimum acceptable package from our side
Here is the floor:

1. **No compulsory redundancies**
2. **No German plant closures**
3. **Preservation of the collective wage framework**
4. **Time-limited employee contribution**
5. **Management and owner contribution**
6. **Investment commitments for future products**
7. **Capacity reduction through working-time, voluntary exits, and redeployment**
8. **Binding job and site security**

## Red line
If the final package contains closures or forced layoffs, then it is not a settlement. It is an ultimatum.

And if the company tries to impose that ultimatum, the union will use every instrument available to defend the workforce and the collective agreement.

## Final point
We are prepared to help build a durable solution.  
We are **not** prepared to pay for it alone.

If management wants real cooperation, then it must stop treating workers as the easiest adjustment variable and come back with numbers that justify a fair, shared-sacrifice settlement. Otherwise, there will be no basis for agreement.
