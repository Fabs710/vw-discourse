We need to be precise here, because “competitiveness” is not a substitute for a settlement.

## Our position remains clear

We **agree** with the works council, the state, and partially even management on one central point: this company needs a **durable restructuring**, not a cosmetic pause.  
We also agree that the company must remain investable and that future products need financing.

But we **do not accept** the conclusion management keeps trying to force:

- **plant closures in Germany**
- **compulsory redundancies**
- **a one-sided wage and job-cut program**
- **the erosion of the collective bargaining framework**
- **workers carrying the full burden of adjustment**

That is not “necessary”; it is a choice. And it is not one we will accept.

## Where we agree with the works council

We share the works council’s core red lines:
- no plant closures as the starting point,
- no forced layoffs,
- no dismantling of the job-security framework,
- no unilateral management diktat.

That is not just a political preference. It is a matter of industrial peace and fairness. If the company wants the workforce to help stabilize the business, then the workforce must not be treated as the disposable cost item while ownership and management keep their freedom intact.

## Where we differ slightly from the works council

I support a **strong bargaining line**, but I want to be equally explicit: if we say “no closures, no layoffs” then we must also be ready to table a **real alternative package with numbers**. Otherwise management will keep claiming there is no credible counter-plan.

So here is our contribution:

### What we are prepared to discuss
- temporary labour-cost contributions
- working-time reductions
- capacity reduction through instruments short of closure
- voluntary exits and natural attrition
- redeployment and qualification
- productivity measures
- bonus restraint or waiver
- a time-limited solidarity contribution

### What those measures must be tied to
- binding site security
- protection against compulsory redundancies
- investment commitments
- future-product commitments
- a fair burden-sharing arrangement

Employees can contribute. We have said that before and we mean it. But contribution is only legitimate if it is **shared**.

## To management: your “arithmetics” argument is not enough

You keep saying the cost base must be cut materially. We hear you. But you still have not shown that closures and forced layoffs are the only route.

If you want us to take your numbers seriously, then put forward:
- the exact savings gap,
- the exact savings needed per site,
- the exact comparison between closure, layoffs, and alternative measures,
- and the cost of conflict, strikes, recruitment damage, and lost know-how.

Because those costs exist too. They are not a footnote. A restructuring that destroys trust, interrupts production, and weakens the employer brand may be “clean” on a spreadsheet and deeply damaging in reality.

And let me say this plainly: if management comes back with a package that assumes the workforce must accept all of the sacrifice while the strategic setup remains untouched, then there will be no basis for agreement.

## To the controlling family and investors

We hear the insistence on long-term value. Fine. But long-term value is not protected by imposing maximal pain on the workforce.

A package that tears up guarantees, creates fear in every plant, and forces permanent insecurity may reduce headcount, but it also weakens:
- motivation,
- retention,
- industrial relations,
- execution,
- and ultimately product quality and productivity.

That is not value creation. That is value destruction dressed up as discipline.

If you want discipline, then contribute through:
- moderated return demands,
- support for investment,
- governance restraint on short-term pressure,
- and acceptance of a socially sustainable solution.

## To the regional state

Your stance is broadly aligned with ours, and that matters. But let me be direct: “no unilateral closures” is not enough. The danger here is not only unilateral action. It is any attempt to use governance to force through closures or compulsory redundancies over the objections of the workforce.

If the state wants to play its role seriously, it should help secure:
- binding site protection,
- a negotiated restructuring,
- and a commitment that adjustment happens through social instruments, not dismissals.

## On numbers and conditions

Since the moderator asked for concrete packages, here is our position:

### Minimum acceptable union package
1. **No compulsory redundancies**
2. **No plant closures in Germany**
3. **Preservation of collective bargaining standards**
4. **A time-limited employee contribution**
5. **Binding investment and product commitments**
6. **Capacity reduction through working-time and voluntary measures**
7. **Owner and management contribution alongside the workforce**

### Fallback option
If management insists on a stronger adjustment, then the fallback is **not** layoffs or closures.  
The fallback is:
- more working-time reduction,
- temporary wage moderation,
- deeper bonus restraint,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- and a jointly monitored productivity program.

That is a hard package. It is not painless. But it is fairer than shifting the burden onto workers who did not create the strategic errors alone.

### Non-negotiable red line
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No German plant closures**
- **No dismantling of the collective wage framework as the price of restructuring**

## What would move us

We can move on:
- the scale and duration of employee contributions,
- the mix of working-time and productivity measures,
- the design of a solidarity package,
- and the timing of implementation.

We will **not** move on:
- closures,
- forced layoffs,
- or a settlement where workers pay and everyone else watches.

## Final point

If management wants an agreement, it must stop demanding that the workforce prove its loyalty by accepting irreversible damage. Loyalty is not surrender.

We are ready for a serious settlement — but it must be:
- socially fair,
- industrially credible,
- and based on shared sacrifice.

Anything else will meet resistance.
