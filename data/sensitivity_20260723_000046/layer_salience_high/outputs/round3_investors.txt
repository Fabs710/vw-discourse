We appreciate the clearer plant-level framing. Our position is unchanged in substance, but the discussion has now sharpened the investment test.

## Where we agree
We agree with management, the controlling family, the state, and the expert on the essentials:

- this must be a **durable** restructuring, not a symbolic truce;
- the company needs **credible margin repair** and future investment capacity;
- **plant-by-plant transparency** is non-negotiable;
- **fixed-cost absorption** is the real economic problem;
- and conflict costs are real and must be part of the valuation.

We also agree that a purely cosmetic no-closure deal would be value-destructive. We would not support that.

## Where we disagree
We do **not** accept the works council and union position that “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” can simply be declared as a baseline and then treated as economically sufficient because a menu of softer instruments is listed.

Listing tools is not the same as proving a savings bridge.

At the same time, we also do **not** accept management’s attempt to make closures and forced redundancies sound like the only serious route. They may be necessary, but they are not yet proven to be the least value-destructive route once conflict risk, implementation delay, and regional damage are included.

That is the key point for us: **prove the net value case**.

## What the expert confirmed
The expert’s analysis supports our central concern:

- if fixed costs are under-absorbed, **soft measures alone may not be enough**;
- but closure is not automatically superior once you include one-off costs and conflict costs;
- the rational answer is likely a **mixed package**, if and only if it is genuinely durable.

That is consistent with our position. We are not demanding closures for ideology. We are demanding a **risk-adjusted, NPV-positive** outcome.

## To management
You now have to do more than say “the arithmetic is bad.”

We need the actual investment case:
- plant-by-plant cost position,
- fixed versus variable split,
- utilization by site,
- savings from each measure,
- one-off restructuring costs,
- conflict-cost sensitivity,
- and the 3–5 year NPV under each scenario.

Without that, management has not justified asking capital markets to accept irreversible damage.

## To the works council
Your minimum package is not investable unless it closes the structural gap. Right now, it is a set of protections, not a proven turnaround plan.

You say alternatives may exist. Fine. Then quantify them. Show:
- how much recurring savings each delivers,
- how much capacity reduction is real,
- how long the concessions last,
- and why the result is durable through 2030.

Until then, “no closures” is a wish, not a financial plan.

And to be direct: if the final package preserves every site and excludes compulsory redundancies under all circumstances, we would regard that as structurally inadequate unless the numbers clearly prove otherwise.

## To the union
Your willingness to discuss time-limited contributions is constructive. But temporary relief only works if it is large enough and durable enough to materially improve returns.

We agree with your point that workers should not carry the burden alone. Management and owners must contribute too. But if the package mostly reduces tension without materially changing economics, we would oppose it.

## To the regional state
We recognize your blocking role and your concern for regional industrial stability. That is legitimate.

But the state should not use that role to freeze an uncompetitive structure in place. Long-term value is not protected by preserving sites that structurally destroy returns.

If you want investor support for a softer path, then that path must still deliver:
- durable savings,
- capacity adjustment,
- and a credible return to target margins.

No one has established that yet.

## To the controlling family
You are right that we cannot accept a cosmetic settlement. We agree.

But we also want to be clear: “some sites, some capacity, and some jobs may have to go” is only acceptable if it is shown to be the **least value-destructive** route.

The burden of proof remains with management and the family bloc to show that the package they prefer is better, on a risk-adjusted basis, than a negotiated mixed alternative.

## What would move us
We could support a package if it contains all of the following:

1. **Verified plant-level savings**
2. **Durable capacity reduction**
3. **Recurring labor-cost savings**
4. **Clear one-off cost and conflict-cost analysis**
5. **A credible path back to margin targets**
6. **Implementation certainty**
7. **Conditional, not unconditional, protections**

## What would not move us
We would not support:
- a cosmetic no-closure settlement with no fixed-cost solution;
- a deal that simply postpones the crisis;
- or an outcome that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive.

## Our bottom line
We are open to a mixed package. We are open to negotiated restructuring. We are even open to avoiding closures if — and only if — the numbers show that the result is economically equivalent and durable.

But we will not support an outcome that leaves the business with:
- persistent overcapacity,
- impaired margins,
- and no credible path back to competitiveness.

So the next step is simple: produce the plant-by-plant matrix, the NPV comparison, and the conflict-cost sensitivity. Then the capital markets can judge whether the proposed compromise is real value creation or just delayed deterioration.
