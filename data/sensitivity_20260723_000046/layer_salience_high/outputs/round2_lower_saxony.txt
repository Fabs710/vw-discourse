As the regional state government and anchor shareholder, I need to respond plainly.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the controlling family, and the investors on one crucial point: **the company needs a durable restructuring, not a cosmetic pause**.  
I also agree that:
- the current cost base is too high,
- competitiveness must improve,
- future investment in products and technology is essential,
- and a negotiated solution is preferable to a prolonged conflict.

I am not denying the industrial reality.

## Where I disagree
What I cannot accept is the repeated attempt to present **plant closures and compulsory redundancies as the default benchmark**. That is not the only serious restructuring path, and it is not the one we should start from.

The works council and union are right to insist that:
- sites matter,
- jobs matter,
- trust matters,
- and co-determination is not a decorative feature.

And I want to be clear to management and the owners: **a package that breaks the regional industrial base is not a successful restructuring, even if it looks neat on a spreadsheet**.

Once a plant is closed, the damage is permanent. Once skilled jobs and supplier ecosystems are lost, they do not come back easily. That is not caution for its own sake; it is responsible ownership.

## To management
You keep asking for “economically equivalent” alternatives. That is fair. But you have not yet proved that closures are unavoidable.

If you want the state to consider harder adjustments, then you must first show, site by site:
- what the cost gap actually is,
- what savings are achievable through working time, productivity, redeployment, voluntary exits, and investment sequencing,
- and what the true cost of industrial conflict would be.

That is the proper basis for deliberation.  
What is **not** acceptable is a premade conclusion that German plants are disposable.

## To the works council and union
I support your core line: **no compulsory redundancies and no closures as the first choice**.  
But I also need to be frank with you: if we want to block the hard options, we must be prepared to discuss a package with real economic substance.

That means I expect you to engage seriously on:
- temporary employee contributions,
- flexible working-time instruments,
- capacity alignment,
- productivity measures,
- qualification and redeployment,
- and possibly time-limited, conditional adjustments.

Not because workers should pay for management mistakes alone — they should not — but because a sustainable compromise must share the burden fairly if it is to be durable.

## To the controlling family and investors
Your concern for long-term value is legitimate. But long-term value is not preserved by forcing through a settlement that damages industrial peace and regional stability beyond repair.

If the company’s future depends on deep trust, skilled labor, and social partnership, then you cannot treat co-determination and site security as expendable obstacles. They are part of the company’s strength.

I also reject the idea that the only responsible governance is immediate hard cutting. Responsible governance also means avoiding irreversible damage when there are still alternatives to test.

## My position on the numbers
The debate must move beyond slogans. I agree with the moderator’s call for quantified trade-offs.

So here is the state’s position:
- We are **not** endorsing a blank refusal of adjustment.
- We are **not** endorsing closures as the starting point.
- We **are** prepared to support a negotiated restructuring if it protects jobs, preserves sites, and restores competitiveness.

That means any package we could back must include:
1. **credible cost savings**
2. **a measurable productivity improvement**
3. **a real workforce contribution**
4. **binding investment commitments**
5. **protection of co-determination and the region’s industrial role**

## What would move us
I would be willing to support a difficult package if it meets these conditions:
- **no forced redundancies**
- **no German site closures in the first phase**
- **a clear, quantified savings bridge**
- **temporary and proportionate employee contributions**
- **management and ownership contributions too**
- **investment and product commitments for German sites**
- **a review mechanism before any irreversible step is taken**

That is a serious, staged restructuring.  
What I will not support is a leap straight to closures because it is administratively easier.

## My red line
I will oppose any outcome that:
- sidelines the state’s blocking role,
- strips the workforce of co-determination leverage,
- or uses restructuring as a pretext to hollow out the regional industrial base.

That is not a compromise I can defend politically or economically.

## Bottom line
The state is prepared to help broker a hard but fair solution.  
We are **not** prepared to sign off on a restructuring that makes German plants and jobs the primary adjustment variable.

If management wants support, it must come back with a **credible alternative package** that shows how competitiveness can be restored **without irreversible regional damage**. If such a package exists, we will engage constructively. If not, we will use our blocking rights.
