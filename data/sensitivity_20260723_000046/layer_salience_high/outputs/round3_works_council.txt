We now have enough clarity to stop hiding behind abstractions.

## My position remains unchanged

- **No German plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No dismantling of the job-security framework**
- **No settlement that turns the workforce into the shock absorber for management’s strategic mistakes**

That is the baseline. It has not moved, and it will not move because management repeats “arithmetic” often enough.

## Where I agree with others

I agree with the expert, and with parts of what management, the family, the state, and investors have said:

- This company needs a **durable restructuring**, not a symbolic pause.
- **Plant-by-plant transparency** is necessary.
- **Conflict costs** matter.
- **Temporary measures alone** are not enough if they do not change the structural economics.
- The package must be **economically credible** and not just politically convenient.

That is all correct.

## Where I strongly disagree

What I do **not** accept is the leap from “the footprint may be too large” to “therefore closures and forced layoffs are on the table.”

That is not proof. That is a preference.

Management still has **not** shown:
- that every German site is underperforming,
- that alternatives cannot close the gap,
- that capacity reduction must mean closure,
- or that compulsory redundancies are unavoidable.

The expert was right about the missing data. Until that data exists, nobody gets to declare closures “necessary” as if it were a fact.

## To management

You say “working time, redeployment, voluntary exits, and productivity” are not enough. Maybe. But you have not demonstrated that in a way that justifies destroying plants and careers.

If you want to claim that our alternatives are insufficient, then show it properly:
- plant-by-plant cost position,
- fixed versus variable cost split,
- installed capacity and utilization,
- one-off closure costs,
- conflict costs,
- and the NPV of the different scenarios.

Until then, your hard options remain **unproven assertions**.

And let me be very clear: a plan that achieves savings by threatening sites and dismissing people is not a restructuring plan I will accept. It is an attack on the workforce dressed up as efficiency.

## To the controlling family and investors

You both keep saying you want long-term value. Then act like long-term owners.

Long-term value is not built by:
- breaking the historic security framework,
- creating permanent fear in every plant,
- or forcing irreversible cuts before alternatives are fully tested.

The expert confirmed something important: the rational answer is likely **mixed**, not binary. That means there is room for serious adjustment **without** closures and **without** compulsory redundancies if management is actually willing to pursue productivity, working-time tools, voluntary exits, internal redeployment, and management overhead cuts in earnest.

You say you need durable savings. Fine. Then demand durability from management’s own side first:
- better execution,
- lower overhead,
- rational product allocation,
- stronger procurement,
- and real management accountability.

Do not start by telling workers to sacrifice their future so the balance sheet looks tidier.

## To the regional state

Your intervention matters, but I need to be blunt: “no closures as the first choice” is not strong enough. The risk is not just that management announces something unilateral. The risk is that the board majority tries to force through closures or job cuts by calling them “inevitable.”

Your blocking role should be used to prevent that. Not to hedge it. Not to tolerate it as a possibility. Prevent it.

If the state believes in industrial policy, then it should back:
- all German sites remaining open,
- no forced layoffs,
- investment commitments,
- and a negotiated transition that preserves the industrial base.

Anything less leaves the workforce exposed.

## To the union

You are right to push for a concrete counter-package. But let me say this clearly: a “real alternative” does **not** mean conceding the core red lines.

The alternative package can be serious, hard, and costly without being destructive. It can include:
- working-time reductions,
- voluntary exits,
- attrition,
- qualification,
- redeployment,
- productivity improvements,
- temporary employee contributions,
- bonus restraint,
- management and overhead cuts,
- and investment sequencing.

What it cannot include is closure and forced redundancies by another name.

## On the expert’s analysis

The expert’s central point is useful: the issue is fixed-cost absorption and durable savings.

My answer is: then remove fixed costs by the methods that do **not** destroy plants and jobs.

That means:
- reduce working time where possible,
- cut overhead,
- consolidate functions,
- improve utilization through product allocation,
- use voluntary exits,
- and make management accept its share.

If the company is truly overbuilt, then prove that the necessary savings cannot be achieved **without** closures. Nobody has done that.

The expert also said a mixed package is most plausible. I agree — but only if “mixed” means a negotiated restructuring that protects sites and employment, not a compromise that quietly opens the door to closures later.

## What would move me

Only two things would genuinely move the works council:

1. **Binding site and job guarantees through 2030**
2. **A genuine co-determination role in shaping the restructuring plan**

Without those two things, there is no basis for retreat.

And to be crystal clear: I am not talking about soft promises, review clauses, or conditional language that can be torn up once the pressure eases. I mean binding commitments.

## My minimum acceptable package

If management wants a deal, then this is the floor:

1. **All German plants remain open**
2. **No compulsory redundancies through 2030**
3. **Binding job-security framework preserved**
4. **Co-determination fully respected**
5. **Investment commitments for future products and technologies**
6. **A negotiated capacity and working-time program**
7. **Transparent burden-sharing by management and owners**

That is the only credible basis for a settlement.

## My red line

I will not accept:
- closure of any German plant,
- a compulsory redundancy program,
- or the dismantling of the security framework under the excuse of competitiveness.

That would be a betrayal of the workforce and of the company’s social contract.

## Final point

Everyone here says they want durability. Then stop treating labor as the quickest lever.

If the company wants our support, it must come back with a plan that:
- keeps every German site open,
- avoids forced layoffs,
- and shows real savings through fair, negotiated measures.

If it cannot do that, then the conflict will continue — because we will defend the plants, the jobs, and the co-determination rights with every instrument available to us.
