I’ll respond plainly.

## Where I agree

I agree with management, the expert, the state, the union, and the works council on one central point: **this cannot be a cosmetic settlement**.

If the company leaves this round with:
- the same structural overcapacity,
- the same uncompetitive cost base,
- and only a temporary truce,

then we will have failed as owners. That would erode long-term value and store up a worse crisis later.

I also agree that:
- future investment in products and technology is essential,
- plant-by-plant transparency is necessary,
- and conflict costs are real and must be considered.

## Where I disagree

I do **not** accept the works council and union line that all German sites are effectively untouchable and that compulsory redundancies are excluded in all circumstances.

That is not a restructuring framework. It is a veto on restructuring.

At the same time, I also do **not** accept management’s implication that closures and layoffs are automatically the right answer simply because they are the cleanest accounting solution. They are not clean if they trigger prolonged conflict, operational disruption, reputational damage, and political blockage.

So I am not aligned with either extreme.

## What the expert changed — and what it did not

The expert helped clarify the real issue: **fixed-cost absorption and durable savings**. That was useful.

What it did **not** prove is that the employee-side package is economically equivalent. It may be part of the answer, but it is not yet demonstrated to be enough.

Equally, the expert did **not** prove that closures are unavoidable. That burden of proof is still on management.

So the position remains:

- employee-side soft measures may help materially,
- closures and redundancies may be necessary in some scenarios,
- but neither side has yet shown a complete plant-level case.

## To management

Your diagnosis may be directionally right, but I still do not think you have made the case with enough precision to ask the board to accept irreversible measures.

I want to see:
- the exact savings gap,
- plant-by-plant cost positions,
- fixed versus variable cost split,
- utilization data,
- one-off closure and redundancy costs,
- and the conflict-cost sensitivity.

Without that, “the arithmetic is bad” is not sufficient governance-grade evidence.

If you want support from the controlling shareholder, give us the actual bridge to sustainable margins, not just urgency.

## To the works council

Your minimum package is politically understandable, but it is **not yet an economically complete answer**.

A binding guarantee through 2030, with no closures and no compulsory redundancies, is only credible if you can show how the company reaches competitiveness **despite** that constraint.

So far, I have heard:
- working-time reductions,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary exits,
- productivity gains,
- bonus restraint,
- temporary employee contributions.

Those are all legitimate tools. But you have not shown that they close the structural gap across the footprint.

You keep asking management to prove closures are necessary. Fair. But you must also prove that your alternative is not just a delay mechanism.

## To the union

You are the most constructive of the employee-side voices because you at least acknowledge that a real counter-package needs numbers and burden-sharing.

That said, your fallback still assumes that temporary concessions can solve a structural problem. They may help; they are unlikely to be enough on their own if the plants remain subscale.

So I support:
- time-limited employee contributions,
- working-time flexibility,
- bonus restraint,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- and qualification.

I do **not** support turning those into a permanent shield against any deeper restructuring if the economics do not work.

## To the regional state

Your role matters, and I value it. But I need to be very clear: “no closures in the first phase” is not the same as a credible long-term answer.

If the state blocks every meaningful adjustment, it may preserve sites temporarily while weakening the enterprise that supports them. That would not be a success for the region either.

I am open to a staged solution, but only if it contains:
- real savings,
- real capacity adjustment,
- real performance discipline,
- and a path to restored competitiveness.

## On closures and compulsory redundancies

Let me be explicit.

I am **not** endorsing closures as a first choice.  
I am **not** endorsing compulsory redundancies as a first choice.

But I am also **not** ruling them out in principle if they are the only way to restore a viable cost base and protect the company’s future.

That is the point the employee side keeps refusing to face. A controlling shareholder cannot accept a promise that preserves every legacy structure regardless of business reality.

## What would move me

A credible package would need to show all of the following:

1. **Verified plant-level savings**
2. **Durable capacity reduction**
3. **Recurring labor-cost improvement**
4. **A credible path back to margin targets**
5. **A workforce contribution that is real, not symbolic**
6. **Implementation certainty through 2030**

If those conditions can be met without closures, I will support that route.

If they cannot, then hard options remain on the table.

## My red lines

What I will **not** accept is:
- preserving all German sites regardless of economics,
- excluding compulsory redundancies categorically,
- and leaving the company structurally uncompetitive.

That would be value-destructive, and it would fail the stewardship obligation of the family holding.
