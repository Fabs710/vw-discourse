We are aligned on the **goal**: a durable restructuring that restores margins, not a cosmetic pause. But we are not aligned on **what evidence is sufficient** to justify the options.

## Where we agree
We agree with management, the family, the state, and the expert that:

- the company has a **structural competitiveness problem**
- **fixed-cost absorption** is the key issue
- a deal must be **durable**
- **plant-by-plant transparency** is essential
- **conflict costs** must be included
- a weak compromise that preserves overcapacity would destroy value

That is the right baseline.

## Where we disagree with the works council and union
Your position still amounts to:  
**no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no meaningful structural downside, but still expect the market to believe the turnaround is real.**

That is not enough for us.

You have proposed many instruments:
- working-time changes
- voluntary exits
- redeployment
- qualification
- productivity gains
- bonus restraint
- temporary employee contributions

We do not dismiss those tools. We would support them **if they produce verified, recurring savings**. But so far, they are presented as a package of intentions, not as a quantified savings bridge.

What matters to us is not whether a measure sounds socially acceptable. What matters is whether it closes the gap in a way that is:
- **durable**
- **measurable**
- **NPV-positive**
- and **credible through 2030**

If the alternatives cannot match the economics, then they are not an acceptable substitute for structural action.

## Where we disagree with management
Management is right to insist that hard options remain on the table. But saying “closures and forced redundancies may be needed” is not yet the same as proving that they maximize risk-adjusted value.

That proof still needs to be done plant by plant.

A closure may look efficient on a narrow cost basis, but if it triggers:
- long strikes
- implementation delay
- supplier disruption
- political blockage
- restart inefficiencies
- and reputational damage

then the value case can collapse quickly.

So we support keeping hard options available, but we do **not** support acting on them without a quantified comparison of:
- recurring savings
- one-off costs
- conflict costs
- timing
- and 3–5 year NPV

## Where we disagree with the state and family
We understand the political and governance constraints. But “no closures in the first phase” or “staged adjustment” is only acceptable if it is a **genuine value-preserving bridge**, not a delay tactic.

A staged process is useful only if it has:
- clear targets
- clear metrics
- clear escalation rules
- and no automatic protection for underperforming assets

If it just postpones the inevitable, it destroys credibility and weakens the company’s margin recovery.

Likewise, long-term value does not mean preserving every site regardless of economics. We will not support a framework that freezes the cost base while hoping growth and product mix magically repair margins.

## Our position on the expert’s analysis
The expert’s core point is right: this is about fixed costs, utilization, and durable savings.

What the expert did **not** establish is that any one route is automatically superior. That’s why we insist on the plant-level matrix.

But the expert also did not validate the employee-side claim that all required savings can be achieved without closures or compulsory redundancies. That burden of proof remains unmet.

## Our non-negotiables
Our core position remains unchanged:

- We will **not** support a cosmetic settlement.
- We will **not** support an outcome that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive.
- We will **not** support a deal where concessions are temporary but the savings are not durable.
- We will **not** support capital allocation to a business that has not proven a credible route back to target margins.

## What would move us
We could support a mixed package if it includes all of the following:

1. **Verified plant-level savings**
2. **Durable capacity reduction**
3. **Recurring labor-cost reduction**
4. **One-off and conflict-cost analysis**
5. **A credible path back to margin targets**
6. **Conditional protections, not unconditional guarantees**
7. **Implementation certainty through 2030**

That means we are open to negotiated restructuring, but only if it is financially equivalent in substance, not just politically palatable in language.

## What we need next
The next step must be the same one the expert and moderator have already flagged:

[SUMMON: independent plant-by-plant restructuring auditor with access to internal cost, utilization, one-off restructuring costs, and conflict-cost data, to produce a risk-adjusted NPV comparison of closure, negotiated restructuring without closures, and status quo]

Without that, this discussion remains a contest of assertions.

## Bottom line
We are open to a negotiated solution. We are not open to a solution that protects every legacy structure and calls that competitiveness.

If the numbers show that non-closure options can truly restore margins on a durable basis, we will back them.  
If they do not, then the hard measures must stay on the table.
