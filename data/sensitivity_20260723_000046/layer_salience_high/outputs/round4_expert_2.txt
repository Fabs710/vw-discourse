## Decision matrix: closure vs negotiated restructuring vs status quo

### 1) Core scenarios to compare
For each German plant, model three cases:

1. **Closure / consolidation**
   - site shutdown or major volume transfer
   - headcount reduction
   - asset write-down / decommissioning
   - production moved to other plants or external sourcing

2. **Negotiated no-closure restructuring**
   - no plant closure
   - capacity reduction through working time, redeployment, voluntary exits, product allocation changes
   - wage / bonus / working-time contribution
   - management overhead cuts
   - possible time-limited job protection

3. **Status quo**
   - no major restructuring beyond normal attrition
   - useful as the baseline to show the cost of inaction

## 3) Financial model structure

### A. Recurring annual effects
For each scenario and plant, estimate:

- labor cost savings
- overhead savings
- productivity gains
- logistics changes
- procurement effects, if material
- lost contribution margin from removed output
- temporary concessions that expire after a defined period

### B. One-off costs
Include:

- severance
- early retirement and bridging payments
- redeployment and retraining
- tooling transfer
- plant decommissioning
- lease / facility exit costs
- IT and systems migration
- ramp-up inefficiency at receiving sites
- inventory write-offs or transfer losses

### C. Conflict / industrial action costs
Estimate separately:

- strike-related lost output
- lost shipments / delayed deliveries
- restart inefficiency
- supplier disruption
- quality and launch delay costs
- management time / crisis response cost

These should be modeled probabilistically, not as a single fixed number.

## 5) What counts as a viable negotiated restructuring?
A no-closure package is economically credible if it:

- closes most of the structural fixed-cost gap
- produces durable savings through 2030 or beyond
- does not rely mainly on temporary wage sacrifices
- keeps conflict costs below the closure case
- preserves acceptable utilization levels
- avoids large hidden costs from underabsorption or excess complexity

In practice, if the savings come mostly from:
- one-time pay moderation,
- temporary bonuses,
- or short-lived working-time reductions,

then the package may improve liquidity but still fail to solve the structural problem.

---

## 6) What would make consolidation economically stronger?
Closure or consolidation becomes more compelling if:

- a plant is structurally underutilized
- its product can be moved with limited capex
- the receiving plant has spare capacity
- fixed cost removal is large and durable
- one-off transfer costs are moderate
- strike / conflict risk is manageable
- the 3–5 year risk-adjusted NPV clearly exceeds the negotiated alternative

## 7) Sensitivity cases to run
At minimum, test these sensitivities:

- strike lasts 1, 2, or 4 weeks
- labor concession lasts 2, 4, or 6 years
- volume transferred at 80%, 90%, or 100% of plan
- receiving plant ramp-up delayed by 3 to 12 months
- severance / exit costs +20% / +40%
- energy, logistics, or materials inflation
- demand downside from weak European market
- quality or launch delay penalty

## 8) Practical interpretation
Without plant-level numbers, nobody can honestly claim either:

- that closures are definitely necessary, or
- that a no-closure package is definitely sufficient.

The evidence standard should be:
- plant-by-plant cost base,
- verified fixed vs variable cost split,
- one-off transition costs,
- conflict costs,
- and risk-adjusted NPV over 3 to 5 years.
