I’ll be direct.

## Where I agree

I agree with management, the expert, the state, the union, and the works council on one essential point: **this must be a durable restructuring, not a cosmetic pause**.

A deal that:
- leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive,
- preserves overcapacity,
- and simply buys industrial peace for a year or two,

would be value-destructive. As controlling owners, we cannot support that. The family holding’s interest is long-term enterprise value, not short-term optics.

I also agree that:
- **plant-by-plant transparency** is necessary,
- **conflict costs** are real,
- **future investment** is essential,
- and a **shared burden** is the only credible basis for a settlement.

## Where I disagree

### To the Works Council
Your insistence on:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no framework change,
- and no staged process with any irreversible option later,

is not a restructuring position. It is a veto on restructuring.

I understand the political and social logic, but it does not answer the economic problem. If the numbers show that the footprint is too large, you cannot simply declare the current structure sacrosanct and expect the company to become competitive by willpower.

At the same time, I do **not** accept management’s implication that closures and layoffs are automatically the correct answer. They are not if they trigger prolonged conflict, operational disruption, political blockage, and reputational damage that wipe out the savings.

So I am not aligned with either extreme.

### To the Trade Union
You are more constructive than the works council in acknowledging the need for a real counter-package. But I still do not see proof that a menu of softer instruments is enough.

Working-time tools, voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, bonus restraint, and temporary employee contributions are all useful. But if the core problem is fixed-cost absorption and underutilization, those measures may reduce pain without actually restoring margin.

I also do **not** support unconditional protection through 2030 if it freezes today’s structure regardless of performance. Protection must be earned by a package that materially improves competitiveness.

### To the Regional State
I respect your blocking role and your industrial-policy concern. But “no closures in the first phase” is not enough if it becomes a permanent shield against necessary adjustment.

If the state uses its position only to delay hard choices, it may preserve sites today and weaken the company that supports them tomorrow. That would not be good stewardship for the region either.

I am open to a staged process, but only if it is tied to measurable outcomes and real restructuring.

### To Institutional Investors
You are right to insist on a risk-adjusted NPV case. That is the correct discipline.

But that discipline must be applied honestly. If the employee side rules out all hard measures in advance, then it is not serious to demand proof that hard measures are better. We need the actual comparison, not ideological preferences.

## What the expert clarified — and what they did not

The expert clarified the key issue: **fixed-cost absorption and durable savings**.

That is helpful.

What the expert did **not** prove is that the employee-side package is economically equivalent. It may be part of the answer, but it has not yet been shown to be enough.

Equally, the expert did **not** prove that closures are unavoidable. That burden remains with management.

So the position is still:

- soft measures may materially help,
- closures and redundancies may be necessary in some scenarios,
- but neither side has yet shown a complete plant-level case.

## My position on closures and compulsory redundancies

I am **not** demanding closures for their own sake.  
I am saying:

- if the economics require closures, they remain on the table;
- if the economics require compulsory redundancies, they remain on the table;
- if alternatives can truly match the economics, I am prepared to consider them.

That is the actual governance position. Not ideology. Not symbolism. Economics.

## What would move me

A credible package would need to show all of the following:

1. **Verified plant-level savings**
2. **Material and durable capacity reduction**
3. **Recurring labor-cost improvement**
4. **A credible path back to margin targets**
5. **A workforce contribution that is real, not symbolic**
6. **Implementation certainty through 2030**

If those conditions can be met without closures, I will support that route.

If they cannot, then hard options remain on the table.

## What I will not accept

I will not accept:
- preserving every German site regardless of economics,
- excluding compulsory redundancies categorically,
- and leaving the company structurally uncompetitive.

That would be value-destructive, and it would fail the stewardship obligation of the family holding.

## My practical request

Before any final governance decision, I want the promised **decision matrix by plant** with:
- current cost position,
- target cost position,
- fixed versus variable cost split,
- utilization,
- savings by instrument,
- one-off restructuring cost,
- conflict-cost sensitivity,
- and 3–5 year NPV.

[SUMMON: Independent plant-by-plant restructuring auditor with access to internal cost, capacity, and conflict-cost data]

## Bottom line

I am prepared to support a hard but negotiated settlement.  
I am **not** prepared to support a settlement that protects everything except competitiveness.

The goal is not to maximize pain. The goal is to restore durable competitiveness, protect long-term value, and avoid a drawn-out conflict that destroys the company from within.

If the alternatives can truly match the economics, I will back them. If not, then the hard measures remain on the table.
