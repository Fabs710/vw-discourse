We agree with the core diagnosis: this business has a structural cost and footprint problem, and a cosmetic compromise would destroy value. We also agree that a prolonged conflict is expensive and should be avoided.

Where we disagree is on the idea that “no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” can simply be asserted as red lines and still be compatible with a credible turnaround. They may be desirable from the workforce’s perspective, but they are not sufficient for shareholders. If those positions block a real and durable reset of the cost base, they are value-destructive.

## Our position on the competing claims

### To management
You are right that the company needs a durable margin reset, not a temporary patch. We support structural action, and we are open to closures if they are economically justified and necessary.

But you have not yet done enough to prove that closures are the only way. The expert input was useful: the burden is on management to quantify whether a non-closure package can achieve equivalent permanent savings and capacity reduction. Until that is shown, closures are an option, not a conclusion.

What we will not support is management using the threat of closures to force a weak settlement that looks tough but leaves the cost base intact. If you want capital-market backing, bring numbers:
- site-by-site economics
- utilization rates
- fixed-cost burden
- savings by scenario
- payback and implementation risk

### To the Works Council and the Union
We hear your concern about job losses and closures, but investors cannot back an arrangement that preserves every site at any cost. If your alternative package does not deliver materially lower capacity, lower fixed cost, and durable labor-cost relief, then it is not an acceptable substitute.

Voluntary-only tools, working-time tweaks, and temporary concessions may help, but they are not enough if overcapacity is structural. We do not accept a solution that delays the problem and then asks shareholders to live with structurally impaired margins.

If you want to avoid closures, then the alternative must be genuinely equivalent in economic effect, not just socially preferable.

### To the State Government
We respect your role and the importance of the industrial base, but a blocking position cannot be used to entrench inefficiency indefinitely. Protecting jobs is not the same as protecting long-term enterprise value. If the footprint is uncompetitive, the company will weaken anyway.

At the same time, your call for hard numbers is correct. We support a quantified comparison before any irreversible decision is taken.

### To the Controlling Family
We appreciate the long-term view, and we agree that a value-destructive confrontation is unacceptable. But long-term ownership also means accepting hard structural change where needed. A solution that preserves appearance while leaving overcapacity and weak margins in place is not stewardship.

## What would move us
We would support a non-closure package only if it clearly includes:
- permanent capacity reduction
- substantial and durable fixed-cost elimination
- measurable workforce reduction at scale
- credible site consolidation inside the remaining footprint
- hard implementation milestones
- no open-ended concessions that expire before the savings are realized

Anything less is not equivalent to closures. It is postponement.

## Our bottom line
We are not attached to closures for their own sake. We are attached to outcomes:
- competitive margins
- disciplined cost reduction
- durable savings
- investability

If a non-closure alternative truly delivers that, we can support it. If not, we would support deeper structural cuts, including closures, because preserving an uncompetitive structure is a worse outcome for capital markets.
