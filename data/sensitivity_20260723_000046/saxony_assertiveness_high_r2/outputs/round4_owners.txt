## My position
The controlling family holding supports a **durable restructuring that restores competitiveness, margins, and long-term value**. We do **not** support a solution that simply preserves the current footprint and leaves the company structurally uncompetitive. And we do **not** support a value-destructive conflict that damages the enterprise, its governance, or labour peace.

So the question is not “closures or no closures” as a slogan. The question is whether the final package **permanently removes enough cost and capacity** to restore acceptable returns **without** triggering avoidable instability.

---

## Where I agree with the employee side
### With the Works Council and the Union
I agree on three important points:
1. The company has a **real structural competitiveness problem**.
2. **Cosmetic or temporary fixes are insufficient**.
3. A durable solution must reduce **fixed cost, overhead, and capacity** in a permanent way.

I also agree that:
- investment in future products and technology is necessary,
- a weak compromise would be bad,
- and the burden of proof is not satisfied by slogans.

So yes: we need a real restructuring.

Where I do **not** agree is the leap from “real restructuring” to “therefore no closures, no compulsory redundancies, and all site protections through 2030.”

That is not yet proven to be economically sufficient.

---

## Where I disagree strongly

### 1) Management still has not proven that closures are necessary
Management keeps saying closures remain on the table because the business case may require them. That may be true — but it is still not proven.

I support the insistence on a **site-by-site quantified comparison**:
- utilization,
- fixed and variable cost,
- direct and indirect headcount,
- logistics burden,
- savings by scenario,
- capex needs,
- implementation risk,
- and margin trajectory to 2030.

Until that is on the table, closures are an option, not a conclusion.

At the same time, I also reject the employee-side implication that “no closures” is automatically equivalent to a viable solution. It may be, but only if the numbers prove it.

---

### 2) A blanket “no closures” line is not enough by itself
I understand why the works council and union are holding that line. But from a governance and value perspective, I cannot treat it as a sufficient answer.

If a plant or site is materially underutilized and structurally cost-damaging, then preserving it indefinitely because closure is politically difficult is not stewardship. It is delay.

And delay is not neutral. In a cyclical, capital-intensive industry, delay erodes margins, reduces investment capacity, and makes the eventual adjustment worse.

So I reject the idea that “no closures” is a complete plan.

---

### 3) Compulsory redundancies cannot be ruled out in advance
I am not saying forced layoffs are desirable. They are not.

But I also will not rule them out in advance if:
- the scale of overcapacity is large,
- voluntary measures are insufficient,
- and no alternative package delivers the same durable effect.

That does not mean they should be the first tool. It means they remain a possible consequence of structural overcapacity if no equivalent alternative is credible.

The employee side is right that forced redundancies would damage trust.  
They are wrong if they assume that reputational discomfort alone makes them inadmissible in all cases.

---

### 4) The state should not turn its blocking role into a veto on reality
I respect the state’s role and the importance of co-determination. But that cannot become a mechanism to freeze every structural change.

A weaker company with too much footprint is not a protected industrial base. It is a slower decline.  
The state is right to insist on hard numbers — but once those numbers are shown, it should not pre-commit to blocking whatever they imply.

---

### 5) Investors are right to reject a cosmetic compromise
I agree with the investors on this point: a deal that preserves overcapacity and calls itself “balanced” would be bad for value.

But I also caution them not to assume that only formal closures can produce discipline. A deep hybrid or non-closure restructuring could be credible — **if** it genuinely removes enough capacity, fixed overhead, and duplication.

That is the test.

- A credible alternative to closures must remove **permanent capacity** and **fixed cost**.

That is exactly the standard I would apply.

What the expert did **not** say is that closures are automatically required. So I reject any attempt to present closures as the only serious option before the side-by-side economics have been shown.

## What would move me
I would support either of these outcomes only if they are properly evidenced:

### 1) A closure-based restructuring
Only if management proves closures or product exits are truly necessary to restore competitiveness.

### 2) A non-closure or hybrid restructuring
Only if it is **economically equivalent**, meaning:
- permanent capacity reduction,
- meaningful fixed-cost removal,
- a real workforce reduction,
- simplified footprint,
- durable labour-cost relief,
- and no hidden preservation of overcapacity.

If the non-closure package is mostly:
- voluntary exits,
- working-time changes,
- and temporary concessions,

then it is not equivalent.
