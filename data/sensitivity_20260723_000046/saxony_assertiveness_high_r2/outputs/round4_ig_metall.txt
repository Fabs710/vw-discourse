## My position
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No plant closures in Germany**
- **No unilateral dismantling of the collective wage framework**
- **No deal where employees carry the whole burden**
- **No “voluntary” program that becomes forced exits by pressure**

That remains our line. It has not changed.

---

## Where I agree
I agree with management, investors, the family, the state, and the expert on one central point: **the company has a real structural competitiveness problem**.

I also agree that:
- cosmetic fixes are worthless,
- temporary measures alone are insufficient,
- and the solution must be durable.

So yes, restructuring is necessary. That is not in dispute.

---

## Where I disagree

### 1) Management still has not proven that closures are necessary
Management keeps saying closures remain a live option because the business case may require them. That is still an assertion, not proof.

The expert’s standard is the right one:
- site-by-site utilization
- fixed and variable cost
- direct and indirect headcount
- logistics burden
- savings by scenario
- NPV to 2030
- implementation risk

Until that is shown, closures are not an economic conclusion. They are a preferred instrument.

A preferred instrument is not the same as a necessary one.

### 2) “Equivalent savings” does not automatically mean “close plants”
The expert was clear: what matters is **permanent capacity reduction** and **fixed-cost removal**.

Exactly. That is what we are prepared to discuss.

But that does **not** mean plant closures are required. It means the company must:
- remove real capacity,
- reduce overhead,
- simplify product and site allocation,
- cut duplication,
- and do it in a way that preserves co-determination and jobs.

If a deep consolidation package can achieve that, then closure is not necessary.

### 3) Compulsory redundancies remain unacceptable
The argument that “voluntary measures may not be enough” does not make forced layoffs legitimate.

If workforce reduction is needed, then the package must be built around:
- voluntary exits,
- partial retirement,
- early retirement,
- redeployment,
- retraining,
- working-time flexibility,
- and functional consolidation.

If management cannot make that work, the answer is not coercion. The answer is to redesign the restructuring so workers are not forced to pay for management’s strategic failure.

### 4) The state must not become the alibi for closure politics
I appreciate the state’s insistence on regional industrial stability. But that role is not to rubber-stamp closures after a spreadsheet presentation.

If the state’s blocking role means anything, it means: **do not treat closure as the starting point**. Test the alternatives properly first.

### 5) Investors are still assuming the conclusion
You are right to reject a cosmetic compromise. We agree.

But you are still acting as if “no closures” automatically means “no credibility.” That is not true.

A credible non-closure package can exist if it:
- permanently reduces capacity,
- lowers fixed cost,
- removes duplication,
- improves utilization,
- and delivers measurable savings to 2030.

Do not confuse “not your preferred solution” with “not a serious solution.”

That distinction matters.

8. **No weakening of co-determination**
