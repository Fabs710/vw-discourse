## Where I agree

### With management

I agree that cosmetic fixes are useless.

### With the expert
I agree with the technical point that a solution must be **durable**, not temporary, and that it must reduce **fixed cost, overhead, and capacity** in a permanent way.

### With the state government
I agree that the company must remain a regional industrial anchor and that the decision cannot be reduced to short-term shareholder logic.

### With investors and the family
I agree on one point only: a weak compromise would be bad.  
A cosmetic deal that preserves overcapacity and calls itself “balanced” is not acceptable.

## Where I disagree

### 1) Management is still not proving that closures are necessary
Management keeps saying closures remain on the table because the business case requires it. That is not enough.

You still have not demonstrated, site by site, that:
- a non-closure consolidation package cannot work,
- the savings gap cannot be closed otherwise,
- and plant closure is the only economically credible route.

Until that is shown, closures are not a necessity. They are a management preference.

The burden is on management to prove why the workforce should lose sites and jobs.  
That burden has not been met.

### 2) “Equivalent savings” is not the same as “closure economics”
The expert has been very clear: the question is whether a non-closure package can permanently reduce capacity and fixed cost enough.

Exactly. That means the real test is **not** whether management can preserve its favorite tool. The real test is whether the company can achieve:
- permanent capacity reduction,
- overhead removal,
- utilization improvement,
- and margin restoration to 2030

**without** closing plants.

If that can be done through consolidation, functional simplification, redeployment, and fair burden-sharing, then closure is not required.

Management keeps talking as if anything short of closure is automatically inadequate. That is still assertion, not evidence.

### 3) Forced redundancies are still unacceptable
The claim that “voluntary measures may not be enough” does not suddenly make compulsory redundancies legitimate.

If the restructuring needs a workforce adjustment, then management must design it around:
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- partial retirement,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time flexibility,
- and internal consolidation.

If that is insufficient, then the problem is not employee refusal. The problem is management trying to force the social cost of overcapacity onto individuals.

We will not accept that.

### 4) The state must not become the alibi for closure politics
I appreciate that the state says it wants to protect the industrial base. But that protection cannot mean greenlighting closures once management presents a spreadsheet.

The state’s blocking role is meaningful only if it is used to prevent a rushed closure agenda before the alternatives are genuinely tested.

So yes, we support the call for a side-by-side model.  
No, that does **not** mean closures become the default if management gets impatient.

### 5) Investors are still hiding behind “equivalence”
You say you want durable savings. Fine. So do we.

But you keep framing “no closures” as if it were an emotional slogan rather than a legitimate industrial strategy. It is not a slogan. It is a red line rooted in jobs, skills, local economies, and long-term stability.

If you want credibility, then back the independent model and accept the possibility that a **deep non-closure consolidation** can be economically valid.

Do not pre-decide the outcome and then demand the model merely decorate it.
