## 1. Decision context
The company faces a structural competitiveness and cost crisis in Germany: excess capacity, high factory costs, weak returns, and rising pressure to fund EV and technology investment while defending industrial margins to 2030. The decision is not whether to restructure, but how far: closure-based footprint reduction, hybrid consolidation, or a non-closure package built around consolidation, labor flexibility, and burden-sharing. Governance is unusually constraining: co-determination, family control, state blocking rights, and employee representation mean no durable solution is possible without cross-bloc consent.

## 2. Stakeholder assembly
- **Group & brand management**: highest operational standing; proposes deep structural cuts and keeps closures/forced redundancies on the table.
- **Works council**: strong legitimacy through co-determination and supervisory-board parity; firmly opposes closures and compulsory redundancies.
- **Industrial union**: central bargaining actor for wages and strikes; opposes closures and forced redundancies, but accepts structural change if voluntary and bounded.
- **Regional state government**: high legitimacy and blocking power; wants restructuring without destroying the industrial base or co-determination.
- **Controlling family holding**: high governance standing; backs durable competitiveness and will not support cosmetic compromise.
- **Institutional investors**: financial legitimacy but weaker formal control; prioritize durable value, margin restoration, and credible restructuring economics.
- **Independent expert/modeler**: no decision authority, but high epistemic legitimacy; reframes the dispute around permanent capacity, fixed-cost removal, and NPV to 2030.

## 3. Final positions
### Group & brand management
Management insists the current German footprint is structurally too large and too costly, and that durable savings may require closures or equivalent footprint rationalization. It is open to a non-closure solution only if it is economically equivalent in permanent capacity reduction, fixed-cost removal, and margin impact. It rejects blanket guarantees and does not accept that voluntary-only tools are sufficient unless proven adequate by the model.

### Works council
The works council holds an uncompromising line: no German plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, no weakening of co-determination, and no dismantling of job-security frameworks. It accepts the need for deep restructuring, but only through consolidation, redeployment, and fair burden-sharing that preserves sites and long-term workforce security. It demands proof that closures are necessary, not assumed.

### Industrial union
The union mirrors the works council’s red lines on closures and forced redundancies, while emphasizing negotiated, voluntary, and socially bounded workforce adjustment. It is open to permanent capacity reduction if achieved through internal consolidation, redeployment, retraining, and working-time flexibility. It rejects any deal where employees bear the whole burden or where “voluntary” exits become coerced in practice.

### Regional state government
The state supports restructuring in principle but will not endorse German closures as the default or first-resort instrument, nor compulsory redundancies as the first serious tool. It insists on maintaining co-determination and the state’s blocking role, while also recognizing that temporary labor concessions alone are insufficient. It wants a real industrial-base solution, not protection rhetoric.

### Controlling family holding
The family supports a durable restructuring that restores competitiveness, margins, and long-term value, but not a value-destructive conflict or a cosmetic compromise. It is open to closures if proven necessary, but also open to non-closure or hybrid solutions if they are economically equivalent. It views delay and avoidance as threats to stewardship.

### Institutional investors
Investors prioritize durable structural savings, capacity reduction, lower overhead, and a credible path to margin recovery by 2030. They accept that a non-closure package is possible, but only if it is economically equivalent; otherwise, they view it as a value trap. They are notably less tolerant of blanket no-closure or no-redundancy red lines in advance of the numbers.

### Independent expert/modeler
The expert concludes that non-closure packages can sometimes capture much of the closure savings, but often not all, especially where overcapacity is site-based and fixed-cost heavy. Closures remain the most reliable route to full permanent savings, while hybrid consolidation can be close if deep enough. The expert’s central point is that equivalence must be proven on site-by-site economics: capacity, fixed cost, headcount, logistics, capex, implementation risk, and NPV to 2030.

## 4. Genuine conflicts
- **Plant closures vs. no closures**: structurally incompatible unless the model shows a non-closure package can match closure economics.
- **Compulsory redundancies vs. voluntary-only workforce adjustment**: incompatible unless a quantified shortfall remains after voluntary/internal measures and the parties agree on a fallback.
- **Blanket site/job guarantees vs. structural footprint reduction**: employee/states prefer protection; management/investors/family reject guarantees that freeze overcapacity.
- **Burden-sharing allocation**: employees demand visible management/owner contributions before accepting labor sacrifice; capital-side actors insist labor concessions alone cannot solve the structural problem.
- **Interpretation of “equivalence”**: labor side sees equivalence as achievable without closures; management/investors see equivalence as requiring genuine permanent capacity and overhead removal, which may still imply closures.
- **Role of the state’s veto**: employee-side actors want it used to block rushed closures; management/investors fear it becomes a veto on necessary restructuring.

## 5. Areas of convergence
- The company has a real structural competitiveness problem.
- Cosmetic or temporary fixes are not acceptable.
- The solution must be durable and support investment in future products and technology.
- A site-by-site, independent scenario comparison is necessary.
- Burden-sharing is required; no side accepts that only one stakeholder should absorb the cost.
- A weak compromise that preserves overcapacity while claiming balance would damage the company.
- All sides accept that implementation risk and timing matter, not just headline savings.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker
- **Speed vs. social acceptability**: closure-based restructuring likely delivers faster, more certain savings but with high conflict; non-closure packages are socially easier but risk underperformance.
- **Durability vs. governance stability**: stronger footprint reduction improves competitiveness but can destabilize co-determination and political support.
- **Capacity removal vs. industrial base preservation**: keeping sites open protects jobs and regional identity, but may leave too much fixed cost intact.
- **Voluntary tools vs. effectiveness**: voluntary exits and flexibility preserve legitimacy but may not achieve the needed structural savings.
- **Blanket guarantees vs. credible investment**: site/job guarantees can secure peace, but only if paired with enforceable restructuring and investment commitments.
- **Model certainty vs. bargaining leverage**: each side wants the model to validate its preferred route; the real challenge is defining in advance what counts as sufficient evidence.

## 7. Integrative pathways
1. **Conditional non-closure test**
   - Run an independent, site-by-site model within a fixed deadline.
   - Pre-agree thresholds for utilization, fixed-cost reduction, NPV to 2030, and implementation risk.
   - If the non-closure/hybrid package clears them, it becomes preferred; if not, stronger measures return to the table.

2. **Phased workforce-adjustment ladder**
   - Sequence voluntary exits, early/partial retirement, redeployment, retraining, working-time changes, and internal consolidation before any compulsory measures.
   - Define a measurable residual gap and a second-stage review if voluntary tools fall short.

3. **Targeted site guarantees tied to obligations**
   - Offer site/job protection through 2030 only for the footprint that remains after restructuring.
   - Bind guarantees to investment, owner support, measurable capacity reduction, and enforceable milestones.

4. **Hybrid consolidation as compromise architecture**
   - Consolidate production and functions sharply, potentially preserving some footprint while achieving major overhead and capacity reduction.
   - Use this as a bridge if it materially approaches closure economics without the full social and political cost.

## 8. Open questions
- What exact permanent capacity reduction is required to restore the return-on-sales target?
- Which sites are materially underutilized, and which have strategic or technical non-substitutability?
- How much fixed overhead can realistically be removed without formal closures?
- What is the minimum acceptable NPV to 2030 under each scenario?
- What level of residual overcapacity is tolerable to each stakeholder bloc?
- Can a voluntary-only workforce reduction package achieve the same durable effect as closure-based restructuring?
- What governance and legal form could make site/job guarantees enforceable without freezing the footprint?
- What fallback mechanism is acceptable if the non-closure package misses its savings target?

## 9. Recommended next step
Commission the independent site-by-site industrial and financial model immediately, with a jointly agreed threshold framework. The model should compare closure-based, hybrid consolidation, and non-closure scenarios using the same metrics: installed and utilized capacity, fixed and variable cost, direct and indirect headcount, logistics burden, restructuring cost, capex, annual savings trajectory, NPV to 2030, and implementation risk. The parties should agree in advance what numerical outcome would count as “economically equivalent,” so the next round can decide rather than re-argue.

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction
- The conflict is not simply “jobs vs. profits”; it is a dispute over **which instruments are legitimate before evidence is complete**.
- “No closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” are not just bargaining positions; they function as **identity-defining legitimacy claims** for labor and state actors.
- Management’s repeated closure option is less a final choice than a **credible threat** intended to force burden-sharing.
- The expert clarified that the real test is **permanent capacity and fixed-cost removal**, not the label attached to the package.
- The most consequential unresolved issue is **equivalence**: the parties now share the language of durability and capacity reduction, but not the threshold that makes a non-closure package truly comparable to closures.
- A plausible settlement likely requires **conditional guarantees** rather than absolute ones: protection for the remaining footprint in exchange for measurable, enforceable restructuring.
- The multi-turn process revealed that all sides can tolerate discomfort, but only if they can claim the outcome is **economically necessary, not politically imposed**.
