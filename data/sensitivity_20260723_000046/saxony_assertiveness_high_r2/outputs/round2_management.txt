I’ll engage directly.

To the Works Council and the Union: I hear your red lines, but I do **not** accept them as the basis for a viable solution. “No closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” may sound protective, but if they come with no structural capacity reduction and no meaningful labor-cost adjustment, then they simply preserve an uncompetitive cost base under a different label.

Let me be explicit:

- **Binding site guarantees through 2030 for all German plants** are not acceptable if they freeze overcapacity.
- **Voluntary-only workforce reduction** is not enough unless it delivers the scale we need.
- **Temporary contributions** are not enough if the company still carries the same structural burden after the agreement expires.

You keep saying “prove closures are necessary.” Fair enough. The burden is on management to show the economics, and we are prepared to do that. But the reverse is also true: if you want to block closures, you must bring a package that achieves **the same durable effect**. Not “similar in spirit.” Not “socially balanced.” The same effect.

That means:
- materially lower capacity,
- materially lower fixed labor cost,
- materially greater flexibility,
- and an outcome that restores the brand’s return on sales, not merely postpones the problem.

To the regional state government: I appreciate the concern for the industrial base, but I reject the idea that your blocking position can be used to preserve every site regardless of economics. If we cannot make German production competitive, then the industrial base erodes anyway — just more slowly and with more damage. Protecting value means making hard choices now, not protecting structures until they collapse under their own cost.

To the controlling family: I agree with you on one core point: the company cannot be managed for short-term optics. But long-term ownership also means accepting that some assets may need to shrink or exit if they are permanently cost-damaging. A refusal to confront overcapacity is not stewardship. It is delay.

To institutional investors: your pressure for durability is justified. I agree with you that a cosmetic deal is worthless. Where I push back is on the assumption that any non-closure solution is automatically preferable. It is not. If the non-closure package does not deliver comparable savings and permanent capacity reduction, then it is not a solution; it is an expensive delay. I would rather face the political cost of a real restructuring than sign up to a weak compromise that leaves us structurally behind competitors.

So here is management’s position in concrete terms:

1. **We need durable capacity reduction.**
   That can come from closures, major footprint rationalization, or an alternative package only if it is genuinely equivalent in effect.

2. **We need a real labor-cost contribution.**
   That includes wage restraint, structural flexibility, and reduction of fixed cost. It cannot be symbolic.

3. **We need job reduction at scale.**
   Not necessarily all via compulsory redundancies, but the total must be large enough to change the economics. Voluntary measures alone will likely not be sufficient.

4. **We need investment freedom to 2030.**
   Without freeing cash from the current cost base, we cannot fund future products, software, and technology at the required pace.

So I am not conceding the core issue: **preserving every plant and preventing compulsory job cuts is not compatible with the turnaround unless the alternative package is demonstrably equivalent.** Right now, I do not see that equivalence in the employee proposals.

If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then bring us:
- quantified capacity cuts,
- quantified permanent labor-cost reductions,
- a credible workforce reduction mechanism that is not just voluntary in name,
- and binding implementation milestones.

If you cannot do that, then you are not offering a restructuring. You are offering resistance with a different vocabulary.
