I will be equally clear.

## My position
The state government will **not** support a restructuring that makes **German plant closures** the default answer or uses **compulsory redundancies** as the first serious tool.

We also will **not** accept any deal that weakens **co-determination** or sidelines the state’s blocking role under the special statute.

That is not a bargaining pose. That is the boundary of what we can responsibly back as an anchor shareholder and regional political actor.

---

## Where I agree with management
Management is right about the diagnosis:
- the cost base is too high,
- the market is tougher,
- temporary cosmetics will not solve the problem,
- and future products and technology need investment.

I also accept the expert’s core point: **temporary labor concessions alone are not enough** if structural overcapacity remains.

So yes, there must be real structural change. I am not defending inefficiency for its own sake.

---

## Where I disagree with management
What I do **not** accept is the leap from “structural change is needed” to “closures and forced redundancies are therefore the natural baseline.”

That has still not been proven.

You keep saying closures remain on the table because the numbers may require it. Fine. Then show the numbers.

I want the same plant-by-plant comparison the expert requested:
- utilization,
- fixed and variable cost,
- direct and indirect headcount,
- logistics burden,
- savings by scenario,
- capex needs,
- implementation risk,
- and margin trajectory to 2030.

Until that is on the table, closures are an assumption, not a conclusion.

---

## Where I disagree with the works council and union
I understand and share your goal of protecting jobs, sites, and the industrial base. I also agree that workers should not be made to pay twice.

But I cannot support a **blanket no-change stance** if the company genuinely has structural overcapacity.

You are right that the burden of proof lies with management.  
You are not right if you imply that any capacity reduction must be ruled out simply because it is uncomfortable.

If German sites are to remain open, the alternative package must actually remove enough fixed cost and capacity to be credible. That means a **real footprint solution**, not just protection language.

---

## On the expert assessment
The expert’s framing is the correct one:

- temporary labor concessions alone are not enough,
- voluntary exits alone are usually not enough,
- a credible alternative to closures must remove permanent capacity and fixed cost.

That is the standard I am prepared to use.

So I do **not** accept the management framing that closures are automatically necessary.  
But I also do **not** accept the employee-side claim that “no closures” is sufficient by itself.

The real question is whether the package:
- removes durable capacity,
- strips out fixed overhead,
- simplifies the footprint,
- and does so with a long-term industrial logic.

---

## What I would support
I am open to either of the following:

### 1) A closure-based restructuring
Only if management proves that closures or product exits are truly necessary to restore competitiveness and that they are the best long-term footprint solution.

### 2) A non-closure or hybrid restructuring
Only if it is **economically equivalent** in effect, meaning:
- permanent capacity reduction,
- significant fixed-cost removal,
- meaningful workforce reduction,
- simplified footprint,
- durable labor-cost relief,
- and no hidden preservation of overcapacity.

If the non-closure package is mostly voluntary exits, working-time changes, and temporary concessions, then it is not equivalent.

---

## What would move me
Before I would support a final settlement, I want a **proper quantified side-by-side comparison**:
- site-by-site utilization
- fixed and variable cost by plant
- direct and indirect headcount
- product allocation
- annual savings by scenario
- NPV to 2030
- implementation risk
- capex needs
- margin trajectory under each option

[SUMMON: independent industrial and financial modeller to produce a side-by-side comparison of closure, partial consolidation, and non-closure scenarios, with plant-level economics and NPV to 2030]

---

## My position on guarantees
I am open to **site and job protection through 2030** only if it is tied to a restructuring that genuinely restores competitiveness.

But I will not support blanket guarantees that freeze an overbuilt footprint and leave the company structurally uncompetitive. A guarantee is only acceptable if it is paired with:
- real capacity reduction,
- real cost removal,
- and credible milestones.

Otherwise it becomes a promise to preserve inefficiency.

---

## Bottom line
I am not attached to closures for their own sake. I am attached to outcomes:
- restored competitiveness,
- durable savings,
- credible margins,
- long-term value preservation,
- and industrial peace.

If a non-closure package can truly achieve that, I would prefer it.

If it cannot, then deeper structural measures, including closures, remain on the table.

What I will not support is a deal that simply postpones the problem and calls it compromise.

And I will say it plainly: **protecting jobs and sites in this state is not a side issue; it is central to the legitimacy of any outcome.**
