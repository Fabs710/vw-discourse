## My position
The state government’s core line remains unchanged:

- **No plant closures in Germany as a default solution**
- **No compulsory redundancies as a first or easy option**
- **No settlement that weakens co-determination or the state’s blocking role**
- **Yes, the company needs a real restructuring**
- **Yes, the restructuring must be economically credible and durable**

I am not defending the status quo. I am defending a restructuring that preserves the company as a regional industrial anchor rather than hollowing it out.

---

## To management
I agree with you on the diagnosis: the cost base is too high, the market is harder, and we need a durable turnaround. I also agree that temporary cosmetics will not solve the problem.

Where I disagree is your insistence that closures and compulsory redundancies are the natural baseline. They are not.

The expert has said something important: **the question is equivalence**. That means you cannot just assert that closures are needed; you must show, with hard numbers, that no alternative package can remove enough fixed cost and capacity.

So my challenge to management is simple:

- Show the **site-by-site economics**
- Show the **capacity utilization**
- Show the **fixed-cost burden**
- Show the **difference between closure, partial consolidation, and non-closure restructuring**
- Show whether an alternative package can achieve the same durable effect

Until then, I do **not** accept “closures first” as a justified conclusion.

Also: your language about “freeing cash” by reducing jobs is too narrow. A company like this cannot be rebuilt by treating plants as line items only. Industrial capability, supplier networks, skills, and regional legitimacy matter too. If you destroy those, you may improve the spreadsheet and damage the enterprise.

---

## To the Works Council
I share your objective: protecting jobs, sites, and the company’s industrial base. I also agree that employees cannot be asked to pay twice.

Where I need to be careful is this: a blanket refusal to all site adjustments can become politically powerful but economically incomplete. If there is genuine overcapacity, the company must be able to adapt.

That said, I do **not** see evidence yet that closures are the only answer. The burden is still on management to prove that. So your red lines remain legitimate, and I will continue to use the state’s influence to prevent closures from being normalized as the default solution.

But I also expect the employee side to help design a package that is not just defensive. If you want to keep every site open, the alternative must be credible enough to withstand investor scrutiny and internal economics.

---

## To the union
I agree with your insistence on no compulsory redundancies and on fair burden-sharing. That is exactly the right principle.

I also take seriously your point that labor should not “pay twice.” If employees accept temporary restraint, working-time flexibility, or voluntary measures, then management and owners must contribute visibly and durably as well.

Where I am less aligned is on any implication that temporary concessions alone are sufficient. The expert is right: temporary measures may help, but they do not solve structural overcapacity. So if the non-closure route is chosen, it must include **permanent** capacity and cost adjustments, not only temporary wage restraint.

That means I can support:
- voluntary exits
- internal redeployment
- qualification
- partial retirement
- targeted working-time changes
- temporary sacrifice if matched by management and owners

But I cannot support a settlement that leaves the company structurally unchanged and only postpones the hard decisions.

---

## To the controlling family
I appreciate your long-term perspective more than management’s short-term framing. You are right that value destruction through paralysis is unacceptable.

But long-term stewardship also means **not forcing the company into a socially explosive restructuring if a better path exists**. A family owner that is serious about the long term should support:
- preserving industrial capacity where possible
- avoiding unnecessary closures
- building a deal that is resilient industrially and socially

I would be very cautious of any approach that treats the state and workforce as obstacles to be managed around. That would weaken trust for years.

---

## To the institutional investors
You are correct that we cannot accept a cosmetic compromise. A deal that preserves the cost base and calls itself “socially balanced” is not credible.

But I also reject the idea that the only credible route is closure. The expert was clear: **non-closure alternatives can be competitive if they permanently reduce capacity, eliminate fixed cost, and simplify the footprint**. That is the standard I am prepared to support.

So: I am not blocking restructuring. I am blocking a reflex closure strategy that has not yet been proven necessary.

## On the expert assessment
The expert’s input is useful because it clarifies the real test.

What I take from it is:

- non-closure alternatives are **not automatically equal**
- but they are **not automatically inadequate either**
- the decisive issue is whether they remove enough **permanent capacity and fixed overhead**

That matters. It means we should not debate slogans. We should debate the actual footprint design.

So I support the next step being a **side-by-side quantified model**:
1. current footprint
2. closure scenario
3. non-closure restructuring
4. hybrid scenario

That is the right basis for discussion.

## My current stance on the package
I am prepared to support a restructuring **if** it meets these conditions:

- no plant closures unless absolutely unavoidable and proven by numbers
- no compulsory redundancies as the default instrument
- substantial but fair labor contribution
- management and owners also contribute
- permanent capacity reduction where needed, but preferably through consolidation and reallocation rather than shutdown
- binding investment commitments for German sites
- protection of co-determination and the state’s veto role

What I will **not** support:
- a package that uses closures as a threat to force labor concessions
- a deal that weakens the company’s regional base for the sake of short-term returns
- a “voluntary” scheme that is really forced exits by pressure
- any arrangement that sidelines the state or the workforce in the governance process

I am not persuaded by assertions that closures are the inevitable answer.  
I am persuaded that the company needs a serious restructuring.
