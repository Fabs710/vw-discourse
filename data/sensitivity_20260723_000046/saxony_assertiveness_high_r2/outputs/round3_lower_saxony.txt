I will be blunt as well.

## My position
The state government will **not** support a restructuring that:
- closes German plants as the default answer,
- imposes compulsory redundancies as the first serious tool,
- or sidelines co-determination and the state’s blocking role.

That is not a negotiating posture; it is a hard boundary.

At the same time, I am **not** defending inefficiency for its own sake. The company needs a durable turnaround. But that turnaround must preserve the company as a **regional economic anchor**, not turn it into a smaller, weaker, more conflict-ridden shell.

---

## Where I agree with management
Management is right on the diagnosis:
- the cost base is too high,
- the market is tougher,
- and temporary cosmetics will not solve the problem.

I also accept the expert’s core logic: **temporary labor concessions alone are not enough** if structural overcapacity remains.

So yes, there must be real structural change.

Where I disagree is management’s leap from “structural change is needed” to “closures and compulsory redundancies are therefore the natural baseline.” They are **not**. That still has not been proven.

If management wants the state to consider even difficult measures, it must first show:
- site-by-site utilization,
- fixed-cost absorption,
- product allocation,
- break-even volumes,
- and the actual savings gap between closure, consolidation, and non-closure options.

Until then, closures are an assumption, not a conclusion.

---

## Where I disagree with the works council and union
I understand and share your objective of protecting jobs, sites, and the industrial base. I also agree that employees should not pay twice.

But I must be equally clear: a **blanket no-change stance** is not enough if the company has genuine structural overcapacity.

You are right that the burden of proof lies with management.  
You are not right if you imply that any capacity reduction must be off-limits simply because it is uncomfortable.

What I need from the employee side is not surrender, but seriousness:
- if German sites are to remain open, then the alternative package must actually remove enough fixed cost and capacity to be credible.

That means we need a **real footprint solution**, not just protection language.

---

## On the expert assessment
The expert’s input matters because it clarifies the real question:

> Can we achieve the same durable effect without closures?

The answer is: **possibly, but only if the non-closure package is deep enough to remove permanent capacity and overhead**.

That is exactly the standard I am prepared to use.

So I do **not** accept the management framing that closures are automatically necessary.  
But I also do **not** accept the employee-side claim that “no closures” is sufficient by itself.

The real issue is whether the package:
- removes durable capacity,
- strips out fixed overhead,
- simplifies the footprint,
- and does so with a long-term industrial logic.

---

## What I want to see next
I support the request for a **side-by-side quantified scenario model** — but it must be independent and site-specific.

[SUMMON: independent industrial-footprint and financial modeller to compare closure, partial consolidation, and non-closure scenarios site by site, with savings, utilization, headcount, capex, and margin trajectory to 2030]

That model must show:
- capacity by plant,
- utilization by plant,
- fixed and variable costs,
- direct and indirect headcount,
- logistics and complexity costs,
- savings by year,
- payback period,
- and implementation risk.

Without that, we are still debating slogans.

---

## My position on the package
### I can support:
- voluntary exits
- partial retirement
- internal redeployment
- qualification and retraining
- temporary working-time flexibility
- a bounded employee contribution
- management and owner contributions
- binding investment commitments for German sites
- consolidation of functions and product streams where it preserves the industrial base

### I cannot support:
- plant closures in Germany as the starting point
- compulsory redundancies as the default tool
- a deal that weakens co-determination
- a settlement that leaves jobs and sites permanently insecure
- “voluntary” programs that are really pressure-driven exits

---

## To management
Your argument is still too binary: either closures or the business fails.

That is not yet demonstrated.

If the expert-style model shows that a **deep consolidation without formal closures** can permanently remove enough cost and capacity, then that route must remain open. If it cannot, then you will have a stronger case — but you do not have that case yet.

So I am not accepting closures as the default. I am insisting on proof.

---

## To the works council and union
I support your insistence that employees should not carry the entire burden.

But I also expect you to engage seriously with a **real footprint redesign**. If the model shows that one or more sites are structurally underutilized, then “keep everything exactly as it is” will not be credible to anyone — not to the market, and not to the state.

That does **not** mean closures are automatically justified.  
It does mean the alternative must be more than a defensive slogan.

---

## To the controlling family
If you are truly long-term owners, then act accordingly:
- protect the company’s industrial presence,
- avoid avoidable social rupture,
- and back a solution that keeps the German base strong.

Long-term value is not created by treating the regional industrial base as expendable overhead.

But I also say this plainly: long-term ownership does **not** entitle anyone to evade necessary restructuring. It does entitle us to insist that restructuring must be **responsible**, not brutal.

---

## To institutional investors
You are correct that a cosmetic compromise would be bad.

But you are wrong if you assume that only closures can produce discipline. A serious, permanent consolidation package can do that too — if it truly removes cost and capacity.

What you should not do is push the company into a rushed closure logic before the alternative has been properly tested.

---

## My bottom line
The state will support a restructuring only if it:
- restores competitiveness,
- preserves the company as a regional anchor,
- protects co-determination,
- and avoids unnecessary plant destruction.

So the next step is clear:

1. **Produce the quantified comparison**
2. **Test whether non-closure consolidation can match the savings**
3. **Only then discuss whether any closure is truly unavoidable**

Until that is done, I will not endorse plant closures or compulsory redundancies.

And I will say it again plainly: **protecting jobs and sites in this state is not a side issue; it is central to the legitimacy of any outcome.**
