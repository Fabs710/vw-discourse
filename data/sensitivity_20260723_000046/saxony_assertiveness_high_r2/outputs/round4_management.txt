I will be equally direct.

## To the Works Council and the Union
I hear your red lines. I do **not** accept them.

You keep saying:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no weakening of co-determination,
- no job-security changes through 2030.

That sounds protective, but it does not answer the real question: **how do we permanently fix an uncompetitive cost and capacity base?**

You keep demanding proof from management. Fine — but the burden of proof cuts both ways. If you insist that all German sites remain open and all structural protections remain intact, then **you** must show how the required savings are achieved at scale, permanently, and with enough speed to matter.

So far, what I hear is mostly a list of tools:
- voluntary exits,
- partial retirement,
- redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary contributions,
- functional consolidation.

Those may help. They do **not** automatically equal the savings from a deep footprint reset. They may reduce pain, but pain reduction is not the same as structural competitiveness.

## On your claim that closures are “political”
No. A plant that is materially underutilized and structurally cost-damaging is not being “politically” discussed when closure is raised. It is being economically assessed.

If a site cannot earn its keep, then preserving it because it is easier than confrontation is not social responsibility. It is deferment. And deferment in this situation means more erosion, weaker margins, and less money for product and technology investment.

That is not acceptable to management, and it should not be acceptable to anyone serious about the company’s future.

## To the expert
I agree with your technical framing:
- permanent capacity removal is necessary,
- fixed-cost reduction is necessary,
- indirect-headcount reduction is necessary,
- complexity reduction is necessary,
- and margin restoration by 2030 is the test.

That is exactly why we cannot settle for a package that protects the current footprint in substance while calling it “consolidation.” If the numbers show that a non-closure package truly removes enough capacity and fixed cost, then it can be considered. But it must be **equivalent in outcome**, not just in language.

And let me be clear: I do **not** accept the implication that “equivalent” means “all sites remain, with voluntary exits and some flexibility.” That is not equivalence. That is delay.

## To the State Government
You say closures should not be the default. I agree they should not be the first slogan. But you are turning that into a veto against reality.

Your role is not to freeze the footprint. Your role is to ensure the company remains an industrial anchor. A company with too much cost, too much capacity, and too little margin is not an anchor. It is a slow-motion decline.

So yes, we will provide the numbers. But those numbers must be judged honestly. If they show that some German capacity is no longer viable, then the state should not hide behind politics and pretend otherwise.

## To the Controlling Family
You talk about long-term stewardship. Then act like it.

Long-term stewardship means:
- restoring competitiveness,
- protecting the company’s ability to invest,
- and not pretending legacy structures are sacrosanct.

I agree that a destructive conflict would be bad. But the greater long-term danger is preserving an uncompetitive structure because no one wants to confront it.

That would not be stewardship. That would be avoidance.

## To Institutional Investors
You are right to reject a cosmetic compromise. On that point, we agree.

But do not confuse seriousness with plant closure as a fetish. We are not pursuing closures for their own sake. We are pursuing a structurally lower cost base and a healthier margin trajectory. If that can be achieved through a deep hybrid or non-closure solution, fine — but then it must actually deliver the same durable economics.

Right now, the employee side has not shown that it can.

## Where I stand on the scenarios
### 1) Closure-based restructuring
This remains on the table because it is the clearest way to remove durable overcapacity, fixed cost, and complexity. It is not the only theoretical route, but it is the most direct one.

### 2) Hybrid consolidation
This is acceptable only if it produces **real permanent savings**:
- fewer lines,
- fewer shifts,
- lower indirect cost,
- lower logistics burden,
- materially higher utilization at the remaining sites,
- and a credible workforce adjustment.

### 3) Non-closure restructuring
This is acceptable only if it is economically equivalent. That means:
- not just voluntary exits,
- not just working-time changes,
- not just temporary pay concessions,
- but a real, durable reduction in capacity and fixed overhead.

If it does not do that, then it is not enough.

## What I will not accept
Let me restate this plainly:
- no deal that preserves the current overcapacity,
- no deal that buys time but not structural savings,
- no deal that asks us to fund future products by leaving today’s cost base untouched,
- no deal that makes the business case depend on endless labor concessions,
- no deal that leaves management without a credible path to the return-on-sales target.

That is the line.

## What would move management
I remain open to a non-closure or hybrid solution **if and only if** it shows, with site-level numbers:
- permanent capacity reduction,
- large fixed-cost removal,
- meaningful workforce reduction,
- simplified site and product allocation,
- durable labor-cost contribution,
- and a credible path to 2030 margins.

If that evidence is produced, then we can discuss form. If it is not, then closures and stronger structural measures remain necessary options.

## My position on co-determination
I respect co-determination. But co-determination is not a mechanism to prevent restructuring; it is a mechanism to shape it responsibly. Using it to entrench an uncompetitive footprint would damage the company and, in the end, the workforce as well.

## Bottom line
I am not retreating.

- The company needs a durable structural reduction in cost and capacity.
- Temporary concessions alone are insufficient.
- A blanket no-closure position is not a solution.
- A blanket no-redundancy position is not a solution.
- If the alternative package is truly equivalent, prove it.
- If it is not, then closures and stronger structural measures remain on the table.

That is the honest position. And it is the only one that protects the company’s competitiveness, margins, and investment capacity to 2030.
