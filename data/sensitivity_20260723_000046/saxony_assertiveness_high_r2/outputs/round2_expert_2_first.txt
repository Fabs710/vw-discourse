Below is my expert assessment of the specific question raised:

## Question
**Can a non-closure restructuring package realistically match the economic effect of closure-based restructuring in this German automotive footprint, and if so under what conditions?**

## Short answer
**Sometimes partially, rarely fully.**  
A non-closure package can **match most of the labor-cost savings** from a closure scenario only if it includes **large, permanent capacity reduction and fixed-overhead removal**. If it relies mainly on **voluntary exits, wage restraint, working-time flexibility, or temporary concessions**, it is **unlikely to match closure-based savings** on a durable basis.

The decisive issue is not the label “closure” versus “no closure,” but whether the package **permanently removes enough capacity, indirect labor, and fixed plant overhead**.

---

## 1) Economic logic of the footprint

In automotive manufacturing, total site cost has four broad components:

1. **Direct labor**
2. **Indirect labor and overhead**
3. **Plant fixed costs**  
   - depreciation / capital charges
   - building and utilities
   - maintenance
   - local management
   - logistics and internal handling
4. **Product and complexity costs**  
   - SKU complexity
   - changeovers
   - duplicated tooling and support functions

A plant closure eliminates or sharply reduces all four.  
A non-closure package usually reduces only parts of 1 and 2 unless it also removes a site, a line, or a full product allocation.

That is why closure-based restructuring usually has the strongest and most durable savings.

---

## 2) When a non-closure package can be close to equivalent

A non-closure package can be economically comparable **only if it is really a footprint consolidation package in disguise**, meaning it includes:

- **permanent capacity reduction**
- **sharp reduction in indirect functions**
- **elimination of a full product line or equivalent volume**
- **large, irreversible headcount reduction**
- **site-level simplification**
- **durable labor-cost reset**
- **no retained “shadow capacity”**

In that case, the economics can approach closure savings, especially if one plant is effectively downsized into a much smaller role and overhead is stripped out.

### Practical benchmark
If the remaining sites continue operating at **healthy utilization levels** and one or more plants are reduced from underutilized to well-loaded, the savings can be material.  
But if sites remain structurally underloaded, the fixed-cost burden persists.

---

## 3) When a non-closure package will not be enough

A non-closure package is **unlikely to match** closure economics if it is built mainly around:

- voluntary exits only
- temporary pay restraint
- bonus reductions
- working-time adjustments without line removal
- redeployment that keeps the same cost base in the group
- deferred investments without footprint simplification
- site guarantees that preserve all current structures

Those measures can help cash flow and labor cost, but they do not typically remove enough **permanent fixed cost**.

### Why
Because the largest savings from a closure scenario come from:
- removing an entire site’s overhead
- eliminating local indirect labor
- simplifying logistics and planning
- avoiding underabsorption of fixed cost
- cutting duplicate support structures

Temporary labor concessions do not do that.

---

## 4) Plant utilization matters more than rhetoric

A key benchmark in automotive plant economics is utilization.

- **Below roughly 75–80% sustained utilization**, a plant often starts to show meaningful underabsorption of fixed cost.
- At materially lower levels, the economics worsen quickly.
- If a site is underutilized and remains open without major consolidation, the cost penalty per vehicle can be substantial.

So the question is not whether a plant can keep operating.  
The question is whether it can operate at a cost per unit that is competitive after the restructuring.

If not, then a non-closure package is mostly a delay.

---

## 5) What “equivalence” would require in numbers

To credibly claim equivalence, management would need a side-by-side model showing:

### A. Closure scenario
- plant(s) closed or major product exits
- headcount removed
- fixed overhead eliminated
- capex required to transfer or consolidate production
- annual savings by year

### B. Non-closure scenario
- specific capacity removed
- exact headcount removed
- indirect function cuts
- site-by-site overhead reductions
- labor-cost reductions
- capex required for consolidation within remaining plants
- annual savings by year

### C. Comparison
- net present value of savings
- payback period
- sensitivity to demand
- sensitivity to EV mix
- execution risk
- residual break-even volume by plant

Without that, equivalence is not established.

---

## 6) My assessment of likely outcomes

### Closure-based restructuring
**Highest savings, highest certainty, highest social and implementation disruption.**

### Partial consolidation without full closure
**Potentially close to closure economics if executed deeply.**  
This is often the best non-closure alternative if one site or line can be removed from the system in substance, even if not formally “closed.”

### Pure non-closure / labor-contribution package
**Usually insufficient** unless overcapacity is modest and the company’s main problem is labor cost rather than footprint excess.

---

## 7) Main implementation constraints

Even if a non-closure package looks good on paper, several issues often erode savings:

- voluntary exit take-up is slower than forecast
- redeployed workers reappear as hidden cost
- indirect functions are harder to cut than direct labor
- site guarantees preserve overcapacity
- investment commitments raise capex without enough savings
- product complexity remains unchanged
- savings expire when temporary concessions expire

These are common failure modes in restructuring plans.

---

## 8) Judgment call

Based on the information provided, I would judge as follows:

- **The management claim that some durable capacity reduction is needed is plausible.**
- **The employee-side claim that a non-closure package can potentially deliver substantial savings is also plausible.**
- **But a non-closure package is only economically equivalent if it permanently removes a large share of capacity and overhead.**
- **If it is mostly voluntary labor measures and temporary concessions, it is not equivalent.**

So the answer is:

> **A non-closure alternative can match closure-based restructuring only in a deep consolidation scenario; a purely labor-based or temporary package will usually fall short.**
