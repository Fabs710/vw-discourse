## Where I agree
I agree with management, investors, the family, and the state on one central point: **the company has a real structural competitiveness problem**. No one on the employee side is denying that.

I also agree that:
- temporary cosmetic fixes are pointless,
- the deal must be durable,
- and future investment is necessary.

So yes: we need a real restructuring.

But I do **not** accept the leap from “real restructuring” to “closures and compulsory layoffs are therefore necessary.” That is still unproven.

## Where I disagree strongly

### 1) Management is still asserting, not proving
Management says closures or equivalent footprint reduction may be needed. Fine. But so far what we have is a **claim**, not a demonstrated necessity.

The expert was clear: equivalence must be shown with numbers:
- plant-by-plant utilization,
- fixed and variable cost,
- headcount by function,
- savings by scenario,
- implementation risk,
- and margin trajectory to 2030.

Until that is on the table, **closures remain a political preference, not an economic conclusion**.

You cannot ask the workforce to surrender plants and jobs on the basis of “trust us.”

### 2) “No closures” is not the problem; overcapacity is
Let me be very clear: the problem is not that employees refuse to let management close plants. The problem is that management wants to preserve the option of closure while shifting all the burden onto labor.

If the company truly needs capacity reduction, then there are alternatives that do not require plant closure:
- product reallocation,
- internal consolidation,
- qualification and redeployment,
- voluntary exits,
- partial retirement,
- working-time flexibility,
- and management/owner contributions.

What I will not accept is a package that keeps closures as a hanging threat while asking us to make concessions first.

### 3) Compulsory redundancies are unacceptable
The claim that “voluntary-only measures won’t be enough” may be convenient for management, but it does not make forced layoffs socially or industrially legitimate.

If the workforce reduction needed is so large that it cannot be handled voluntarily, then that is precisely why management must pursue **footprint consolidation without closures** and **cost-sharing by ownership and management**.

Forced redundancies would destroy trust for years and make every future transformation conflictual. That is a strategic error, not a solution.

### 4) The expert input does not defeat our position
The expert did **not** say closures are inevitable. The expert said:

- non-closure packages can work **if they permanently reduce capacity and fixed overhead**;
- temporary wage restraint and voluntary exits alone are usually insufficient;
- partial consolidation can come close to closure economics if executed deeply.

That actually supports our position more than management’s. It means the real issue is **how deep the consolidation goes**, not whether a site is officially shut.

So the right answer is not “close plants.”  
The right answer is **strip out overcapacity without destroying the social contract**.
