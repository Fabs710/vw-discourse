We are prepared to back a restructuring that restores margins and competitiveness. We are **not** prepared to back a deal that protects every site and every job in a way that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive.

## Where we agree
We agree with management, the family, the state, and the expert on the central diagnosis:
- the company has a real structural cost and footprint problem;
- temporary fixes are not enough;
- a durable solution is required;
- and the package must be judged by hard numbers, not slogans.

We also agree with the employee side on one important point:
- a cosmetic compromise would be value-destructive.

## Where we disagree
### 1) “No closures” is not a sufficient answer
The works council and union keep repeating “no plant closures” as a red line. We understand the social and industrial logic, but from a shareholder perspective that is **not** a solution unless they can show equivalent permanent savings and capacity reduction.

So far, they have not done that.

If the company has structurally too much capacity, then preserving all sites simply shifts the problem into weaker margins, higher fixed costs, and repeated crisis negotiations later. That destroys equity value.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” is not enough on its own
We prefer voluntary measures over forced layoffs, because conflict and strikes destroy value too. But if the workforce reduction needed is large, then a purely voluntary scheme often will not be sufficient.

If the employee side wants to rule out compulsory redundancies, then it has to bring a package that still delivers:
- permanent headcount reduction,
- lower indirect cost,
- lower fixed overhead,
- and credible utilization improvement.

If it cannot, then it is asking investors to accept structurally impaired returns.

### 3) Site guarantees to 2030 can become value traps
We do not reject guarantees in principle. We reject **blanket guarantees that freeze an overbuilt footprint**.

A guarantee is only acceptable if it is paired with:
- measurable capacity reduction,
- real cost removal,
- and enforceable milestones.

Otherwise it becomes a promise to preserve inefficiency.

## Where we stand on the scenarios
### Closure-based restructuring
We can support closures **if** management proves they are necessary to restore competitiveness and if they are part of a coherent, durable footprint reset.

### Non-closure restructuring
We can support a non-closure alternative **only if it is economically equivalent**:
- permanent capacity reduction,
- meaningful fixed-cost elimination,
- major workforce reduction,
- simplified plant and product allocation,
- durable labor-cost relief,
- and credible implementation.

Temporary wage restraint, voluntary exits, and working-time flexibility **by themselves** are not enough.

### Hybrid compromise
This may be the most realistic route, but only if it is not just a disguised delay tactic. A hybrid package must still remove enough cost and capacity to restore margins, not merely defer the problem.

## What would move us
We need an independent, site-by-site scenario comparison showing:
- utilization by plant,
- fixed and variable cost,
- direct and indirect headcount,
- annual savings by scenario,
- NPV to 2030,
- implementation risk,
- and the margin trajectory under each option.

## Bottom line
Our position is simple:
- We support **durable structural cost reduction**.
- We do **not** support a cosmetic compromise.
- We do **not** support preserving an uncompetitive footprint because closures are politically difficult.
- We are open to a non-closure solution only if it truly matches the economic effect of closures.

If the employee side wants our support, it must show — in numbers — how it gets the company back to competitive margins without leaving structural overcapacity in place. Until then, closures remain a legitimate option, not a failure of imagination.
