We agree with the core diagnosis: **the company has a real structural cost and footprint problem, and cosmetic fixes are not acceptable**.

Where we **agree** with management, the family, the state, and the expert:
- temporary concessions alone will not restore competitiveness;
- the solution must be durable, not symbolic;
- the package must remove enough **capacity, fixed cost, and complexity** to lift margins;
- and the discussion must be grounded in a **site-by-site quantitative model**.

Where we **disagree** with the employee side is on the presumption that **“no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” can be treated as firm red lines before the numbers are shown**.

That position may be understandable socially, but from a capital-market perspective it is only acceptable **if** it still delivers:
- permanent capacity reduction,
- meaningful fixed-cost removal,
- a real reduction in indirect overhead,
- and a credible margin path to 2030.

So far, that has **not been proven**.

### On the works council and union
You are right that management has not yet earned a blank cheque.  
But you are **not** right to imply that closures are merely a political preference and not an economic possibility.

A non-closure package is only acceptable if it is **economically equivalent**, not just less painful. That means:
- not just voluntary exits and working-time flexibility,
- not just temporary wage restraint,
- not just redeployment and retraining,
- but **durable structural simplification** at scale.

If the independent model shows that deep consolidation without formal closure can truly achieve that, we would support it.  
If it cannot, then preserving every site becomes a value trap.

### On compulsory redundancies
We prefer voluntary solutions because conflict destroys value.  
But we do **not** accept a blanket rule that rules out compulsory redundancies regardless of the scale of overcapacity.

If voluntary tools are sufficient, use them.  
If they are not, the answer is not to preserve an uncompetitive cost base indefinitely.

### On the state and co-determination
We respect the state’s role and the importance of co-determination. But those institutions are not meant to freeze the footprint forever. They are meant to shape a viable restructuring. A weak company with too much capacity is not a protected industrial base.

### On the family
Long-term ownership should mean supporting a restructuring that restores **returns, not just continuity**. We agree with the principle that the enterprise must remain a strong industrial anchor. But that requires hard restructuring, not polite delay.

## What would move us
We remain open to a **hybrid or non-closure package** only if the independent model shows:
- permanent capacity reduction,
- substantial fixed-cost savings,
- meaningful workforce reduction,
- lower logistics and complexity cost,
- and a credible margin trajectory to 2030.

Without that, we continue to regard **closures as a legitimate and potentially necessary option**, not an ideological preference.

## [SUMMON: independent site-by-site industrial and financial modeller]
We need the side-by-side comparison now, site by site, covering:
- utilization,
- fixed and variable costs,
- direct and indirect headcount,
- logistics burden,
- capex,
- NPV to 2030,
- implementation risk,
- and margin impact under closure, hybrid, and non-closure scenarios.

## Bottom line
We will back the restructuring that **most credibly restores competitive margins and disciplined cost reduction**.  
We will **not** back a settlement that protects every site in a way that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.  
If the alternative package can genuinely match closure economics, prove it. If not, the market will not reward a cosmetic compromise.
