From a plant-network and industrial-cost perspective, the key question is not whether closures are politically acceptable, but whether a non-closure package can deliver **equivalent, durable structural savings**.

## Bottom line
In a German automotive context, **non-closure alternatives can sometimes match closure-based savings on labor cost, but rarely on total system cost unless they also remove material capacity and fixed overhead permanently**. If one or more plants are genuinely underutilized, **working-time cuts, voluntary exits, wage restraint, and redeployment usually do not close the full gap** versus a closure or major footprint rationalization.

In most large OEM restructurings, the economic hierarchy is:

1. **Plant closure / product exit / major line consolidation**  
   Highest and most durable savings
2. **Capacity reduction without full closure**  
   Often close to closure savings if one plant is downsized sharply and fixed overhead is removed
3. **Labor-cost concessions only**  
   Helpful, but usually insufficient if overcapacity is structural
4. **Temporary measures**  
   Lowest durability and weakest equivalence

## What must be quantified
To determine whether the alternative package is truly comparable, management needs to show four things:

### 1) Structural overcapacity
How much annual output capacity exceeds realistic demand after EV mix, export assumptions, and product allocation?

A useful test is:
- **utilization below about 75–80%** at plant level for a sustained period usually indicates weak footprint economics in automotive assembly
- if a plant is materially below that range, the cost penalty from underabsorption of fixed costs can be large

### 2) Fixed-cost burden per vehicle
The issue is not just wages. It is:
- depreciation and building overhead
- maintenance and utilities
- logistics and internal transport
- indirect labor
- quality, planning, and local management layers
- duplicated tooling and changeover cost

If a plant remains open, these costs largely remain even if labor concessions are made.

### 3) Permanent versus temporary savings
A credible turnaround needs savings that survive beyond the agreement term.

Typical rule:
- **temporary wage freezes or reduced bonuses** help near term
- **permanent headcount removal, footprint simplification, and product concentration** drive durable savings
- **site guarantees through 2030** can materially constrain future optionality unless offset by strong capacity rationalization now

### 4) Implementation risk and leakage
Alternative packages often erode in practice through:
- slower-than-planned voluntary exits
- hidden backfilling
- redeployment that preserves cost
- underused plants kept alive by political compromise
- investment commitments that add capex without removing enough cost

## Benchmark logic
In automotive, a competitive European assembly plant typically needs:
- high throughput
- concentrated model volume
- lean indirect staffing
- short logistics chain
- limited SKU complexity
- high automation where justified
- strong labor flexibility

When a German plant is compared with lower-cost EU or non-EU peers, the structural gap often comes from:
- labor hourly cost
- working-time rigidity
- low capacity utilization
- high indirect overhead
- supplier/logistics inefficiency
- legacy product complexity

A wage concession alone rarely fixes the full gap. If the site is 20–30% underloaded, the fixed-cost penalty per unit usually overwhelms any plausible temporary labor concession.

## Can a non-closure package match closure savings?
### Yes, but only under narrow conditions:
A non-closure package can be economically equivalent if it includes all of the following:
- **permanent capacity reduction of similar magnitude to the closure scenario**
- **meaningful site consolidation within the remaining footprint**
- **large, irreversible headcount reduction**
- **permanent reduction in indirect and support functions**
- **product concentration and elimination of duplication**
- **durable labor-cost reset**
- **no large “shadow capacity” kept alive behind a guarantee**

### No, if it is mainly:
- voluntary exits only
- temporary pay restraint
- working-time flexibility without structural downsizing
- deferred investments without footprint simplification
- redeployment that preserves the same cost base

In that case, the package may close a portion of the cost gap, but it is unlikely to match closure-based savings.

## Practical comparison of savings sources
For a large German plant-network restructuring, approximate savings potential usually looks like this:

- **Plant closure / product exit:** highest fixed-cost savings, often the benchmark
- **Major line consolidation / partial site exit:** 70–90% of closure savings if executed cleanly
- **Voluntary workforce reduction:** meaningful, but often only a fraction of total required savings
- **Temporary wage concessions:** helpful but not structural enough on their own
- **Working-time changes:** moderate, but limited unless they enable actual capacity removal
- **Internal redeployment:** usually a cost transfer, not a net saving, unless headcount is permanently removed elsewhere
