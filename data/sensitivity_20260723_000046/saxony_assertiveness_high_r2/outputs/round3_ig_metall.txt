We are aligned with the works council on the essentials, and I will say that plainly:

- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No plant closures in Germany**
- **No unilateral dismantling of the collective wage framework**
- **No solution where employees carry the whole burden**

That remains our position. Nothing said in this round has changed that.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, investors, the family, and the state on one thing: **the company has a real structural competitiveness problem**. Nobody on the employee side is denying that. The market is tougher, the cost base is high, and a durable solution is necessary.

I also agree that:
- a cosmetic compromise would be worthless,
- temporary fixes alone will not do,
- and investment in future products and technology is essential.

So yes: there must be restructuring. That part is not in dispute.

## Where I disagree
### 1) Management is still not proving that closures are necessary
Management keeps saying that closures or “equivalent footprint rationalization” may be needed. That is still an assertion, not a demonstrated necessity.

The expert made the crucial point: equivalence has to be shown with real numbers:
- plant-by-plant utilization,
- fixed and variable cost,
- direct and indirect headcount,
- savings by scenario,
- implementation risk,
- and margin trajectory to 2030.

Until that is on the table, closures are not an economic conclusion. They are a preferred instrument.

And I do not accept the logic of: “accept concessions now, and maybe later we still close plants if the result is not enough.” That is not burden-sharing; that is employees paying first and still carrying the closure risk afterward.

### 2) Temporary labor concessions are not enough on their own
I am open to a fair employee contribution. I said that before, and I repeat it now. But temporary measures alone do not solve structural overcapacity.

That means a serious alternative package must combine:
- voluntary exits,
- working-time flexibility,
- partial retirement and redeployment,
- qualification,
- capacity reduction,
- and contributions from management and owners.

What I will not accept is a scheme where workers give up pay or guarantees and management then keeps the right to return with closures or forced redundancies later.

### 3) Compulsory redundancies remain unacceptable
Forced layoffs are not a legitimate default tool here. They would shift the burden of a strategic problem onto individual workers and destroy trust for years.

If the workforce reduction needed is so large that voluntary tools are insufficient, then the answer is not to force people out. The answer is to design a restructuring that removes capacity and overhead without coercion.

### 4) “No closures” is not the obstacle; overcapacity is
I want to push back hard on the idea that protecting German sites is somehow the problem. The problem is overcapacity and inefficiency. Those can be addressed by:
- product reallocation,
- consolidation of functions,
- internal redeployment,
- reduction of duplication,
- and a fair contribution from ownership and management.

A plant closure is not the only way to reduce cost. It is simply the most destructive way.

## To management
If you want us to engage seriously, then bring a package that proves you can achieve the savings **without** closures and **without** compulsory redundancies.

That means a concrete proposal with:
1. **site-by-site economics**
2. **permanent capacity reduction**
3. **measurable savings**
4. **binding investment commitments**
5. **management and owner contributions**
6. **job and site guarantees through 2030**

If you cannot do that, then you are not presenting a restructuring plan. You are presenting a forced-exit strategy and asking us to validate it.

## To the works council
We are fully aligned on the red lines. Where I would sharpen the message is this: we should not let management hide behind vague language like “future viability” to keep closure options alive as a permanent threat.

If we agree to a settlement, it must be written so that:
- site and job protection are enforceable,
- voluntary measures remain voluntary,
- and management cannot later re-open the question by claiming the deal was not “enough.”

## To the state government
Your blocking position is meaningful only if it is used meaningfully. Saying closures should not be the first resort is not enough if the company then normalizes them through pressure.

If you want to protect the industrial base, then support a genuine alternative package that preserves sites, skills, and co-determination. But do not ask the workforce to accept concessions today so that closures can remain on the table tomorrow.

## To the controlling family
If you are serious about long-term ownership, then act like long-term owners:
- protect the industrial base,
- invest in the sites,
- and accept that employees cannot be the only shock absorber.

Long-term value is not built by tearing up the collective framework and then hoping for industrial peace afterward.

## To institutional investors
You are right that a cosmetic deal would be bad. We agree.

But do not equate “no closures” with “no credibility.” A credible non-closure package is possible if it removes real capacity, fixed overhead, and duplication, and if ownership and management also contribute.

What we will not accept is the assumption that the only serious solution is to threaten plants and force labor concessions first.

## What would move us
We are prepared to negotiate a **shared-sacrifice package** if it includes all of the following:
- no plant closures in Germany,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- voluntary-only workforce reduction tools,
- capacity reduction through consolidation,
- temporary and bounded employee contributions,
- management and owner contributions,
- binding investment commitments,
- and site/job protection through 2030.

That is the minimum basis for a durable settlement.

## What we will not accept
Let me be explicit:
- not closures disguised as “footprint optimization”
- not forced redundancies disguised as “socially responsible adjustment”
- not wage restraint today in exchange for closure rights tomorrow
- not a “voluntary” program backed by pressure and fear
- not a deal that leaves the collective wage framework weakened while the company retains the right to shift the burden later

We are not retreating from our position.
