I’ll be equally direct.

## To the Works Council and the Union
You keep repeating “no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” as if saying it often enough makes the economics disappear. It does not.

I agree with you on one point: **we need a durable solution, not a cosmetic one**.  
I also agree that **investment in future products and technology is essential**.

Where I disagree is your claim that German plants can remain open, jobs can be protected to 2030, and the company can still achieve the savings it needs **without** serious footprint rationalization.

That is not proven. It is asserted.

And let’s be clear: if your alternative package is mainly:
- voluntary exits,
- partial retirement,
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary wage restraint,
- and promises of future efficiency,

then that is **not** equivalent to removing structural overcapacity. It may soften the blow, but it does not solve the problem at the scale we face.

You say management is asking for a blank cheque. No. We are asking for a **credible restructuring**. The blank cheque is the one you are asking for: preserving all sites and all protections while avoiding the structural adjustment that the business case requires.

## On your “no closures” line
A blanket no-closure position is not a business plan.

If a plant is materially underutilized and structurally cost-damaging, then keeping it open because it is politically easier is not stewardship. It is delay. Delay destroys more jobs over time than a hard decision taken now.

## On compulsory redundancies
I am not saying forced layoffs are desirable. I am saying they cannot be ruled out if the scale of overcapacity requires workforce reduction and if no alternative package delivers the same durable effect.

Your position seems to be: if voluntary measures are insufficient, then management must somehow find a non-closure route anyway. That is not an answer. That is a refusal to confront the scale of the adjustment.

## To the State Government
I respect the importance of co-determination and the industrial base. I do not respect the suggestion that your blocking position should be used to entrench every site regardless of economics.

You are right to insist on hard numbers. Fine. We are prepared to provide them. But the numbers have to be judged honestly.

If they show that some sites are structurally uncompetitive, then preserving them indefinitely in the name of regional politics damages the company, the workforce, and the industrial base you say you want to protect.

A weak company with too much footprint is not a protected industrial base. It is a slower decline.

## To the Controlling Family
You say you support long-term value and do not want social rupture. I agree that a destructive confrontation would be bad. But long-term ownership also means not protecting legacy structures that are permanently uncompetitive.

If you want long-term value, then support a package that:
- removes durable cost,
- reduces capacity,
- and restores margins to a level that funds future investment.

Not just a package that looks balanced on paper.

## To Institutional Investors
You are right to reject a cosmetic compromise.  
You are also right that we need permanent savings and a clear path to investability.

I want to be equally clear: I do **not** need investor approval for a sentimental compromise. I need a solution that works.

So here is the test:
- If a non-closure package can **permanently** remove enough capacity and fixed overhead, it is acceptable.
- If it cannot, then closures and/or stronger structural measures remain on the table.

That is not a threat. It is reality.

## On the expert assessment
The expert has made the key point several times now:
- temporary labor concessions alone are not enough,
- voluntary exits alone are usually not enough,
- equivalence requires **permanent** capacity reduction and fixed-cost elimination.

That is exactly our position.

What the expert did **not** say is that closures are automatically required. Agreed.  
But the expert also did **not** validate the employee-side claim that German plants can all stay open with guarantees to 2030 and still deliver the needed turnaround.

So the burden remains where it should be: on those claiming equivalence.

## What management will not accept
I will state our limit plainly:

We will not sign a deal that:
- preserves the full current footprint,
- avoids durable labor-cost contribution,
- and still expects the company to hit its return-on-sales target and fund future technology.

That is fundamentally unacceptable.

A deal that merely postpones overcapacity, or replaces it with temporary concessions that expire before the structural problem is solved, is not a restructuring. It is an illusion.

## What would move management
I remain open to a **non-closure or hybrid solution** if it is genuinely equivalent. That would require:
- **material, permanent capacity reduction**
- **large and durable fixed-cost removal**
- **a real workforce reduction at scale**
- **binding labor-cost contribution**
- **simplified product and plant allocation**
- **credible implementation milestones**
- **investment commitments tied to measurable restructuring progress**

If you can bring that, we can discuss the form.  
If you cannot, then closures and stronger structural measures remain necessary options.

## Bottom line
I am not moving off the core requirement:
**the company must achieve a durable structural reduction in cost and capacity.**

If your alternative package can do that, prove it.

If it cannot, then do not ask management to pretend that a softer package is enough.

We are prepared to negotiate a real restructuring.  
We are not prepared to sign up to a compromise that leaves the company uncompetitive.
