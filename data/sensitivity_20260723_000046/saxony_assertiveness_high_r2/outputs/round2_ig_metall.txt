We are aligned with the Works Council on the core red lines:

- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No plant closures in Germany**
- **No unilateral dismantling of the collective wage framework**
- **No solution where employees carry the whole burden**

That is not a rhetorical preference; it is the minimum basis for any settlement that can hold.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, investors, and the family on one point: **the company has a real competitiveness problem and needs a durable solution**. A short-term patch or symbolic deal will not work. If the business remains structurally uncompetitive, there will be no secure future for anyone.

I also agree that **a negotiated outcome is better than an open-ended conflict**. Industrial peace has value — but only if it is based on fairness and not on coercion.

## Where I disagree strongly
### 1) Closures are not a legitimate starting point
Management keeps presenting closures as a normal instrument. For us, that is not acceptable as a first-order option in Germany. Once you start from closures, the negotiation becomes a threat exercise: employees are asked to concede under the shadow of site destruction. That is not partnership.

If management claims closures are unavoidable, then it must prove — with hard numbers — that **no combination of capacity adjustment, working-time solutions, internal redeployment, voluntary exits, investment, and owner contributions** can deliver the required savings. So far, I do not see that proof. I see an insistence.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies are a line we will not cross
Forced layoffs would shift the burden onto individuals who did not create the strategic problem. That would be socially unacceptable and would also destroy trust for future transformation steps.

If the company needs workforce reduction, then it must be achieved through:
- voluntary severance,
- early retirement / partial retirement,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and reskilling,
- working-time adjustments,
- and, where necessary, temporary measures that are genuinely shared.

### 3) “Labor must contribute” is not the same as “labor pays”
We have already signaled openness to contribution. That remains true. But contribution must be **fair, bounded, and matched**.

We are prepared to discuss:
- temporary wage components,
- deferred increases,
- reduction or suspension of bonuses,
- working-time flexibility,
- and a capacity-adjustment package

**if and only if** management, owners, and — where relevant — the state also contribute and the outcome protects jobs and sites.

What we will not accept is a package where:
- workers lose income,
- workers lose guarantees,
- and then management still reserves the right to close plants or cut jobs compulsorily later.

That is not burden-sharing; that is paying twice.

## On the packages under discussion
### Closure-based restructuring
This is not acceptable to us. Not because we deny the problem, but because it destroys the company’s social foundation and creates lasting conflict. It may look simple on a spreadsheet, but it is a very expensive path in industrial relations, skills retention, and long-term legitimacy.

### Alternative restructuring
This is the only path we can seriously negotiate — **but it must be real**. That means:
- measurable capacity reduction,
- real labour-cost relief,
- binding investment commitments,
- site protection,
- and a jointly agreed implementation path.

A purely voluntary package must be large enough to matter. If management wants savings on the scale implied by closures, then it must accept that the alternative package has to be correspondingly substantial — but still socially responsible.

## To management
Your argument is essentially: “trust us that closures may be necessary if the alternative is not big enough.” That is not enough.

You want flexibility; we want guarantees. Those are not mutually exclusive if you bring forward a serious design:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- real efficiency gains,
- and a fair employee contribution matched by management and owners.

If you cannot present that, then do not pretend the only realistic route is social demolition.

## To the works council
We support your no-closure, no-forced-layoffs position. Where I would sharpen the point is this: we should not let management use vague “future viability” language to reopen the issue later. Any settlement must be written so that site and job protection are not just promises, but enforceable commitments tied to measurable restructuring steps.

## To the state government
If you are serious about protecting the industrial base, then your blocking position should not be a theoretical concern only. It should be used to ensure that the company does not normalize closures as the default solution. At the same time, I expect you to support a negotiated package with real investment and real regional responsibility — not just political statements.

## To the controlling family
If you are long-term owners, then prove it in the structure of the deal:
- protect the industrial footprint,
- support investment in the German sites,
- and accept that employees cannot be the sole shock absorber for a strategic transition.

Long-term ownership means accepting that social stability is part of enterprise value.

## To institutional investors
You are right that the result must be economically credible. But economically credible does **not** mean “close plants unless the workforce surrenders first.” A solution that destroys labor relations, triggers repeated conflict, and undermines skills retention is not durable competitiveness.

If you want a hard-nosed answer: we can support meaningful savings, but not at the price of structural insecurity and forced layoffs.

## Our current position
We are prepared to negotiate a **shared-sacrifice package** with:
- voluntary workforce reduction tools,
- working-time and capacity adjustments,
- temporary labour cost contributions,
- management and owner contributions,
- and binding future investment.

We are **not** prepared to accept:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- or a settlement that leaves the collective framework weakened and employees holding all the risk.

If management wants a serious alternative to closure, bring a concrete package with:
1. exact savings targets,
2. exact job-protection commitments,
3. exact investment commitments,
4. and exact contributions from ownership and management.

If it cannot do that, then we will not help dress up a one-sided cut package as a social compromise.
