Thank you. Within my remit, the specific question is whether a **non-closure restructuring package** can plausibly deliver savings comparable to a **closure-based package**, and what the implementation constraints are in a large German automotive manufacturer.

## Short answer
**Yes, in principle—but only under fairly strict conditions.**  
A non-closure package can sometimes achieve a very large share of the savings from closures, **if** the company has enough structural flexibility in:
- capacity utilization,
- shift systems and working-time arrangements,
- product allocation,
- workforce sizing through attrition/voluntary exits,
- and genuine productivity improvements.

However, **non-closure measures usually do not fully replicate closure economics** unless the company is able to remove substantial fixed cost, idle capacity, and overhead. If the problem is true long-term overcapacity at one or more sites, then non-closure measures often deliver **partial equivalence, not full equivalence**.

## 1) What closures actually buy
A plant closure is powerful because it can remove several cost layers at once:
- direct manufacturing labor,
- maintenance and utilities,
- site overhead,
- local logistics and internal handling,
- management layers,
- depreciation on some assets,
- and, importantly, future fixed-cost commitment.

The savings are durable because the capacity is taken out of the system.

By contrast, if a site remains open, many fixed costs continue:
- building and infrastructure costs,
- shared services,
- some engineering and logistics overhead,
- management complexity,
- and residual underutilization costs.

So the key issue is not whether labor cost can be reduced; it is whether the company can **permanently eliminate enough structural cost and excess capacity** without closing sites.

## 2) What non-closure measures can realistically achieve
A credible non-closure package can include:

### Capacity and footprint measures
- discontinuing one or more shifts,
- consolidating production lines,
- moving selected model variants between plants,
- reducing line speeds or operating days,
- converting a site from full production to partial production or a function-specific role.

### Workforce measures
- natural attrition,
- voluntary severance,
- early retirement bridges where lawful and affordable,
- internal redeployment,
- temporary working-time reductions,
- hiring freezes,
- targeted non-replacement of vacancies.

### Labor-cost measures
- temporary pay moderation,
- bonus deferral or reduction,
- reduction of supplementary allowances,
- longer working hours for the same pay in defined formats,
- productivity-linked concessions.

### Operating model measures
- logistics simplification,
- procurement savings,
- fewer indirect roles,
- engineering and administrative consolidation,
- stricter investment prioritization.

These can be substantial. In a large manufacturer, **hundreds of millions to low billions of euros annually** can sometimes be extracted if the footprint is materially streamlined. But whether that matches closure-level savings depends on the scale of overcapacity.

## 3) The main constraint: fixed cost removal
This is the decisive point.

If a site is structurally underutilized, then a non-closure alternative must still remove:
- enough direct labor,
- enough indirect labor,
- enough footprint overhead,
- and enough future capex burden.

If it does not, then the company may reduce reported labor cost while leaving the core structural problem intact.

In practice, the hardest part is usually not wages. It is:
- **excess capacity**
- **duplicated functions**
- **weak product allocation**
- **too many sites competing for too few models**

Without changes there, a non-closure package risks becoming a prolonged subsidy to underused capacity.

## 4) What makes a non-closure package credible
For a non-closure settlement to be credible, it should include all of the following:

1. **Site-by-site savings model**  
   Each plant should be assessed separately for:
   - utilization,
   - fixed cost base,
   - labor share,
   - logistics cost,
   - product pipeline,
   - and break-even volume.

2. **Quantified capacity reduction**  
   Not just “efficiency,” but a defined reduction in output capability, shifts, or product mix.

3. **Workforce reduction path**  
   A clear mechanism for headcount reduction without compulsory redundancies if that is the political red line:
   - attrition,
   - voluntary programs,
   - internal transfer,
   - retirement pathways.

4. **Binding labor contribution**
   A temporary but material concession from labor if a cost reset is required.

5. **Owner and management contribution**
   Otherwise the burden-sharing claim is not credible.

6. **Investment and product commitments**
   Otherwise the workforce is being asked to fund a decline.

7. **Enforcement and triggers**
   If performance milestones are missed, the package should specify what happens next. Without this, the agreement is not operationally robust.

## 5) My assessment of the likely outcome
Based on the scenario you described, the company appears to face **structural overcapacity and a weak cost position in Germany**. In that situation:

- A **non-closure package can probably deliver a meaningful share of required savings**.
- It may be sufficient **if the company’s actual need is primarily cost reduction rather than hard capacity elimination**.
- But if at least one German plant is genuinely redundant at current and foreseeable volumes, then a non-closure deal may only postpone the adjustment, not solve it.

So the decisive question is empirical:
**Are there plants or lines with persistently insufficient utilization even after model allocation changes and shift reductions?**
If yes, then the savings gap between a non-closure package and a closure package may be too large to bridge permanently.

## 6) What I would not assume
I would not assume:
- that closure is automatically necessary,
- or that non-closure measures are automatically enough.

Both claims are often made too quickly.

The right answer is a **quantified alternative analysis**:
- plant by plant,
- function by function,
- direct and indirect cost,
- savings timing,
- one-off restructuring costs,
- and durability of savings over 3 to 5 years.

## Bottom line
My expert view is:

- **A negotiated non-closure restructuring can be real and substantial.**
- **It can be economically equivalent to closure only in some cases, not all.**
- The closer the company is to genuine long-term overcapacity, the more difficult it becomes for non-closure measures to fully substitute for a shutdown.
- Therefore, the parties should compare **net present value of savings**, **implementation risk**, and **labor-relations durability**, site by site, rather than debating closures in the abstract.
