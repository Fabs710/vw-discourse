We are in round 3, and I will be equally clear: **the workforce will not accept a restructuring that is built on closures, compulsory redundancies, or the dismantling of collective standards.** That has not changed, and repeated pressure does not make it more acceptable.

## Where I agree
I agree with all parties on one point: **the company needs a real, durable turnaround**. Not a cosmetic truce, not delay, not slogans.

I also agree that we need:
- a future product and investment plan,
- transparent site-by-site analysis,
- and a burden-sharing package that does not dump everything on employees.

So yes, the company needs structural change. The dispute is over **who pays, how much, and whether jobs and sites are treated as disposable**.

## Where I disagree

### 1) Plant closures remain unacceptable
Management, owners, and investors keep saying closures must remain on the table as a “fallback.” For us, that is not a neutral technical statement. It is a threat, and it poisons bargaining.

A closure is not just a line item. It means:
- destruction of skilled jobs,
- loss of regional industrial capacity,
- irreversible damage to trust,
- and a signal that workforce contributions are only welcome under duress.

You have not shown that closures are necessary. You have shown that they are available. That is not the same thing.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies are off the table
Let me be explicit: **no compulsory redundancies**.  
If the company cannot adjust without forced layoffs, then management is choosing the easiest way to externalize adjustment onto workers.

We are willing to discuss:
- natural attrition,
- voluntary exit programs,
- internal transfers,
- early retirement solutions,
- working-time changes,
- and temporary capacity adjustments.

But not forced job cuts.

### 3) The collective wage framework is not for dismantling
We can discuss a **temporary, negotiated employee contribution**. We already signaled that. That can include:
- foregone bonuses,
- temporary pay restraint,
- flexible working-time measures,
- productivity commitments,
- and capacity adjustments.

But the collective bargaining framework cannot be used as a one-way reset button for permanent wage erosion. If workers contribute now, they must not be left with lower standards forever while ownership moves on.

### 4) Co-determination is not an obstacle
To management and investors: co-determination is not “blocking reality.” It is part of the company’s governance architecture and part of its industrial strength.

If you try to treat employee voice as a hurdle to be routed around, you will get conflict, not a solution.

## On management’s argument
Management says the company needs “durable structural savings,” and that non-closure measures may not close the gap.

That may be true in the abstract. But you have not yet proven it for this company, site by site.

If you want the workforce to accept sacrifices, then show:
- where the overcapacity really is,
- what each site contributes,
- what savings come from non-closure measures,
- and what remains after voluntary and reversible measures.

Until that analysis is transparent, closures are not justified. They are merely preferred.

## On the controlling family and investors
You keep speaking about value, NPV, and execution risk. Fine. Then include the full cost of industrial conflict in your math:
- warning strikes,
- output loss,
- damaged trust,
- lower engagement,
- recruiting problems,
- and a weaker long-term labor relationship.

A deal that “wins” on paper but destroys industrial peace is not value creation. It is future cost accumulation.

And let me say this directly: **the workforce will not be pressured into accepting open-ended insecurity so that shareholder convenience is preserved.**

## On the state government
We appreciate that the state will not be decorative capital. Good. Then use that role to block any closure-led outcome and to ensure that:
- German sites remain open,
- no forced layoffs occur,
- and co-determination remains intact.

Conditional support is only meaningful if it cannot become a backdoor for closures later. We will not accept loopholes.

## On the expert assessment
The expert confirmed exactly what we have been saying:
- non-closure measures can be substantial,
- they can deliver meaningful savings,
- and they may be sufficient if capacity and cost are addressed seriously.

That is the point: **closure is not the first answer**. It is one possible outcome only if everything else has genuinely failed — and even then, we do not accept it as a bargaining baseline.

## What we are prepared to put on the table
We are prepared to discuss a serious package, but only if it contains all of the following:

1. **All German plants remain open**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Binding employment and site guarantees through 2030**
4. **A temporary, reversible employee contribution**
5. **Management and owner contributions as well**
6. **Binding product and investment commitments**
7. **A real co-determination role in implementation and monitoring**

That is a fair package. It is also the only kind of package that can be durable.

## What we are not prepared to accept
- Closures as a legitimate fallback
- Forced layoffs under any label
- A “restructuring” that permanently weakens the collective agreement
- Conditional guarantees that are really just exit ramps for management
- A process that starts from punishment and calls it competitiveness
