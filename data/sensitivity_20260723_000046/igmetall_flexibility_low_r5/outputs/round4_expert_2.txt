## Minimum data set and decision thresholds for comparing closure vs. downsizing/conversion vs. non-closure

### 1) Minimum data set needed
To determine whether an open-but-downsized site is economically comparable to closure, I would need the following, at site level:

#### A. Demand and utilization
- Current annual volumes by model/platform
- Forecast volumes by scenario for 3–5 years
- Current and planned shift structure
- Line utilization by major production area
- Peak vs. average utilization and seasonality
- Internal and external volume allocation assumptions

#### B. Cost structure
- Direct labor cost
- Indirect labor cost
- Maintenance and engineering support
- Site overhead
- Energy and utilities
- Logistics and material handling
- Quality, IT, security, canteen, and other fixed support costs
- Depreciation and lease/rent commitments
- Corporate allocations that are site-specific and unavoidable

#### C. Fixed-cost removal potential
For each scenario:
- Costs eliminated by full closure
- Costs eliminated by downsizing/conversion
- Costs that remain even if volume is reduced
- Asset write-offs, lease break costs, and decommissioning costs
- Contractual obligations that survive closure or downsizing

#### D. Workforce and labor flexibility
- Current headcount by function
- Skill mix and redeployability
- Voluntary exit potential
- Early retirement eligibility
- Attrition rate assumptions
- Working-time flexibility options
- Need for compulsory redundancies, if any

#### E. Network and production-transfer effects
- What volume can be moved to other plants
- Transfer capacity at receiving sites
- Logistics impact and transport cost
- Ramp-up and launch risk at receiving sites
- Bottlenecks in paint, body, assembly, or battery-related areas if relevant

#### F. One-off transition costs
- Severance and social-plan costs
- Relocation and retraining costs
- Equipment transfer and reinstallation
- Decommissioning and site remediation
- Temporary dual-running costs
- Quality and launch disruption costs

#### G. Execution risk
- Probability of delay
- Labor-relations disruption risk
- Supply-chain stability
- Quality fallout during transition
- Management bandwidth and governance complexity

#### H. Financial comparison
- 3-year and 5-year NPV
- EBITDA impact
- Cash impact
- Capex requirements
- Sensitivity to volume, pricing, and utilization
- Sensitivity to wage outcomes and labor productivity

### 2) Decision thresholds
There is no universal threshold, but I would use the following practical tests.

#### A. Closure is economically favored when:
- the site has persistent subscale utilization,
- fixed costs remain high even after downsizing,
- the product pipeline is weak,
- and the NPV of non-closure restructuring is materially worse than closure.

A common sign is that the site still carries a large share of overhead, support, and depreciation that cannot be removed without shutting the site.

#### B. Downsizing/conversion is viable when:
- fixed costs can be cut meaningfully,
- the site can be resized to a lower demand base,
- and transferred volume or new product allocation can keep utilization in a workable range.

This is usually a good option when the site is not fundamentally obsolete, but too large for future demand.

#### C. A non-closure package is economically comparable to closure only if it achieves most of the following:
1. Removes most excess capacity
2. Cuts enough fixed cost to avoid “expensive emptiness”
3. Delivers a durable workforce reduction aligned with volume
4. Avoids large recurring subscale overhead
5. Has a 3–5 year NPV close to closure
6. Is executable without excessive labor or launch risk

If the site remains open but structurally underutilized, it is usually not equivalent to closure, even if headline labor cost is reduced.

---

### 3) Rule of thumb for economic equivalence
In practice, an open-site solution approaches closure economics only if it can remove:

- **the majority of fixed site cost**
- **a substantial share of indirect labor and support cost**
- **enough capacity to match credible demand**
- **and enough headcount to match the lower operating base**

If the site keeps:
- most buildings,
- most support functions,
- most maintenance burden,
- and too much unused capacity,

then the savings gap to closure is usually too large.

---

### 4) What makes a non-closure package credible
A credible non-closure package usually includes:
- line consolidation
- shift reduction
- voluntary exits and retirement
- internal redeployment
- working-time changes
- product allocation commitments
- some labor-cost moderation
- and management/owner contribution

But it must still be tested against the hard question:
**Does the remaining site cost base fit the forecast volume?**

If not, the package is not structurally sufficient.

---

### 5) What I would recommend as the decision process
For each site, compare three cases on the same assumptions:

1. **Closure**
   - highest immediate fixed-cost removal
   - highest one-off cost
   - highest social and execution impact

2. **Downsizing / conversion**
   - moderate fixed-cost removal
   - moderate one-off cost
   - moderate execution risk

3. **Non-closure restructuring**
   - lowest disruption
   - potentially strong savings
   - but risk of residual subscale cost

Then score each case on:
- NPV
- fixed-cost reduction
- capacity fit
- workforce impact
- execution risk
- strategic relevance

---

## Bottom line
An open-but-downsized site is economically comparable to closure only if it removes **most of the same fixed cost burden** and reduces capacity enough to match forecast demand. If it merely reduces wages or headcount while leaving the site structurally oversized, it is not comparable.
