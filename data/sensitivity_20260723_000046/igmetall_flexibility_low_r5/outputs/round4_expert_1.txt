## Short answer
A non-closure package can be economically credible, but only if it removes enough **fixed cost**, **capacity**, and **structural labor cost** to materially narrow the gap versus closure. In many automotive plants, that is possible in part—but **not always equivalent** to closure economics. The deciding issue is site-specific. At some sites, downsizing plus product reallocation can approximate closure value; at others, the residual fixed-cost burden of keeping the site open remains too high.

## Minimum data set needed
To compare closure, downsizing/conversion, and non-closure scenarios properly, I would need at least:

1. **Current and forecast utilization**
   - line-by-line and shift-by-shift
   - by model, platform, and volume scenario

2. **Site cost structure**
   - direct labor
   - indirect labor/support functions
   - energy, maintenance, logistics, tooling
   - site overhead and depreciation
   - corporate allocations

3. **Fixed-cost removal potential**
   - what cost disappears on closure
   - what cost remains if the site stays open at lower volume
   - termination/lease/asset write-down effects

4. **Capacity and network effects**
   - achievable volume reduction
   - line consolidation options
   - transfer of production to other plants
   - bottlenecks and launch constraints

5. **Workforce reduction paths**
   - attrition
   - voluntary exits
   - early retirement
   - redeployment
   - working-time reduction
   - compulsory redundancy requirement, if any

6. **One-off restructuring costs**
   - severance
   - transfer/logistics
   - decommissioning
   - retraining
   - equipment relocation
   - restart and launch costs elsewhere

7. **Execution risk**
   - timing
   - labor-relations risk
   - supply-chain risk
   - quality ramp risk
   - engineering and product-launch risk

8. **3–5 year NPV**
   - discounted cash flow under each scenario
   - with common assumptions on volume, margin, and capex

## Decision thresholds
I would not use a single universal threshold. I would use site-specific thresholds, but as a practical rule:

### A non-closure package is economically comparable to closure only if it achieves:
- **most of the fixed-cost reduction**, not just variable-cost savings
- a **material capacity reduction** aligned with forecast demand
- a **durable labor-cost reset**
- **no hidden permanent subscale overhead**
- and an **NPV close enough to closure** that the strategic and political benefits of keeping the site open justify the remaining gap

### In practice, that usually means:
- removing at least the bulk of the site’s excess shift/line cost
- reducing headcount enough to match future volume
- eliminating duplicated support functions
- and securing sufficient product volume to avoid an underutilized “open but expensive” plant

## When non-closure is likely sufficient
Non-closure restructuring is more likely to work if the site has:
- transferable product capacity
- modular production lines
- realistic line consolidation potential
- meaningful voluntary-exit potential
- and enough future volume from new models to keep utilization acceptable

## When closure is likely hard to avoid
Closure or full conversion becomes more likely if the site has:
- persistent low utilization
- heavy site-specific fixed costs
- weak product pipeline
- high transfer costs
- or a labor-cost structure that remains uncompetitive even after negotiated concessions

## Important practical point
The phrase “no closures” is not, by itself, a restructuring plan. Likewise, “closure is available” is not, by itself, a restructuring plan. What matters is whether the chosen path creates:
- a sustainable cost base,
- competitive utilization,
- and reliable execution.

## My professional view
A **site-by-site independent comparison** is the right next step, and it should be done before any irreversible decisions. But I would be cautious about assuming that non-closure measures can fully replicate closure economics everywhere. They sometimes can, partially or nearly; they often cannot do so completely.
