## Where I agree

I agree with all of you on one thing: **this company needs a real, durable turnaround**.  
Not a symbolic deal. Not a temporary ceasefire. Not a package that preserves an uncompetitive cost base and calls that success.

I also agree that:
- investment and product commitments matter,
- labour should not carry the full burden alone,
- and the analysis must be **site-by-site and quantified**.

That part is non-negotiable, and I welcome the fact that the expert has confirmed the central issue: **non-closure measures can be substantial, but they are not automatically equivalent to closure economics**.

That is the factual core of this dispute.

## Where I disagree — fundamentally

### 1) “No closures” is not a restructuring position
The works council and union keep saying closures are unacceptable as a matter of principle. I reject that.

If a plant is structurally overcapacity, if its fixed cost cannot be removed enough through alternative measures, and if it cannot be made competitive at future volumes, then closure remains a legitimate option. Not ideological. Not punitive. Legitimate.

You are asking management to accept permanent inefficiency in the name of trust. That is not stewardship. It is denial.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be an absolute veto
I am not saying forced layoffs are the preferred instrument. They are not. But I will not tie the company’s hands in advance if the savings gap cannot otherwise be closed.

If voluntary exits, attrition, redeployment, working-time changes, and productivity measures are enough, then fine. If they are not enough, then the workforce cannot demand that management absorb the shortfall forever.

A blanket ban on compulsory redundancies before the numbers are tested is not a settlement. It is a demand that the company carry overcapacity indefinitely.

### 3) Binding guarantees through 2030 cannot be unconditional
The repeated demand for blanket site and employment guarantees through 2030 is exactly the kind of rigidity we cannot afford if we are trying to restore competitiveness.

I am open to conditional protections tied to measurable milestones. I am not open to signing away restructuring flexibility before the adjustment has even been properly defined.

A guarantee that prevents necessary change is not stability. It is postponement.

### 4) Co-determination is part of governance, not a veto over competitiveness
I respect the governance framework. I do not accept the argument that co-determination can be used to block economically necessary restructuring.

The state’s and workforce’s role is real. But it does not mean the business model can remain frozen while the market moves on.

## On the expert assessment

The expert has been very clear:  
- closure removes most site-level fixed cost in one step,
- non-closure packages can be meaningful,
- but whether they are economically equivalent depends on site-specific data.

That is exactly why I keep pushing for the quantified comparison.

But let me be equally clear: **the fact that non-closure measures can be substantial does not prove that they are sufficient everywhere**.  
And that is the part the labour side keeps skipping over.

If the numbers show that an open-but-downsized site can genuinely achieve comparable NPV savings, then I will take that seriously.  
If they do not, then closure remains on the table.

You also say management is using analysis as pressure. Of course it is pressure. That is what restructuring is. The alternative is pretending that the company can meet its target without structural change.

You are not being asked to surrender trust. You are being asked to confront the cost base.

And your position still has not answered the key question:  
**What exact package, at which site, removes enough fixed cost and capacity to match closure economics?**

Not in general. Site by site.

## To the union
You are right that temporary employee contributions should be limited in time and paired with owner and management contributions. I agree.

What I do not accept is the claim that temporary measures plus voluntary exits will automatically solve a structural problem. They may help. They may not be enough.

If the company needs a lasting reset, then the contribution must be materially meaningful, not merely symbolic.

You say we are using closures as leverage. I say you are using the threat of strikes and absolute red lines to avoid the hard choices. Both sides are exerting pressure. The question is which pressure produces a viable company.

## To the regional state government
You are right to insist that the regional industrial base matters. It does. But it does not follow that every site must be preserved at any economic cost.

Your blocking role cannot become a guarantee of structural stagnation. If it does, the result will be worse: slower decline, weaker investment, and eventually more severe job losses.

If you want to protect the region, support a package that actually restores competitiveness. Do not back a framework that protects every site on paper while eroding the company’s future.

## To the controlling family
You say you do not want a blank cheque. Good. Neither do I.

But I also do not accept a governance environment in which every hard decision is blocked until the company is out of time. That is exactly how industrial decline happens.

You want value preservation. So do I. But value is not preserved by preserving every current structure. It is preserved by making the business investable, competitive, and profitable again.

## To the institutional investors
You are right to focus on value, execution, and NPV. That is the correct language here.

But then you must be consistent: if the quantified analysis shows that non-closure measures do not fully close the cost gap, you cannot oppose closures in principle and still claim to be protecting value.

I also take note of your point that conflict is expensive. It is. And prolonged industrial conflict is one reason I am pushing for clarity now. The longer we delay hard decisions, the more expensive this becomes.

- strategic relevance of each site
