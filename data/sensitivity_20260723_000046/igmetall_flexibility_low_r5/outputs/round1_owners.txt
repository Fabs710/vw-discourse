I appreciate the seriousness of the positions already put on the table. From the perspective of the controlling family owners, I want to be equally direct.

### What we agree on
We agree that:

- the company needs a **credible turnaround**, not delay;
- the current cost base is not competitive enough;
- the German footprint has to become structurally stronger if we want to protect the company’s future;
- and a solution that ignores investment in future products would be irresponsible.

We also agree that a one-sided burden on the workforce would be both unfair and strategically shortsighted. Labour peace matters, and a stable transition has real value.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the idea that the family holding can support an outcome that preserves the current structure almost intact while simply layering on temporary concessions.

That would not restore competitiveness. It would only defer the problem and weaken the enterprise further.

I also do **not** accept any approach that treats ownership control as irrelevant. The controlling shareholders have a duty to protect the long-term value of the company. That means supporting restructuring where necessary, but not allowing the business to drift into a permanent cost disadvantage.

### On closures and layoffs
I am not here to dictate day-to-day plant decisions, but I have to be clear: if there is no credible route to durable savings, then management cannot be expected to rule out the full range of restructuring tools in advance.

At the same time, I recognize that **plant closures are not a first-choice instrument**. They are highly disruptive, politically costly, and only justified if negotiated alternatives truly fail to deliver the needed structural change.

So my position is:

- I am **not aligned with closure as an end in itself**.
- I am **not prepared to endorse permanent industrial decline** in order to avoid difficult decisions.
- I am open to a negotiated package **if and only if** it produces a real cost reset and capacity adjustment.

### On guarantees
I am cautious about blanket guarantees through 2030. If they freeze the company into an uncompetitive position, they would damage value and ultimately employment as well.

That said, I do see value in **conditional guarantees**:
- tied to a credible restructuring package,
- linked to investment commitments,
- and paired with measurable productivity and cost improvements.

In other words: guarantees can be part of the solution, but only if they are earned by a package that genuinely restores margins.

### On employee contributions
I support the principle that everyone should contribute. That includes:
- labor-cost moderation,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity gains,
- and capacity rationalization where needed.

I do **not** support a situation where owners are expected to fund the transition while the operating model remains structurally uncompetitive. Nor do I support symbolic concessions that look cooperative but do not materially change the economics.

### On the state’s position
I respect the regional state’s role and understand the political and economic consequences. But I would caution against turning the special statute into a veto over any meaningful restructuring. If the only acceptable outcome is “no closures, no layoffs, no structural change,” then the company will be trapped.

That would harm not only shareholders but also workers and the region in the long run.

### Bottom line
The family holding will support a settlement that:
- restores competitiveness,
- delivers durable margin improvement,
- protects long-term enterprise value,
- and avoids unnecessary instability.

We will **not** support a purely cosmetic compromise that preserves all existing structures while pretending the crisis has been solved.

If labour and the state are prepared to engage on a package with real structural effect, then we can back management in pursuing a negotiated solution. If not, then tougher measures remain legitimate.
