We are now in round 4, and I will not soften the substance:

**No plant closures. No compulsory redundancies. No dismantling of the collective wage framework.**  
That remains our line.

I will engage the arguments directly.

## Where I agree

### With the expert
The expert has confirmed what we have been saying from the beginning: **non-closure measures can be substantial**. That matters. It means the company is not forced into closures as a first resort.

The expert also made the key distinction correctly:
- closure removes a lot of fixed cost quickly,
- but open-site restructuring can still remove a significant amount if it includes capacity reduction, shift changes, voluntary exits, product reallocation, and cost sharing.

That is exactly why management cannot justify closures by slogan. They must prove a real residual gap, site by site.

### With the state
I agree that the company needs a durable turnaround and that the burden cannot fall only on employees. I also agree that the regional industrial base matters. The state is right to demand investment, product commitments, and a serious process.

### With the works council
We are aligned on the core red lines:
- no closures,
- no forced layoffs,
- no weakening of co-determination,
- and no “restructuring” that simply socializes management failure onto the workforce.

## Where I disagree

### 1) Management is still trying to normalize closure as an “option”
That is not neutral. It is pressure.

When management says closures remain legitimate unless proven otherwise, what workers hear is:  
**“Accept worse terms or we may close a site.”**

That is not a fair bargaining baseline. It is coercive leverage. And I reject it.

If management believes a plant is truly unsustainable, then they must prove:
- the exact overcapacity,
- the exact fixed-cost burden,
- the exact savings from non-closure alternatives,
- and why those alternatives fail.

Until then, closure is not justified. It is only threatened.

### 2) The family and investors are overplaying “economic reality”
You keep framing the issue as if there are only two worlds:
- full closure or
- permanent inefficiency.

That is false.

There is a third path:
- capacity adjustment,
- working-time changes,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- temporary wage moderation,
- management and owner contributions,
- and investment commitments that make the sites viable.

That is the path we put on the table already. It is not cosmetic. It is industrial restructuring with shared sacrifice.

### 3) The proposed “decision ladder” is dangerous if closure is an endpoint
The moderator’s ladder is presented as procedural neutrality. It is not neutral if the final step is closure.

For us, the problem is simple: once closure becomes a legitimate procedural outcome, the bargaining pressure shifts entirely onto the workforce. Management can always say, “We tried alternatives.” That creates a pathway to plant loss by attrition and pressure, not by necessity.

We do **not** accept closure as a legitimate fallback. The process must be designed to keep plants open, not to prepare the ground for shutting them down.

### 4) “No unilateral closure announcements during analysis” is not enough
The works council is right to demand that, and I support it. But we need more:
- no closure announcements,
- no forced redundancy planning,
- no preemptive outsourcing or transfer of work,
- and no use of the analysis period to quietly hollow out sites.

If management wants a real process, it must be frozen in the relevant areas while the analysis is underway.

## On the collective agreement
Let me be explicit: the collective wage framework is not a disposable variable.

We are prepared to discuss a **temporary, negotiated employee contribution** if — and only if — it is paired with:
- owner contribution,
- management contribution,
- binding investment and product commitments,
- and real employment/site security.

What we will not accept is a permanent wage reset disguised as temporary crisis management.

If workers give up bonuses, accept pay restraint, or adjust working time, then that must be:
- limited in time,
- reversible,
- and matched by concrete protection.

## On “durability”
Management keeps saying temporary measures are not enough. Fine — then show us what durable means in the real world without closures.

Durability can come from:
- eliminating excess shifts,
- consolidating lines,
- reducing internal duplication,
- reallocating product volume,
- using attrition and retirement,
- improving flexibility,
- and investing in future models.

Durability does **not** require plant closures by definition. That claim has not been proven.

## What we are prepared to offer
We remain ready to negotiate a serious package that includes:
1. temporary employee contribution,
2. voluntary exits and retirement pathways,
3. internal redeployment,
4. working-time adjustments,
5. shift and capacity reductions,
6. productivity measures,
7. management and owner contributions,
8. binding investment and product commitments,
9. preservation of all German sites,
10. no compulsory redundancies.

That is a real burden-sharing package.  
It is not surrender. It is a fair deal.

## What we are not prepared to accept
- plant closures
- compulsory redundancies
- a permanent weakening of wages
- a restructuring that treats the workforce as the only adjustable cost
- any process that turns co-determination into decoration
