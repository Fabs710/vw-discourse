We share one starting point: **the company needs a credible turnaround, not cosmetic delay**. I do not dispute that the cost base must come down and that the German footprint must remain competitive over the long term.

But I have to be equally clear: **a restructuring that relies on plant closures, mass redundancies, or the permanent weakening of the co-determination framework is not acceptable from the state’s point of view.** That would damage the regional economy, undermine trust, and put the company’s role as an industrial anchor at risk.

### Where I agree
I agree with management and with labour on several essentials:

- the situation is serious,
- competitiveness has to improve,
- there must be a durable industrial plan,
- and short-term fixes alone are not enough.

I also agree with the works council and the union that **the workforce cannot be asked to bear the burden alone**. If employees are expected to contribute, then owners, management, and the company itself must also contribute through investment, restraint, and a credible future plan.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the framing that the only serious path is closures plus layoffs. That is too narrow and too destructive. Before anyone talks about closing German plants, we need to exhaust alternatives that preserve sites and jobs:

- capacity reductions without closures,
- working-time and shift adjustments,
- negotiated productivity measures,
- temporary or staged labour-cost contributions,
- and investment commitments tied to a realistic product strategy.

If those tools can achieve the necessary savings, then there is no justification for gutting employment in the state.

I also reject any attempt to treat the state shareholder as a passive bystander. We are not decorative capital. Under the special statute, we have a legitimate co-determining role, and we will use it if necessary to prevent outcomes that permanently damage the region.

### On guarantees
Management is right that guarantees cannot be empty if they merely freeze an uncompetitive structure. But the reverse is also true: **without meaningful site and employment guarantees, there is no basis for asking the workforce to make sacrifices.** If employees are to accept flexibility or moderation, they need something durable in return.

So I would support a negotiated package only if it includes:

- preservation of key German sites,
- no forced redundancies,
- a clear path to securing jobs through a transition period,
- and binding commitments on investment, new models, and future production.

### On the acceptable path
A fair settlement must be a **social-partnership restructuring**, not an imposed liquidation of capacity. That means:

1. real structural savings, yes;
2. but achieved primarily through negotiated and reversible measures, not closures;
3. with the burden shared across all sides;
4. and with long-term industrial commitment to the German footprint.

### My bottom line
I am **not** prepared to support a solution that removes plants from Germany or breaks the state’s co-determination and veto role. If management insists on making closures the central instrument, it will face resistance not only from labour but also from the state shareholder.

I am prepared to back a tough restructuring **if it protects jobs, preserves sites, and secures the company’s future in the region**. That is the line I will defend.
