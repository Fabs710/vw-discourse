I will be direct.

I agree with all sides on one point: **this company needs a durable turnaround, not a cosmetic truce.** The current cost base is too high, the market is tougher, and future investment has to be funded. I do not dispute that.

But I do **not** accept the conclusion that closures, forced redundancies, or weakening the state’s and workforce’s governance role are the only serious answer.

## Where I agree
### With management
- You are right that the restructuring must be **real**, not symbolic.
- You are right that **investment and product commitments** matter.
- You are right that the workforce cannot be asked to carry the whole burden.

### With the expert
- The expert has confirmed that **non-closure measures can be substantial**.
- The expert has also confirmed that the real question is **site-by-site fixed-cost removal and capacity reduction**, not slogans.

### With the works council and union
- You are right that closures are **not a trivial technical tool**.
- You are right that **industrial peace, trust, and skilled jobs** are part of the company’s strength.
- You are right that the company cannot simply ask the workforce to pay for management’s strategic failures.

## Where I disagree
### 1) I do not accept closures as the starting assumption
Management and owners keep saying closures remain a legitimate fallback. I understand why you say that, but I do **not** accept it as the default frame.

A plant closure in this state is not just an economic line item. It means:
- lost jobs,
- damage to the regional industrial base,
- weakened supplier networks,
- and long-term political and social fallout.

Before anyone treats closure as justified, I expect a **transparent site-by-site comparison** showing that non-closure measures truly cannot deliver the required structural effect.

### 2) I do not accept compulsory redundancies as a routine instrument
If the only way management can make the numbers work is by forcing layoffs, then the restructuring is too blunt and too socially destructive.

I am prepared to support:
- natural attrition,
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement solutions,
- internal transfers,
- working-time measures,
- productivity gains,
- and temporary labour-cost moderation.

I am **not** prepared to support forced job cuts as the first or easy answer.

### 3) I do not accept any outcome that sidelines co-determination or the state’s statutory role
This must be said clearly: **the state’s blocking-minority rights and board role are not ornamental.** They exist to protect the regional and public interest.

I will not support any outcome that:
- bypasses the state’s veto role,
- weakens co-determination,
- or turns governance into a channel for unilateral restructuring by the strongest capital voice.

That is a line I will not cross.

## On management’s argument
You say the company needs durable structural savings and that non-closure measures may not be enough. That may be true — but it has not yet been proven in the way that would justify closures.

If you want serious support, then bring the data:
- plant-by-plant utilization,
- direct and indirect cost,
- fixed-cost burden,
- product allocation,
- one-off restructuring costs,
- and savings durability over 3 to 5 years.

Without that, closures are still a choice, not a necessity.

## On the works council and union
You are right to insist on jobs, sites, and co-determination. But I will also say this plainly: **“No closures” by itself is not yet an operational plan.**

If you want to hold that line — and I understand why you do — then the alternative must be quantified and enforceable:
- what shifts are removed,
- what capacity is taken out,
- what headcount reduction is achieved by voluntary means,
- what labor-cost contribution is made,
- what investment is committed,
- and how the savings compare to closure-based options.

That analysis needs to be real, not rhetorical. If it has not been done properly yet, then it should be done now.

## On the family holding and investors
I hear your concerns about value destruction. They are legitimate. But I do not accept the implicit message that the only way to protect value is to keep closures on the table as a pressure instrument.

Industrial conflict also destroys value. So does governance that ignores regional legitimacy and workforce trust. A deal that “wins” financially but tears apart the company’s social foundation is a weak deal.

## My position
I can support a restructuring only if it meets these conditions:

1. **German sites are preserved wherever economically feasible**
2. **No compulsory redundancies if alternatives can absorb the adjustment**
3. **A transparent site-by-site comparison of closure vs. non-closure scenarios**
4. **Binding investment and product commitments**
5. **Meaningful contributions from owners, management, and labour**
6. **Preservation of the state’s co-determination and veto role**
7. **A durable solution that protects the company as a regional anchor**

## What I cannot support
I will not support:
- a closure-led restructuring by default,
- mass layoffs as a shortcut,
- any erosion of the state’s statutory role,
- or a package that guts regional employment while calling it competitiveness.

## What I want next
The next step must be a **fully quantified site-by-site restructuring package**, comparing:
- closure,
- downsizing/conversion,
- and non-closure restructuring,

with:
- net present value of savings,
- one-off costs,
- employment effects,
- and execution risk.

If that analysis is not already available in usable form, then we need independent expertise to compare it properly.

[SUMMON: an independent industrial network-planning and cost-analysis expert with full site-level access to compare closure, downsizing/conversion, and non-closure restructuring scenarios, including fixed-cost removal, capacity effects, headcount implications, and NPV of savings.]

## Bottom line
I am **not** here to sign off on the orderly disappearance of German jobs or to let co-determination become decorative.

I am prepared to support a tough, negotiated restructuring — but only if it protects the regional industrial base, preserves the state’s role, and produces a credible long-term future for the company.
