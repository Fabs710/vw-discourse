From the perspective of dispersed institutional shareholders, the key question is not who “wins” the political argument, but whether the final package credibly restores **risk-adjusted equity value** through **durable margin improvement**.

### Where we agree
We agree with management, the family holding, and even the state on one central point: **the current cost base is not competitive**. A solution that merely postpones adjustment, protects every structure, and relies on wishful thinking would destroy value. That would be unacceptable for shareholders.

We also agree that:
- the company needs a **credible industrial future**,
- savings must be **durable**, not cosmetic,
- and the turnaround has to be tied to **investment and product competitiveness**, not just cost cuts in isolation.

### Where we disagree
What we do **not** support is a restructuring that becomes an open-ended conflict with no clear value outcome.

In particular:
- **Prolonged strike risk is value-destructive.**
- **A purely symbolic compromise is value-destructive.**
- **A settlement that preserves an uncompetitive cost base is value-destructive.**
- **A package that creates uncertainty without real savings is value-destructive.**

So while we understand the workforce’s red lines and the political constraints, we will not back an outcome that leaves margins structurally impaired just to avoid difficult decisions.

### On plant closures and layoffs
We are not attached to closures as an ideological matter. We are attached to **financial outcomes**.

If management can achieve equivalent or better savings through:
- capacity consolidation,
- working-time changes,
- productivity improvements,
- targeted labour-cost reductions,
- and a credible reduction in structural overhead,

then that is preferable to closure-driven disruption, provided the economics are real and enforceable.

But if the “alternative” is just delayed sacrifice, temporary concessions, and no meaningful reset of the cost base, then that is not acceptable.

### On guarantees
We are skeptical of blanket job or site guarantees through 2030 if they effectively freeze the company into a high-cost structure.

We can support **conditional guarantees** only if they are linked to:
- measurable cost reduction,
- genuine capacity adjustment,
- and a credible path back to competitive margins.

Guarantees without enforceable restructuring would be a transfer of risk from labour to shareholders, and we would not support that.

### On employee contributions
A fair restructuring requires some contribution from labour if the company is to recover. That does not mean one-sided punishment, but it does mean:
- wage moderation,
- flexibility,
- productivity gains,
- and acceptance that the status quo cannot remain untouched.

If there is no meaningful employee contribution, then the burden falls too heavily on equity holders, and the plan will not be credible.

### On management
Management must also be held to account. We will not support a vague “turnaround story” unless it includes:
- clear savings targets,
- timing,
- implementation discipline,
- and evidence that this will actually move returns on sales and cash generation.

### Bottom line
We are prepared to support a tough restructuring **if and only if** it credibly restores competitiveness and does so with sufficient certainty.

We will **not** support:
- a cosmetic settlement,
- a prolonged stand-off,
- or a package that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

If a negotiated solution can deliver real cost reduction, capacity adjustment, and a believable return to margin targets, we can back it. If not, we will side with the case for stronger measures.
