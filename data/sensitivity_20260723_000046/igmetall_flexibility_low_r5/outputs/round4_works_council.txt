## Our position has not changed

- compulsory redundancies,

## Where I agree
I do agree on one important point: this company needs a **real industrial turnaround**. We are not defending stagnation. We are defending a viable future for the company **and** for its workforce.

I also agree with the expert and the moderator that the debate must be **site-by-site and quantified**. Fine. Then let’s do that properly, independently, and with full access to the data — not with selective management assumptions.

## Where I disagree

### 1) Management is still trying to turn “analysis” into pressure
You say closures are not ideology, just an option if the numbers require it. But in a bargaining room, that is not neutral. It is leverage. And everyone knows it.

We do **not** accept closure as a legitimate fallback. If a site is being threatened, management must prove — with hard, transparent figures — that **all** non-closure alternatives fail. Not “may fail.” Not “might be weaker.” Fail.

Until that proof exists, closure is not on the table in any meaningful sense.

### 2) Investors keep treating conflict as an argument for surrender
You keep saying industrial conflict destroys value. Of course it does. But that is not a reason to hand management a blank cheque to close plants or force job cuts. It is a reason to stop escalating conflict by threatening workers’ livelihoods.

The cost of warning strikes is real. The cost of broken trust, destroyed skills, regional damage, and permanent insecurity is much larger.

### 3) The state is right to defend the regional base — but not enough
I appreciate the state’s rejection of closures as a starting assumption. But I want to be frank: saying you “do not accept closures as the default frame” is not the same as using your blocking power to stop them.

If the state is serious, it should make clear that:
- no closure path gets political support,
- no forced redundancy path gets political support,
- and no governance maneuver can bypass co-determination.

Anything less leaves the workforce exposed.

### 4) The controlling family still wants the option to break the historic settlement
You talk about economic reality, but what I hear is: “we want the right to tear up the social framework if profit targets are not met quickly enough.”

That is precisely what we reject.

If the company needs adaptation, then ownership must adapt too:
- by investing,
- by sharing burden,
- by backing product allocation,
- and by accepting binding job and site guarantees.

Not by demanding permanent insecurity from the workforce.

## On the expert’s argument
The expert has confirmed something important, but you are all selectively using only half of it.

Yes:
- non-closure measures may not always fully equal closure economics.

But equally:
- non-closure measures can be **substantial**,
- they can remove a significant part of the cost burden,
- and the decisive issue is site-specific feasibility.

That means the correct conclusion is **not** “therefore closures are justified.”  
The correct conclusion is: **prove the gap first, independently, and with full transparency.**

And until that is done, the default must be:
- all plants stay open,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and restructuring happens through negotiated, reversible, and socially responsible measures.

## On the proposed decision ladder
The moderator proposes a ladder: non-closure first, then downsizing, then closure if thresholds fail.

We reject that as framed, because it makes closure a procedurally legitimate endpoint. That is exactly the danger. A “threshold” is just a technical way of pre-authorizing a plant closure after enough pressure and enough managerial discretion.

No.  
Our line is simpler:
1. Test non-closure alternatives seriously.
2. If management claims a residual gap, prove it independently.
3. Then return with a package that preserves the site and the jobs.

Closure is not a step in a rational process. It is an outcome we will oppose.
