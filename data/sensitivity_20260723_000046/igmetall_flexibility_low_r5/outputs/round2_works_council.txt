Management, owners, and investors keep saying “credible turnaround,” but let us be precise: **a turnaround that is built on plant closures and forced redundancies is not credible to the workforce, and it will not be supported by us.**

I acknowledge one point from all sides: **the company does need durable competitiveness and a future product and investment plan.** On that, there is no dispute.

But where you all cross a line is here:

- you treat **closures** as a legitimate default option,
- you treat **compulsory redundancies** as an acceptable fallback,
- and you treat **binding job and site security** as something the workforce must “earn” after already giving up leverage.

That is the wrong order. It is also the wrong answer.

### To management
You say the current cost base is too high and that alternatives must deliver the “same structural effect” as closures. I reject the premise that only plant closures can create structural effect.

There are other instruments that are serious, durable, and less destructive:
- capacity reductions through **shift and line adjustments**,
- **working-time flexibility**,
- negotiated **productivity improvements**,
- targeted **cost moderation**,
- and a fair contribution from all sides.

What you are really asking for is not merely efficiency. You are asking the workforce to accept permanent insecurity so that management can keep strategic freedom. We will not trade away jobs and sites for promises.

### To the family owners and investors
You keep speaking about “value” and “risk-adjusted returns,” but let me remind you: **the workforce is not an abstract cost item.** German plants, skilled workers, and co-determination are part of the company’s industrial strength.

A “value-creating” restructuring that destroys trust, triggers strikes, and tears out the job-security framework is not value-creating. It is a self-inflicted wound.

You also say you do not want cosmetic compromise. Fine. Neither do we. But a **real** solution is not a closure package dressed up as realism.

### To the regional state government
We appreciate that you are clearly not willing to be decorative capital. Good. Then use that position consistently:
- no closures,
- no forced layoffs,
- no bypassing co-determination,
- and no breaking the social compact that has sustained this company.

If the state is serious about protecting the industrial base, it should back **binding preservation of sites and employment through 2030**, not a “conditional” guarantee that evaporates the moment management gets impatient.

### Our position in round 2
We are prepared to discuss a **serious negotiated contribution**, but only under these conditions:

1. **All German plants remain open.**
2. **No compulsory redundancies.**
3. **Binding site and job guarantees through 2030.**
4. **No unilateral dismantling of the historic job-security framework.**
5. **Meaningful co-determination in designing and monitoring the restructuring.**
6. **A credible investment and product commitment for each German site.**

If you want employee contribution, then put real commitments on the table first:
- investment volumes,
- product allocation,
- capacity planning,
- training and qualification,
- and enforceable guarantees.

Then we can talk about reversible measures, temporary sacrifices, and productivity improvements. But we will not negotiate under threat of closure.

### On the claim that alternatives are insufficient
That claim must be proven, not asserted. We have not yet seen a transparent, site-by-site comparison showing that non-closure measures cannot deliver the needed savings. Until then, closure is not a necessity; it is a choice.

And if management tries to make it a fait accompli, it will meet resistance:
- in the supervisory board,
- in collective bargaining,
- and on the shop floor.

We are not here to manage the orderly disappearance of German jobs. We are here to defend them.
