I will be direct.

I agree with everyone on one point: **this company needs a real restructuring, not a cosmetic truce**. The current cost base is too high, the competitive pressure is real, and investment in future products has to be secured. I am not denying that.

But I do **not** accept the conclusion that closures and compulsory redundancies are the necessary default. For the regional state, that would be too risky, too irreversible, and too damaging to the industrial base we are responsible for protecting. The burden of proof is still on those who want to cut plants and jobs.

## Where I agree
### With management
- You are right that the solution must be **durable**, not temporary.
- You are right that **investment and product commitments** are essential.
- You are right that **the burden cannot sit on employees alone**.

### With the expert
- Non-closure measures can be substantial.
- The real question is **site-by-site fixed-cost removal and capacity adjustment**, not slogans.
- A proper comparison must include **NPV, execution risk, and one-off costs**.

### With labour
- Trust matters.
- Co-determination matters.
- The workforce should not be treated as disposable cost.
- A negotiated contribution is possible if it is fair and reversible.

## Where I disagree
### 1) Closures are not the starting point I accept
Management keeps saying closures remain legitimate unless proven otherwise. I understand the logic, but I do not accept that as the default frame.

A closure in this state is not just a line item. It means:
- lost jobs,
- damage to supplier networks,
- weakening of the regional economy,
- and a political and social cost that does not disappear from the balance sheet.

If anyone wants to argue for closure, then I expect a **transparent site-by-site proof** that non-closure measures cannot deliver the required result.

### 2) Compulsory redundancies are not a routine instrument
I am not prepared to support forced layoffs as an easy answer. If the restructuring depends on them, then the adjustment is too blunt and too socially destructive.

We are prepared to support:
- natural attrition,
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- redeployment,
- working-time changes,
- productivity measures,
- and temporary labour-cost moderation.

I do **not** accept forced redundancies as the first or preferred route.

### 3) Blanket guarantees are not acceptable to management — but neither is a blank cheque
I understand management’s rejection of unconditional guarantees through 2030. At the same time, workers and the state are right that any contribution must buy real security.

So I will not support open-ended insecurity. Any deal has to include:
- measurable job protection,
- site protection where economically feasible,
- and a real monitoring role for co-determination and the state.

### 4) Co-determination is not an obstacle to competitiveness
I reject the idea that employee voice and the state’s statutory role are just blocks to be worked around. They are part of the company’s governance architecture and part of its strength.

If restructuring is done by sidelining those institutions, you get conflict, not a durable turnaround.

## My position
I can support a tough restructuring only if it meets these conditions:

1. **A quantified site-by-site comparison** of closure, downsizing, and non-closure options
2. **Proof that non-closure measures have been fully tested**
3. **No plant closure unless the evidence is overwhelming**
4. **No compulsory redundancies unless there is no viable alternative**
5. **A fair, temporary employee contribution paired with owner and management contributions**
6. **Binding investment and product commitments**
7. **Protection of the state’s co-determination and blocking role**
8. **Preservation of the company as a regional economic anchor**

## What I cannot support
- A closure-led restructuring by default
- Mass layoffs as a shortcut
- Any outcome that weakens our statutory role
- A package that destroys regional employment while calling it competitiveness
- A process that treats the state as decorative capital

## What I want next
Before anyone talks about closure as a serious option, I want an **independent, site-level comparison** with full access to the data:
- utilization,
- fixed costs,
- capacity effects,
- workforce implications,
- one-off restructuring costs,
- and 3–5 year savings durability.

[SUMMON: independent industrial network-planning and restructuring finance expert with full site-level access to compare closure, downsizing/conversion, and non-closure scenarios, including fixed-cost removal, capacity effects, headcount impact, one-off costs, execution risk, and 3–5 year NPV.]

## Bottom line
I am not here to endorse the orderly disappearance of German jobs or the erosion of regional industrial strength.

I am prepared to support a difficult, negotiated restructuring — but only if it genuinely protects:
- jobs,
- sites,
- co-determination,
- and the company’s long-term role as a regional anchor.

If the numbers show that non-closure solutions can achieve that, I will support them.  
If not, then I will not back a process that sacrifices the region for a quick accounting fix.
