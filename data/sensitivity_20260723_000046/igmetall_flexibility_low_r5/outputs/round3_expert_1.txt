From an industrial restructuring and workforce-planning perspective, the exchange has now converged on the right technical question:

**Can a non-closure package remove enough fixed cost, capacity, and workforce expense to be economically equivalent to a closure-based option at each site?**

## What is clear
1. **The company likely needs structural adjustment**, not just temporary cost moderation.
2. **Non-closure measures can be material**, especially if they include:
   - shift elimination,
   - line consolidation,
   - product reallocation,
   - automation,
   - logistics simplification,
   - voluntary exits,
   - early retirement,
   - wage moderation,
   - and management/owner contributions.
3. **But non-closure measures are not automatically equivalent to closure economics.**
   - A closure removes most site-level fixed cost in one step.
   - A downsizing/restructuring package often leaves residual overhead, underused utilities, maintenance, security, local administration, and network inefficiencies.
4. **The real issue is site-specific.**
   - One plant may be adjustable without closure.
   - Another may have too much fixed-cost burden, poor line fit, or insufficient volume to remain economic.

## What an economic equivalence test should include
For each site, you would normally compare:

- **capacity before and after**
- **unit cost before and after**
- **fixed-cost removal**
- **headcount reduction**
- **one-off restructuring cost**
- **implementation timing**
- **execution risk**
- **NPV over 3–5 years**
- **strategic value of the site** in future product allocation

A non-closure package is only close to equivalent if it achieves, in practice:

- enough **capacity removal** to match demand,
- enough **fixed-cost reduction** to materially improve margins,
- enough **workforce reduction** to lower recurring labor cost,
- and enough **operational simplification** to avoid stranded cost.

## Practical benchmark
As a rough restructuring rule, if a site remains open, the package usually has to do most of the following to rival closure economics:

- remove a large share of excess capacity,
- cut local overhead sharply,
- reduce staffing materially,
- eliminate underutilized shifts or lines,
- and avoid large ongoing “half-empty plant” costs.

If those conditions are not met, the site often remains structurally expensive even after concessions.

## Main constraint
The biggest constraint is that **the economics of an open site can deteriorate if utilization falls too far**. Then even aggressive concessions may not offset the burden of:
- underabsorbed fixed cost,
- low throughput,
- duplicated functions,
- and weak product mix.

That is why management is correct to ask for quantified proof, but also why labor is correct to demand site-by-site evidence rather than abstract claims.

## My expert view
If asked narrowly:  
**A non-closure restructuring can sometimes match closure economics, but only if it meaningfully removes fixed cost and capacity at the site level.**  
Where a plant is fundamentally overcapacity relative to future volume, a non-closure package often becomes only a partial substitute, not a full equivalent.
