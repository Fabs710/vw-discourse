## Expert assessment: can non-closure measures match closure-based savings site by site?

### Short answer
**Sometimes partially, rarely fully.**  
A non-closure package can produce substantial savings in a large German automotive footprint, but it usually cannot replicate the full economics of a true plant closure unless the site can be reduced to a sharply smaller role, with major permanent removal of fixed cost and capacity.

In practical terms:

- **If the problem is mainly excess labor cost and moderate overcapacity:** non-closure measures can often get **most** of the way there.
- **If the problem is true structural overcapacity at one or more plants:** non-closure measures usually achieve only **partial equivalence** to closure.
- **If a site is strategically redundant at foreseeable volume:** closure or conversion to a much smaller role tends to be the only way to remove enough fixed cost durably.

---

## 1) What closure-based restructuring usually delivers
A closure removes more than headcount. It can eliminate:

- direct production labor
- indirect plant labor
- maintenance and site services
- logistics and internal handling
- local management layers
- some engineering / support functions
- utilities and occupancy costs
- future capex obligations
- underutilization drag on the network

That is why closure is economically powerful. It converts a fixed-cost burden into a permanent exit from the network.

### Typical effect
A closure can deliver:
- **high annual savings**
- **high durability**
- **clear capacity removal**
- but also **high one-off costs**
  - severance
  - decommissioning
  - environmental / remediation
  - transfer costs
  - supply-chain reconfiguration
  - possible write-downs

---

## 2) What non-closure restructuring can realistically deliver
Non-closure measures can be strong if combined. The main levers are:

### A. Capacity and footprint reduction without closure
- removing shifts
- consolidating lines
- reducing operating days
- moving models between plants
- repurposing a plant for fewer variants
- reducing line speed

This can take out meaningful capacity, but if the site remains open, many fixed costs remain.

### B. Workforce reduction without compulsory redundancies
- attrition
- non-replacement
- voluntary severance
- retirement bridges
- internal redeployment
- hiring freeze

These are often feasible, but they are slower and less certain than closure-based headcount removal.

### C. Labor-cost moderation
- pay restraint
- bonus reduction
- allowance changes
- temporary working-time changes
- productivity-linked concessions

These can improve annual cost, but they usually do **not** remove structural overcapacity by themselves.

### D. Indirect cost reduction
- engineering consolidation
- admin simplification
- procurement savings
- logistics redesign
- shared-services centralization

Useful, but again not a substitute for fixed-capacity removal if the footprint is genuinely too large.

---

## 3) The core economic test: fixed cost removal
The main question is not “Can we cut jobs?”  
The question is: **Can we permanently remove enough fixed cost and excess capacity while keeping the site open?**

If a plant remains open, you typically still carry:

- building and infrastructure costs
- residual management overhead
- some indirect labor
- site security and upkeep
- utilities and compliance costs
- a share of group complexity costs
- future reinvestment needs

So a non-closure package may reduce variable cost sharply, but leave the fixed-cost base too high.

### Practical implication
A non-closure plan is most credible when it includes at least one of the following:

1. **Major product withdrawal from a site**
2. **A permanent shift reduction**
3. **A conversion to a smaller specialist role**
4. **Very large headcount reduction through voluntary means**
5. **A strong labor-cost reset plus productivity improvement**
6. **A meaningful consolidation of overhead and support functions**

Without those, it risks becoming a postponement package.

---

## 4) Site-by-site logic: when non-closure can work
A non-closure alternative is more likely to be sufficient when a site has:

- still-competitive logistics
- good asset condition
- adaptable line architecture
- enough volume to justify a smaller footprint
- a product pipeline that can be narrowed but not eliminated
- workforce reductions achievable by attrition and voluntary exits
- low remediation cost for partial restructuring

In such a case, you may achieve:
- durable savings
- a smaller workforce
- lower labor cost
- fewer shifts
- more focused product scope

This can be close to closure economics if the site is converted into a materially smaller operation.

---

## 5) Site-by-site logic: when closure is usually hard to avoid
A closure becomes more likely when a site has:

- chronically low utilization
- no credible future model allocation
- high fixed overhead
- duplicate capability elsewhere in the network
- high logistics penalty
- aging infrastructure
- weak adaptability to EV or other future platforms
- substantial indirect labor that cannot be removed without shutting operations
- persistent loss-making even after shift and staffing reductions

In such a case, non-closure measures may produce visible pain but not full structural correction.

### Common pattern
Management often can “buy time” with:
- fewer shifts
- hiring freezes
- wage moderation
- voluntary exits

But if the site still lacks adequate volume, the company continues to absorb fixed cost. Then the restructuring savings are not durable enough.

---

## 6) Transition cost and timing comparison

### Closure-based package
**Pros**
- highest and fastest structural savings
- decisive capacity removal
- clearer long-term cost reset

**Cons**
- very high labor-relations risk
- high political resistance
- major one-off costs
- implementation complexity
- supply-chain disruption

### Non-closure package
**Pros**
- lower immediate conflict
- preserves site and social stability
- more flexible politically
- can be phased in

**Cons**
- savings come more slowly
- may not fully remove fixed cost
- risk of partial or temporary impact
- may require repeated renegotiation
- can become trapped in compromise without full reset

---

## 7) What a credible non-closure package must contain
To be economically comparable to closure, it should include all of the following:

1. **Quantified capacity reduction**
   - not just “efficiency”
   - explicit shifts, lines, volumes, or days removed

2. **Headcount path**
   - by site and function
   - with timing
   - voluntary and attrition-based where possible

3. **Fixed-cost reduction plan**
   - indirect labor
   - support functions
   - maintenance
   - logistics
   - footprint rationalization

4. **Material labor-cost contribution**
   - temporary or structural, but defined
   - with duration and reversibility clear

5. **Product and investment commitments**
   - to avoid funding decline

6. **Trigger points**
   - what happens if savings are missed
   - what rights management retains
   - what review dates exist

Without those, it is not a restructuring plan; it is a delay mechanism.

Before any final decision, the company should run a **site-by-site net present value comparison** covering:

- fixed overhead

That comparison should be done for:
1. **closure**
2. **partial conversion / downsizing**
3. **non-closure restructuring**

Only then can the parties judge whether a non-closure deal is economically comparable or only politically preferable.

## Bottom line
**Non-closure restructuring can be real, substantial, and sometimes sufficient.**  
But in a large automotive footprint with genuine structural overcapacity, it usually does **not** fully replicate closure economics unless it removes a lot of fixed cost, not just wages.

So the right comparison is not “closure versus nothing.”  
It is **closure versus a quantified downsizing and productivity package**, site by site, with explicit savings, timing, one-off costs, and durability.
