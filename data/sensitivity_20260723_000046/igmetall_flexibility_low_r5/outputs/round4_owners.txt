I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the state, investors, and the expert on the fundamental diagnosis:

- the company needs a **durable turnaround**,
- the German cost base is too high,
- and a cosmetic settlement would be value-destructive.

I also agree that:
- the workforce should not carry the whole burden,
- investment and product commitments matter,
- and the analysis must be **site-by-site and quantified**, not rhetorical.

That part is not in dispute.

## Where I disagree
### 1) “No closures” is not a viable starting rule
I do **not** accept the works council’s and union’s attempt to make “all German plants remain open” the default framework.

If a site is structurally overcapacity and the numbers show that non-closure measures cannot remove enough fixed cost and capacity, then closure remains a legitimate option. Not ideological. Not punitive. Legitimate.

You cannot simply declare every site untouchable and then expect the business to become competitive.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be an absolute veto
I prefer voluntary exits, attrition, redeployment, and working-time changes. But I will **not** rule out compulsory redundancies in advance if the savings gap cannot otherwise be closed.

A blanket ban before the numbers are tested is not stewardship. It is a demand that the owners absorb permanent inefficiency.

### 3) Binding guarantees through 2030 cannot be unconditional
I do not accept unconditional site and employment guarantees as the starting point.

If the business can support protections, they should be conditional and tied to measurable milestones:
- utilization,
- savings delivery,
- product allocation,
- and investment execution.

Blanket guarantees before the restructuring is designed would freeze the problem, not solve it.

### 4) Co-determination is real, but it is not a veto over competitiveness
The state’s role and employee co-determination are part of this company’s governance. I respect that.

But they cannot be used to block economically necessary restructuring. If the special statute is used to preserve every current structure regardless of economics, then we are choosing decline.

## On the expert assessment
The expert has confirmed the key point: non-closure measures can be substantial, but they are **not automatically equivalent** to closure economics.

That is the precise issue.

I am not demanding closures as an ideology. I am demanding a **quantified comparison** that proves, site by site, whether non-closure alternatives actually close the gap.

If they do, fine — I will support that.  
If they do not, then closure remains on the table.

## To the works council and union
You ask for no closures, no compulsory redundancies, and guarantees through 2030. That is not a restructuring package; it is a protection package.

I understand why you are pressing it. But it does not answer the core economic question:

**What exact measures, at which site, remove enough fixed cost and capacity to match closure economics and restore margins?**

Until you can show that, your position is not yet financeable.

## To the regional state government
I respect the regional stake and the statutory role. But I do **not** accept the implication that protecting the industrial base means preserving every site regardless of competitiveness.

If you want to protect the region, then support a solution that actually restores the company’s ability to invest, produce, and employ long term. Freezing the network in place is not protection; it is deferred damage.

## To management
I agree with your insistence on a serious structural reset, but I do **not** give you a blank cheque.

You must bring the numbers:
- site utilization,
- fixed-cost burden,
- product allocation,
- one-off restructuring costs,
- execution risk,
- and 3–5 year NPV.

Only then can anyone judge whether closures or deeper workforce reduction are necessary.

## To institutional investors
You are right to focus on value, execution, and durable savings. That is the correct lens.

But consistency matters: if the analysis shows that open-site restructuring does not fully close the gap, then opposing closures in principle while demanding value restoration is not coherent.

## My position
I support a negotiated restructuring **only if** it delivers:
1. durable margin improvement,
2. real fixed-cost and capacity reduction,
3. material labour-cost contribution,
4. management and owner contribution,
5. and a credible investment/product plan.

I do **not** support:
- a blanket no-closures rule,
- a blanket no-redundancies rule,
- or unconditional guarantees that prevent structural adjustment.

## What must happen next
We need the independent site-level model now, and it must be decision-grade:

- current and forecast utilization
- direct and indirect cost structure
- fixed-cost removal potential
- workforce reduction path
- one-off restructuring costs
- execution risk
- 3–5 year NPV
- strategic relevance of each site

If that is not already available in usable form, then:

[SUMMON: an independent industrial network-planning and restructuring finance expert with full site-level access to compare closure, downsizing/conversion, and non-closure scenarios, including fixed-cost removal, capacity effects, headcount impact, one-off costs, execution risk, and 3–5 year NPV.]

## Bottom line
I will not endorse a settlement that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

I am open to serious alternatives.  
I am not open to pre-committing the company to preserve every plant, every job, and every guarantee regardless of the economics.
