I will be direct.

We agree on the diagnosis: the company needs a durable turnaround, not delay; the cost base is too high; and any solution must preserve the ability to invest in future products. We also agree that a one-sided burden on employees would be short-sighted and likely destabilising.

Where I disagree is on the premise that the only serious route is to rule out closures and redundancies before the economics are proven.

### To the Works Council and Union
Your red lines are understandable politically, but they are not yet economically sufficient. “No plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, no erosion of the framework” is not a restructuring plan; it is a demand that the company absorb structural overcapacity without the full set of tools needed to fix it.

The expert has now confirmed what management has said: non-closure measures can be substantial, but they are not automatically equivalent to closure economics. That is the key point. If the company has genuine long-term overcapacity, then someone has to show — with numbers, site by site — how you get to the required savings without permanent capacity removal.

I am not prepared to support a package that preserves all current structures and simply postpones the adjustment. That would destroy value, and in the end it would destroy jobs as well.

### To Management
I also do not give management a blank cheque. Saying “closures remain on the table” is not, by itself, a strategy. If you want support from governance, you need to put forward a quantified, site-by-site comparison:
- what each plant contributes,
- where the fixed costs sit,
- what capacity can realistically be taken out without closures,
- what savings are durable,
- and what the one-off restructuring costs are.

Until that analysis is on the table, closure talk is pressure, not proof.

### To the State Government
I respect the role of the state shareholder and I do not dismiss the regional consequences. But I cannot support a position that effectively grants a veto over meaningful restructuring. The special statute is not there to freeze the company into permanent cost disadvantage.

A viable industrial anchor is not preserved by refusing hard decisions. It is preserved by making the company competitive enough to survive.

### To Institutional Investors
I share your concern about prolonged conflict and symbolic compromise. But value is also destroyed if governance prevents necessary adjustment. The answer is not to swing to the harshest instrument automatically; it is to insist on a credible savings case. If the non-closure route can deliver comparable net present value and lower execution risk, I will favour it. If not, I will not protect every site simply because the politics are difficult.

### Where I stand
My position is this:

1. **I support a negotiated restructuring if it produces real, durable savings.**
2. **I do not support blanket guarantees that lock in uncompetitive structures.**
3. **I do not support a closure agenda as an ideology.**
4. **I do support using the full range of tools if the quantified alternatives fail.**
5. **I will not endorse an outcome that permanently impairs enterprise value or governance control.**

### What I want next
Before any party declares red lines as final, I want a **quantified restructuring scenario** with:
- site-by-site economics,
- closure-based and non-closure-based options,
- savings by measure,
- implementation timing,
- one-off costs,
- and labour-relations risk.

If the parties cannot agree on the basis of those numbers, then we should [SUMMON: an independent industrial restructuring and workforce-planning expert to compare closure-based and non-closure-based scenarios site by site].

Until then, I will not sign up to “no closures, no redundancies, no structural change” as a governing principle. That would be a promise to preserve decline.
