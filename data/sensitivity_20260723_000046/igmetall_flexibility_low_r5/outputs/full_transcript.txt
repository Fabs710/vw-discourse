[Works Council (Betriebsrat) (Plant-level employee representation under co-determination) — Round 1]

- **No plant closures in Germany.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No unilateral dismantling of the historic job-security framework.**

The workforce did not create this crisis alone, and it must not be made to pay for it with permanent job destruction and site closures. We are prepared to discuss **alternative restructuring measures** that protect all German plants and preserve employment, including:

- capacity adjustments,
- flexible working-time solutions,
- negotiated productivity measures,
- fair but limited employee contributions,
- and a binding investment and product commitment for the future.

But any serious solution must include:

- **binding site and employment guarantees through 2030,**
- **no forced layoffs,**
- **meaningful co-determination in shaping the restructuring,**
- and a credible industrial plan that secures the German footprint long term.

If management comes with a closure agenda or presents layoffs as a fait accompli, we will oppose it decisively — in the boardroom, in collective bargaining, and, if necessary, on the shop floor. We are ready to negotiate, but only on a basis that protects jobs, sites, and the future of the German plants.

## Short answer
**Yes, in principle—but only under fairly strict conditions.**

## 1) What closures actually buy
A plant closure is powerful because it can remove several cost layers at once:
- direct manufacturing labor,
- maintenance and utilities,
- site overhead,
- local logistics and internal handling,
- management layers,
- depreciation on some assets,
- and, importantly, future fixed-cost commitment.

The savings are durable because the capacity is taken out of the system.

By contrast, if a site remains open, many fixed costs continue:
- building and infrastructure costs,
- shared services,
- some engineering and logistics overhead,
- management complexity,
- and residual underutilization costs.

So the key issue is not whether labor cost can be reduced; it is whether the company can **permanently eliminate enough structural cost and excess capacity** without closing sites.

A credible non-closure package can include:

### Capacity and footprint measures
- discontinuing one or more shifts,
- consolidating production lines,
- moving selected model variants between plants,
- reducing line speeds or operating days,
- converting a site from full production to partial production or a function-specific role.

### Workforce measures
- natural attrition,
- voluntary severance,
- early retirement bridges where lawful and affordable,
- internal redeployment,
- temporary working-time reductions,
- hiring freezes,
- targeted non-replacement of vacancies.

### Labor-cost measures
- temporary pay moderation,
- bonus deferral or reduction,
- reduction of supplementary allowances,
- longer working hours for the same pay in defined formats,
- productivity-linked concessions.

### Operating model measures
- logistics simplification,
- procurement savings,
- fewer indirect roles,
- engineering and administrative consolidation,
- stricter investment prioritization.

If a site is structurally underutilized, then a non-closure alternative must still remove:
- enough direct labor,
- enough indirect labor,
- enough footprint overhead,
- and enough future capex burden.

In practice, the hardest part is usually not wages. It is:
- **excess capacity**
- **duplicated functions**
- **weak product allocation**
- **too many sites competing for too few models**

[Expert: an independent industrial restructuring  — Round 3]
From an industrial restructuring and workforce-planning perspective, the exchange has now converged on the right technical question:

**Can a non-closure package remove enough fixed cost, capacity, and workforce expense to be economically equivalent to a closure-based option at each site?**

## What is clear
1. **The company likely needs structural adjustment**, not just temporary cost moderation.
2. **Non-closure measures can be material**, especially if they include:
   - shift elimination,
   - line consolidation,
   - product reallocation,

   - voluntary exits,
   - early retirement,
   - wage moderation,
   - and management/owner contributions.
3. **But non-closure measures are not automatically equivalent to closure economics.**

4. **The real issue is site-specific.**
   - One plant may be adjustable without closure.
   - Another may have too much fixed-cost burden, poor line fit, or insufficient volume to remain economic.

## What an economic equivalence test should include
For each site, you would normally compare:

- **capacity before and after**
- **unit cost before and after**
- **fixed-cost removal**
- **headcount reduction**
- **one-off restructuring cost**
- **implementation timing**
- **execution risk**
- **NPV over 3–5 years**
- **strategic value of the site** in future product allocation

A non-closure package is only close to equivalent if it achieves, in practice:

## Short answer
A non-closure package can be economically credible, but only if it removes enough **fixed cost**, **capacity**, and **structural labor cost** to materially narrow the gap versus closure. - **most of the fixed-cost reduction**, not just variable-cost savings

- removing at least the bulk of the site’s excess shift/line cost
- reducing headcount enough to match future volume

#### A. Closure is economically favored when:
- the site has persistent subscale utilization,
- fixed costs remain high even after downsizing,
- the product pipeline is weak,
- and the NPV of non-closure restructuring is materially worse than closure.

#### B. Downsizing/conversion is viable when:
- fixed costs can be cut meaningfully,
- the site can be resized to a lower demand base,
- and transferred volume or new product allocation can keep utilization in a workable range.

This is usually a good option when the site is not fundamentally obsolete, but too large for future demand.

#### C. A non-closure package is economically comparable to closure only if it achieves most of the following:
1. Removes most excess capacity
2. Cuts enough fixed cost to avoid “expensive emptiness”
3. Delivers a durable workforce reduction aligned with volume
4. Avoids large recurring subscale overhead
5. Has a 3–5 year NPV close to closure
6. Is executable without excessive labor or launch risk

If the site remains open but structurally underutilized, it is usually not equivalent to closure, even if headline labor cost is reduced.

- **the majority of fixed site cost**
- **a substantial share of indirect labor and support cost**
- **enough capacity to match credible demand**
- **and enough headcount to match the lower operating base**

If the site keeps:
- most buildings,
- most support functions,
- most maintenance burden,
- and too much unused capacity,

then the savings gap to closure is usually too large.

- working-time changes

- The multi-turn dialogue exposed a hidden split within the pro-restructuring camp: management/family/investors agree on the need for hard economics, but still differ on how much social conflict is tolerable and how much proof is required before invoking closure.
