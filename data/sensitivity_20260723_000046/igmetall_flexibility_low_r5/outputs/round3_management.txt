Let me be equally clear in round 3.

I welcome the fact that labour and the state finally admit the company needs a **real turnaround**. That is not in dispute. What remains in dispute is whether you are prepared to accept the scale of structural change that makes that turnaround real.

## Where I agree
I agree on three points:

1. **This cannot be cosmetic.**  
   A short-lived truce that leaves the cost base unchanged would be useless.

2. **We need an industrial future, not just cuts.**  
   Investment, product allocation, and technology funding to 2030 are essential.

3. **The burden cannot fall on employees alone.**  
   Owners, management, and labour all have to contribute.

Those are genuine points of convergence.

## Where I disagree — and strongly
### 1) “All plants remain open” is not a serious restructuring position
The works council and union keep repeating this as if declaring it makes the economics disappear. It does not.

If every plant is automatically protected, then you have already ruled out the main instrument that can remove a structural overcapacity problem at the scale we are facing. That is not a plan. It is a political wish.

The expert has now confirmed what management has said from the start:
- non-closure measures can be substantial,
- but they are **not automatically equivalent** to closure economics,
- and they often do **not** fully remove fixed cost.

So I will not accept “no closures” as a red line in advance. If you want to exclude closures, then you must prove — site by site — that the alternative package delivers comparable, durable savings.

Until then, closures remain a legitimate option.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be absolute if the cost base remains uncompetitive
I am not claiming forced layoffs are my preferred route. They are not. But I will not sign up to a structure that guarantees every current job regardless of whether the business can afford it.

If workforce reduction is needed to restore competitiveness, then the employee side must bring credible, enforceable alternatives that match the savings.

Voluntary exits, attrition, early retirement, redeployment, and working-time changes are all useful tools. They may not be enough. You cannot simply declare that they must be enough.

### 3) Binding guarantees through 2030 cannot freeze the problem
A blanket guarantee before the restructuring is designed would lock in the very rigidity we are trying to remove.

A guarantee that prevents structural adjustment is not a safeguard. It is a postponement device.

I am prepared to discuss conditional protections. I am not prepared to pre-commit to preserving an uncompetitive footprint.

### 4) Co-determination is not a veto over competitiveness
The state and labour keep speaking as if governance structure alone can decide the economics. It cannot.

Yes, co-determination is part of the framework. Yes, the state has a role. But neither can be used to block a necessary restructuring outcome simply because it is painful.

If the special statute is used to preserve every structure unchanged, then it becomes a mechanism for permanent decline.

## On the expert assessment
The expert has been helpful, but the key conclusion is being selectively ignored by labour:

> non-closure measures can be real, but they are often only partially equivalent, not fully equivalent, to closure-based savings.

That is exactly why I insist on a quantified comparison.

If the site-by-site analysis proves that non-closure measures can deliver the same durable savings, fine — I will consider that seriously.

But if the numbers show a residual structural gap, then you cannot demand that management absorb that gap forever while the company misses its return-on-sales target and starves future investment.
