I will be direct.

I agree with everyone on the **diagnosis**: this company needs a durable turnaround, the current German cost base is too high, and a cosmetic deal would be worse than no deal. I also agree that the workforce should not bear the full burden and that investment and product commitments are part of any credible package.

Where I do **not** agree is the attempt by labour and the state to make **“no closures, no compulsory redundancies, binding guarantees through 2030”** the starting framework. That is not a restructuring plan. It is a demand to preserve the current footprint regardless of whether it is economically sustainable.

## To the Works Council
You are right that trust matters and that closures are economically and socially destructive. But you have not yet shown that **all** German sites can remain open while still delivering the necessary structural savings.

You keep asserting that non-closure measures will suffice. That may be true in part; the expert has confirmed they can be substantial. But the expert also confirmed the key point: **non-closure measures are not automatically equivalent to closure economics**, especially if there is genuine fixed-cost overcapacity.

So I reject your attempt to exclude closures in advance. If the company has redundant capacity, then management must retain the ability to remove it. Otherwise you are asking owners to finance permanent inefficiency.

I also reject the idea that binding site and employment guarantees through 2030 should be granted up front. Guarantees are not a substitute for competitiveness. If the business cannot compete, no promise will save jobs in the long run.

## To the Trade Union
I agree that compulsory redundancies should be avoided if possible. They are not my preferred instrument. But I do **not** accept an absolute ban if the savings gap cannot otherwise be closed.

Your position assumes that voluntary exits, attrition, working-time changes, and temporary pay moderation will always be enough. The expert has made clear that this is not a safe assumption. If the numbers do not work, then the company cannot be forced to preserve every current job.

I do accept the idea of a **temporary, negotiated employee contribution**. But it must be material enough to matter, and it must be paired with measurable management, ownership, and investment commitments. Symbolic concessions will not restore margins.

## To the Regional State Government
I respect your role and I understand the regional stakes. But your blocking position cannot become a veto over economic reality. The special statute exists to protect the company and the region, not to freeze the business into a declining structure.

I do not accept any arrangement that weakens governance control or makes the company structurally uncompetitive in the name of preserving every site. That would destroy long-term value and, eventually, more jobs than it saves.

At the same time, I do agree with you on one point: a closure-led outcome must not be the first move. It must be the outcome of a **proven** comparison, not a reflex. That is why I support a transparent, site-by-site analysis.

## To Management
I agree with you that closures cannot be ruled out as an ideology. But I do **not** give you a blank cheque either.

You need to stop speaking in generalities and table the numbers:
- site-by-site utilization,
- direct and indirect cost,
- fixed-cost burden,
- product allocation,
- one-off restructuring costs,
- and savings durability over 3 to 5 years.

Only then can anyone judge whether non-closure measures are enough. Until then, closure talk is only pressure, not proof.

## To Institutional Investors
You are right that prolonged conflict destroys value. You are also right that governance must not block necessary restructuring. I agree with that.

But I will not support a financially blunt deal that buys short-term relief at the price of a broken industrial base. Value is not protected by preserving every site at any cost, but neither is it protected by provoking avoidable industrial war. The answer is a quantified, enforceable restructuring, not slogans from either side.

## My position
I support a negotiated restructuring **only if** it produces:
1. durable margin improvement,
2. real fixed-cost and capacity reduction,
3. material labour-cost contribution,
4. owner and management contribution,
5. and a credible investment/product plan.

I do **not** support:
- a blanket no-closures principle,
- a blanket no-redundancies principle,
- or binding guarantees that prevent structural adjustment before the economics are tested.

## What must happen next
We need a **fully quantified site-by-site restructuring package** comparing:
- closure,
- downsizing/conversion,
- and non-closure restructuring,

including:
- NPV of savings,
- one-off costs,
- capacity effects,
- headcount implications,
- and execution risk.

If that analysis is not already available in usable form, then we need it independently verified.

## Bottom line
I will not endorse a settlement that preserves decline.  
I will support a tough settlement that restores competitiveness.  
And I will not allow governance to be used to block the hard choices if the numbers show they are necessary.
