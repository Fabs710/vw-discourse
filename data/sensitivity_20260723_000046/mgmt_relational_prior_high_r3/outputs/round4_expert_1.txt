On the narrow economic question: **yes, non-closure measures can sometimes deliver savings that approach closure-level savings, but only under fairly demanding assumptions.** In many cases they can close a large share of the gap; in fewer cases they fully match the economics of closing a plant.

## Bottom line
A closure usually delivers savings because it removes:
- fixed labor and overhead,
- site running costs,
- depreciation/maintenance tied to the asset base,
- and often some production footprint redundancy.

A non-closure package can replicate much of that only if it is **broad, durable, and enforceable**. The closer you get to a plant closure, the more the package has to include:
- substantial headcount reduction,
- capacity consolidation,
- shift elimination,
- wage or allowance concessions,
- procurement/overhead cuts,
- and sometimes outsourcing or model reassignment.

If the non-closure package relies mainly on **temporary concessions**, it almost never matches closure economics over time.

## What typically drives the gap
Closure savings are often:
- **large in annual run-rate terms**
- but come with **significant one-time charges**:
  - severance
  - site remediation
  - transfer/relocation costs
  - contract termination
  - pension or impairment impacts
  - supply-chain reconfiguration

A non-closure package avoids some of those one-time costs, but it usually leaves more of the fixed cost base intact unless it includes hard structural changes.

## Realistic equivalence ranges
Very broadly, based on restructuring economics:

- **Light non-closure measures**  
  e.g. short-time work, wage freeze, voluntary exits only  
  → usually recover only **10–30%** of closure-type savings.

- **Moderate packages**  
  e.g. shift cuts, some headcount reduction, temporary wage/bonus concessions, tighter overhead  
  → often **30–60%** of closure economics.

- **Strong non-closure packages**  
  e.g. substantial workforce reduction, major capacity consolidation inside the footprint, permanent productivity measures, durable labor-cost relief  
  → can reach **60–90%** in some cases.

- **Full equivalence**  
  is possible only if the alternative effectively removes the same fixed-cost base as closure, which usually means the plant is already being functionally downsized to near-shutdown economics.

## Timing matters
A closure tends to produce savings faster once implemented, though the first year may be burdened by one-time costs. Non-closure alternatives usually:
- take longer to negotiate,
- take longer to implement,
- and often produce savings more gradually.

That matters because:
- if the company needs **immediate cash relief**, closure or deep footprint action tends to be stronger;
- if the company can accept a **1–3 year ramp**, a negotiated non-closure package can be economically credible.

## The key constraint: durability
The critical test is not just annual savings, but whether they are:
- **permanent or reversible**
- **contractually enforceable**
- **not dependent on goodwill**
- and **not expiring after 12–24 months**

Temporary wage cuts or deferred bonuses are useful for bridging liquidity, but they do not equal a permanent reduction in structural cost.

## What would make a non-closure package credible
For a German auto plant, a non-closure alternative can be credible if it includes some combination of:

1. **Material headcount reduction**
   - through voluntary exits, retirement, redeployment
   - usually needs to be large enough to remove a meaningful share of fixed labor cost

2. **Shift and capacity reduction**
   - fewer shifts
   - lower utilization of the plant
   - model transfer to other sites
   - removal of duplicate functions

3. **Labor-cost contribution**
   - temporary wage moderation
   - reduction of bonuses/allowances
   - working-time flexibility
   - but ideally paired with structural changes

4. **Overhead and indirect cost reduction**
   - engineering, admin, logistics, and support functions
   - shared-services rationalization

5. **Procurement and productivity gains**
   - lower conversion cost
   - better line efficiency
   - scrap reduction
   - higher throughput per worker

## What usually cannot match closure
A package usually falls short if it depends mainly on:
- a time-limited wage concession,
- voluntary attrition alone,
- a hiring freeze,
- or general “efficiency improvements” without hard headcount/capacity changes.

Those can help, but they rarely replicate the fixed-cost removal of closure.

## Practical judgment for this scenario
Given the facts you provided — structural overcapacity, high German cost base, competitive pressure, and a need for durable margin repair — my judgment is:

- **A non-closure package can plausibly deliver a large share of closure-equivalent savings.**
- **It may be sufficient if the closure alternative is only modestly better and if the company can tolerate slower implementation.**
- **It is unlikely to fully match closure economics unless it includes real capacity reduction and material headcount cuts.**

So the real comparison is not “closure vs. no change.” It is:
- **closure**
versus
- **a deep, enforceable restructuring without closure**

And the latter can be economically serious — but only if it is close to a true footprint reduction, not a temporary labor concession.
