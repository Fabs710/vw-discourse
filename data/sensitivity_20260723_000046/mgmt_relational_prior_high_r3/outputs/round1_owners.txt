I’ll be direct.

I agree with one central point from management: **the current cost and capacity structure cannot simply be preserved as-is**. If the business remains structurally uncompetitive, that damages the company, the workforce, and the owners alike. As controlling shareholder, I cannot support a solution that merely postpones the adjustment and erodes long-term value.

But I also agree with the employee side and the state on an equally important point: **a destructive closure-first strategy is not automatically the right answer**. Plant closures, mass compulsory redundancies, and a prolonged industrial conflict would be value-destructive, reputationally damaging, and inconsistent with stewardship of this enterprise.

So my position is this:

- I support a **credible restructuring that restores margins and competitiveness**
- I do **not** support a solution that permanently weakens the enterprise through instability
- I am **not** prepared to endorse a deal that protects every legacy guarantee while leaving the economics untouched
- I am also **not** prepared to back a management approach that treats closures as the first and only serious lever

Where I differ from the Works Council and Union is on absolutes. Saying **“no closures, no forced layoffs, no site changes under any circumstances”** is not a viable governance position if the numbers do not work. If the company truly has structural overcapacity, then some capacity reduction has to happen. The question is whether it is achieved through a hard closure path or through a negotiated package that produces comparable savings with less disruption.

Where I differ from management is on method and sequencing. If you come in with closures as the default and only later ask whether there is another route, you are increasing the risk of a breakdown that the company may not be able to afford. The burden of proof is on management to show that the proposed cuts are necessary, proportionate, and that no less destructive alternative can deliver the same economic effect.

What would move me:

1. **A durable competitiveness plan**
   - not a short-term wage concession
   - but a structure that genuinely improves margins and productivity

2. **A negotiated labour contribution**
   - yes, employees will likely have to contribute
   - but that contribution must be part of a balanced package, not a one-sided extraction

3. **Real capacity correction**
   - I will not support a plan that leaves excess capacity untouched
   - but I am open to whether that is done via closure, consolidation, working-time redesign, or other binding measures

4. **Protection of long-term industrial capability in Germany**
   - not every site can be sacred
   - but we should not sacrifice strategic capability for a short-lived accounting fix

5. **Governance-respecting implementation**
   - no attempt to bypass co-determination
   - no attempt to sideline the state’s blocking position
   - no public confrontation that weakens trust before a settlement is even possible

So my message to management is: bring a plan that proves the savings are real, durable, and sufficient without creating avoidable instability.  
My message to labour is: if you want to avoid closures, then you need to help deliver a package that is economically equivalent, not just politically preferable.  
My message to the state is: I support preserving the company’s regional and industrial base, but not at the price of locking in uncompetitive structures.

In short: **I will back tough restructuring, but only if it is a serious long-term solution and not an unstable compromise or a destructive confrontation.**
