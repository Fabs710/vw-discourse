I appreciate that both of you have at least made clear you are willing to talk, but let me be equally direct: **the current cost and capacity base is not sustainable**. If we leave German operations structurally as they are, we will continue to miss the return-on-sales target, bleed margin, and starve the investment budget needed for the next product cycle and the technology path to 2030.

So I agree with one point: **this cannot be a purely top-down dictate**. We need an agreement. But I do **not** agree with the premise that every plant must remain open, regardless of economics, or that the company can be made competitive through only temporary measures and accounting adjustments. That would be a cosmetic solution, and I will not support it.

Let me be precise:

- **Plant closures are on the table.**
- **Capacity reductions are on the table.**
- **A meaningful labour-cost contribution is on the table.**
- **Forced redundancies are not our preferred route, but we will not rule out all workforce reductions if we cannot get a durable settlement.**

The core issue is simple: German factory costs are above target, and the market is shrinking. A deal that only preserves every site and every guarantee while asking the company to absorb the full adjustment is not acceptable. It would lock in an uncompetitive structure and undermine the future of the brand.

At the same time, I am not dismissing alternative measures outright. If you can bring forward a package that delivers **comparable structural savings** — not just deferred pain — then I am prepared to consider it. That would need to include, at minimum:

1. **A real and durable labour-cost contribution**
   - not symbolic,
   - not temporary,
   - not easily reversible.

2. **A binding reduction in capacity**
   - whether through closures, consolidation, or other measures that genuinely remove excess supply.

3. **A credible reduction in headcount**
   - through any mix of attrition, transfers, early retirement, or other tools,
   - but with a scale sufficient to change the economics.

4. **Revised site guarantees**
   - if there are guarantees, they must be conditional on a meaningful cost package,
   - not an open-ended promise that prevents restructuring.

5. **A commitment to future investment and productivity improvement**
   - because savings are not the goal in themselves,
   - they are the precondition for funding the next generation of products and technology.

What I will not accept is a “shared sacrifice” formula that shifts the burden almost entirely into deferred wage concessions, working-time tweaks, and qualification programs while leaving overcapacity untouched. That does not solve the structural problem.

I also want to address the union’s point about management and owners contributing. I understand that sentiment, and yes, everyone must carry responsibility. But let’s be honest about where the immediate structural gap sits: **the German production footprint and labour-cost base**. That is where the adjustment has to happen first and most materially. Without that, no amount of rhetoric about fairness will restore competitiveness.

So here is the practical position from management:

- If you want to avoid closures, bring a package that **actually replaces the savings and capacity removal closures would deliver**.
- If you want to protect sites, then show how the necessary cost reduction and productivity gain will be achieved elsewhere, with enforceable commitments.
- If not, we will continue with our restructuring proposal, including the possibility of plant closure and deeper workforce reduction.

I will be frank: if the response is simply “no closures, no layoffs, no real cost contribution,” then there is no serious path to a settlement. That would leave us with a cost base that the company cannot carry.

We are prepared to negotiate, but the negotiation has to start from the reality of the crisis, not from a demand to preserve the status quo.
