From the perspective of dispersed capital-market shareholders, the central question is not whether the restructuring is painful. It clearly is. The question is whether it restores a credible path to **competitive margins, disciplined cost reduction, and investable cash flow**.

On that basis, I agree with management on the diagnosis: **the current German cost and capacity base cannot simply be preserved**. If the company keeps missing its return-on-sales target, the equity story deteriorates further, and all stakeholders end up worse off. We have seen enough “waiting for the market to improve” approaches in European autos to know they do not create value.

That said, I do **not** support a closure strategy if it is only a blunt instrument or if it triggers a prolonged, value-destructive conflict without delivering clear and durable savings. A cosmetic settlement, or one that preserves all legacy structures while deferring the real adjustment, would be unacceptable.

So our position is:

- **Yes** to deep, durable structural cost reduction
- **Yes** to meaningful capacity reduction
- **Yes** to labor-cost contributions if they are real and lasting
- **No** to a purely symbolic compromise
- **No** to a prolonged strike that destroys margins and confidence
- **No** to a solution that leaves the cost base uncompetitive

To the Works Council and the Union: I understand the concern about jobs and closures, but your current red lines are too absolute to be credible if the economics require adjustment. “No closures, no forced layoffs, no site changes” may be politically understandable, but from an investor standpoint it is only acceptable if you can show **equivalent savings and capacity removal** through other binding measures. So far, you have not shown that. Working-time tweaks, redeployment, and qualification are useful tools, but they are not a substitute for structural correction unless they produce the same financial effect.

To the regional state government: we share the concern about preserving industrial capability, but the state cannot support a structure that protects employment today while leaving the company structurally uncompetitive tomorrow. A regional employment strategy is not sustainable if it comes at the expense of the company’s competitiveness and capital allocation.

To the controlling family: we appreciate the emphasis on stewardship and long-term value. But stewardship means more than avoiding public conflict. It means backing a solution that actually resets the cost base. If management’s plan is necessary to restore margin, then it should be supported decisively and implemented quickly. Uncertainty is expensive.

Where I stand clearly:
- I would support a package that delivers **durable savings, credible capacity correction, and enforceable labor-cost relief**.
- I would not support a deal that buys peace at the expense of margin.
- I would not support an indefinite negotiation that leaves investors financing delay.

If there is an alternative to closures, it must be **measurable, binding, and financially equivalent**. Otherwise, it is not an alternative — it is delay.

So my message is simple: produce a restructuring that is economically real, or expect capital-market patience to run out.
