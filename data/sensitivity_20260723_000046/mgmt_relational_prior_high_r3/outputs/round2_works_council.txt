I will respond very clearly:

### Our position has not changed
- **No plant closures in Germany**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No dismantling of the job-security framework**
- **No bypassing of co-determination**

Those are not bargaining chips to us. They are the minimum basis for any serious discussion.

### Where I agree
I agree with all sides on one point: the company needs a **durable competitiveness solution**, not a temporary patch. I also accept that a restructuring package must be economically credible and must involve contributions from more than just the workforce.

I am prepared to discuss:
- working-time adjustments
- redeployment
- qualification and training
- natural attrition
- early retirement
- temporary, negotiated cost contributions
- capacity optimisation **within existing sites**

### Where I disagree
I reject the idea that **plant closures are necessary to be credible**. That is not a fact; it is a management choice.

I also reject the claim that the only way to restore competitiveness is to make workers pay through:
- closures
- forced layoffs
- and the breaking of guarantees

That would be the easy answer for management, but it would be socially reckless and strategically short-sighted. It would destroy trust, trigger resistance, and weaken the company’s industrial base.

### To management
You keep saying the current cost base is unsustainable. Fine. Then show us a plan that is **equally effective without destroying jobs and sites**.

If you want savings, then bring us something concrete:
- a binding productivity program
- a fair contribution from top management and owners
- investment commitments for the German plants
- product allocation that secures utilization
- and a restructuring that relies on **non-forced** measures

What you have not done is prove that closures are unavoidable. You have asserted it. That is not enough.

### To the trade union
I support your resistance to closure and forced layoffs. But I want to be equally clear: any “shared sacrifice” must not become a back door for dismantling worker protections. We can discuss contributions, yes — but only if:
- the burden is genuinely shared,
- all German sites remain open,
- and no compulsory redundancies are introduced.

### To the state government and controlling family
If you want a stable settlement, then you must use your influence to stop a closure-first strategy. The state’s blocking position and the family’s governance power should not be used to pressure the workforce into accepting threats as a negotiating method.

### Minimum acceptable outcome
For the works council, the minimum acceptable outcome is:

1. **Every German plant remains open**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Binding site and employment guarantees through 2030**
4. **A real employee role in shaping the restructuring**
5. **A fair package of alternative measures that reduces costs without destroying jobs**

### One concession we can consider
If management drops closures and forced layoffs, we can discuss:
- a **temporary and clearly limited employee contribution**
- plus flexible working-time arrangements
- plus redeployment and qualification
- plus productivity measures at plant level

But that concession is only on the table if it is tied to **hard guarantees**, not promises. No guarantees, no deal.

### Bottom line
If management comes back with a package that still contains closures or compulsory redundancies, then there is no basis for agreement. We will oppose it in the supervisory board, in the plants, and publicly.

If management wants a real settlement, then it must stop treating the workforce as the adjustment variable of first resort.
