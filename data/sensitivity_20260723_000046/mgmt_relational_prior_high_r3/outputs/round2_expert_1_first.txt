Yes—**non-closure measures can sometimes deliver savings comparable to closure-related savings, but only under fairly strict conditions**. In many cases they can match a meaningful share of closure savings; in fewer cases they can fully replicate them.

## Bottom-line assessment

For a large German auto plant or footprint, **plant closure savings are usually broader and more durable** than any single non-closure measure because they remove:
- fixed overhead
- maintenance and utilities
- indirect labor
- logistics complexity
- depreciation on underused assets
- and, most importantly, excess capacity permanently

By contrast, non-closure measures mostly reduce **unit cost** or **cash cost** while leaving much of the fixed footprint in place.

So the key question is not “can alternatives save money?”—they can.  
It is: **can they save enough, fast enough, and durably enough to equal the economic effect of closure?**

## Short answer

- **If the savings target is modest to medium**: yes, non-closure measures can often match it.
- **If the savings target is equivalent to shutting one whole site or a large share of a site**: only a broad, tightly executed package has a realistic chance.
- **If the target includes permanent elimination of excess capacity plus overhead**: alternatives rarely fully match closure unless they include some form of deep consolidation, not just labor concessions.

## What closure typically saves

A closure of a German automotive plant can save, at a rough order of magnitude:
1. **Direct labor** tied to that site
2. **Indirect labor and site overhead**
3. **Maintenance, energy, security, facilities, IT, local logistics**
4. **Some capex and sustaining investment**
5. **Complexity costs** from managing an extra footprint

For a large plant, that can easily amount to **hundreds of millions of euros per year** in fully loaded annual savings, depending on output, staffing, and how much production is genuinely removed versus transferred elsewhere.

However, closure also often creates **one-time costs**:
- severance
- pension bridging
- transfer costs
- write-downs
- tooling relocation
- supplier disruption
- ramp-up costs elsewhere

So closure is usually best understood as:
- **high one-time cost**
- **high recurring savings**
- **strong structural effect**

## What non-closure measures can realistically deliver

### 1) Workforce reduction without closure
This is often the biggest lever.

Possible mechanisms:
- voluntary exits
- early retirement
- natural attrition
- hiring freezes
- internal redeployment
- retirement bridging
- temporary hours reduction

**Savings potential:** material, but slower than closure.  
If done deeply enough, it can remove a large share of labor cost. But unless headcount reduction is large and permanent, it does not remove the full fixed-cost burden of the site.

**Typical limitation:** it reduces cost but not footprint.

### 2) Reduced shifts / output rationalization
Cutting a shift, reducing overtime, or lowering line speed can save:
- direct labor
- energy
- variable support costs
- some maintenance

But it can also **raise unit fixed cost** if the plant remains open with lower utilization.

So this only works economically if:
- the market no longer needs the capacity, and
- the remaining volume is concentrated into fewer plants efficiently

In other words, this is often a **partial substitute for closure**, not a full substitute.

### 3) Wage moderation / pay reductions / bonus restraint
This can deliver fast, visible labor-cost relief.

**Economically useful if:**
- it is broad-based
- durable for several years
- and large enough to change the cost base materially

**But** in most industrial settings, wage moderation alone is usually insufficient to replicate closure savings unless the wage bill is very large relative to total site cost.

A useful rule of thumb:
- If the relevant gap is mostly **labor cost**, wage measures can matter a lot.
- If the gap is mostly **capacity duplication and overhead**, wage measures alone will not close it.

### 4) Capex deferral
Useful for cash preservation, but **not equivalent to structural savings** unless it reflects genuine excess capacity elimination.

Deferring capex can help for 1–3 years, but it is usually:
- temporary
- sometimes value-destructive
- and often just pushes the cost into the future

It is **not a substitute** for closure-level savings if the business truly has structural overcapacity.

### 5) Outsourcing / make-or-buy changes
This can reduce fixed cost and convert some overhead into variable cost.

Potentially helpful where:
- non-core functions exist
- supplier scale is better
- processes can be standardized

But in automotive manufacturing, outsourcing often brings:
- transition risk
- quality risk
- logistics cost
- and sometimes weaker savings than expected once total landed cost is counted

Good for selective functions, not usually enough alone for a major footprint problem.

### 6) Productivity improvements / lean programs
These can be meaningful, but the economics depend on whether the plant is:
- underperforming due to process inefficiency, or
- simply carrying too much capacity for the demand base

If it’s the former, productivity gains can be large.  
If it’s the latter, productivity programs help but do not eliminate the structural surplus.

A mature auto plant can often wring out some percentage points of labor productivity, but not usually enough to offset an entire site’s fixed-cost burden.

## When non-closure measures can match closure economics

They can come close if all of the following are true:

1. **The required savings are mostly labor-related**, not footprint-related.
2. **There is substantial voluntary attrition potential**.
3. **Utilization can be lifted meaningfully** across remaining sites.
4. **The company can remove enough overhead and indirect labor** without keeping the old structure alive.
5. **Employee concessions are durable**, not just one-off.
6. **Management can implement quickly** and enforce the package.

In practice, that usually means a package combining:
- headcount reduction
- shift/volume reallocation
- wage moderation
- productivity gains
- selective outsourcing
- capex prioritization
- and some consolidation of functions

That is a **real restructuring**, not a soft alternative.

## Where non-closure measures usually fall short

They usually fall short when the closure case is driven by:
- persistent overcapacity
- duplicated fixed overhead
- a plant that is structurally subscale
- excess logistics complexity
- or a site with poor asset utilization that cannot be filled

In that case, you can reduce labor cost, but the facility still remains:
- too expensive per unit
- underutilized
- and structurally burdened by fixed cost

Then the alternative measures often become **delay plus partial savings**, not a full substitute.

## Timing constraints

This is crucial.

### Closure
- One-time pain
- recurrent savings after implementation
- strongest medium-term effect

### Non-closure package
- Often slower to implement
- savings may be phased in over 1–3 years
- requires recurring negotiation and compliance
- may leak if output falls or if utilization assumptions prove wrong

So even if the total present value of savings is similar on paper, **timing matters**.  
If the company needs immediate competitiveness improvement, closure tends to be more decisive.

## What would be needed to test equivalence

To determine whether alternatives are truly equivalent, you would need:

1. **Site-level cost data**
   - direct labor
   - indirect labor
   - facilities
   - depreciation
   - logistics
   - energy
   - maintenance

2. **Capacity and utilization by site**
   - current output
   - forecast volume
   - transferability of models
   - ramp-up constraints elsewhere

3. **One-time closure costs**
   - severance
   - pension
   - relocation
   - write-downs
   - tool transfer
   - disruption costs

4. **Detailed alternative-package savings**
   - FTE reductions
   - wage concessions
   - shift changes
   - outsourcing savings
   - capex deferrals
   - productivity gains

5. **Discounted cash flow comparison**
   - not just annual headline savings
   - but NPV over 5–10 years

Without that, no one can honestly say whether the alternatives are truly equivalent.

## Bounded judgment for a German auto plant context

Given the scenario you described, my judgment would be:

- **A well-designed alternative package could plausibly recover a large portion of closure savings**
- **It might reach equivalence if the site is not deeply overcapacity and if labor concessions are large and durable**
- **But if management genuinely needs structural capacity removal, then non-closure measures are unlikely to fully replicate closure economics unless they include very deep consolidation and headcount reduction**

A practical range:

- **Partial replacement**: very realistic
- **Near-equivalence**: possible but only with broad, hard measures
- **Full equivalence to closure**: difficult, and often unlikely without some actual footprint reduction

## Final conclusion

So the answer is:

**Yes, non-closure measures can sometimes be economically equivalent to closure-related savings, but only when the restructuring package is broad, durable, and large enough to eliminate a substantial share of the same fixed and labor costs that closure would remove.**

If the company’s problem is mainly **labor cost and some excess shifts**, alternatives may be enough.  
If the problem is **structural overcapacity and an underutilized site**, alternatives usually fall short unless they include real consolidation and deep headcount reduction.
