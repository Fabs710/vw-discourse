I’ll respond directly and plainly.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on one point: **the company needs a durable solution, not a cosmetic delay**.

I also agree that:
- a deal has to be **negotiated**, not simply imposed;
- **co-determination** must be respected;
- and future investment in Germany only makes sense if the restructuring is economically credible.

So yes: this cannot be a short-term patch.

## Where I disagree
What I do **not** accept is the idea that we can restore competitiveness while preserving every site, every guarantee, and every legacy cost structure.

That is the core disagreement.

### To the Works Council
You say:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no dismantling of guarantees,
- every German plant must remain open.

I need to be blunt: **that is not a viable starting point if the numbers do not work**.

I respect your role, but you have not answered the central economic question: **how do we remove enough structural cost and capacity to restore margins by 2030 without closing or consolidating anything?**

Working-time changes, redeployment, qualification, and attrition are useful tools. I am not dismissing them. But they are **not enough by themselves** if they do not produce the same durable savings as structural footprint adjustment.

If your answer is simply “find the savings elsewhere,” then I say clearly: **show me the equivalent savings in binding, enforceable terms**. Otherwise, it is not an alternative; it is delay.

### To the Union
I hear your call for shared sacrifice. On principle, I agree that **employees should not carry the burden alone**. Management, owners, and the wider governance structure all have responsibilities.

But I do **not** accept your red line that the collective wage framework must remain untouched no matter what. If labour costs remain above target, then a serious contribution from the workforce is part of the solution.

And let me be equally clear: a **temporary** contribution is not enough unless it is substantial enough to change the economics. I am not interested in symbolic gestures or one-off concessions that leave the structural cost base unchanged.

### To the State Government
I appreciate your emphasis on regional stability. But we cannot confuse **regional protection** with **industrial viability**.

If the company is left with an uncompetitive footprint, then no amount of political protection will save jobs in the medium term. Preserving every site today while weakening the business tomorrow is not responsible stewardship.

So yes, I welcome your support for a negotiated solution — but not if it becomes a veto on real structural change.

### To the Controlling Family
I agree with you on the need for long-term value creation and I also agree that a reckless closure-first approach could be destructive.

But let’s not pretend that the main issue is communication style. The main issue is substance:
- we have structural overcapacity,
- German factory costs are too high,
- and the current setup misses the brand’s return target.

If we do not materially reduce cost and capacity, we will continue destroying value through inaction.

### To Investors
Your point is correct: patience is not infinite, and delay is expensive.

But I would also say this: yes, the solution must be economically real, and yes, it must be durable. That is exactly why I am not willing to sign up to a weak compromise that preserves the old structure and merely postpones the pain.

## What I am prepared to consider
I am still open on **how** the restructuring is achieved.

I am prepared to discuss a package that avoids some closures **if and only if** it delivers **comparable, enforceable, and durable savings**. That would need to include all of the following:

1. **A meaningful labour-cost contribution**
   - real, not symbolic
   - durable, not short-lived
   - large enough to materially change competitiveness

2. **A binding reduction in capacity**
   - through closures, consolidation, or other hard measures
   - but not a package that leaves overcapacity intact

3. **A credible headcount reduction**
   - through voluntary exits, early retirement, transfers, and other instruments where possible
   - but of a scale that actually restores the cost base

4. **Revised site and job guarantees**
   - only if they are conditional on the restructuring package
   - not blanket guarantees that freeze the company in place

5. **Clear investment commitments**
   - if employees contribute, then the company must also commit to future product allocation and technology investment
   - but those commitments must be affordable because the restructuring has created room for them

## What I will not accept
Let me be explicit. I will not accept:
- a deal that rules out closures in advance,
- a deal that rules out meaningful workforce reduction,
- a deal that leaves the German cost base structurally uncompetitive,
- or a deal that buys peace by delaying the necessary adjustment.

That would be a **cosmetic compromise**, and it would fail the company.

## On industrial action
If this turns into a prolonged strike, that is harmful for everyone — yes. But the responsibility for avoiding escalation is not mine alone. If the employee side insists on absolute red lines that make economic correction impossible, then the conflict is being prolonged by choice.

## Bottom line
Here is my position in one sentence:

**I am prepared to negotiate a tough restructuring package, but only one that delivers durable, material cost and capacity reduction sufficient to restore competitiveness by 2030.**

If you want to avoid closures, then bring me a package that is **economically equivalent**.  
If you cannot do that, then closures and deeper workforce reductions remain on the table.

That is the reality we have to deal with.
