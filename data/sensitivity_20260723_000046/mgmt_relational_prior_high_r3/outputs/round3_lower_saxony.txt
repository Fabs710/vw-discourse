I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family, investors, and the expert on one point: **this cannot be solved with slogans or half-measures**. We need a package that is **durable, quantified, and enforceable**.

I also agree that the company cannot ignore structural overcapacity or an uncompetitive cost base. If the German footprint is genuinely too expensive, then the answer cannot simply be “wait and hope.”

And I agree with labour that **workers must not carry the entire burden**. A settlement that shifts all adjustment onto jobs and sites would be unacceptable politically, socially, and economically.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept management’s repeated implication that plant closures and deeper workforce reductions are the default or inevitable answer.

That is exactly the point where I draw the line:  
**no outcome that guts regional employment or sidelines the state’s co-determination and veto role.**

So when management says, “If you can’t match closure economics, closures stay on the table,” my response is: then prove that closing sites is truly the least destructive viable option, not merely the easiest one.

The expert has been clear that **non-closure measures can be substantial** and, in some cases, approach closure-level savings. That means closures are **not automatically necessary**. They are only justified if management can show, site by site, that alternatives truly fall short.

## To management
You have not yet shown me a credible, site-specific case that closing German plants is the only responsible option.

What I need from you is not another general statement about competitiveness, but:

- the savings gap by site,
- the annualized effect of closure,
- the NPV of non-closure alternatives,
- the one-time costs of each path,
- and the implementation risks.

Until that is on the table, closure is not a proven necessity. It is a pressure instrument.

And I want to be equally clear: I will not support using closure threats to force the workforce into accepting a weaker deal.

## To the Works Council and Union
I support your insistence that German plants are not disposable. I also support the principle that **forced layoffs are not the first answer**.

But I need to be frank: a simple “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no changes” line is not enough unless it is paired with a package that can actually carry the economics.

That means:
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- early retirement,
- voluntary exits,
- productivity improvements,
- and a **real** employee contribution if it protects sites and jobs.

I am open to that. I am **not** open to a position that refuses any burden-sharing while expecting the state and shareholders to absorb the cost.

## To the controlling family
You are right to warn against a value-destructive confrontation. I agree.

But I also expect you to use your governance influence to prevent management from forcing a closure-first logic that would permanently damage trust in the company’s social partnership. This is not just a labour-relations issue; it is a long-term industrial strategy issue.

If the family wants sustainable value, then it should support a settlement that preserves viable sites, keeps the company rooted in Germany, and respects co-determination as a strength rather than an obstacle.

## To investors
You are right to insist on a quantified comparison. I support that.

But I reject the assumption that closures are the benchmark and everything else is inferior by definition. The benchmark is **restored competitiveness without destroying the company’s regional base**.

If a negotiated package can deliver comparable savings through a mix of:
- workforce adjustments,
- cost moderation,
- capacity optimization,
- and investment discipline,

then that is not softness. That is smart restructuring.

## My minimum acceptable outcome
My minimum acceptable outcome is unchanged:

1. **No gutting of regional employment**
2. **No removal of the state’s co-determination and veto role**
3. **A negotiated restructuring, not a imposed closure plan**
4. **A serious burden-sharing package involving management, owners, and labour**
5. **A durable industrial future for the company in Germany**

## One concession I can consider
If management comes forward with a **binding, quantified alternative** that credibly matches the necessary savings **without plant closures**, I am prepared to consider:

- a temporary and clearly limited state-supported flexibility on timing,
- support for a negotiated transformation package,
- and conditional backing for labor-side contributions.

But that only works if:
- jobs are protected,
- plants are preserved wherever viable,
- and the state’s role is fully respected.

## What I am not prepared to accept
I will not support:
- a closure-led restructuring that guts employment,
- a plan that weakens or bypasses co-determination,
- a deal that treats the state’s role as decorative,
- or a settlement where workers pay while others simply watch.

I am open to restructuring.  
I am not open to regional cut-and-run restructuring.

If management can prove a non-closure package that truly works, I will engage seriously.
