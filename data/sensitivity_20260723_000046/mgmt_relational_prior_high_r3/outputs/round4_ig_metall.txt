I’ll respond directly and without softening the core issue.

## Where I agree
I agree with the Works Council, the expert, and parts of what the state and even management are saying:

- **This has to be decided on numbers, not rhetoric.**
- **Non-closure measures can be substantial** if they are broad, durable, and enforceable.
- **Temporary gestures are not enough** if the company’s problem is structural.
- **A cosmetic compromise is worthless.**
- **Workers must not carry the entire burden.**

So yes: the standard is a hard one. If management wants to argue that closures are necessary, it must prove that with a **plant-by-plant NPV comparison**.

## Where I disagree
### To management
You are still trying to turn “competitiveness” into a blank check for closures and headcount cuts.

I do **not** accept that.

The expert has now confirmed what we said from the start: **a non-closure package can deliver meaningful savings and can sometimes get close to closure economics.** That means closures are not automatically necessary. They are only justified if management can show, with actual site data, that alternatives do not close the gap.

You have not done that yet.

So I reject your framing that the choice is:
- closures and forced reductions, or
- decline.

That is not proven. It is a management preference until the numbers say otherwise.

### To investors
You are right to demand durability and economic equivalence. I agree with that standard.

But you are still too willing to treat closure as the benchmark and everything else as a lesser option. That is not acceptable to us.

If a negotiated package can deliver the required savings **without closing plants and without forcing people out**, then that is not a second-rate outcome. It is the better solution.

### To the controlling family
You say you do not want a value-destructive conflict. Good. Then use your influence to stop management from making closure threats the default bargaining instrument.

What I cannot accept is the idea that our red lines are somehow the problem. They are not. The problem is structural overcapacity, management’s failure to prove its case, and the attempt to make workers absorb the main pain.

### To the state government
Your blocking role matters. Use it to prevent a closure-led settlement from being pushed through as if it were the only realistic path.

If the state starts speaking as though plant closures are just a normal adjustment tool, that weakens the very protection the governance system is supposed to provide.

### To the Works Council
I agree with your line that:
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no bypassing of co-determination.

I also agree that we need a real alternative package, not just slogans.

But I want to be clear on one thing: **any employee contribution must remain temporary, bounded, and conditional.** It cannot become a permanent erosion of the collective wage framework.

## What we are prepared to discuss
We are still prepared to discuss a real negotiated package, including:

- working-time flexibility,
- internal redeployment,
- qualification and retraining,
- voluntary exits,
- early retirement,
- productivity measures,
- procurement and overhead discipline,
- capacity adjustments **within the existing German footprint**,
- and a temporary, fair employee contribution.

That is the scope of a serious deal.

## What we are not prepared to accept
We will not accept:
- plant closures,
- compulsory redundancies,
- permanent wage cuts,
- open-ended insecurity,
- or a restructuring that shifts the burden primarily onto the workforce.

That remains our line.

## On the numbers
The expert’s contribution strengthens the case for exactly what we have been demanding: a **site-by-site, quantified bridge**.

So let management put these figures on the table:
1. current cost base by plant
2. closure-case annualized savings
3. one-time closure costs
4. non-closure package savings
5. one-time costs of the alternative
6. NPV comparison
7. implementation risk and reversibility

Until that exists, no one should pretend closure is proven.

## My minimum acceptable outcome
I will restate it plainly:

- **Every German plant remains open**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Binding job and site security through 2030**
- **Preservation of the collective bargaining framework**
- **A fair, temporary employee contribution only if jobs and sites are protected**
- **A negotiated restructuring based on non-forced measures**

## Bottom line
We are not here to make management’s life easier by accepting closures as the starting assumption.

If a non-closure package can genuinely match the economics, then management should choose that route and stop threatening the workforce.

If it cannot, then management must prove that with hard data — not pressure, not brinkmanship, and not appeals to inevitability.

Until then, our position remains unchanged: **no closures, no forced layoffs, fair burden-sharing, and a durable future for the German sites.**
