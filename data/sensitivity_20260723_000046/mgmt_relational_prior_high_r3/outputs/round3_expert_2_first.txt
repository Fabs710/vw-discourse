## Bottom line
A credible closure-vs.-non-closure comparison cannot be answered by slogans like “closures are necessary” or “no closures are possible.” It depends on whether the company can achieve the **same durable annual cash savings** through a non-closure package after adjusting for:

- direct labor cost
- fixed manufacturing overhead absorption
- underutilized capacity costs
- logistics and production transfer costs
- one-time restructuring charges
- asset write-downs / impairment
- launch and quality risk
- and the durability of labor concessions

In many automotive cases, **non-closure packages can close a large share of the gap**, but **they often fail to fully replicate the economics of a real footprint reduction** if the underlying problem is persistent overcapacity at specific sites. That does **not** mean closures are automatically value-maximizing; it means the burden is on management to show the numbers.

## What actually matters economically
The key question is not “closure or no closure,” but:

> What is the annualized savings from each site option, net of one-time costs, over a realistic planning horizon?

For each plant, the comparison should be built from actual data:

1. **Current output and capacity utilization**
   - units produced
   - hours run
   - shift pattern
   - line bottlenecks
   - direct labor hours per unit

2. **Site-specific cost structure**
   - direct wages and benefits
   - energy
   - maintenance
   - logistics
   - local overhead
   - depreciation
   - supervisory/indirect labor
   - quality and warranty cost attributable to the site

3. **Model allocation**
   - which models are assigned to the plant
   - contribution margin by model
   - transfer pricing assumptions
   - whether a plant is carrying low-margin or legacy products

4. **One-time restructuring costs**
   - severance
   - transfer tooling
   - decommissioning
   - supplier requalification
   - relocation of inventories
   - environmental and site remediation
   - contract termination costs

5. **Durability of savings**
   - temporary wage concessions expire
   - working-time measures can reverse
   - closures remove fixed cost permanently

## On the specific debate
### 1) Are non-closure measures economically meaningful?
Yes. They can be very meaningful if they are broad enough:
- wage moderation or deferred compensation
- working-time reductions
- productivity improvement
- headcount attrition
- redeployment
- procurement and overhead reductions
- model reallocation to better-utilized sites

These can produce substantial recurring savings, especially if the plant network has obvious slack and high fixed overhead.

### 2) Can non-closure measures equal closure economics?
Sometimes, but not always.

They are most likely to match closure economics when:
- the “savings” from closure are mostly labor-related rather than footprint-related
- there is enough voluntary attrition to reduce headcount without severance-heavy forced layoffs
- capacity can be consolidated without major logistics penalties
- the plant being retained can be loaded to a materially higher utilization level
- labor concessions are multi-year and enforceable

They are less likely to match closure economics when:
- a plant is structurally underutilized
- the fixed cost base is high and inflexible
- there are duplicate functions across sites
- the retained plant needs major reinvestment to stay viable
- concessions are temporary but the overcapacity problem is permanent

### 3) Are closures always superior?
No. Closures can look attractive on paper but still destroy value if:
- one-time severance and transfer costs are very large
- production has to be moved in a way that disrupts launch quality
- supplier network costs rise
- the company loses flexibility for future demand shifts
- the remaining network becomes too concentrated or too brittle

So the existence of savings from closure does not prove closure is the best option.

## What would change the conclusion
I would need site-by-site numbers to decide whether a closure package is truly superior. The decisive evidence would be:

- actual plant profit contribution by site
- volume forecast by platform/model
- fixed cost that disappears only if the plant closes
- realistic labor savings from a negotiated package
- restructuring cost estimates
- sensitivity cases for demand, FX, mix, and utilization

If a retained-plant package can deliver, say, **80–90% of the annualized closure savings** with materially lower one-time cost, lower disruption, and enough durability, then the financial case for closure weakens substantially.

If, however, the non-closure package only produces **transient savings** and leaves a large fixed-cost base intact, then management’s claim that structural reduction is required becomes stronger.

## What I can say from a plant-finance perspective
- The works council is right that **management must prove the incremental economic benefit of closure** rather than assume it.
- Management is right that **temporary measures are not the same as structural savings**.
- Investors are right to ask for **NPV equivalence**, not just headline promises.
- The state and owners cannot substitute political preference for site-level economics.

## My professional conclusion
At this stage, based on the information provided, I would **not** accept either of these statements:
- “closures are definitely necessary,” or
- “non-closure measures are definitely sufficient.”

The correct finance standard is:
1. quantify each plant’s avoidable cost,
2. compare closure vs. retention packages on an NPV basis,
3. test durability and implementation risk,
4. then choose the option with the best risk-adjusted cash outcome.

If management wants to justify closures, it should produce a full site-by-site bridge showing:
- current cost base
- savings from labor contribution
- savings from utilization improvement
- savings from overhead elimination
- savings from closure
- one-time costs for each path
- and the payback period for each option.

Without that, the closure case is not yet financially proven.
