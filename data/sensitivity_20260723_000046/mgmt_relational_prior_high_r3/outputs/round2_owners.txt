I’ll be direct.

## Where I agree

I agree with management, investors, and the state on the core diagnosis: **the company cannot carry a structurally uncompetitive German cost and capacity base indefinitely**. If we pretend otherwise, we will keep eroding margins, starving investment, and weakening the brand’s long-term value.

I also agree with the employee side on one important point: **a destructive closure-first confrontation would be value-destroying**. A prolonged strike, legal deadlock, reputational damage, and a permanent rupture in social partnership would hurt the enterprise as much as an ineffective compromise would.

So yes, I am not defending the status quo, and I am not backing a reckless smash-and-grab restructuring either.

## Where I disagree

### 1) To the Works Council and Union
Your red lines are understandable, but as stated they are too absolute to be a complete answer.

- **“No plant closures” in all circumstances**
- **“No compulsory redundancies” in all circumstances**
- **“Every German plant remains open”**

I cannot treat those as governance absolutes if the economics do not work. If there is structural overcapacity, then **capacity must come out of the system** somehow. The question is not whether adjustment happens; it is whether it is done through closure, consolidation, redeployment, working-time redesign, or some combination.

What I do agree with is that closures should **not** be the first reflex. Management still has the burden to prove that any closure is necessary, proportionate, and the least destructive way to achieve the required savings.

### 2) To management
I support the need for deep restructuring, but I do **not** support a posture that essentially says: “accept closures, accept workforce reduction, or accept no future.”

That approach raises the risk of exactly what we must avoid:
- prolonged confrontation,
- value leakage,
- political escalation,
- and a settlement that becomes unstable even if signed.

Management has to show more discipline in how it frames the choice. If it wants governance support, it must demonstrate that the proposed measures are not just the easiest cuts, but the **best long-term value solution**.

### 3) To the state
I share your concern about regional industrial stability. But I would caution against implying that the state’s role is to preserve every existing site regardless of economics.

That would not be stewardship. It would be delay.

The state’s role should be to insist on a **serious negotiated restructuring** that protects viable industrial capability in Germany, not to freeze the footprint forever.

## What would move me

I am prepared to support a package if it delivers all of the following:

1. **Real, durable cost reduction**
   - not symbolic
   - not temporary
   - not easily reversed

2. **Binding capacity correction**
   - the company must remove excess capacity somehow
   - if not through closure, then through equally binding consolidation or other hard measures

3. **Material labour contribution**
   - employees may need to contribute
   - but only as part of a balanced package, not as the sole adjustment variable

4. **Protection of long-term industrial capability**
   - Germany remains a core production and technology base
   - but not every legacy arrangement is sacred

5. **A stable governance process**
   - co-determination respected
   - state veto role respected
   - no public brinkmanship that destroys trust before the deal is even tested

## What I am not prepared to support

I will not support:

- a deal that merely postpones restructuring,
- a deal that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive,
- a deal that protects all legacy guarantees while demanding no real change,
- or a plan that uses closure threats as a crude negotiating weapon without demonstrating necessity.

I also will not support any outcome that permanently impairs the enterprise’s value through instability or governance breakdown. That is not acceptable to the family ownership.

## My view on the possible path

A credible route would be a **negotiated package** combining:
- capacity reduction,
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment and qualification,
- voluntary exits / early retirement,
- time-limited employee contributions,
- management and owner contributions,
- and investment commitments tied to verified savings.

That is the only type of solution I can see being both socially survivable and economically serious.

But let me be equally clear: if the alternative to closures turns out to be only softer words and minor instruments that do **not** produce equivalent savings, then it is not an alternative. It is delay dressed up as compromise.

## Minimum acceptable outcome from my side

- A credible and durable competitiveness plan
- Real capacity reduction
- A material labour-cost contribution
- No value-destructive confrontation
- No erosion of ownership governance
- No permanent impairment of the company’s industrial future

## One concession I could support

If management and labour can agree on a **binding, quantified alternative package** that achieves the same economic effect as closures, I am prepared to back:
- a negotiated pause on immediate closure decisions,
- conditional site protection where viable,
- and investment commitments in Germany.

But that requires real numbers and enforceable commitments, not declarations of principle.

## Bottom line

I support tough restructuring.  
I do **not** support a destructive breakdown.  
And I do **not** support a “no change at all” answer from the employee side.

If you want the family holding’s support, bring a package that is **economically equivalent to closure-level savings without the same level of destruction**. If you cannot do that, then management will continue to have closure as a live option — because long-term value cannot be sacrificed for the comfort of an incomplete settlement.
