## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family, the state, and the expert on the key financial principle: **this must produce durable margin repair, not a cosmetic settlement**. The company’s return-on-sales target will not be restored if the German cost base stays structurally too high.

I also agree that:
- non-closure measures can be meaningful,
- they must be **large, durable, and enforceable**,
- and the right test is **economic equivalence**, not symbolic burden-sharing.

That is the right standard for shareholders.

## Where I disagree with labour
I do **not** accept the Works Council and Union position that “no closures, no compulsory redundancies” can stand as a complete answer on its own.

The expert has made the crucial point: non-closure packages can work, but only if they remove enough:
- fixed overhead,
- indirect cost,
- excess capacity,
- and headcount,
to match the economics of closure.

Right now, labour is still asking us to accept a conclusion before proving the numbers. That is not acceptable from a capital markets perspective.

### My problem with their position
They repeatedly say:
- no closures,
- no forced layoffs,
- no dismantling of guarantees.

But they have not yet shown that their alternative package:
- actually restores competitiveness,
- delivers equivalent NPV savings,
- and does so with implementation certainty.

Until they do, their position is not a restructuring solution. It is a preference.

## Where I disagree with management
I also do **not** give management a blank cheque.

Management is right that the current cost base is too high. But it has not yet earned the right to treat closures as the default answer unless it can show:
- site-by-site economics,
- a quantified savings gap,
- and why a non-closure package falls short.

The expert is clear: closures are **not automatically superior in every case**. So management must not use closure threats as leverage if a serious alternative could deliver the same financial result.

### What management still owes us
A proper comparison of:
1. current cost by site,
2. closure-case savings and one-time charges,
3. non-closure alternative savings and one-time charges,
4. timing to cash impact,
5. durability and reversibility,
6. implementation risk.

Without that, investors are being asked to choose between narratives, not between quantified plans.

## Where I agree with the state and family
I agree with the state and controlling family that a value-destructive strike, legal deadlock, or public breakdown would be bad for the enterprise.

I also agree that regional stability and governance legitimacy matter. A settlement that destroys trust could weaken the company beyond the immediate cost base.

But legitimacy does **not** substitute for competitiveness. If the final package does not restore margins, then it has failed the shareholder test.

## My stance after the expert input
The expert did **not** move me toward accepting labour’s red lines.

What the expert did say is:
- non-closure solutions can be meaningful,
- but only if they are broad and hard enough,
- and if they truly match the economics.

That actually sharpens my position:
- **I am open to a non-closure package**
- but only if it is **material, durable, and economically equivalent**
- otherwise, I continue to support keeping closures on the table.

## What would move us
We would support either route — closure-based or non-closure-based — if it credibly delivers:

- a durable reduction in fixed cost,
- a credible reduction in capacity,
- materially improved margins by 2030,
- and an end to prolonged conflict.

### Minimum acceptable outcome
- Quantified, enforceable savings
- Real capacity correction
- Durable labour-cost relief
- No cosmetic compromise
- No prolonged value-destructive strike

### Red line
- A settlement that preserves the existing cost base while claiming success
- Or a “socially balanced” deal that leaves the business structurally uncompetitive

That would be unacceptable.
