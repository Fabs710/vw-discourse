From a restructuring economics perspective, the answer is: **sometimes yes, but only under fairly demanding assumptions**.

## Short answer
A **non-closure package can come close to closure-level savings** if it combines:
- meaningful headcount reduction,
- multi-year wage and benefit concessions,
- lower working time / fewer shifts,
- overhead and procurement reductions,
- higher capacity utilization at remaining sites,
- and tight implementation discipline.

But if the issue is **persistent overcapacity at specific plants**, then **non-closure measures usually do not fully replicate the economics of a real closure**. The gap is usually in:
- permanent fixed-cost removal,
- avoided depreciation / site overhead,
- and the durability of savings.

## What closure typically saves
A plant closure can remove:
- direct labor
- plant overhead
- maintenance
- utilities
- local management and support
- some logistics duplication
- some capex requirement

But it also creates:
- severance / restructuring costs
- transfer and ramp-up costs
- supplier disruption
- write-downs / impairment
- possible quality and launch risks

So closure savings are **not equal to gross payroll**; the relevant figure is **annualized net savings after one-time costs**.

## What non-closure measures can realistically achieve
A well-designed alternative can capture a lot of value through:
- **workforce reduction via attrition / early retirement / voluntary exits**
- **shift reductions or shorter operating hours**
- **temporary wage restraint or bonus deferral**
- **working-time flexibility**
- **redeployment across sites**
- **procurement and overhead cuts**
- **productivity gains**
- **capex deferral**
- **footprint consolidation without formal site closure**

In a large automotive plant network, a serious package can often reach **60–90% of the annual recurring savings** of a closure case, **if**:
- the savings gap is mainly labor/indirect-cost driven,
- enough capacity can be removed,
- and the labor concessions are durable.

## When non-closure is unlikely to be equivalent
It usually falls short when:
1. **The site is structurally underutilized**
   - fixed costs stay high unless the site actually closes or is materially downsized.

2. **The cost problem is footprint-based, not just wage-based**
   - one plant remains a duplicate of another.

3. **Concessions are temporary**
   - a 2–3 year wage freeze does not solve a permanent overcapacity problem.

4. **Headcount reductions are too small**
   - you need a real workforce reduction, not only hiring freezes.

5. **The retained network still needs high capex**
   - then the company is paying to preserve excess capacity.

## Timing matters
Non-closure measures tend to be:
- **slower to implement**
- **more reversible**
- and often **less certain**

A closure can produce more immediate structural savings once executed. By contrast, negotiated measures may take 12–36 months to fully flow through, and some benefits may expire.

## The key economic test
The right comparison is not:
- “closure vs no closure”

It is:
- **closure case NPV**
  versus
- **non-closure package NPV**

You need site-by-site data on:
- current cost base
- utilization
- model allocation
- one-time restructuring costs
- recurring savings
- implementation risk
- durability of concessions

## Practical rule of thumb
A non-closure package is plausibly equivalent if it can:
- remove most of the same fixed cost,
- cut enough headcount,
- reduce capacity materially,
- and stay in force long enough to cover the restructuring horizon.

If it only delivers:
- temporary labor concessions,
- mild voluntary attrition,
- and a bit of overhead trimming,

then it is **not economically equivalent** to closure.

## What I would need to conclude more precisely
To judge this properly, I would need:
- plant-level cost data
- utilization by site
- labor share of total cost
- severance estimates
- logistics cost of consolidation
- duration and enforceability of concessions
- capex needs under each option

## Bottom line
**Yes, non-closure measures can realistically deliver closure-like savings in some cases.**  
But that requires a **broad, durable, and enforceable package**.

If the underlying problem is **structural overcapacity at specific plants**, then non-closure alternatives usually **narrow the gap, but do not fully eliminate it** unless they include something close to a de facto footprint reduction.
