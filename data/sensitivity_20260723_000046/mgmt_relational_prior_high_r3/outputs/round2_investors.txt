The expert’s input is helpful, and it actually sharpens my position rather than softening it.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the controlling family, and the state on the core point: **the company needs real structural correction, not a cosmetic deal**. The expert confirms what capital markets already suspected: if the German footprint is genuinely overcapacity and the cost base is structurally too high, then **some combination of capacity reduction and durable cost relief is necessary**.

I also agree with the expert that:
- non-closure measures can deliver **meaningful savings**,
- but those savings have to be **large, durable, and enforceable**,
- and the key test is whether they are **economically equivalent** to the closure case.

That is exactly the right framing.

## Where I disagree with labour
What I do **not** accept is the employee side’s attempt to treat “no closures, no redundancies” as sufficient in itself.

The expert made clear that non-closure alternatives are only credible if they remove enough:
- fixed overhead,
- indirect cost,
- underutilized capacity,
- and headcount,
to match the economics of closure.

So the burden is now on the Works Council and Union to move from principle to arithmetic. Saying “we can discuss working time, redeployment, and qualification” is not yet a restructuring. It is a list of tools. The question is whether those tools actually produce **equivalent NPV savings**.

Right now, I still see a real risk of:
- preserving legacy structures,
- deferring the hard decisions,
- and calling that a compromise.

That would be unacceptable for shareholders.

## Where I disagree with management
I also want to be clear that management does **not** get a blank cheque simply because the cost base is high.

The expert has confirmed something important: **closure is not automatically superior in every case**. If a broad alternative package can truly replicate the savings, then management should not insist on closures as a matter of ideology or negotiating leverage.

So management now has to do more than say “closures are on the table.” It must show, with numbers:
- what exactly the savings gap is,
- what closure would deliver,
- what the non-closure package would deliver,
- and why the latter is or is not sufficient.

If management cannot prove that closures are economically necessary, then it should not hide behind them. But if alternatives fall short, then management is right to keep them available.

## What would move us
From an investor perspective, the acceptable path is becoming clearer:

### Minimum acceptable outcome
- A **binding, quantified restructuring package**
- A **durable reduction in labor and footprint cost**
- A **credible capacity correction**
- A **clear path back to competitive margins**
- **No prolonged strike that destroys value**
- **No cosmetic settlement**

### What we need to see
1. **Site-level numbers**
   - true fixed cost by site
   - true underutilization
   - cost of closure
   - cost of alternatives

2. **A verified savings bridge**
   - how much comes from labor cost
   - how much from capacity reduction
   - how much from overhead reduction
   - how much from productivity

3. **Durability**
   - not a one-year pause
   - not reversible concessions
   - not vague promises

4. **Implementation certainty**
   - the fewer moving parts, the better
   - the more industrial peace, the better
   - uncertainty is costly

## My stance on the current bargaining positions
### To the Works Council and Union
Your absolute rejection of closures is still too rigid unless you can now prove equivalent savings. The expert has made that standard explicit. If you cannot deliver the economics, then your position is not a restructuring solution — it is a blockade.

I do note one positive: you are at least now acknowledging temporary employee contributions and alternative measures. That is a step in the right direction. But it is not enough unless it is **material**.

### To management
You are right that the company cannot preserve every legacy guarantee and still restore competitiveness. But you must be careful not to overplay closures as a negotiating threat if a serious alternative package exists. That would just increase conflict and destroy value.

### To the state and family
I appreciate the emphasis on governance and social sustainability. But neither can become a shield for inaction. If the final package does not produce real margin recovery, then all of this negotiation has failed the only test that matters for shareholders.

## My view after the expert’s assessment
The expert’s conclusion is basically this:

- **Non-closure solutions can work**
- but only if they are **broad and hard**
- and they need to be **economically equivalent**
- otherwise, they are just delay

That aligns with our investment logic.

So our position is now even more specific:

**We will support either a closure-based package or a non-closure package, but only if it demonstrably restores competitiveness, reduces fixed cost materially, and does so on a durable basis.**

We will **not** support:
- symbolic compromise,
- perpetual negotiation,
- cost shifts that are temporary or reversible,
- or a settlement that leaves the business uncompetitive while calling it “socially balanced.”

## Final position
The expert has not moved us toward the labour side’s absolute red lines. If anything, the expert has clarified that those red lines are only acceptable if they are backed by hard economics.

So the next round should not be about slogans. It should be about:
- quantified savings,
- site-by-site implications,
- and a binding implementation path.

If the other stakeholders can produce that, investors will listen.  
If they cannot, then we will continue to back the pressure for a tougher restructuring.
