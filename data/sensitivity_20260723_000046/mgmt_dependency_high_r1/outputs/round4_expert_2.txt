## My structured assessment

### 1) Economic feasibility of a no-closure route
A no-closure, no-forced-redundancy package is usually viable only when the required gap is in the range that can be closed by:
- **capacity reduction inside the footprint**
- **shift cuts**
- **site specialization**
- **program consolidation**
- **headcount reduction via attrition / voluntary exits / redeployment**
- **temporary labour-cost moderation**
- **management overhead cuts**

That means the plan must remove enough fixed cost that the retained plant network is actually cheaper to run at the lower volume base.

If it does not reduce fixed cost materially, the company keeps too much structure for too little output.

## 2) Practical threshold
Using the ranges already stated, I would treat the following as a reasonable decision rule:

- **Savings gap < €1.0bn per year**: no-closure plan is generally plausible
- **€1.0bn–€1.5bn per year**: feasible only with aggressive internal consolidation
- **€1.5bn–€2.0bn+ per year**: no-closure becomes increasingly hard to make credible without de facto site exits or equivalent footprint reduction

That is not a legal threshold; it is an industrial cost logic threshold.

## 3) Capacity and headcount implication
A no-closure package that is truly comparable to a closure-based plan would usually need roughly:

- **10–20% capacity reduction**
- **10–15% workforce reduction**
- plus a meaningful reduction in indirect labor and overhead

If the company cannot reach at least that order of magnitude, then it is unlikely to generate enough structural savings by 2030.

## 4) Savings comparison
As a rule of thumb:

### No-closure internal consolidation
- **Annual run-rate savings:** about **€0.7bn to €1.5bn**
- **One-time costs:** about **€0.8bn to €1.8bn**
- **Margin uplift by 2030:** roughly **+1.0 to +2.5 points**

### Closure-based restructuring
- **Annual run-rate savings:** about **€1.3bn to €2.5bn+**
- **One-time costs:** about **€2bn to €4bn+**
- **Margin uplift by 2030:** roughly **+2.0 to +4.0 points**

So closures usually deliver more savings, but they also bring much higher execution and conflict risk.

## 5) Delivery risk
This is where the no-closure route can be stronger:

### Advantages of no-closure
- lower industrial conflict
- lower legal / co-determination blockage risk
- better continuity of output
- less supplier disruption
- lower reputational damage

### Risks of no-closure
- savings come slower
- residual overhead remains
- reliance on voluntary exits may underdeliver
- complexity may survive longer than planned
- easy for the package to become cosmetic

### Risks of closure-based restructuring
- strike escalation
- implementation delays
- governance blockage
- higher one-time charges
- plant exit can consume more time than projected

## 6) Bottom-line conclusion
If the company’s required savings gap is **modest to moderate**, a no-closure plan can be credible and may be the more deliverable solution.

If the required gap is **very large**, a no-closure plan becomes less credible unless it includes such deep internal consolidation that it begins to resemble a footprint reduction in all but name.
