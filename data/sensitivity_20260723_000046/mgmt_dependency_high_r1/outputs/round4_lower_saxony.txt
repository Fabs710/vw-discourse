I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the union, the works council, the family, and investors on one basic point: **the status quo is not sustainable**. This company needs a durable restructuring, not another postponement.

I also agree with the expert that a **no-closure** solution is only credible if it is **structural**:
- real capacity reduction,
- real headcount reduction,
- labour-cost contribution,
- site specialization,
- and management overhead cuts.

That is the correct standard.

## Where I disagree
### 1) I do not accept closures as the starting answer
To management, the controlling family, and investors: you keep speaking as if German plant closures are the natural consequence of economic reality. They are not. They are a choice.

And in this company, with co-determination, a special-law blocking minority, and a regional public responsibility, that choice is not one I will support lightly. A closure-first approach threatens:
- jobs in the state,
- supplier stability,
- regional economic balance,
- and the company’s legitimacy as an industrial anchor.

So I am clear: **no German plant closures as a first resort**.

### 2) I do not accept compulsory redundancies as a standing threat
The workforce cannot be asked to enter a “partnership” while management keeps the right to force people out later. That destroys trust and makes any deal fragile.

If the company needs workforce reduction, then the first route must be:
- attrition,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- working-time measures,
- and internal mobility.

I am not prepared to endorse a restructuring that normalizes forced layoffs.

### 3) I do not accept a cosmetic no-closure plan
To the works council and union: I understand the red lines, and I share the concern about hollowing out sites. But I will not support a package that simply preserves every structure and hopes the numbers improve on their own.

The expert has made that plain: a no-closure path only works if it still removes **enough** capacity and cost. So the burden is on all sides to show a real industrial plan, not just a defensive slogan.

## On the expert assessment
The expert’s analysis is useful because it clarifies the real test:
- a no-closure restructuring **can** work,
- but only if it is deep enough to close a substantial part of the gap,
- and only if it is backed by implementation discipline.

That means the question is not “closures or nothing.”
The question is whether the company can achieve:
- sufficient capacity reduction,
- sufficient cost reduction,
- and sufficient margin improvement
**without** destroying plants and social trust.

That is the right test, and it should be used.

## To management
You are right to demand a credible economic package. But then show us the **full comparison**:
- what savings come from internal consolidation,
- what savings come from closures,
- what the one-time costs are,
- what the execution risks are,
- and what the 2030 margin effect is in each case.

Do not simply assert that closures are necessary. Prove it.

And if a no-closure package can deliver meaningful structural savings, then you must be prepared to take it seriously.

## To the works council and union
I support your insistence that:
- no German site should be sacrificed casually,
- and no workforce should be forced to pay for management failures alone.

But I need the same level of seriousness from you:
If all plants stay open, then what exact and quantified contribution are you prepared to accept on:
- capacity,
- shifts,
- program allocation,
- working time,
- and labour cost?

A no-closure solution is only credible if it has hard numbers behind it.

## To the controlling family
You are right that cosmetic compromise is not enough. I agree.

But stewardship also means recognizing the full strategic value of this company: not just balance-sheet discipline, but industrial continuity, regional legitimacy, and stable governance. A solution that tears up the company’s social fabric is not stewardship; it is short-termism.

## To investors
You are right to demand durability and execution certainty. But in this governance structure, a closure-driven plan is not automatically the lowest-risk route. It may well be the most contested and least executable one.

If a no-closure package can truly deliver:
- material savings,
- lower complexity,
- and a credible 2030 trajectory,
then that is the route to back.

If it cannot, then we should know that from numbers, not from slogans.

## My position
I will support a negotiated restructuring **only if** it meets these conditions:

1. **No immediate German plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies as a first-resort tool**
3. **Real capacity reduction inside the footprint**
4. **Meaningful but time-bound labour contribution**
5. **Management and owner contribution**
6. **Binding investment and product commitments for Germany**
7. **Preservation of co-determination and the state’s veto role**

I will **not** support:
- a plan that guts regional employment,
- a plan that makes German plants disposable,
- or a plan that removes the state’s co-determination role.

We should stop trading generalities and force the economics to be tested properly.

**Can the company achieve the required structural savings without closures and without compulsory redundancies, or not?**

If yes, I am open to it.
If no, then management must still not expect automatic support for closures that damage our regional base.

That is my position.
