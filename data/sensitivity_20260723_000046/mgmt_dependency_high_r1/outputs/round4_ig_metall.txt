I will be equally direct in round 4.

## Where I agree
I agree with the works council, the state, and — on one point — even management and investors: **the status quo is not sustainable**. The company needs a durable restructuring, not another temporary patch.

I also agree with the expert that a **no-closure** package can only work if it is **structural**, not cosmetic. That is correct.

And I agree that labour must contribute. We have said that already in our counter-proposal. So no one should pretend the union is refusing reality.

## Where I disagree

### 1) I do not accept that closures are a necessary solution
To management, the family, and investors: you keep repeating that the footprint is too large and the cost base too high. That may be true. It does **not** prove that closing German plants is required.

What it proves is that you want the workforce to carry the burden through the most brutal instrument available.

That is not the same as economic necessity.

**No German plant closures.**

### 2) I do not accept compulsory redundancies as part of a credible settlement
This is the other line we will not cross.

If you reserve the right to force people out, then the whole notion of a negotiated restructuring becomes hollow. It says to the workforce: “Contribute now, but know that management can still impose pain later.”

That is not a settlement. That is coercion.

**No compulsory redundancies.**

### 3) I do not accept dismantling the collective wage framework
Management keeps drifting toward language that implies the collective wage order must be broken to make restructuring “real.” That is unacceptable.

A fair contribution by labour is possible. The destruction of collective standards is not.

## On the expert assessment
The expert’s statement is important, but it does **not** support the closure-first position.

What it says is:
- a no-closure plan can plausibly deliver **a substantial part** of the savings gap,
- but only if it includes real capacity reduction, headcount reduction, labour-cost moderation, and internal consolidation.

That is exactly the route we have been arguing for.

So the expert does **not** prove closures are necessary.  
The expert proves only that **serious internal restructuring** is required.

That distinction matters.

## To management
You say you need durable savings and implementation certainty. Fine. Then stop speaking in abstractions and put the numbers on the table.

We need a transparent comparison of:
- the savings gap,
- what internal consolidation delivers,
- what overhead cuts management will take itself,
- what investment commitments are made,
- what workforce reduction you are actually demanding,
- and how that compares to a closure-based plan.

But do **not** come back with a disguised closure agenda and call it compromise.

If your strategy only works by threatening jobs and sites, then your strategy is the problem.

## To the controlling family
You speak about stewardship. Then act like stewards.

A steward does not begin by making German plants disposable. A steward does not try to break the collective wage framework and then call the outcome durable.

If you want long-term value, then you have to accept that the workforce is not a cost variable to be crushed. It is part of the company’s capability.

## To investors
You want risk-adjusted value and execution certainty. So do we want a viable company.

But in this governance structure, a closure-driven plan is not the low-risk route you think it is. It faces:
- co-determination vetoes,
- labour mobilization,
- strike escalation,
- state resistance,
- and likely implementation delay.

If you care about execution, then back the option that can actually be carried through: a **no-closure internal consolidation plan** with binding job and site security.

## To the regional state
Your position is close to ours, and we appreciate that. But it must become concrete.

If you want a serious no-closure settlement, then insist on:
- binding site commitments,
- binding investment commitments,
- preservation of the job-security framework,
- and a hard rejection of forced redundancies.

Do not let “competitiveness” become a code word for social destruction.

## What we are prepared to accept
We are prepared to discuss, in a binding package:
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary labour-cost moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- internal mobility,
- shift and capacity reductions,
- and site specialization.

But only under these conditions:
1. **Every German plant remains open**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Binding job security through 2030**
4. **The collective wage framework is preserved**
5. **Investment and product commitments are secured**
6. **Management and owners also contribute**

## What we will not accept
- plant closures of any German site
- forced layoffs
- a disguised closure plan
- a “temporary” pact that weakens protections permanently
- a restructuring where workers carry all the pain
- the dismantling of the collective wage framework

## What needs to happen next
We do **not** need more general speeches about whether closures are “in principle” efficient. We need a real test of the no-closure alternative.

If management claims the gap cannot be closed without closures, then prove it with transparent site-by-site data and a genuine comparison.

Until that is done, the union’s position remains unchanged:

**No plant closures. No compulsory redundancies. No dismantling of the collective wage framework.**

And if management continues to push a fait accompli, they should expect the workforce to defend its sites and its rights with all available means.
