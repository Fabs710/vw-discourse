I will be equally clear in this round.

We have now heard enough to know exactly where the fault line runs: management, the controlling family, and investors want to force the workforce to pay for a strategy problem through **closures, forced layoffs, and the erosion of protections**. I reject that.

## Where I agree
I agree with everyone on one point: **the status quo is not sustainable**. The company needs a durable solution, not symbolic delay. I also accept that the economics must improve materially by 2030.

But that does **not** mean the only credible route is to close German plants or threaten compulsory redundancies. That is a choice management is pushing — not an inevitability.

## Where I disagree

### 1) “No closures” is not a sentimental slogan; it is a legitimate red line
To management: your argument is still the same—“the numbers do not work.” Fine. Then show the full numbers.

Because so far what you have not shown is that **every German plant must be closed or reduced as a precondition for competitiveness**. You have shown a preference for the bluntest instrument. That is not the same thing.

And let me be very explicit:  
**The works council will not support German plant closures.**  
Not now, not as a first resort, and not as a bargaining chip.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies cannot be ruled out” is exactly the threat we reject
You can call it “fallback leverage” if you want. From our side, it is a direct attack on job security and trust. Once management reserves the right to force people out, the whole promise of a negotiated settlement becomes hollow.

We are not prepared to legitimize a restructuring that says:  
“Accept cuts voluntarily, or we will impose them later.”

That is not partnership. That is pressure politics.

### 3) A job guarantee through 2030 is not unrealistic if management stops using closures as the starting point
I disagree completely with the claim that binding security “locks in uncompetitive structure” by definition. That is only true if management refuses to use other levers seriously.

The expert has already confirmed something important: **the economics can potentially be improved without formal closures, if the company accepts real internal consolidation, specialization, voluntary exits, attrition, and productivity measures**.

That is the route that must be tested — not the route that starts with destroying sites and threatening people’s livelihoods.

## On the expert assessment
The expert’s analysis is useful, but it also reveals the key point management is trying to avoid:

- If “no closures” means **all sites stay fully loaded and unchanged**, then yes, the economics likely do not close.
- But that is **not** what we are saying.

What we are saying is:
- **Every German plant remains open**
- capacity can be reduced,
- programs can be shifted,
- working time can be adjusted,
- voluntary exits and redeployment can reduce headcount,
- and the footprint can be specialized.

That is a restructuring too. It is just one that does **not** sacrifice sites and people first.

So the expert does **not** prove closures are necessary. It proves only that **management must do real industrial work instead of hiding behind closure threats**.

## To management
You asked for a quantified package. Here is the issue: you keep demanding that labour produce a fully engineered alternative while refusing to rule out the very measures that poison trust.

If you want the works council to engage seriously on capacity and flexibility, then stop treating German sites as expendable before the discussion has even begun.

Bring us:
- a site-by-site utilization plan,
- the programs you want to consolidate,
- the overhead reductions at management level,
- the investment commitments,
- and the exact workforce reduction path that relies on **voluntary** and **reversible** means.

Do not come back with a disguised closure plan and call it compromise.

## To the controlling family
You say you support “stewardship.” Then act like stewards.

A steward does not start by threatening plant closures in a company with a co-determined governance structure and historic job protections. A steward does not break the social compact and then blame the workforce for resisting.

If your position is that every protection must be sacrificed until the market is happy, then you are not preserving the company — you are destabilizing it.

## To investors
You keep saying you will only support what is “risk-adjusted value.” Fine. Then factor in the actual risks:
- strike escalation,
- loss of trust,
- weak implementation,
- social conflict,
- and the long-term damage of hollowing out the German industrial base.

A closure-driven plan is not automatically the lowest-risk plan. In this company, because of co-determination and the state’s blocking position, it may be the **least executable** plan.

So I will say this plainly:  
If you back a closure agenda, you are backing a confrontation that can block, delay, and damage the restructuring far more than a negotiated no-closure path.

## To the regional state
Your position is right in substance: no closures, no forced redundancies, and real industrial stability. But support must be more than political sympathy.

If you want a serious no-closure settlement, then use your influence to insist on:
- binding site commitments,
- binding investment commitments,
- and a clear rejection of forced redundancies as a tool of first resort.

Do not let management convert “durability” into a code word for closures.

## What we will accept
We are prepared to discuss:
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary labour-cost contributions,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- internal mobility,
- program shifts,
- and capacity reductions **within Germany**.

But only under these conditions:
1. **Every German plant stays open**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Binding job security through 2030**
4. **Co-determination and existing protections remain intact**
5. **Investment and product commitments are legally or contractually anchored**

## What we will not accept
- German plant closures
- forced layoffs
- dismantling of protections
- vague promises in exchange for permanent sacrifices
- “temporary” concessions with no binding security

## Bottom line
The burden of proof is on management and the owners, not on the workforce.

If they want a settlement, they must prove that competitiveness can be restored **without closing plants and without forcing people out**. Not with slogans. With numbers, site plans, and binding commitments.

Until then, the works council stands firm:
**No plant closures. No compulsory redundancies. No dismantling of the job-security framework.**

And if management continues to push a fait accompli, they should expect the workforce to defend its sites and its rights with all available means.
