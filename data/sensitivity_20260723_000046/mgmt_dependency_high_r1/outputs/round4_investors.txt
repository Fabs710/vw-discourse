We agree on the diagnosis and on the need for a durable restructuring. We do **not** agree that keeping every plant open and excluding compulsory redundancies in advance is automatically compatible with restoring competitiveness.

## Where we agree
- The status quo is not sustainable.
- A cosmetic settlement would destroy value.
- A prolonged strike would be damaging and should be avoided if possible.
- Labour contributions matter.
- A no-closure route is not automatically weaker **if** it is truly structural.

## Where we disagree

### 1) “No closures” is not a sufficient answer by itself
To the works council, union, and state: your position is understandable politically, but from our perspective it is only acceptable if it still delivers **hard, durable savings**.

Right now, the argument is still too abstract. “Capacity reduction inside the footprint” sounds reasonable, but we have not yet seen a package that proves it can close the economics gap on a **risk-adjusted, 2030 run-rate basis**.

If the no-closure plan leaves residual overcapacity, too much fixed cost, and weak margin recovery, then it is not a solution. It is delay.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be treated as an absolute if the economics do not close
We prefer voluntary exits, attrition, redeployment, and working-time measures. Those are better for execution and industrial peace.

But we cannot support a structure that removes all fallback leverage before the savings are proven. If the package underdelivers, the company needs credible escalation options. Otherwise management is being asked to sign up to an open-ended cost problem.

That does **not** mean forced redundancies should be the first resort. It does mean they cannot be ruled out categorically before we know whether the internal package actually works.

### 3) Job security through 2030 only matters if the cost bridge is real
A long guarantee is not valuable if it locks in an uncompetitive cost base. Markets do not reward intentions; they reward margins and execution.

If the no-closure path can only work with very substantial internal consolidation, headcount reduction, shift cuts, and labour-cost moderation, then that is the price of protecting sites. If not, then the promise is not credible.

## What we need to see
We are still missing the actual economics. Specifically:
- the savings gap after internal consolidation,
- the annual run-rate savings from the no-closure plan,
- the one-time costs,
- the headcount trajectory,
- the capacity reduction by site/program,
- and the implementation risk compared with closure-based restructuring.

Without that, no one can honestly say the no-closure route is sufficient.

## To management
You are right that the company needs structural savings, not slogans. But “closures remain on the table” is not enough either. Put the numbers down clearly.

We want a proper comparison:
1. no-closure internal consolidation plan
2. closure-based plan
3. annual savings
4. one-time costs
5. headcount impact
6. capacity reduction
7. implementation risk
8. impact on return-on-sales by 2030

If the no-closure package can genuinely deliver the required structural improvement, we will support it. If it cannot, then the case for deeper measures becomes much stronger.

## To the works council and union
We hear your red lines. But red lines are not a business plan.

If you want investors to back a no-closure solution, you need to show:
- how much capacity comes out,
- what labour-cost contribution is made,
- how many jobs are reduced,
- what shifts and programs disappear,
- and how the economics improve sufficiently to restore competitive margins.

If the answer is mainly “trust us,” that will not be enough.

## To the regional state
We agree that social stability and industrial continuity matter. But political opposition to closures does not replace a viable cost structure.

If you want to defend a no-closure settlement, then support a package that is **hard enough** to be credible. Otherwise you are defending form over substance.

## To the controlling family
You are right that cosmetic compromise is unacceptable. We agree.

But “closures stay on the table no matter what” is also not a value-maximizing answer if a serious internal consolidation can close most of the gap. The correct question is not ideology; it is whether the numbers work.

## Our position
We would support a no-closure settlement **only if** it includes all of the following:
1. real capacity reduction inside Germany,
2. material headcount reduction,
3. meaningful labour-cost moderation,
4. management and overhead cuts,
5. clear investment commitments,
6. binding milestones and review triggers,
7. and a demonstrated path back to competitive margins by 2030.

We will **not** support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a plan that keeps the cost base structurally impaired,
- or a deal that merely postpones the correction.
