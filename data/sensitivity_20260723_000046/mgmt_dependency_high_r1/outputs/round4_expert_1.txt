## Bottom line
A **no-closure, no-forced-redundancy** package can be **economically credible only if it removes enough fixed cost and capacity inside the German footprint**.  
If it relies mainly on soft flexibility, hiring freezes, and temporary wage restraint, it will **not** close the gap.

In practical terms:

- **No-closure plan** can plausibly deliver **~50–70%** of the savings of a closure-based plan.
- In a stronger variant with deep internal consolidation, it may reach **~70–85%**.
- To be credible by 2030, it likely needs **at least 10–20% capacity reduction** and **roughly 10–15% workforce reduction**, mostly via attrition, voluntary exits, redeployment, and program consolidation.
- If the required gap is larger than that, then **plant closures or equivalent site exits become economically difficult to avoid**.

## What the economics say

### 1) Fixed-cost logic
Car plants have high fixed costs:
- plant overhead
- maintenance
- logistics
- utilities
- indirect labor
- engineering support
- quality systems

If volume falls but the site count stays unchanged, unit cost rises sharply unless fixed cost is removed. That is why:
- **shift cuts alone are usually insufficient**
- **headcount cuts without site specialization are often insufficient**
- **temporary labor concessions help, but do not structurally solve overcapacity**

### 2) No-closure plan: what it can realistically do
A serious no-closure package would need:
- shift elimination at selected sites
- line-rate reduction
- platform and program concentration
- functional consolidation in engineering, admin, and logistics
- voluntary departures and attrition
- working-time reductions / flexibility
- purchasing and supplier-cost actions
- management overhead cuts

This can create meaningful savings, but it has limits because:
- you retain site overhead
- you retain network complexity
- you often retain more indirect staff than a closure plan would
- savings ramp more slowly than under a full closure

Likely impact:

- **larger and faster fixed-cost removal**

## Margin effect by 2030
If the brand is missing its return-on-sales target, the restructuring needs to improve margin by roughly **several percentage points** in a realistic turnaround case.

### No-closure path
A strong internal consolidation might improve operating margin by about:
- **+1.0 to +2.5 percentage points**

### Closure-based path
A deeper footprint reset might improve operating margin by about:
- **+2.0 to +4.0 percentage points**

These are broad estimates. The actual improvement depends on:
- volume recovery
- EV mix
- pricing
- China exposure
- supplier costs
- labor settlement
- implementation timing

## The key threshold question
The decisive issue is whether the company’s **required savings gap** is:
- **small enough** for internal consolidation, or
- **too large** without plant exits.

### If the gap is approximately:
- **€0.5bn to €1.2bn/year**: a no-closure plan is often feasible
- **€1.2bn to €2.0bn/year**: borderline; needs aggressive site specialization and workforce reduction
- **above €2.0bn/year**: no-closure becomes difficult unless utilization improves materially or the company accepts major internal rationalization that approaches a de facto footprint exit

## My view on “no closures, no forced redundancies”
That package is only credible if it includes all of the following:
1. **binding site specialization**
2. **material capacity cuts**
3. **real workforce reduction via voluntary means**
4. **meaningful labor-cost moderation**
5. **management overhead cuts**
6. **review triggers with fallback options**

Without those, it is mostly a delay mechanism.

## Risk assessment
### No-closure plan risks
- slower savings realization
- complexity remains high
- unions may resist work intensification
- savings may erode if volumes disappoint
- execution depends on voluntary exits and successful redeployment

### Closure plan risks
- higher conflict risk
- legal/co-determination blockage risk
- strike and reputational damage
- supplier disruption
- longer implementation timeline than management may assume

## Practical conclusion
From a restructuring economics standpoint:

- **A no-closure plan is possible**
- but **only if it is deeply structural**
- and it likely needs **meaningful capacity reduction plus 10–15% workforce reduction**
- If management’s required savings are materially larger than that, then **some form of plant exit becomes increasingly hard to avoid**
