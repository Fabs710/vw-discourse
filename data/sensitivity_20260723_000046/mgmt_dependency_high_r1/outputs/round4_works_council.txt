I will be just as clear in round 4.

The discussion is now trying to normalize a dangerous idea: that because management wants “structural savings,” German plants must be made disposable and compulsory redundancies must remain on the table. I reject that completely.

## Where I agree

I agree with the others on three points:

1. **The status quo is not sustainable.**
2. **The company needs durable savings by 2030.**
3. **A cosmetic compromise would be pointless.**

That is the extent of my agreement.

## Where I disagree

### 1) I do not accept closures as a “necessary option”
To management, the family, and investors: you keep repeating the same claim in different words. “The footprint is too large.” “The cost base is too high.” “The market is shrinking.” None of that proves that a German plant closure is required.

It proves only that you want the workforce to carry the burden through the most brutal mechanism available.

That is not an economic law. It is a political choice.

And let me say it plainly:  
**No German plant closures.**

Not as a starting point.  
Not as leverage.  
Not as a fallback threat.

### 2) I do not accept compulsory redundancies as part of a “credible” package
The repeated suggestion that forced layoffs must remain possible is exactly what destroys trust and makes a negotiated settlement unstable.

If management insists on keeping the right to fire people against their will, then every promise of partnership is hollow. You are asking the workforce to accept sacrifices while you retain the power to impose more later.

That is unacceptable.

**No compulsory redundancies.**

### 3) I do not accept that job security through 2030 is only possible if rights are weakened first
The state and the expert are right about one thing: a no-closure solution must be real, not cosmetic. I agree. But the conclusion management draws from that is wrong.

A no-closure solution is not the same as “do nothing.” It means:
- capacity reduction inside Germany,
- shift reductions,
- site specialization,
- program consolidation,
- voluntary exits,
- attrition,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- and temporary labour contributions.

That is a restructuring. It just does **not** destroy sites or break the job-security framework.

So no, management does not get to say: “If we cannot close plants, then nothing serious can happen.” That is false.

## On the expert assessment
The expert’s point is useful, but it actually strengthens my position.

Yes, a no-closure plan only works if it is structural. Exactly. That is what we have been saying.

But the expert also says that a no-closure plan can plausibly close a **substantial part** of the gap, and in some cases most of it, if it is built correctly. That is the critical fact management keeps trying to bury.

So the expert does **not** prove closures are necessary.  
The expert proves only that management must stop using vague threats and start presenting a real internal consolidation plan.

## To management
You say you need a durable structural reduction. Fine. Then show the structure.

Put the actual numbers on the table:
- what savings remain after internal consolidation,
- which programs are to be moved,
- where shift cuts happen,
- how much overhead management will cut,
- what investment is being committed,
- and how much labour-cost moderation you are asking for.

But do **not** come back and tell us that the only serious answer is closures or forced layoffs.

If your plan only works by breaking the co-determined social contract, then your plan is the problem.

## To the controlling family
You speak about stewardship, but stewardship does not begin with threatening historic job guarantees.

A real owner contribution means:
- accepting that workers are not the sole adjustment variable,
- accepting internal restructuring over closure politics,
- and respecting the governance structure that has served this company for decades.

If you want long-term value, then stop treating German plants as expendable assets.

## To investors
You want execution certainty and margin recovery. So do we want a viable company.

But you need to understand the reality of this governance structure:
- co-determination is not a nuisance,
- the workforce has veto power over closures,
- and trying to force a closure agenda through this company will produce delay, conflict, and damage.

So if you really care about execution certainty, then you should back the option that is actually executable:  
**a no-closure internal consolidation plan with binding job and site security.**

A closure-based plan may look harder on paper, but in this setting it is more likely to blow up.

## To the regional state
Your position is close to ours, and I appreciate that. But it must become concrete.

If you want a serious no-closure settlement, then you should insist on:
- binding site commitments,
- binding investment commitments,
- preservation of the job-security framework,
- and a hard rejection of compulsory redundancies.

Do not let “competitiveness” become a code word for social destruction.

## What we are prepared to accept
We are prepared to discuss, in a binding package:
- working-time flexibility,
- temporary labour-cost moderation,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- internal mobility,
- shift and capacity reductions,
- and site specialization.

But only under these conditions:
1. **Every German plant remains open**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Binding job security through 2030**
4. **Co-determination remains intact**
5. **Investment and product commitments are secured**

## What we will not accept
- plant closures of any German site
- forced layoffs
- a disguised closure plan
- a “temporary” pact that weakens protections permanently
- a restructuring that makes the workforce pay for management’s failure
