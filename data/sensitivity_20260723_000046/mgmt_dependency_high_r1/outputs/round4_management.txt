I’ll respond directly.

## First, the common ground
I do agree on a few points:
- The status quo is not sustainable.
- A cosmetic deal would be a waste of time.
- Any settlement must be durable, not temporary.
- A prolonged strike would damage the company, the workforce, and our ability to invest.

So yes: we need a negotiated restructuring that is real.

## Where I sharply disagree
### 1) “No closures” cannot be an absolute red line
To the works council and union: you keep repeating that every German plant must remain open. That is **not** a plan. It is a political position.

The expert has now been clear: a no-closure solution can only work if it still delivers **real capacity reduction, headcount reduction, labour-cost moderation, and internal consolidation**. I accept that. But that also means you cannot exclude plant rationalisation in advance if the internal measures do not close the gap.

We are not talking about ideology. We are talking about whether the German footprint can be made competitive. If it cannot, then preserving every site simply means preserving overcapacity.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be a blanket veto
I understand the preference for voluntary exits, attrition, redeployment, and flexibility. Those are our first choice too. But if you insist that forced measures are ruled out under all circumstances, then you are asking management to sign up to a restructuring with no enforcement mechanism.

That is not credible.

A serious restructuring needs a fallback. Not because we want confrontation, but because a company in this position cannot be built on promises alone.

### 3) Job security through 2030 is only meaningful if it is economically supported
I do not reject security as a principle. I reject security **without a hard economic bridge**. If the package preserves too much cost and too much capacity, then it simply postpones the correction and makes the eventual pain worse.

Security is valuable only if the business remains viable under it.

## To the works council
You asked for the numbers. Fine. But then answer the same question honestly:

- What exact capacity do you remove?
- What exact workforce reduction do you accept?
- Which shifts disappear?
- Which programs are consolidated?
- What labour-cost contribution is on the table?
- And what savings does that deliver on a 2030 run-rate basis?

If your answer is only “keep every plant open and trust that flexibility will do the rest,” then that is not enough. That is a delay tactic, even if you do not mean it that way.

## To the union
You say labour must contribute. I agree. But contribution has to be **material and durable**:
- temporary wage moderation,
- bonus restraint,
- flexible working time,
- internal mobility,
- voluntary exits,
- productivity gains,
- and support for footprint simplification.

What I cannot accept is a package that gives management nothing but a promise of future conflict if savings are missed. That would not restore competitiveness.

## To the regional state
I appreciate your emphasis on industrial continuity. But “no closures” is not a substitute for a viable operating model.

If you want a no-closure path, then help make it economically real:
- support internal consolidation,
- support re-skilling and redeployment,
- support site specialization,
- and support a binding labour contribution.

But do not ask management to accept an uncompetitive structure in the name of symbolism.

## To the controlling family
I agree that cosmetic compromise is unacceptable. On that point, we are aligned.

Where I disagree is if stewardship is interpreted as protecting every legacy structure regardless of economics. Stewardship means preserving the company’s future earnings power, not just avoiding difficult decisions.

If the internal path can genuinely close the gap, I will evaluate it seriously. But if it cannot, then the option of deeper structural action remains on the table.

## To investors
You are right to demand durable savings and implementation certainty. I share that objective.

But I also need to be blunt: a closure-first plan is not automatically the lowest-risk route in this governance environment. It may well be the most disruptive and least executable. So the right question is not whether closures sound decisive. It is whether a quantified internal consolidation plan can deliver the same structural effect.

If it can, good. If it cannot, we should not pretend otherwise.

## My position, stated clearly
I am prepared to consider a no-closure restructuring **only if** it contains:
1. **Real capacity reduction**
2. **Real workforce reduction**
3. **Meaningful labour-cost contribution**
4. **Site specialization and program consolidation**
5. **Management and owner contribution**
6. **Binding milestones and fallback measures**

What I am **not** prepared to accept is:
- preserving the whole footprint,
- preserving the full cost base,
- and calling that restructuring.
