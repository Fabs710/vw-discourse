I will be direct.

We are now beyond rhetoric. The company needs a restructuring that is **economically real**, **governable**, and **implementable**. I do not support a cosmetic settlement that preserves the current cost base and only postpones the problem. Equally, I do not support a value-destructive confrontation that burns cash, trust, and time.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the state, investors, and even the employee side on one essential point: **the status quo is not sustainable**. The current German footprint and cost structure must change.

I also agree with the expert that a no-closure path can work **only** if it is structural:
- real capacity reduction,
- real headcount reduction,
- labor-cost moderation,
- site specialization,
- and management/overhead cuts.

That is the right standard.

## Where I disagree

### 1) A blanket “no closures” line is not acceptable
To the works council and union: I understand why you are drawing the line there, but I cannot treat “every plant stays open” as an absolute commitment before the economics are proven.

If the internal consolidation package cannot close the gap, then plant rationalization must remain on the table. Not because closures are desirable, but because **preserving every site at any cost would destroy value**.

So I do **not** support a categorical exclusion of closures in advance.

### 2) A blanket “no compulsory redundancies” line is also not acceptable
I prefer voluntary exits, attrition, redeployment, and working-time measures. Those are the first instruments I would want used.

But if the package does not deliver enough savings and capacity reduction, then a permanent ban on fallback measures is not credible. A restructuring needs enforcement logic, not hope.

So I do **not** support a categorical exclusion of compulsory redundancies in advance either.

### 3) A job-security pledge is only worthwhile if the business can carry it
I do not dismiss security commitments lightly. Stable labor relations matter. But a pledge through 2030 only has value if the company remains competitive under it.

If the pledge locks in too much cost or too much footprint, it merely defers the correction and increases the eventual pain. That is not stewardship. That is delay.

## To the works council and union
You argue that closures and forced redundancies are unnecessary if the company does real internal restructuring. I am open to testing that. But you need to stop speaking as if the burden of proof sits only with management.

If you want a no-closure alternative to be taken seriously, then it must be **quantified** and **credible**. That means:
- how much capacity comes out,
- which shifts disappear,
- which programs are consolidated,
- what headcount reduction is achieved,
- what labor-cost contribution is made,
- and how this gets the company back to target margins by 2030.

If the answer is only “keep all sites open and trust flexibility,” that is not a plan.

## To management
You are right to insist on durable savings and not accept a symbolic deal. But stating that “the numbers don’t work” is not enough. Show the actual bridge.

I need a comparison that is more than a headline:
1. no-closure internal consolidation plan
2. closure-based plan
3. annual savings
4. one-time costs
5. execution risk
6. headcount impact
7. margin effect by 2030

Without that, we are debating preferences, not facts.

## To the regional state
I respect your focus on regional stability and your concern about industrial legitimacy. That matters. But “no closures” cannot become a political shield for an uncompetitive structure.

If you want a no-closure path, then help make it economically real:
- support internal consolidation,
- support redeployment and training,
- support site specialization,
- and accept that the footprint still has to shrink structurally.

Political opposition alone does not solve the economics.

## To investors
You are right to demand a package that is durable and credible. But I want the same discipline from you: do not confuse a harsh-sounding plan with an executable one.

In this governance structure, a closure-driven plan may be the most disruptive and least executable route. If a no-closure package can truly deliver the required structural savings, it may well be the better value outcome.

But if it cannot, then we should not pretend otherwise.

## My position
I will support a negotiated restructuring only if it meets these conditions:
- real structural savings,
- real capacity reduction,
- meaningful workforce reduction,
- management and owner contribution,
- and implementation certainty.

I will **not** support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a deal that preserves the uncompetitive cost base,
- or a prolonged conflict that destroys value.
