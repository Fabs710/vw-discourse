I will be equally direct.

I agree with the **diagnosis** that the status quo is not sustainable. I also agree that a merely symbolic compromise would be irresponsible. This company does need a durable restructuring, and it needs to restore competitiveness by 2030.

But I do **not** accept the leap from “structural change is needed” to “German plant closures and compulsory redundancies are therefore justified.” That is still a choice, not a law of nature.

## Where I agree
- The company needs **material, durable savings**.
- A prolonged strike would be **damaging** for everyone.
- Labour must contribute if we want a serious settlement.
- Management cannot be expected to carry the full burden alone.
- A cosmetic deal that preserves everything unchanged is not credible.

## Where I disagree
### 1) I do not accept closures as the starting point
Management, the controlling family, and investors keep speaking as if closures are the natural discipline mechanism. In a German co-determined company with a special-law blocking minority and a legitimate regional public interest, that is not an acceptable default.

You have **not** shown that the only way to restore competitiveness is to close German plants. What has been shown is that the footprint must be made leaner and more focused. That is not the same thing.

### 2) I do not accept compulsory redundancies as a foregone conclusion
If forced layoffs are kept as a standing threat, then every promise of partnership is hollow. I understand wanting fallback options. But a restructuring that begins by reserving the right to force people out is not social partnership; it is pressure politics.

### 3) I do not accept a restructuring that guts the regional industrial base
My responsibility is not only to abstract returns. It is to:
- jobs in the state,
- supplier stability,
- skills,
- industrial continuity,
- and the company’s role as a regional anchor.

A solution that destroys that base may look clean on paper and still be a strategic failure.

## On the expert assessment
The expert has clarified the real issue: a no-closure path can work **only if** it still delivers **real capacity reduction, workforce reduction, and cost reduction**.

That is important. It means:
- “no closures” does **not** mean “no change”;
- and it also means management cannot hide behind simple closure threats instead of testing serious internal restructuring.

So I accept the expert’s conclusion that a credible package must include:
- capacity cuts,
- headcount reduction,
- labour-cost relief,
- site specialization,
- and management overhead cuts.

What I do **not** accept is the idea that this necessarily requires plant closures.

## To management
You keep saying the numbers don’t work without deeper structural measures. Fine. Then put the numbers on the table in a way that can be tested:

- What is the quantified savings gap?
- How much comes from capacity reduction?
- How much from overhead reduction?
- How much from labour cost?
- How much from product and investment effects?
- What is the cost of each option?

If you want my support, I need a **real, quantified comparison** between:
1. closure-based restructuring, and
2. a no-closure restructuring with real internal consolidation.

Not slogans. Not threats. A proper comparison.

And let me be clear: if the no-closure option is only credible by reducing capacity, shifting volumes, specializing sites, and lowering headcount through voluntary means, then that is the route I want tested first.

## To the works council and union
I support your core line that the workforce should not be the sole shock absorber. That is right.

But I also need to be frank: if “no closures” is interpreted as “every site stays in place, every structure remains, and the burden is mostly externalized to owners and management,” then you will not get a stable settlement.

If you want my backing, then the labour side must be prepared to discuss:
- real capacity reduction,
- meaningful but time-bound labour contributions,
- internal mobility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- and site specialization.

That is the price of making a no-closure solution economically credible.

## To the controlling family and investors
I agree that cosmetic compromise is not enough. But neither is insisting that the only serious route is one that blows up trust and risks a prolonged confrontation.

A long-term owner should understand that:
- trust,
- skills,
- co-determination,
- and industrial stability

are part of value, not obstacles to it.

If you push a closure-first logic too hard, you may win a paper decision and lose execution, peace, and regional legitimacy. That is a bad trade.

## My position
I am prepared to support a **negotiated restructuring** if it meets these conditions:

1. **No immediate German plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies as a first-resort tool**
3. **Binding site and job security for a defined period**
4. **Real capacity reduction inside the footprint**
5. **Meaningful labour contribution that is time-bound and fair**
6. **Management and owner contribution**
7. **Investment and product commitments for Germany**
8. **Preservation of co-determination and the state’s veto role**

I am **not** prepared to support:
- a plan that guts regional employment,
- a plan that treats German plants as disposable,
- or a plan that destroys the state’s co-determination role.

We should stop arguing in abstractions and force the economics to be tested properly.
