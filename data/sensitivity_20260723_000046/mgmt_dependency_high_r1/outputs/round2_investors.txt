We agree on the diagnosis that **the current structure is not acceptable**. The company needs **durable cost reduction, capacity adjustment, and a credible route back to target margins**. From a capital-markets perspective, that is the only way to protect enterprise value.

Where we **disagree** is on the idea that the answer must be framed as either:
- preserve every German plant and every legacy guarantee, or
- accept a destructive closure-and-strike cycle.

That is too crude, and it is exactly the kind of binary that destroys value.

### Our view on the current positions

#### To management
You are right that **temporary fixes are not enough**. You are also right that the economics must close in a durable way.

But you still have not shown a **quantified, credible package** that proves closures are unavoidable. Saying “the numbers don’t work” is not enough. Markets need:
- a clear cost bridge,
- a quantified capacity plan,
- expected margin impact,
- timing,
- and implementation risk.

If you want capital-market support, bring **verifiable savings** and a **deal that can actually be executed**. If the only path is industrial confrontation, then you are destroying shareholder value, not creating it.

#### To the works council and union
We hear your refusal of closures and compulsory redundancies. We understand the social logic, but from our side the limit is clear:  
**we will not support an outcome that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.**

If “no closures” means the cost base remains too high and margins stay impaired, then that is not a solution. It is value destruction with better language.

That said, we are open to an alternative package if it is real. It must include:
- meaningful labour-cost relief,
- measurable capacity reduction,
- productivity gains,
- voluntary exits and redeployment where possible,
- and binding industrial commitments that do not simply postpone the problem.

A labor contribution that is only temporary or reversible will not be enough.

#### To the regional state
We agree that prolonged conflict is bad and that the industrial base matters. But “no closures” cannot become a veto on serious structural change if the company’s competitiveness is at stake.

The question for us is not whether every site remains politically desirable. It is whether the final package restores **return on sales** and protects long-term value. If not, then it is not stewardship; it is drift.

#### To the controlling family
We agree with you on one essential point: **cosmetic compromise is not acceptable**. A deal that preserves every legacy structure while leaving the company uncompetitive would be a failure.

But we also need to be frank: if the ownership line is effectively “closures remain on the table no matter what,” then that weakens the incentive to find a serious negotiated alternative. Capital markets will back a hard restructuring only if it is both **durable and executable**. A prolonged stalemate is not value-preserving leverage; it is erosion.

### Our bottom line
We would support the option that most credibly delivers:
1. **durable margin recovery**,  
2. **disciplined structural cost reduction**,  
3. **quantified and enforceable savings**,  
4. **implementation certainty**, and  
5. **limited conflict duration**.

We will **not** support:
- a cosmetic settlement,
- a deal that preserves the uncompetitive cost base,
- or a prolonged strike that destroys earnings and confidence.

### What we need next
Stop repeating red lines and bring a **quantified alternative**. Specifically:
- What is the expected annual savings?
- What capacity comes out?
- How many jobs are reduced, and by what mechanism?
- What is the labour contribution?
- What is the investment offset?
- What is the timeline to margin recovery?

If the answer is credible without closures, we will assess it on merit. If not, then management’s case for deeper structural action becomes stronger.
