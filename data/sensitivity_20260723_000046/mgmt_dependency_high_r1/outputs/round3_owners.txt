I’ll be direct.

We are now at the point where the debate has to move from principle to economic substance. I agree with everyone on one thing: **the status quo is not sustainable**. The company needs a durable restructuring that restores competitiveness, margin, and investment capacity. I also agree that a prolonged strike cycle or a symbolic compromise would destroy value.

Where I do **not** agree is with any framing that turns **“no closures”** into an absolute, or **“no compulsory redundancies”** into a blanket veto, before we have a package that demonstrably closes the gap. That would substitute political comfort for industrial reality.

## Where I agree
### With management
You are right that:
- the German cost base is structurally too high,
- temporary fixes are not enough,
- and we need durable savings, not cosmetic peace.

I also accept the expert’s point that a no-closure path is only credible if it still delivers **real capacity reduction, workforce reduction, and labor-cost moderation**.

### With investors
You are right to insist on:
- quantified savings,
- implementation certainty,
- and a package that genuinely restores margins.

I share your rejection of a deal that simply preserves every legacy structure and calls it reform.

### With the state and labour representatives
You are right that:
- prolonged conflict is value-destructive,
- trust matters,
- and a negotiated settlement is preferable to open confrontation.

## Where I disagree
### 1) A blanket “no closures” line is too rigid
I cannot support a position that rules out plant rationalisation in advance, regardless of economics. If some capacity must leave the system to restore competitiveness, then that must remain on the table.

Not because closures are desirable in themselves — they are not — but because **value cannot be protected by preserving every site at any cost**.

### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be absolute if no alternative closes the gap
I prefer voluntary exits, redeployment, attrition, and negotiated flexibility. But if those measures do not produce sufficient savings, then a structure that permanently forbids fallback options is not credible governance.

### 3) A job-security guarantee is only meaningful if it is economically credible
A guarantee through 2030 sounds good, but if it locks in an uncompetitive structure, it merely postpones the adjustment and increases the eventual pain.

## What I need to see
I am open to a negotiated settlement **if** it is economically real. That means I need a package with numbers, not slogans.

Specifically:
1. **Annual recurring savings**
2. **Capacity reduction by site or program**
3. **Headcount reduction path**
4. **Labor-cost contribution**
5. **Management overhead cuts**
6. **Investment and product commitments**
7. **Implementation timeline to 2030**
8. **Fallback mechanisms if milestones are missed**

## My view on a possible landing zone
A no-closure package is not ruled out by principle. But it is only acceptable if it is, in economic substance, a **real footprint reduction inside the German system**:
- fewer shifts,
- fewer programs,
- site specialization,
- lower support and overhead,
- and materially lower headcount through voluntary means.

If “no closures” means “every site remains fully loaded and essentially unchanged,” then I say plainly: **that will not do**.

## To the works council and union
I hear your refusal of closures and forced layoffs. I understand the social and political logic, but you need to answer the economic question with a concrete plan.

Show us:
- which capacity comes out,
- how much labor cost comes out,
- which sites are specialized,
- and how the company returns to target margins without relying on hope.

If you can produce a quantified package that genuinely closes the gap without formal closures, I will assess it seriously.

If not, then your position is not a solution; it is a boundary line.

## To management
You also have obligations here. Saying “the numbers don’t work” is not enough. Put the numbers on the table:
- the savings bridge,
- the footprint logic,
- the one-time costs,
- and the margin impact of each option.

If you want support for structural change, bring a comparison that shows why your preferred route is superior to an internal consolidation alternative.

## My position as controlling shareholder
I will support a settlement only if it meets the long-term interests of the enterprise:
- restores competitiveness,
- improves margins durably,
- preserves industrial capability,
- and avoids a value-destructive conflict.

I will **not** support:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a deal that preserves the uncompetitive cost structure,
- or a prolonged conflict that burns value.
