I will be direct.

We all agree on the diagnosis: **the status quo is not sustainable**. The company needs a durable restructuring that restores competitiveness and margins. I also agree that a prolonged conflict would destroy value. Where I part ways with some of you is on the assumption that **“no closures, no compulsory redundancies, preserve everything”** can be the default answer without hard economic consequences.

### Where I agree
- I agree with management that the German cost base is structurally too high.
- I agree with the investors that we need **real, durable savings**, not cosmetic peace.
- I agree with the state and labour representatives that **a prolonged strike would be value-destructive**.
- I agree that any solution should preserve the company’s long-term industrial base and not create irreversible damage.

### Where I disagree
#### 1) A blanket “no closures” line is too rigid
I understand the political and social reasons for opposing closures. But as controlling owners, we cannot endorse a position that makes structural adjustment impossible before the numbers are even tested. If some capacity truly has to leave the system to restore competitiveness, then that must remain part of the discussion.

What I cannot support is a line that says every site stays open **regardless of economics**. That would be protecting form over substance, and it would ultimately damage the whole enterprise.

#### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be an absolute if no alternative closes the gap
I would prefer voluntary exits, redeployment, natural attrition, and negotiated flexibility. But if those do not deliver enough, then we should not pre-emptively remove all fallback options. A credible restructuring needs credible leverage.

#### 3) A job guarantee is only valuable if it is economically credible
A guarantee through 2030 sounds attractive, but if it locks in an uncompetitive structure and simply defers the adjustment, then it is not a stewardship solution. It is a future crisis written in advance.

### What I would consider acceptable
I am open to a package if it delivers **quantified, durable, and enforceable** savings. That means I would look for:
- meaningful labour-cost relief,
- working-time and flexibility measures,
- productivity improvements,
- redeployment and natural attrition,
- capacity reduction where justified,
- and a serious investment commitment for the German footprint.

I am **not** insisting on closures as a slogan. I am insisting that the final package must close the economics gap, not merely preserve comfort. If labour and the state can bring forward an alternative that genuinely matches the savings and durability of structural cuts, I will examine it seriously.

### On burden sharing
Burden sharing must be real and balanced:
- labour contributes through cost moderation and flexibility,
- management contributes through operational discipline,
- owners support a credible long-term plan,
- and the company commits to future investment tied to competitiveness.

But burden sharing is not the same as burden avoidance. If the package leaves the company structurally impaired, then it is not a compromise I can support.

### My bottom line
I am prepared to back a negotiated settlement **if** it is economically real, durable, and stabilising.  
I will **not** support a deal that simply preserves every current site and protection while leaving the company uncompetitive.  
And I will **not** support a prolonged conflict that destroys value.

So the next round should not repeat principles. It should put forward a **quantified alternative package** that answers one question:

**How exactly do you restore competitiveness and margins by 2030, at a scale that is believable to owners, markets, and customers?**

If there is a serious answer, I am ready to assess it. If not, then the pressure for deeper structural measures remains justified.
