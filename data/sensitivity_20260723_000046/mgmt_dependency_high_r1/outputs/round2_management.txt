I’ll be equally direct: **the positions I’m hearing from the works council and union still do not close the economic gap.** They rest on a premise I cannot accept — that we can restore competitiveness while keeping every German plant open, ruling out compulsory redundancies, and preserving the existing cost and protection structure essentially intact.

That is not a restructuring. That is a delay.

### Where I agree
I agree on one point: **this must be a negotiated settlement**, not a diktat. I also agree that we need a durable solution, not a temporary patch. And I agree that the company’s future industrial base in Germany matters.

But agreement on those basics does **not** mean agreement on the substance. The substance is this:  
**our German cost and capacity structure is too heavy for the market we now face.**

### Where I disagree — and why
#### 1) “No plant closures” is not a viable starting line
The works council says every German plant must stay open. I cannot commit to that. Not because I want conflict, but because **the numbers do not support preserving every site unchanged**.

We have:
- a shrinking European market,
- severe price pressure,
- higher-cost German production,
- and a brand that is missing its return targets.

If we protect every site for sentimental or political reasons, we will end up protecting decline. That is unacceptable.

#### 2) “No compulsory redundancies” cannot be an absolute
I understand the desire to avoid forced layoffs. I do. But if we cannot achieve sufficient savings through voluntary means, redeployment, attrition, and structural redesign alone, then **compulsory measures cannot be ruled out in advance**.

I am not saying they are the first choice. I am saying they cannot be taken off the table before we have a package that actually works.

#### 3) A 2030 job guarantee without structural change is not credible
A binding employment guarantee sounds attractive, but if it locks in an uncompetitive structure, it simply postpones the crisis and makes the eventual adjustment harsher.

I will not sign up to a promise that looks stable on paper and fails in reality.

### What management needs
Management needs a **durable reduction in cost and capacity**, not a symbolic gesture. That means:

- real workforce reduction,
- lower labour cost,
- capacity rationalisation,
- and a structure that allows us to fund future products and technology.

If the employee side believes this can be achieved entirely through flexibility and internal reshuffling, then bring the quantified package. Not slogans. Not principles. **Numbers.**

### To the works council
You ask us to show how we close the economics gap without closures or forced job cuts. My answer is simple: **you have not yet offered a package that does that either.**

If you want to keep every plant open, then you need to explain:
- where the capacity comes out,
- how the cost base falls materially,
- how the return-on-sales gap closes,
- and how the company funds investment to 2030.

Right now, I am hearing refusal, not a solution.

### To the union
You say you are willing to contribute, but only if there is site and job security. That combination is exactly the problem. You want the security outcome first, while leaving the economics unresolved.

A fair contribution from labour must be **meaningful and durable**. That means:
- real labour-cost relief,
- no cosmetic time-limited adjustment that snaps back after a year,
- and no expectation that management absorbs all structural pain.

If the contribution is modest, temporary, or reversible, then it is not enough.

### To the regional state
I hear your concern about the political and regional impact of closures. I understand it. But a blocking stance against all structural change is not responsible governance if it leaves the company uncompetitive.

Supporting the industrial base does **not** mean preserving every current structure. It means supporting the package that keeps the business viable in 2030.

### To the controlling family and investors
I agree with both of you on one essential point: **cosmetic compromise is not enough**. We need savings that are real, lasting, and sufficient.

But let me be clear: if the insistence is “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no meaningful labour-cost change,” then what remains is a cost base the market will punish. That is not value preservation.

### My position
So here is the management line, plainly stated:

- We are open to a negotiated settlement.
- We are open to alternatives to closures if they deliver **comparable structural savings**.
- We are **not** open to a deal that preserves the current footprint and cost structure.
- We need a **genuine labour-cost contribution**, capacity reduction, and a sustainable workforce adjustment.
- If those conditions are not met, then deeper structural measures, including plant rationalisation, remain on the table.

### What I want in the next round
I want each side to stop repeating red lines and answer one question:

**What exact package closes the cost gap and restores competitiveness by 2030?**

If your answer is “keep every plant, keep all guarantees, and ask management to hope the market improves,” then we are nowhere near a solution.

If your answer is a quantified package with:
- substantial cost relief,
- capacity reduction,
- workforce adjustment,
- and binding commitments on future investment,

then we can talk seriously.

Until then, I will keep the structural options open, because I am not prepared to sign off on a deal that leaves this company uncompetitive.
