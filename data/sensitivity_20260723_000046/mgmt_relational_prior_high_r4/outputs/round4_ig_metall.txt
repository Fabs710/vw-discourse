I’ll respond plainly.

## Where I agree
I agree with the **works council** and the **state** that a restructuring cannot be a pretext for destroying the industrial base. I also agree with the **expert** that a serious non-closure package must be real, quantified, and durable — not symbolic.

And I agree with **management**, in one limited sense: the company does need a structural answer, not a temporary patch. We are not pretending the competitiveness problem does not exist.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept management’s repeated move to treat **closures** and **compulsory redundancies** as the default benchmark for seriousness.

That is exactly the wrong test.

A closure may produce more gross savings on a spreadsheet, but it also creates:
- industrial conflict,
- political veto risk,
- execution risk,
- loss of skills,
- damage to supplier networks,
- and lasting destruction of trust.

Those are not side issues. They are part of the economic calculation. If you ignore them, your “highest savings” case is incomplete.

So I reject the notion that:
**more theoretical savings = better restructuring**
regardless of the social and execution damage.

## To management
You still have not shown that a **hard hybrid** cannot close the gap.

The expert has been clear: a hybrid can deliver **most of the economically relevant savings** if it includes:
- real capacity reduction,
- lower labor cost,
- voluntary exits and attrition,
- overhead cuts,
- and binding commitments.

That is the burden of proof you have not met. Until you do, closure remains a **choice**, not a necessity.

If you want our cooperation, then stop speaking in generalities and put the numbers on the table:
- annual recurring savings
- one-time restructuring costs
- headcount effect
- capacity effect
- implementation risk
- and what part is supposed to come from labour, what part from management, and what part from ownership

## To the works council
I support your core red lines:
- no plant closures
- no compulsory redundancies
- no permanent wage erosion
- no weakening of co-determination

I also agree that “no closure today, closure tomorrow” is unacceptable.

But I want to be equally honest: if we are serious about avoiding closures, then we must be prepared to accept **real capacity reduction inside the network**. That means not cosmetic adjustments, but:
- shift reductions,
- model transfers,
- line simplification,
- elimination of duplicated overhead,
- internal redeployment,
- voluntary exits,
- and working-time flexibility.

That is a hard compromise, but it is the only path that keeps the plants open and still changes the economics.

## To the state government
Your role matters. But your blocking power must be used to stop **one-sided restructuring**, not to freeze every change.

I support you when you insist that the region is not a spreadsheet. I do **not** support turning that into a veto on all meaningful adjustment.

If you want the German industrial base preserved, then you should back a settlement that:
- keeps every German plant open,
- preserves viable roles for each site,
- protects co-determination,
- and makes the company invest in future products and skills.

That is the path to regional value. Not closures by stealth, and not hollow plants either.

## To the controlling family
If you are serious about stewardship, then back a deal that does **not** force workers to carry the whole burden.

Stewardship means:
- management contributes,
- owners contribute,
- and the workforce contributes in a bounded, fair way.

It does **not** mean pushing the union toward accepting closures as the price of being heard.

## To investors
You want durable, enforceable savings. Fine. So do we.

But risk-adjusted value cuts both ways:
- yes, closure can save more in a clean model,
- but no, that does not automatically make it superior if it triggers a major industrial conflict and weakens the company’s long-term performance.

A hard hybrid with binding commitments can be the better value solution if it is designed properly. The question is not “what is the maximum theoretical cut?” The question is “what actually survives implementation and restores competitiveness?”

## My position on the three bridge proposals

### 1) Hard hybrid package
This is the only serious basis for negotiation.

But only if it includes:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- real capacity reduction,
- voluntary exits and attrition only,
- temporary and fair employee contribution,
- binding investment and product commitments,
- and a credible verification mechanism.

If any of those are missing, it is not a hard hybrid. It is a disguised downsizing plan.

### 2) Phased restructuring with mutual trigger clauses
I remain skeptical, but not dismissive.

It can only be acceptable if it is truly **mutual**:
- if management misses investment, utilization, or product commitments, labour concessions end;
- if targets are met, the agreed flexibility continues.

What I will not accept is a trap where workers concede first and management gets a second bite at the apple later.

### 3) Site-role differentiation
This is only acceptable if every site remains a **viable industrial site**, not a shell.

Specialization is fine. Slow decline under a new label is not.

## My answer to the facilitator’s question
> What precise form and duration of employee contribution would you accept if it were tied to binding investment, product allocation, and a hard rollback clause if commitments are missed?

Here is the answer:

We can discuss a **time-limited employee contribution** if it is:
- temporary,
- proportional,
- clearly defined in duration,
- collectively bargained,
- reversible if commitments are broken,
- and matched by management and owner contributions.

What we cannot accept is a contribution that becomes:
- a permanent wage cut,
- a disguised reset of the collective agreement,
- or a one-way transfer of the crisis onto labour.

## What I will insist on
Any workable package must include:
1. **No plant closures**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **No permanent erosion of the collective wage framework**
4. **Real capacity reduction without site destruction**
5. **Fair, time-limited employee contribution**
6. **Management and owner contribution**
7. **Binding investment and product commitments**
8. **Rollback if the company fails to deliver**

## Bottom line
The workforce is not refusing change. We are refusing a restructuring where workers pay first, pay most, and bear all the risk.

If management wants a deal, it must prove that a **hard hybrid** can actually work. If it cannot, then it has not made a restructuring case — it has made a conflict case.
