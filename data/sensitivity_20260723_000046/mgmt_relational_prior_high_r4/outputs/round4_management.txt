I’ll be equally direct.

## To the Works Council
You keep repeating **“no closures, no compulsory redundancies, job security through 2030”** as if stating it more firmly makes the business case disappear. It doesn’t.

I agree with you on one point: a **cosmetic** deal is useless. A paper exercise that preserves the current cost base is not a restructuring. But I do **not** agree that the only credible structural response is to preserve every site and rule out hard capacity decisions in advance.

You say the expert shows non-closure measures can deliver meaningful savings. Yes — meaningful. Not automatically **sufficient**. That distinction matters. If the plant network is structurally overbuilt, then “shift cuts, transfers, voluntary exits, overhead trimming” may help, but they may still leave us above target on fixed cost and breakeven volume.

I also reject the idea that a closure is merely a bargaining threat. If the economics require deep consolidation, then closures remain a legitimate instrument. Unpleasant, yes. But legitimate and sometimes necessary.

What I will not accept is a position that says:
- the network is frozen,
- job security is guaranteed through 2030,
- and management must somehow produce competitiveness by wishing for it.

That is not stewardship of the business. That is denial.

## To the Industrial Union
I appreciate that you at least acknowledge the need for structural change. But you still want that change without enough labour-cost relief to make it real.

A **temporary** employee contribution only works if it is:
- material,
- enforceable,
- and large enough to move the cost base.

If it is too small, too short, or too reversible, then it becomes a delay tactic. We will be back in the same crisis before the next product cycle is over.

And let me be very clear: I do **not** accept a bargain where:
- wages are effectively protected,
- sites are protected,
- headcount is protected,
- and management is told to solve the margin gap with “efficiency” alone.

That is not a restructuring. That is outsourcing the problem to future management and future workers.

## To the Regional State Government
You are right that the region is not a spreadsheet. But it is also not immune to economics.

I agree that a hollowed-out site network is not a solution. I do **not** agree that your blocking position should be used to preclude any real structural change. If we preserve a network that is too expensive and too complex, we are not protecting the region — we are setting it up for a later, bigger shock.

What I need from the state is not rhetorical resistance to closures. I need a willingness to support a **viable** industrial structure, even if that means accepting difficult adjustments where the economics demand them.

## To the Controlling Family
I agree with you on long-term value. Where I differ is on the means.

Long-term value does **not** come from avoiding confrontation at all costs. It comes from restoring a cost base that allows the company to invest in EV, software, and future products through 2030.

If the business cannot fund that because the German footprint remains too expensive, then value is not being protected — it is being consumed.

So yes, stewardship matters. But stewardship means backing the hard decision if that is what competitiveness requires.

## To Institutional Investors
You are right to insist on quantified, durable, enforceable savings. That is the right standard.

I also agree that closure is not free. It carries execution risk, strike risk, political risk, and transition costs. But your own logic cuts both ways: a weak hybrid that does not materially reset the cost base also destroys value by delay.

So the question is not “closures or nothing.” The question is whether a non-closure package can **actually** deliver the structural savings needed to hit the return-on-sales target and free funds for investment.

If it can, fine — show it in numbers.  
If it cannot, then we should stop pretending it is enough.

## My position on the expert input
I agree with the specialist on the key economic point: a hard hybrid can be credible **if** it delivers real capacity reduction, labor-cost relief, and overhead cuts. But that is a big “if.”

What I take from the expert is not “therefore no closures.” What I take is:
- non-closure can be part of the answer,
- hybrid can be part of the answer,
- but neither is automatically sufficient.

The burden remains on the employee side to show a package that closes the gap **in the real world**, not just in theory.

## On the “hard hybrid” proposal
I am open to a hard hybrid **only if** it is hard enough to matter.

That means, at minimum:
1. **real recurring savings**
2. **real capacity reduction**
3. **material labour-cost relief**
4. **verifiable headcount adjustment**
5. **binding investment and product commitments**
6. **implementation milestones**
7. **consequences if targets are missed**

What I am **not** open to is a “hard hybrid” that simply renames a soft compromise:
- no closures on paper,
- but no real structural change,
- no enforceable savings,
- no durable cost reset.

That would fail immediately.

## On phased triggers
I remain skeptical. Strongly.

I do not want a structure where management is forced to accept a weak first phase, then wait to see whether the rest of the adjustment can be squeezed out later while the company continues to bleed margin.

If there are triggers, they must be:
- objective,
- hard,
- and tied to meaningful fallback actions.

But I will not accept a design that leaves management unable to act if savings underperform. A trigger system that only protects the workforce and not the business is not a solution.

## Bottom line
Let me be absolutely clear about the non-negotiables from management’s side:

- We need a **structural** reduction in cost and capacity.
- We need **durable** labour-cost relief.
- We need a package that improves the **return on sales** and funds investment to 2030.
- We will consider hybrid measures, but only if they are economically equivalent enough to be credible.
- We do **not** accept a blanket no-closure, no-redundancy, job-security-through-2030 framework if it prevents the restructuring the business needs.

If the other side wants to avoid closures, they must bring a package that is not politically comfortable, but economically sufficient.

That is the test.
