Let me respond directly.

### First: I agree with one point
The expert is right that this is not a game of slogans. We need **quantified, durable savings**. I welcome that clarity, because it exposes the real issue: whether the employee-side proposal actually closes the competitiveness gap, or whether it merely postpones it.

### But I disagree with the core premise coming from the works council and union
You keep repeating:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- job security through 2030,
- no structural erosion of the legacy framework.

That combination may be politically understandable, but economically it is **not credible as a binding starting point**. It assumes the company can restore competitiveness while preserving the existing cost structure and footprint almost intact. That is exactly what management cannot accept.

The independent analysis actually reinforces my position: **non-closure measures can help, but they are less likely to fully replicate the structural cost relief of closures or deep consolidation**, especially if the problem is as serious as we have said. In other words: your preferred model may reduce pain, but it does **not automatically solve the problem**.

### To the Works Council
You say management has not proven that every German plant must stay open only if workers take a hit. That is not the right test.

The real test is simpler:
- What is the gap to the required margin?
- What package closes it?
- And does that package change the cost base structurally, not just temporarily?

If you want to avoid closures, then you need to show how your alternative delivers **the same economic effect**. Not “some savings.” Not “less disruption.” The same structural effect.

So far, you have not done that.

### To the Union
You are saying “bounded and reversible” contributions. I have no objection to a contribution being time-limited **if** it is substantial enough to matter. But let’s not pretend that a temporary concession, even if well designed, is a substitute for a structural reset.

A temporary wage concession, a limited bonus freeze, or some flexibility on working time can help bridge the gap. They do not by themselves remove the burden of:
- excess capacity,
- too many fixed costs,
- and too much labor cost per vehicle.

If the company enters 2027 or 2028 still carrying the same structural excess, then we have failed.

### To the Regional State Government
You rightly warn against a value-destroying conflict. I agree. But a blocking posture that prevents hard restructuring also destroys value — just more slowly.

The state should not confuse preservation of every existing structure with protection of the region. A viable industrial base is better than a frozen one. If we protect every plant regardless of economics, we risk losing competitiveness altogether. Then the region loses jobs anyway, only later and with more damage.

### To the Controlling Family
You say governance stability matters. Correct. But stability without restructuring is just managed decline.

I also need to be blunt: the company cannot carry a cost base that is above target and still fund the EV transition, software, and future products to 2030. Those investments do not appear by wishing for consensus. They require money. That money comes from structural savings.

So I am not prepared to sign up to a package that leaves the balance sheet and operating cost base materially unchanged.

### To Investors
You are right to ask for numbers, and you are also right that a deal must be enforceable. But I want to be clear: if the numbers show that a non-closure package is insufficient, then I will not dress it up as success simply because it avoids confrontation.

That is the key point. We are not here to avoid hard decisions. We are here to make the company competitive.

### Where I am prepared to move
I remain open to a **hybrid package** if — and only if — it produces real structural savings. That would mean some combination of:
- capacity rationalization,
- material labor-cost contributions,
- reduced indirect costs,
- voluntary exits and attrition,
- internal redeployment,
- shift and working-time changes,
- and binding investment commitments.

But I need to be explicit: **a hybrid package is acceptable only if it includes sufficient structural reduction in cost and capacity.** If it is merely a way of preserving all sites while trimming around the edges, then it is not enough.

### What I cannot accept
I cannot accept:
- a blanket no-closure commitment,
- an absolute ban on workforce reduction,
- and a 2030 job-security guarantee
if those terms prevent the necessary structural reset.

That is not a negotiating detail. It is the core issue.

### My position for the next phase
Bring me a quantified package with:
1. the exact annual savings,
2. the headcount effect,
3. the capacity effect,
4. the timing of realization,
5. the cost of concessions,
6. and the enforceability mechanisms.

If a non-closure package truly delivers comparable structural savings, then I will consider it seriously. But if it does not, then I will not support a cosmetic compromise.

### Bottom line
I am **not** moving to your red lines.  
I am moving only on one condition: **equivalent structural effect**.

Anything less leaves the company uncompetitive, underfunded, and stuck with the same problem in a different form.
