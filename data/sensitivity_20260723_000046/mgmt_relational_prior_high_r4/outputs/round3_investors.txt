We will be direct.

## Where we agree
We agree with management, the family, and the experts on the central diagnosis:
- the current German cost base is too high,
- overcapacity is a real problem,
- and the company needs **durable** structural improvement, not a cosmetic gesture.

We also agree with the employee-side stakeholders on one point:
- a prolonged strike or a broken social compact would destroy value.

That part is important. Shareholders do **not** benefit from a scorched-earth conflict.

## Where we disagree
We do **not** accept the employee-side attempt to make
- no closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- and job security through 2030

into the starting point for any deal.

That may be understandable from a labour perspective, but as capital providers we have one test: **does the package restore competitive margins on a risk-adjusted basis?** If the answer is no, then it is not acceptable, even if it is politically elegant.

We also do **not** support management treating closures as the only serious option **without first proving the gap**. Closures may be justified, but they are not free:
- they increase execution risk,
- raise strike probability,
- and can destroy value if the transition is messy.

So neither side gets a blank cheque.

## Our position
We would support **only** a package that is:
1. **quantified**
2. **durable**
3. **enforceable**
4. **large enough to restore margins**
5. **credible in execution**

That means we can support:
- a hybrid package,
- or even a non-closure package,

**if and only if** it produces real structural savings and a credible return to competitive profitability.

## What must be in any acceptable package
At minimum:
- measurable recurring cost reduction
- real capacity reduction, not just accounting reshuffling
- material workforce adjustment, preferably through voluntary exits and attrition first
- meaningful labor-cost relief
- management and owner contribution
- binding investment and product commitments for the German sites
- clear milestones and consequences if targets are missed

## What we will not support
We will not support:
- a cosmetic compromise that preserves the existing cost base,
- a deal that simply kicks the problem into 2027/2028,
- or a settlement that leaves margins structurally impaired.

We also will not support a closure-driven solution if management cannot show that:
- the savings are large enough **net of transition costs**,
- the execution risk is manageable,
- and the strike/political risk does not wipe out the gain.

## On the current debate
### To management
You still need to show the numbers. “Structural reset” is not enough. We want:
- annual savings,
- one-time costs,
- timing,
- headcount impact,
- capacity impact,
- and the expected return-on-sales effect.

If closures are necessary, prove it. If they are not, stop using them as leverage and bring a serious hybrid.

### To the works council and union
A non-closure package can be credible **only if** it is real, not symbolic. “No closures” is not enough if the result is a still-uncompetitive company. If you want to avoid closures, you need to accept:
- genuine capacity reduction,
- meaningful temporary contributions,
- and enough workforce adjustment to make the economics work.

### To the state and the family
Governance stability matters, but so does economic reality. Do not back a deal that protects the structure while weakening the business. That is value destruction by delay.

## Bottom line
Our support is conditional and narrow:
- **Yes** to a tough, quantified restructuring that restores competitiveness.
- **Yes** to a hybrid or non-closure solution **if it closes the gap in practice**.
- **No** to a cosmetic settlement.
- **No** to a deal that leaves the cost base uncompetitive.
- **No** to a closure path that destroys more value than it creates.

If the sides want our backing, bring a package that clearly shows the path back to competitive margins. Without that, we would rather walk away than endorse a structurally weak compromise.
