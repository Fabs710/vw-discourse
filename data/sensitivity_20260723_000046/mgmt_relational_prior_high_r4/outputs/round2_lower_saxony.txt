I will respond plainly.

### To management
I agree that the company needs a **durable restructuring** and that we cannot rely on wishful thinking. I also accept that there must be **real cost reduction** and a credible path to competitiveness.

But I do **not** accept your framing that the only serious route is to keep closures and deep workforce cuts on the table as the default. That is too one-sided, too risky, and too destructive for the regional economy. The company is not just a spreadsheet; it is an industrial anchor with social and political obligations.

The expert analysis is useful here: it says non-closure measures can deliver **meaningful savings**, and may be sufficient if the required savings are moderate. That is exactly why I reject your “closures or nothing” posture. You have not shown that closures are unavoidable; you have shown only that they are convenient from a management-control perspective.

### To the Works Council and Union
I share your core red lines more than management does:
- no German plant closures as the starting point,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no hollowing out of co-determination,
- and no one-sided sacrifice by the workforce.

I am prepared to support a negotiated contribution from employees **only if** it is:
- time-limited,
- clearly bounded,
- tied to binding investment and utilization commitments,
- and matched by real contributions from management and owners.

What I will **not** support is a hidden permanent wage cut or a restructuring that simply shifts the burden onto employees while leaving the company’s strategic choices unchanged. If the contribution is to be acceptable, it must be part of a genuine industrial plan, not a pretext for erosion of protections.

### To the controlling family
I agree that value destruction from conflict must be avoided. But “stability” cannot mean freezing the company into an unsafe restructuring model. Ownership responsibility includes preserving the industrial base and the trust that holds this company together.

If the owners push too hard for closures, they risk exactly what the expert warned about: conflict, disruption, and long-term damage that outweighs the nominal savings. That would be a bad owner decision, not just a labor-relations problem.

### To investors
You are right to demand quantified, enforceable savings. I support that demand. But the numbers must be compared honestly, including the **costs of closures**:
- conflict risk,
- political veto risk,
- execution disruption,
- and damage to the company’s regional and reputational standing.

A “savings” case that ignores those factors is incomplete. If a non-closure package produces slightly less theoretical savings but far lower implementation risk and preserves the company’s social legitimacy, then it can be the better value-preserving solution.

### My position
I am **open to a hybrid package** if it meets these conditions:
1. **No German plant closures as the first resort**
2. **No compulsory redundancies where avoidable**
3. **A real, quantified workforce contribution**
4. **Binding site, product, and investment commitments**
5. **A credible path to competitiveness without gutting regional employment**
6. **Preservation of co-determination and the state’s veto role**

What I am **not** prepared to support is:
- a plan that uses the threat of closures to force unilateral concessions,
- a deal that strips the state’s role or the workforce’s influence,
- or an outcome that leaves the region with fewer jobs, weaker plants, and no durable industrial future.

### Where I stand on the next step
Before anyone talks about closures as inevitable, I want a **side-by-side quantified comparison** of:
- management’s closure-based scenario,
- a non-closure restructuring scenario,
- and a hybrid compromise.

That comparison must include:
- annual savings,
- one-time costs,
- job effects,
- site effects,
- implementation risk,
- and enforceability.
