We agree with the basic diagnosis from management and the family owners: **the current German cost base and capacity footprint are not sustainable if the company is to restore margins and compete credibly**. A cosmetic response would be value-destructive, and we would not support it.

Where we **do not** align is on any approach that treats plant closures or large-scale forced layoffs as the default solution, because that creates three investor concerns immediately:

1. **execution risk** from prolonged labor conflict,
2. **one-off disruption** that can destroy near-term earnings and volume,
3. **structural damage** if the restructuring weakens the brand and operating stability without producing durable savings.

From our perspective as capital-market shareholders, the test is simple: **does the package credibly and durably restore competitive margins and return on sales?** If yes, we can support painful measures. If no, we will not back a settlement just because it is framed as “tough.”

### To management
We support the insistence on **material, durable cost reduction**. But you need to be careful not to overplay closures as the only credible instrument. If you can achieve comparable savings through capacity rationalization, working-time flexibility, compensation restraint, and headcount reduction via attrition and voluntary exits, that is likely superior from a risk-adjusted value perspective.

What we need from you is not rhetoric about resolve, but a **numbers-based bridge to competitive margins**:
- quantified savings,
- timing,
- enforcement,
- and proof that the cost base will stay down.

If the plan still leaves German operations structurally uncompetitive, then it is not sufficient.

### To the Works Council and Union
We hear the red lines on closures and compulsory redundancies, but we will be blunt: **a package that protects everything and changes little is not acceptable**. Investors have already absorbed years of pressure on margins and returns. We are not prepared to underwrite a settlement that preserves historical guarantees while failing to restore competitiveness.

That said, if you are willing to trade flexibility, working-time changes, and a bounded employee contribution for **credible site protection and investment**, that is a serious basis for support. What matters is not who “wins” in principle, but whether the company emerges with:
- lower unit labor costs,
- lower excess capacity,
- and a realistic path back to target returns.

### To the Regional State Government
We agree that a disorderly industrial conflict would be harmful. But a blocking posture that prevents structural adjustment would be equally harmful, just more slowly. The region’s interest is not the preservation of every current site configuration; it is a **viable industrial base with jobs that survive because the company is competitive**.

### To the Controlling Family
We agree that governance stability matters and that value destruction from conflict must be avoided. But stability cannot become a shield for delay. If the family wants market confidence to hold, it needs to back a plan that shows real cost discipline and credible implementation. “Negotiated” is not enough unless it is also **economically sharp**.

### Our position
We are **prepared to support a tough restructuring** if it delivers:
- durable cost reductions,
- measurable capacity rationalization,
- a credible workforce adjustment,
- and a clear return to competitive margins.

We are **not** prepared to support:
- a purely symbolic compromise,
- a deal that leaves the cost base structurally impaired,
- or an endless conflict that destroys value while preserving legacy structures.

So our message is:
**Show us a fully quantified, enforceable restructuring that restores competitiveness.**
If the only way to get there is through closures, then that case must be proven convincingly.
But if comparable savings can be achieved without closures, that is the better outcome for shareholders — and we would support it.
