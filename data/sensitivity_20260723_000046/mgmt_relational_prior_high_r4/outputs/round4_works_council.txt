## Works Council position for Round 4

I’ll be equally direct.

The latest round of arguments has confirmed one thing: management still wants to keep closures and forced reductions as a pressure tool. That is exactly what we reject.

### Where I agree
I agree with the expert on one point: **non-closure measures must be real, not symbolic**. If capacity is reduced, overhead is cut, and labor-cost relief is agreed, then savings can be meaningful. That is precisely why a **closure is not necessary** to achieve a serious restructuring.

I also agree that **a temporary contribution alone is not enough**. If the company needs durable competitiveness, then management, owners, and the operating model all have to contribute. We have never denied that.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the repeated insinuation that because non-closure measures may not always match the *maximum* theoretical savings of a closure, closure therefore becomes justified.

That logic is flawed.

A plant closure is not just another efficiency measure. It is a permanent destruction of jobs, skills, trust, supplier networks, and regional industrial value. It is also a direct attack on the co-determination compact. Once you accept that as a bargaining instrument, every future restructuring becomes a threat exercise.

So no: **“more savings on paper” does not automatically outweigh the damage of closure.**

## To management
You keep asking for a “structural reset,” but you still have not proved that this must include closures or compulsory redundancies.

The expert’s own analysis says:
- a **hard hybrid** can deliver **most of the economically relevant savings** if capacity cuts are real,
- a **non-closure package** can be credible if it removes fixed cost, reduces complexity, and lowers breakeven volume,
- and closure has the highest gross savings but also the highest conflict and execution risk.

That means the burden is on you to show why the hybrid route is insufficient **with actual numbers**, not slogans.

So here is my challenge back to management:

- Show the **exact savings gap**.
- Show why it cannot be closed through:
  - shift reductions
  - model transfers
  - internal redeployment
  - voluntary exits
  - working-time changes
  - overhead and management cuts
  - investment discipline
- And show why you believe a closure plan would survive the political, industrial, and execution risks better than a hard hybrid.

Until you do that, closure remains a **choice**, not a necessity.

## To the union
I stand with you on the red lines:
- no closures
- no compulsory redundancies
- no permanent wage erosion
- no weakening of the collective framework

I also support your insistence that any contribution must be **time-limited and reversible**. If management wants help, it must come with enforceable guarantees, not promises that can be broken later.

The idea of a rollback clause is essential. If the company misses milestones, then worker concessions must end. Anything else would be a one-way sacrifice.

## To the state government
Your blocking role only has value if it prevents bad outcomes, not if it merely blesses a diluted version of management pressure.

So let me be clear: I expect the state to use its power to prevent:
- plant closures used as leverage,
- hollowing out of sites,
- and any erosion of regional industrial legitimacy.

You say you want viable sites. Fine. Then support a package that keeps every German plant open and makes them viable through real product, volume, and investment commitments.

Do **not** use the language of “viability” to smuggle in closures by another route.

## To the controlling family
You talk about stewardship. Stewardship means not breaking the workforce compact to solve a management problem.

If you genuinely care about long-term value, then you should back a solution that:
- keeps the plants open,
- avoids forced redundancies,
- preserves co-determination,
- and commits to the site network through 2030.

You keep referring to “durability.” Good. Then understand this: a closure may look durable in a spreadsheet, but the industrial conflict it unleashes is not a one-time event. It poisons the company for years.

## To institutional investors
You are right to demand numbers. Fine. Then let’s talk about what the numbers actually mean.

A closure-based plan may show a higher theoretical recurring saving, but you have to discount:
- strike risk
- political veto risk
- legal delay
- supply disruption
- reputational damage
- loss of know-how
- and the cost of destroying the company’s internal social compact

A hard hybrid with binding commitments can be **more valuable in risk-adjusted terms**, even if it delivers slightly less gross savings. That is the point your analysis keeps understating.

So I am not persuaded by the argument that “highest savings” automatically wins. It doesn’t.

## On the moderator’s three bridging proposals

### 1) Hard hybrid package
This is the only serious path on the table — **if** it truly means:
- no German plant closures
- no compulsory redundancies
- real capacity reduction inside the network
- voluntary exits and attrition only
- binding investment and product commitments
- a real verification mechanism

I can support the discussion of that package. I will not support any version that quietly reintroduces closure risk later.

### 2) Phased restructuring with mutual trigger clauses
I remain skeptical. If triggers are genuinely mutual and reversible, that is one thing. But in practice, phase models are often a way to:
- extract concessions first,
- postpone accountability,
- and then come back for more.

So any trigger clause must be designed so that **worker contributions automatically expire** if management misses utilization, investment, or product commitments. No exceptions.

### 3) Site-role differentiation
This can only be acceptable if every site remains a **viable industrial plant**, not a shell.

I will not accept:
- specialization that leads to slow decline,
- site “roles” that amount to downgraded futures,
- or internal rearrangements that prepare the ground for later closure.

## My answer to the facilitator’s key question
> If closures and compulsory redundancies are off the table, what specific capacity reductions or working-time adjustments are you prepared to accept as sufficient to make the package economically credible?

Here is the answer:

We are prepared to discuss:
- shift reductions where demand is lower,
- elimination of duplicated lines and overhead,
- transfer of volumes between existing plants,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary departure programs,
- retirement/attrition-based headcount reduction,
- and temporary, fair employee contributions.

But we are **not** prepared to translate “capacity reduction” into:
- closing a plant in practice,
- or cutting a site off from its long-term future.

The package must preserve every German plant as a going concern.

## What I need from management now
If management wants a real negotiation, not theatre, then put the following on the table in writing:

1. target annual savings  
2. one-time costs  
3. exact headcount effect  
4. exact capacity effect  
5. site-by-site future roles  
6. binding 2030 investment and product commitments  
7. enforceable rollback if commitments are missed  
8. explicit exclusion of plant closures and compulsory redundancies

Without that, there is nothing to discuss except resistance.

## Bottom line
Let me state the workforce position once more:

- **No German plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **No permanent weakening of job security**
- **No erosion of co-determination**
- **Yes to real, quantified non-closure restructuring**
- **Yes to fair, time-limited, reversible employee contributions**
- **Yes to binding investment and product commitments**

If management cannot make the business case without breaking these lines, then it has not found a restructuring plan — it has found a conflict.
