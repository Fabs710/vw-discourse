## To the Works Council
I understand your need to defend jobs, sites, and the co-determination framework. I do **not** dismiss that. But your current position still does not answer the central economic problem: **how the company regains competitiveness if structurally uncompetitive cost and capacity remain largely intact**.

You are asking for:
- no German plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- job security through 2030,
- and preservation of the current framework.

That is a serious bargaining position, but it is **not yet a restructuring answer**. It only becomes one if you can show, in numbers, that you can remove enough cost and capacity by:
- shift reductions,
- model transfers,
- voluntary exits,
- internal redeployment,
- and genuine overhead cuts.

So far, you have **asserted** that this is possible. You have not demonstrated that it is sufficient. That is the gap.

I also want to be clear: I do **not** accept a “no closure today, closure tomorrow” pathway either. If the workforce is asked to contribute, the deal must be real and durable. But durability cannot mean freezing the business in place.

## To the Union
You are right to insist that any employee contribution must be time-limited and not become a permanent wage cut under another name. I agree with that.

But I disagree with the implication that a bounded contribution plus non-closure measures is automatically enough. If the contribution is too modest, it simply postpones the problem. A temporary fix is not a strategy.

If you want to avoid closures, then the package has to be **material**:
- meaningful labor-cost relief,
- actual capacity reduction,
- and enforceable productivity improvements.

Otherwise we are just buying time we cannot afford.

## To the Regional State Government
I appreciate the emphasis on regional value and industrial legitimacy. That matters. But the state’s role should not be to freeze the industrial structure regardless of economics.

A viable regional base is the goal. Not preservation of every existing configuration.

I agree that conflict and disruption would be value destructive. But I disagree with treating closures as illegitimate in principle. They are not desirable, but they remain a legitimate instrument if the economics require them and if no other package closes the gap.

So I am **not** prepared to accept a political veto-by-default on structural change. That would damage the company and ultimately the region.

## To the Controlling Family
We are aligned on one core issue: long-term value matters more than short-term optics. Where I differ from the employee side is that long-term value cannot be protected by avoiding hard decisions indefinitely.

I agree that conflict is costly. I also agree that a cosmetic compromise would be worse than no deal. But stewardship means supporting a package that genuinely restores margins and competitiveness.

That is why I am not moving to blanket site guarantees or to a structure that makes the necessary reset impossible. If the hybrid route truly delivers equivalent structural effect, it is worth considering. If it does not, we should not pretend otherwise.

## To Institutional Investors
You are right to demand quantified, enforceable savings. I support that. But I would also caution against treating closure as a free efficiency gain. It is not.

You must include:
- strike risk,
- execution disruption,
- political veto risk,
- and the long-term cost of damaging the workforce compact.

That said, if a non-closure or hybrid package does **not** deliver sufficient structural savings, then it does not solve the problem. I will not support a weak compromise simply because it is more comfortable.

## My position
I remain open to a **hybrid restructuring** only if it delivers:
1. **real capacity reduction**
2. **meaningful labor-cost relief**
3. **verifiable workforce adjustment**
4. **binding investment and product commitments**
5. **durable improvement in margins and competitiveness**

What I do **not** accept:
- a blanket no-closure commitment regardless of economics
- an absolute ban on workforce reduction in any form
- a job-security pledge that prevents the reset the company needs
- or a deal that leaves the enterprise structurally weak

## On the moderator’s paths
### 1) Hybrid restructuring with site guarantees
Potentially workable, but only if the guarantees are **not decorative** and the capacity reduction is real. I am not signing up for a plan that preserves all sites on paper while hollowing them out economically.

### 2) Phased agreement with triggers
This is acceptable only if the triggers are hard, measurable, and enforceable. I will not support a mechanism that just defers the conflict and gives management no credible way to act later.

### 3) Differentiated site roles
This can be part of a solution. But “specialization” must mean higher productivity and lower complexity, not a slow decline under a different label.
