Let me be very clear in this round.

I hear the moderator’s attempt to turn this into a “hybrid compromise” exercise. But from the workforce side, the starting point does **not** change:

- **No German plant closures**
- **No compulsory redundancies**
- **Binding job security through 2030**
- **Preservation of co-determination and the job-security framework**

Anything that undermines those four pillars is not a compromise; it is a dressed-up retreat by labour.

## To management
You keep saying you need “structural” savings and that anything less is cosmetic. Fine. Then show us a structural plan that does **not** rely on closures or forced layoffs.

What you have not done is prove that the only credible route is to sacrifice plants and jobs. You keep asserting it. That is not the same as demonstrating it.

The expert already made one point that matters: **non-closure measures can deliver meaningful savings** and, in some cases, a very large share of what is needed. That means your closure-first framing is not the only economic option. It is a preference, not a necessity.

So I reject the idea that the workforce must accept plant closures or compulsory redundancies because management wants a faster route on paper.

## To investors
You ask for “durable” and “enforceable” savings. So do we. But you are cherry-picking the durability of cost cuts and ignoring the durability of industrial damage.

A closure is not a neutral efficiency measure. It destroys trust, skills, supplier networks, regional value, and the internal compact that has made this company strong for decades. If you want risk-adjusted value, then you must count:
- strike risk,
- execution disruption,
- political veto risk,
- reputational damage,
- and the long-term cost of blowing up the workforce relationship.

Your “slightly lower savings” argument for hybrid options is exactly why a non-closure route is the sensible one. If a hybrid can protect the network and still save materially, then closures are not justified. If the hybrid saves less than closures, that does **not** automatically make closures acceptable. It means management must cut elsewhere, not use the workforce as the fallback.

## To the controlling family
Ownership responsibility is not just about margin. It is about stewardship. Stewardship means you do not break the industrial base to solve a management problem.

You say you want long-term value. Then support a solution that preserves the workforce compact and the German site network. A family owner should understand that once trust is broken, it is not easily restored.

## To the regional state government
You are right to insist that the region is not a spreadsheet. But I need more than rhetoric about regional value. The state must use its blocking role to **prevent closure as a bargaining chip**.

If there is a genuine industrial future, then it must be built on:
- site security,
- product commitments,
- investment,
- qualification,
- and internal redeployment.

Not on threatening shutdowns to force concessions.

## To the union
We are aligned on the core red lines, but I want to sharpen the definition of what can be discussed.

A “time-limited employee contribution” is only acceptable if it is:
- strictly temporary,
- proportional,
- tied to binding guarantees,
- and reversible if the company fails to deliver on investment and utilization.

Anything else becomes a permanent transfer from labour to capital.

I will not endorse a package where workers pay today and management keeps the option to reopen the same attack tomorrow.

## On the moderator’s hybrid / phased / differentiated-site suggestions
These are not neutral technical designs. They are ways of softening the language around what is, in practice, a structural rollback.

### 1) “Hybrid restructuring with site guarantees”
A hybrid is only acceptable if it means **real savings without closures**. If it means “keep the sites but make them weaker, smaller, and more vulnerable,” then it is not a compromise I can support.

### 2) “Phased agreement with trigger-based review”
I am highly sceptical. Trigger-based logic often becomes an exit ramp for management:
- first you win temporary concessions,
- then you argue the triggers were not met,
- then you reopen the pressure for closures or layoffs.

That is not stability. That is a staged concession trap.

### 3) “Differentiated site roles”
This is only acceptable if it preserves every German plant as a viable industrial site, not as a token shell. Relegating sites to reduced or marginal roles is often the first step toward eventual closure. We know that pattern. We will not be naïve about it.

## My answer to the specific question posed to the works council
> If management presents a package with no closures, no compulsory redundancies, and binding investment commitments, but requires measurable capacity cuts and a time-limited employee contribution, what exact form of capacity reduction would you accept as the minimum needed to call this a genuine compromise?

My answer is this:

A genuine compromise must remove **overcapacity without removing plants**. That means:
- shift reductions where needed,
- internal reallocation of volumes,
- transfer of models between existing plants,
- elimination of duplicated overhead,
- voluntary exit programs,
- and working-time adjustments.

What I would **not** accept is “capacity reduction” that translates into:
- a plant being hollowed out,
- a site losing its long-term future,
- or a hidden pretext for eventual closure.

If management wants a number, then the burden is on management to present the savings gap and show how much can be closed **without closures or forced redundancies**. I am not going to invent a percentage to bless a process that I fundamentally distrust.

## What I will concede
I will not concede on the core line. But I will say this:

If management puts on the table a **binding, enforceable, non-closure package** with:
- credible investment commitments,
- product allocation for German sites,
- voluntary and attrition-based workforce reduction only,
- temporary and fair employee contributions,
- and a genuine co-determination role in implementation,

then we are prepared to negotiate the details of working-time, internal redeployment, and qualification.

That is as far as I will go.

## What I will not accept
I will not accept:
- “no closure today, closure tomorrow”
- “no layoffs in name, forced exits in practice”
- “temporary contribution” becoming a permanent wage cut
- a phased process designed to wear the workforce down
- any weakening of the job-security framework through 2030
- any erosion of co-determination under the guise of flexibility

## Bottom line
If the company cannot restore competitiveness without plant closures and compulsory redundancies, then management has failed to make the case for a socially responsible restructuring.

And if management can restore competitiveness **without** those measures, then it should stop using them as leverage and put forward the honest non-closure plan now.

That is the test.
