Yes. Here is the comparison in a compact, decision-useful form.

## 1) Relative economics of the three restructuring paths

### A. Closure-based plan
**Typical profile**
- **Recurring savings:** highest
- **One-time costs:** highest
- **Headcount reduction:** highest
- **Capacity reduction:** highest and most permanent
- **Implementation risk:** high
- **Conflict risk:** very high
- **Long-term regional value:** weakest, unless the remaining network becomes clearly stronger

**Economic logic**
A closure removes the most fixed cost and the most persistent overcapacity. In automotive manufacturing, that is usually the strongest lever for improving breakeven volume and return on sales.

**Main drawback**
The transition costs are large: severance, write-downs, logistics disruption, political and industrial conflict, and the risk that execution damage eats part of the nominal gain.

---

### B. Non-closure plan
**Typical profile**
- **Recurring savings:** moderate at best
- **One-time costs:** lowest to moderate
- **Headcount reduction:** moderate, usually slower
- **Capacity reduction:** moderate, often partial
- **Implementation risk:** lower initially
- **Conflict risk:** lower
- **Long-term regional value:** highest

**Economic logic**
This works only if there is enough duplication in the current network to remove meaningful cost without shutting plants. It depends heavily on:
- shift cuts,
- voluntary exits,
- working-time changes,
- overhead reductions,
- model transfers,
- and labor-cost relief.

**Main drawback**
If structural overcapacity is real and durable, a pure non-closure package often does not go far enough. It may reduce cash burn and buy time, but not fully restore competitiveness.

---

### C. Hybrid plan
**Typical profile**
- **Recurring savings:** medium to high
- **One-time costs:** medium
- **Headcount reduction:** medium to high
- **Capacity reduction:** meaningful, but usually less than closure
- **Implementation risk:** medium
- **Conflict risk:** medium
- **Long-term regional value:** better than closure, weaker than pure non-closure

**Economic logic**
This is often the best compromise in industrial restructuring when:
- the network has duplicated capacity,
- but closure triggers excessive conflict or political blockage,
- and management needs a durable but not maximal cost reset.

A hybrid becomes economically credible if it includes **real capacity removal inside the existing network**, not just temporary concessions.

---

## 2) Practical savings ranking

In steady-state terms, the ranking is usually:

**Closure-based > Hybrid > Non-closure**

But on a **risk-adjusted basis**, the ranking can change if closure triggers:
- prolonged strikes,
- legal blockage,
- supply disruption,
- delayed implementation,
- or major reputation damage.

Then a **well-designed hybrid** can outperform closure in net present value, even if its gross savings are smaller.

---

## 3) What matters most in this case

From an industrial-economics perspective, the central question is:

### Can a no-closure or hybrid package remove enough of the fixed-cost base and capacity surplus to lower the breakeven volume materially?

If yes, it can be a rational solution.

If no, then it is likely only a postponement of the same restructuring problem.

In a large German automotive plant network, the most important variables are:
1. **fixed overhead removed**
2. **labor cost per vehicle**
3. **effective capacity reduction**
4. **voluntary exit volume**
5. **durability of concessions**
6. **execution friction**

A temporary employee contribution helps, but it does not by itself solve a structural overcapacity problem unless it is paired with real capacity and overhead reduction.

---

## 4) My quantitative judgment, stated cautiously

Without company-internal data, I would use this rule of thumb:

- **Pure non-closure** can sometimes deliver **a large minority** of the savings needed, but often not the full structural fix.
- **Hybrid** can often deliver **most of the economically relevant savings** if capacity cuts are real and labor-cost relief is material.
- **Closure-based** maximizes gross savings but also carries the largest transition cost and conflict risk.

So if management wants a **durable step-change**, the most plausible alternative to closure is not “no closure at all,” but a **hard hybrid** with:
- real capacity rationalization,
- voluntary exits/attrition,
- overhead cuts,
- time-limited labor contribution,
- and enforceable investment/product commitments.

---

## 5) Bottom line

If the question is **“which path saves the most?”**  
→ **Closure-based**

If the question is **“which path is most likely to work without major execution failure?”**  
→ Often **hybrid**

If the question is **“which path best preserves long-term regional value?”**  
→ **Non-closure**, but only if it is economically sufficient, which is not guaranteed
