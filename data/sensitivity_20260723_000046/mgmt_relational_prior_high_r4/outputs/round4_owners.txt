## To the Works Council
I agree with you on one point: a cosmetic deal is worthless. If “non-closure” just means preserving the current cost base and postponing the adjustment, I would reject it too.

But I do **not** accept your central claim that because a hard hybrid is preferable socially, it is therefore automatically sufficient economically. You have repeated that non-closure measures can be “real.” I accept that. What you have **not** shown is that they are enough to restore competitiveness **at the required scale and durability**.

That is the gap.

You keep framing closures as a bargaining weapon or a management preference. From my perspective as controlling owner, closures are not desirable, but they remain a legitimate governance option if the network remains structurally overbuilt and the alternative cannot close the margin gap. I will not accept a position that rules that out in advance.

Your red lines may be understandable, but they do not answer the economic problem. If a hard hybrid truly delivers comparable recurring savings, then show it in numbers, site by site. If it does not, then your position is asking the owners to accept a structurally weaker company for the sake of avoiding hard decisions. That is not stewardship.

## To the Industrial Union
I agree with your insistence that any employee contribution must be temporary, fair, and reversible. I would go further: if the company misses agreed milestones, then employee concessions must fall away. That is reasonable.

Where I disagree is your tendency to treat any serious cost reset as “workers paying for the crisis alone.” That is too simple. The company is facing structural pressure, not a management communication problem. If the labor side wants a no-closure settlement, then the contribution must be material enough to matter. A symbolic contribution is not serious enough for the situation we are in.

## To the Regional State Government
I support your role in protecting regional value, but I disagree with the implication that preserving regional value means preserving the current footprint unchanged.

It does not.

A viable industrial base is better than an oversized one. If the company remains too expensive to compete, then the region eventually loses more than it would through a difficult but successful restructuring. I will not support a governance posture that freezes the structure and thereby weakens the business.

## To the Controlling Family
I agree with you that stewardship and long-term value matter more than short-term optics. That is exactly why I am not prepared to endorse a weak compromise.

A hard hybrid is acceptable only if it produces:
- real recurring savings,
- real capacity reduction,
- meaningful labor-cost relief,
- verifiable headcount adjustment,
- and durable margin improvement.

If it does not, then it is not a solution; it is delay.

## To Institutional Investors
I agree with your emphasis on quantified, enforceable savings and on risk-adjusted value. That is the right test.

I also agree that closures are not free. They carry execution risk, strike risk, political risk, and transition costs. But the inverse is also true: a non-closure package that is too soft or too reversible simply defers the problem and destroys value more slowly.

So I am not choosing “closures first.” I am choosing “credible structural reset first.” If a hard hybrid can prove it closes the gap, I will consider it. If it cannot, then we should not pretend that avoiding closures has solved anything.

## My position
I remain open to a **hard hybrid** only if it is hard enough to matter.

That means:
1. **real capacity reduction**
2. **material labor-cost relief**
3. **verifiable workforce adjustment**
4. **binding investment and product commitments**
5. **durable improvement in margins and return on sales**
6. **clear enforcement if targets are missed**

What I do **not** accept:
- a blanket no-closure rule regardless of economics
- an absolute ban on compulsory workforce reduction in any form
- a job-security pledge that freezes the company’s structure through 2030
- or a compromise that leaves the enterprise structurally uncompetitive

## What I need before I move
The conversation is now at the point where words are no longer enough. I want a **site-by-site quantified package** comparing:
- annual recurring savings
- one-time costs
- capacity reduction
- headcount effect
- implementation risk
- and the expected impact on return on sales

If the hard hybrid closes the competitiveness gap credibly, then it deserves support.

If it does not, then I will not hide behind polite formulas. The company needs the option to take deeper structural measures.
