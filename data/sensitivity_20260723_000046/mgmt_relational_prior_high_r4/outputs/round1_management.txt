Let me respond very clearly.

I agree with one point: this crisis cannot be solved by a short-term cosmetic exercise. We do need a durable industrial solution, and we do need investment in future products, technology, and utilization. On that, we have common ground.

But I fundamentally disagree with the core red lines you are setting as absolute, because they do not reflect the economic reality we are facing.

### What is not negotiable for management
- The current cost base in Germany is too high.
- The current capacity footprint is too large for the European market we actually have.
- The brand cannot reach its return-on-sales target without structural action.
- If we do not reduce cost and capacity materially, we will simply postpone the crisis and weaken the group further.

So I need to be explicit: **I am not prepared to accept a deal that preserves every plant, preserves every job, and leaves the underlying structure unchanged.** That would not be restructuring. That would be avoidance.

### On plant closures
You say closures are off the table. I cannot accept that as a starting premise. The company must have the option to close or significantly consolidate sites if that is what the economics require. I am not making a symbolic threat; I am saying the board and owners will not fund permanent overcapacity.

That said, I am not insisting on closures as an ideological goal. If you can bring forward an alternative package that achieves the **same structural savings** through other means, then I am prepared to examine it seriously. But it must be comparable in scale, durability, and enforceability.

### On compulsory redundancies
Forced layoffs are not my preferred route, but I also cannot promise there will be no headcount reduction. If we avoid closures, then we still need substantial labor-cost and staffing relief. That can come through a mix of:
- attrition,
- voluntary programs,
- internal redeployment,
- working-time reductions,
- and negotiated employee contributions.

But the outcome must still be a **real reduction in headcount and cost**. A promise to protect every job through 2030 while also asking management to restore competitiveness is, frankly, incompatible.

### On wages and guarantees
I reject the idea that the existing wage and protection framework is untouchable. The end of the old guarantees was not a rhetorical move; it reflected a structural shift in the economics of the business.

We are prepared to discuss alternatives to blunt wage cuts, but the employee side must contribute materially. That means:
- lower labor costs,
- flexibility in working time,
- possible reduction or suspension of certain payments,
- and a revision of legacy guarantees.

If there is no employee contribution, there will be no credible restructuring.

### What I can accept
I remain open to a package that avoids outright closures **if** it delivers:
1. a significant and durable reduction in capacity,
2. a genuine labor-cost contribution,
3. a material reduction in total employment cost,
4. a binding path to restore competitiveness,
5. and clear investment commitments for the sites that remain.

### What I cannot accept
I will not accept a proposal that:
- protects all sites unconditionally,
- rules out meaningful capacity reduction,
- leaves German cost levels essentially intact,
- and asks management to pretend that competitiveness will somehow return on its own.

That is not a plan. It is a delay.

So my message is simple: if the employee side wants to avoid closures, then bring forward a package that is **equally tough in economic terms**. If you cannot, then you are asking the company to keep a structure it cannot afford.
