I will be very direct.

## To management
I agree with you on one point: the company needs a **durable restructuring** and it cannot simply “wish away” the competitiveness problem. The German cost base has to come down, and the business must regain room to invest.

Where I **do not** agree is your attempt to keep **closures and compulsory redundancies** as the implicit default while treating the workforce and the state as obstacles. That is not a neutral economic position; it is a choice to put the burden of adjustment first and hardest on the regional industrial base.

The expert input is important here: it says a **hard hybrid** can be economically credible if it includes real capacity reduction, labor-cost relief, and overhead cuts. That means you have **not** proved that closures are necessary. You have proved only that they remain an option if negotiations fail. That is a very different statement.

So my position is:

- I accept the need for **real savings**
- I accept the need for **real capacity adjustment**
- I do **not** accept closures as the starting point
- I do **not** accept compulsory redundancies as the main instrument
- I do **not** accept a deal that weakens the state’s co-determination and veto role

If you want my support, then bring a package that preserves the German industrial anchor and shows, in hard numbers, that the gap can be closed **without gutting sites or jobs**.

## To the works council
I largely agree with your core line:
- no plant closures,
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no hollowing out of sites,
- no weakening of co-determination,
- and no permanent erosion of the collective framework.

That said, I also hear the expert clearly: a non-closure solution must be **real**, not symbolic. So I do not think the answer is simply to say “nothing changes.” If the company has structural overcapacity, then there must be **real internal adjustment**.

What I would support is a package that preserves all German plants **as viable industrial sites**, while allowing:
- shift reductions
- line simplification
- model transfers
- internal redeployment
- voluntary exits and attrition
- temporary, fair, reversible employee contribution
- targeted working-time flexibility

That is the only kind of adjustment I can support. I will not back a “specialization” model if it is just a slow-motion closure in disguise.

## To the union
I agree strongly that any employee contribution must be:
- strictly time-limited,
- proportional,
- collectively bargained,
- and reversible if management fails to deliver.

I also agree that a temporary contribution cannot become a permanent wage cut through the back door.

But I need to be equally clear: the contribution must be **material enough to matter**. If it is too small, then we merely buy time and return to the same crisis later. That would be irresponsible to the company and to the region.

So I am open to a serious labor contribution, but only if it is matched by:
- management discipline,
- owner responsibility,
- binding investment,
- product commitments,
- and real capacity reduction **without closures**.

## To the controlling family
I agree that stewardship matters. But stewardship is not served by using the threat of closures to force a one-sided settlement.

If long-term value is the standard, then the question is not only what saves the most on paper. It is what preserves:
- industrial trust,
- skills,
- regional legitimacy,
- and the company’s role as an economic anchor.

A family owner should understand that destroying the workforce compact can damage the enterprise for years. So I would urge you to support a **hard hybrid**, not a shortcut to closures.

## To institutional investors
You are right that the company needs quantified, enforceable savings. I do not dispute that.

But I do dispute any logic that says: “If closures save more in theory, then closures are automatically the superior answer.” They are not, because they carry:
- strike risk,
- veto risk,
- execution disruption,
- political fallout,
- and long-term damage to the company’s social compact.

Risk-adjusted value matters. A hybrid that is hard enough can be economically superior to a closure plan if it is more likely to be implemented cleanly and durably.

## My position on the three bridge options

### 1) Hard hybrid package
This is the only serious route I can support at this stage, **if** it includes:
- no German plant closures
- no compulsory redundancies
- real capacity reduction
- voluntary exits and attrition first
- temporary employee contribution
- binding investment and product commitments
- enforceable review and rollback provisions

That is the minimum. Anything weaker is not a settlement; it is deferral.

### 2) Phased restructuring with mutual trigger clauses
I remain cautious.

A trigger model is only acceptable if it is genuinely **mutual** and not a trap for the workforce. That means:
- if management misses investment, utilization, or product commitments, labor contributions end or reverse;
- if targets are met, the agreed flexibility continues.

I will not endorse a phased deal that quietly prepares a second round of pressure for closures later.

### 3) Site-role differentiation
This can be acceptable only if every German site remains a **going concern**. I will not support “specialization” that is really a polite way of saying one site is being prepared for decline.

## On the expert’s finding
The expert has confirmed the crucial point: **non-closure measures can capture a large share of the needed savings, but not automatically all of them**. That is exactly why management must now show the numbers site by site, not simply repeat that closures are available.

The burden is on those calling for deeper cuts to demonstrate:
- the exact savings gap,
- why it cannot be closed by a hard hybrid,
- and why the regional damage of closures would be justified.

Until that is shown, I will not move from my position.

## My bottom line
I will support a negotiated restructuring only if it:
- protects the state’s co-determination and veto role,
- preserves the German site network,
- avoids mass layoffs and plant closures,
- and still delivers real, durable competitiveness improvements.

I am **not** prepared to sign up to a settlement that uses the region as the shock absorber for management failure.
