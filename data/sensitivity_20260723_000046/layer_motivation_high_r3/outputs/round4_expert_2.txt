## Short answer
A **non-closure package** becomes financially equivalent to a **closure-based package** only if it delivers **most of the same durable annual savings** with **meaningfully lower execution risk and lower one-time cost**.

In practical screening terms, I would use:

- **Non-closure equivalence threshold:** about **70–85% of closure-based run-rate savings**
- **plus** materially better implementation probability
- **plus** lower restructuring cash cost
- **plus** low risk of later erosion/renegotiation

If it lands below that band, it usually stops being a true substitute and becomes a delay mechanism.

---

## Decision framework

### 1) Compare risk-adjusted NPV, not gross savings
For each scenario:

\[
NPV = PV(\text{annual savings} \times \text{probability of realization}) - \text{one-time restructuring cost} - \text{delay cost} - \text{erosion risk}
\]

The comparison should include:

- annual EBIT uplift from cost savings
- one-time severance/exit/relocation/transition costs
- implementation delay from strikes, vetoes, legal action, or board deadlock
- probability-weighting of realization
- durability of savings through 2030+

---

## 2) What makes closure-based restructuring attractive
A closure package usually has:

### Advantages
- larger structural savings
- clearer long-run footprint reduction
- stronger fixed-cost reset

### Disadvantages
- high one-time cash cost
- high conflict risk
- high delay risk in a co-determined German governance setting
- possible partial value destruction if blocked or watered down

So closures are not automatically best; they are only best if they can actually be implemented.

---

## 3) What makes non-closure packages viable
A non-closure package can compete if it includes enough of the following:

- working-time reduction
- wage moderation / temporary labor-cost contribution
- voluntary exits
- redeployment
- shift elimination / line consolidation
- product specialization by site
- SG&A / overhead reduction
- productivity gains
- management and owner contribution
- binding durability clauses

It must be more than a temporary truce.

---

## Rule-of-thumb threshold
If the closure case yields, say, 100 units of annual run-rate savings, then a non-closure case is financially credible if it can deliver approximately:

- **70–85 units of durable run-rate savings**
- with lower upfront cost
- and materially higher implementation probability

If it only delivers 40–60 units, it is usually not equivalent unless the closure case is highly likely to fail or be delayed severely.

---

## Sensitivity logic
The break-even point shifts with execution risk:

### Non-closure becomes more attractive when:
- closure is likely to be blocked
- strikes or legal delays are expected
- one-time closure costs are large
- management needs faster time-to-benefit

### Closure becomes more attractive when:
- non-closure concessions are temporary or reversible
- capacity remains structurally too high
- savings leak over time
- labor framework prevents sufficient fixed-cost reduction
