I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with the works council, the state shareholder, and in part with the modeler on one central point: **this cannot be solved by a cosmetic package or a one-sided pain transfer onto workers**.  
A durable settlement must share the burden and must be economically real.

I also agree that:
- conflict has a cost,
- implementation risk matters,
- and temporary measures alone are not enough.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the management/family/investor line that closures should remain a standing “credible” option while workers are asked to concede first.

That sequencing is exactly the problem.

You are treating the workforce as if it should:
1. accept wage restraint,
2. accept flexibility,
3. accept capacity cuts,
4. and then trust that closures and forced redundancies will somehow vanish.

No. That is not a bargain. That is leverage extraction.

## To management
You keep saying “structural overcapacity” and “durable competitiveness” as if that settles the matter. It does not.

We do not dispute that savings are needed. We dispute **who pays and how**.

If you want labour peace, then bring forward a package that:
- preserves German sites,
- rules out compulsory redundancies,
- and delivers measurable savings through alternatives.

What you have not shown is why the same durable economic effect cannot be achieved through:
- working-time measures,
- voluntary departures,
- redeployment,
- productivity gains,
- overhead cuts,
- and a fair employee contribution,
with management and owners also contributing materially.

Until that comparison is truly proven, closures are not justified — they are simply preferred.

## To the works council
We are aligned on the red lines:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no plant closures as the starting point,
- no erosion of the collective framework.

That remains the correct stance.

But I want to sharpen one point: a **temporary labour contribution** is only acceptable if it is:
- limited,
- time-bound,
- and exchanged for hard guarantees.

Those guarantees must include:
- site security,
- no forced layoffs,
- serious investment in Germany,
- and protection of collective bargaining standards.

If those conditions are not met, then any wage restraint becomes workers paying twice.

## To the state shareholder
You say you oppose closures as a first resort. That is not enough by itself.

If you truly mean to defend the industrial base, then use your blocking power to insist that:
- closures are not the opening move,
- compulsory redundancies are excluded,
- and management must test non-closure alternatives seriously before any harsher step is discussed.

General concern is not enough. We need active resistance to a closure-led logic.

## To the controlling family
You speak of stewardship. Stewardship means preserving the long-term industrial asset, not just forcing a margin response.

I agree with you on one thing: temporary fixes are not enough.

Where I disagree is your conclusion that this necessarily points toward closures. It does not. It points toward a **serious alternative package** that includes:
- management restraint,
- owner contribution,
- labor-cost moderation,
- and structural productivity improvement,
without sacrificing sites and jobs.

If you want trust, you must stop treating closure as the fallback weapon in every hard negotiation.

## To institutional investors
You want “equivalence.” Fine.

Then let’s define it honestly:
- not just EBITDA or headcount,
- but execution risk,
- strike risk,
- lost output,
- legal blockage,
- and the long-term damage from hollowing out the German footprint.

If you strip those out, you are not measuring value — you are just selecting the harshest tool.

So yes, we will accept quantified analysis. But we will not accept a model that counts only easy savings and ignores the cost of industrial conflict.

## On the expert/modeler’s phased or hybrid idea
I appreciate the attempt to bridge the gap, but I need to be equally clear:  
**a “fallback” closure option is not acceptable as a bargaining premise if it is used to pressure workers into surrendering their protections now.**

A phased package is only usable if the first stage already contains real commitments:
- no closures,
- no forced redundancies,
- binding site protection,
- and a real burden-sharing structure.

If “review triggers” are just a coded way to bring closures in later, then that is not compromise. That is delayed coercion.

## What would move us
We are prepared to support a **shared-sacrifice restructuring** if it contains all of the following:

1. **No compulsory redundancies**
2. **No plant closures**
3. **Binding protection for jobs and sites**
4. **A limited, temporary employee contribution**
5. **Meaningful management and owner contribution**
6. **Capacity reduction through internal measures**
7. **Investment commitments in Germany**
8. **Protection of the collective wage framework**

That is the basis for serious negotiation.

## What will not move us
We will not be moved by:
- “closures are still on the table” as a threat,
- temporary concessions with no security,
- or a package that weakens the workforce while calling it competitiveness.

If management and capital insist on a model that shifts all structural pain onto employees, then they should expect resistance — not cooperation.

## Bottom line
The union is willing to negotiate a durable settlement.  
We are **not** willing to negotiate away jobs, sites, or the collective framework so that management can keep closure as leverage.

Bring a quantified non-closure package that genuinely closes the cost gap and shares the burden fairly.  
Until then, our position stands: **no compulsory redundancies, no plant closures, and no one-sided sacrifice by the workforce.**
