I will respond plainly, because this discussion is now circling the same point: **everyone says they want a durable solution, but the employee side is still asking us to guarantee the outcome before the savings are secured. That does not work.**

## Where I agree
I agree with several points made across the table:

- This cannot be a cosmetic deal.
- A short-lived patch would be useless.
- Conflict, strikes, and legal deadlock would damage everyone.
- We need a package that is economically real and implementable.
- Future investment in Germany matters.

On that, we are aligned.

## Where I disagree — fundamentally
I do **not** accept the works council’s and union’s repeated premise that:

- **all German plants must remain open**,  
- **no compulsory redundancies are allowed**,  
- **closures may not even remain a credible fallback**,  
- and yet the company should still be expected to restore competitiveness and fund its future.

That is not a restructuring plan. That is a request to freeze the current structure and hope the economics fix themselves.

That is unacceptable.

## To the works council
You say closures are “a managed shutdown by another name.” That is not correct.

A managed restructuring is exactly what we are trying to do: reduce structural overcapacity, cut fixed costs, and restore medium-term margins. If the industrial footprint is too large for the demand outlook and cost position, then refusing to adjust it is not defending the company — it is weakening it.

You keep demanding a fully quantified alternative that preserves every German site and avoids all forced job losses. I have been clear already: **if you can show a package with equivalent durable savings and lower execution risk, I will consider it**.

But you have not done that. What you have offered is:
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- productivity improvements,
- temporary contributions,
- and investment commitments.

Those are useful tools. But by themselves, they do **not** yet demonstrate a durable structural reset of the required scale.

And no, a 2030 guarantee is not the minimum condition for trust if it blocks the very adjustment the company needs to survive competitively. Trust is earned through a viable package, not by locking in inefficiency.

## To the union
You say temporary labor contribution is only acceptable if paired with hard guarantees. I reject that sequencing.

We do not get hard guarantees first and structural savings later. The company needs the economic reset now, and the protections must be part of a balanced deal — not a precondition that prevents the deal from being real.

You also say closures are simply a management preference. No. Closures remain on the table because the company has a structural competitiveness problem, not because we enjoy confrontation.

If a non-closure package can produce:
- sufficient fixed-cost reduction,
- real capacity reduction,
- durable labor-cost relief,
- and implementation certainty,

then fine. We will support it.

But a package built mainly on:
- temporary restraint,
- limited exits,
- and vague assurances of future cooperation

will not close the gap.

That is not a serious answer to the current cost position.

## To the state shareholder
You emphasize the industrial base and I understand why. But protecting the base requires more than protecting every location in its current form.

If the company remains structurally uncompetitive, then the industrial base erodes anyway — just more slowly and with less control. That is the real risk.

So I welcome your insistence that this should not be a closure-first reflex. I agree with that. But I do **not** accept the idea that closures should be effectively ruled out in advance.

If non-closure measures truly deliver the necessary savings, then they will be preferable. But if they do not, then blocking deeper structural change simply postpones the crisis.

That would be a failure of stewardship.

## To the controlling family
You talk about stewardship and I agree that this is the right lens. Stewardship means protecting the long-term enterprise value.

That is exactly why I am insisting on structural measures.

Where I disagree is your attempt to frame closures as merely a last-resort abstraction that can be left hanging in the background while we all negotiate around it. The background option matters because it changes bargaining reality. If the employee side refuses to engage on meaningful structural adjustment, then yes, closures remain part of the necessary set of tools.

You want a quantified comparison. Fine. The comparison must be serious:
- annual savings,
- ramp timing,
- one-time costs,
- execution risk,
- strike risk,
- and the effect on return-on-sales and future investment capacity.

That is exactly the framework we need.

But I will not accept a model that treats the avoidance of plant change as automatically superior, regardless of whether it actually fixes the economics. That is not stewardship. That is sentiment.

## To institutional investors
You are right to insist on risk-adjusted NPV and not slogans. I agree.

But I want to be equally clear: a model that counts only gross savings and ignores implementation risk is also incomplete. That is why I have been saying from the beginning that a purely closure-led approach is not automatically the best option.

At the same time, a model that counts only conflict cost and ignores the cost of preserving excess capacity is equally incomplete.

So let’s be honest:
- non-closure measures are acceptable **only if they materially and durably reduce the cost base**;
- closure remains necessary if they do not.

That is not ideological. That is financial discipline.

## On the expert/modeler’s phased or hybrid ideas
I appreciate the attempt to bridge the gap, but I need to be very direct: **a “no closures now, maybe later” package does not solve the core issue unless the later option is real and enforceable.**

What I will not accept is a structure where:
- management gives up the only credible adjustment lever,
- while the employee side gives only temporary concessions,
- and the company is left with the same cost problem in two years.

That is not a settlement. It is a delay.

If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then the alternative must be **equivalent in durable economic effect**. Not symbolic. Not partial. Equivalent.

That means real measures on:
- headcount,
- capacity,
- labor cost,
- productivity,
- and fixed overhead.

If those are not there, then the no-closure demand is simply not compatible with our fiduciary duty.

I am **not** prepared to accept:
