We will be direct.

## Our position
As capital-market shareholders, we support a restructuring that **credibly and durably restores competitive margins**. We do **not** support a cosmetic compromise, a temporary labor truce, or a package that leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive.

At the same time, we are **not married to plant closures as such**. If a non-closure or hybrid package can deliver the **same durable economic effect** with lower execution risk, we would prefer that. But the burden of proof sits with those rejecting closures. So far, we have not seen a quantified alternative that clearly closes the gap.

---

## To management
We agree with your core diagnosis:
- the cost base is too high,
- overcapacity is real,
- and the brand needs a margin reset to fund future investment.

We also agree that **temporary fixes are not enough**.

Where we disagree is on the need to frame closures as an almost default fallback without first exhausting a serious quantified comparison of alternatives. In this governance structure, closures may be economically credible, but they are also likely to be **implementation-risk heavy**. A strategy that looks decisive but gets bogged down in strikes, vetoes, and delay destroys value fast.

So our support is conditional:
- show us the **savings target**,
- show us the **NPV**,
- show us the **execution probability**,
- show us the **time-to-benefit**,
- and show us that the package is durable, not temporary.

If non-closure measures really cannot deliver the needed fixed-cost reset, then say so plainly with numbers. We are willing to accept hard measures if they are economically justified. But “necessity” by itself is not enough.

---

## To the works council
We agree with you on one important point:  
**conflict costs are real**, and so is the loss of skills and industrial capability if restructuring is mishandled.

Where we disagree is on your insistence that no closures and no compulsory redundancies should be treated as categorical starting points, regardless of the economics. That is not a serious investor test. A shareholders’ duty is not to preserve every site; it is to preserve enterprise value.

If you want closures off the table, then bring an alternative that is:
- quantified,
- durable,
- enforceable,
- and large enough to matter.

Working-time flexibility, voluntary exits, redeployment, productivity gains, and temporary employee contributions are all reasonable tools. But unless they produce real permanent savings, they are not a substitute for structural capacity reduction.

And to be blunt: a 2030-style guarantee that freezes the footprint while the cost problem remains unresolved would be a red flag for us. We would not back a settlement that protects short-term comfort but undermines long-term competitiveness.

---

## To the union
We agree that employees should not carry the whole burden. A temporary wage contribution or bonus moderation can be part of a fair package only if it is matched by real commitments from management and owners.

But we do **not** accept the sequencing that says:
1. guarantee sites and jobs first,
2. then ask capital to believe the economics will somehow work out later.

That is backwards.

If the labor contribution is modest and reversible, it will not solve the structural problem. If it is large enough to matter, then it must be paired with durable capacity and cost reduction. Anything less is a delay tactic dressed up as social partnership.

---

## To the state shareholder
We understand your concern for the regional industrial base, and we respect the blocking power you bring. But your role cannot be to make restructuring impossible in advance.

A responsible anchor shareholder should help ensure:
- the company remains viable,
- the German footprint remains meaningful,
- and the long-term capital base is not hollowed out.

That requires discipline, not just protection. If non-closure measures can deliver the required savings, fine. If not, then blocking necessary change would be value-destructive.

So we support your insistence on avoiding a closure-first reflex. We do **not** support using political leverage to preserve an uncompetitive structure.

---

## To the controlling family
We agree with your stewardship framing more than you might think: long-term value matters, not just quarterly optics.

Where we differ is in the implication that closures remain a bargaining lever primarily to pressure labor into conceding. In this governance system, that approach risks triggering the very value destruction you say you want to avoid.

We want the same thing you claim to want:
- durable competitiveness,
- investment room,
- and an implementable deal.

So prove it. Put a real scenario comparison on the table. If non-closure measures can achieve most of the savings with better implementation certainty, that is the better value outcome. If they cannot, then closures may be justified — but only after the numbers have been tested, not assumed.

---

## Our financial test
We are looking for a package that delivers:

1. **Material annual fixed-cost reduction**
2. **Durable effect through 2030 and beyond**
3. **High probability of implementation**
4. **Lower risk of protracted strikes or deadlock**
5. **A credible return to target margins**

That is the standard.

We are open to:
- selective capacity reduction,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- working-time instruments,
- productivity and overhead cuts,
- management restraint,
- owner contribution,
- and investment commitments.

We are **not** open to:
- a cosmetic compromise,
- a temporary peace treaty,
- or a settlement that leaves the company structurally weak.

---

## On the expert input
The expert/modeler’s basic framework is right: the real question is **risk-adjusted NPV**, not slogans.

From that standpoint:
- closures likely have the highest gross savings,
- but also the highest conflict and delay risk,
- while non-closure packages are only acceptable if they get close enough to the same durable effect.

That is the key point. We are not ideologically attached to closures. We are attached to **credible margin restoration**.

If the employee side can produce a rigorous non-closure alternative with equivalent risk-adjusted economics, we would welcome it. If not, then deeper structural measures remain necessary.

---

## Bottom line
Our position is straightforward:

- **Yes** to a durable restructuring.
- **Yes** to burden-sharing.
- **Yes** to alternative measures if they genuinely close the cost gap.
- **No** to cosmetic deals.
- **No** to a settlement that preserves uncompetitive margins.
- **No** to blanket exclusions of necessary structural change.

If management and the employee side cannot produce a quantified, enforceable non-closure package with equivalent economic effect, then closures cannot be ruled out. Not because we prefer disruption, but because we cannot support a solution that fails the value test.
