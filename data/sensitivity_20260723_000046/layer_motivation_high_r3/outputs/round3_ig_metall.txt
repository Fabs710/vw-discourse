I’ll be equally direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with all sides on one point: **this cannot be a cosmetic arrangement**.  
The company has a real structural problem, and everyone here knows that a short-lived patch or a vague promise will fail.

I also agree that **burden-sharing must be real**. Employees cannot be asked to absorb the full cost while management, owners, and the balance sheet are protected.

And I agree that **implementation risk matters**. A deal that cannot be delivered because it explodes into strikes, legal blockages, and supervisory-board deadlock is not a solution.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the idea that the only serious restructuring path is plant closures plus forced redundancies.

That is not a neutral “economic necessity.” It is a choice about who pays, who loses security, and whose future is sacrificed first.

### To management
You keep describing closures and workforce reductions as “structural” and therefore unavoidable. That is too simplistic.

If you want labour peace, then bring a **quantified comparison**:
- how much cost reduction is actually required,
- what can be achieved through capacity reduction without closures,
- what comes from management-side savings,
- what comes from owners,
- and what remains for labour to contribute.

Right now, you are asking the workforce to accept the most painful instruments first, while offering only the promise that this is “necessary.” That is not enough.

I am not moved by the argument that “trust does not pay the bills.” No, but neither does destroying trust and then hoping productivity survives.

### To the works council
We are aligned on the core red lines:
- no compulsory redundancies,
- no plant closures as the starting point,
- no dismantling of the collective framework.

That remains correct.

But I want to be clear: **if employees are asked to contribute, it must be part of a genuine package, not an open-ended wage sacrifice**. Temporary restraint, bonus waivers, or flexibility can only be discussed if they are matched by:
- binding site protection,
- no forced layoffs,
- investment in German production,
- and a clear future for the workforce.

You are right that workers should not pay twice for management’s mistakes. That is exactly why any labour contribution must be conditional and limited.

### To the state shareholder
I appreciate your position that closures should not be the default and that regional industrial strength matters.

But it is not enough to say you oppose closures in principle. Use your blocking position to insist on a **serious negotiated alternative**. That means:
- management and owners contribute materially,
- capacity is reduced through non-closure measures where possible,
- and the workforce is not made the sole adjustment variable.

If the state shareholder wants a socially legitimate solution, it must push for a package that preserves jobs and sites while still restoring competitiveness.

### To the controlling family
You speak of stewardship, and I take that seriously. Stewardship means protecting the long-term industrial asset, not just preserving a quarterly target.

I agree that value destruction from prolonged conflict is real. But I do not accept the implication that because of that, the workforce should concede major structural losses up front.

If you want a workable settlement, then support a deal in which:
- management contributes through overhead and execution discipline,
- owners contribute through capital support and realistic expectations,
- and employees contribute through limited labour-cost moderation and flexibility.

That is stewardship.  
A plan that shifts the whole burden onto workers is not.

### To institutional investors
You are right to insist on quantified savings and durable margins. That is legitimate.

But your “equivalent economic effect” test must include the real costs of:
- strike risk,
- lost production,
- legal and governance deadlock,
- and the erosion of skills and commitment.

If you strip those out, you are not evaluating realism; you are just preferring a hard cut and hoping the social system absorbs it.

So yes, bring numbers. But bring the **full** numbers.

## What would move us
We are open to a serious package if it contains all of the following:

1. **No compulsory redundancies**
2. **No German plant closures**
3. **Binding protection for sites and jobs**
4. **A fair employee contribution that is temporary and limited**
5. **Meaningful management and owner contribution**
6. **Capacity reduction through non-closure measures**
7. **Investment commitments for future products and technology in Germany**

That is the line for negotiation.

## What is not acceptable
Let me be explicit. We will not accept:
- closures presented as a foregone conclusion,
- forced layoffs,
- a one-sided wage cut,
- or a “deal” that weakens the collective wage framework and leaves insecurity in place.

## My challenge back to management
If you claim closures are unavoidable, then demonstrate it with a scenario comparison that includes:
- execution risk,
- conflict cost,
- industrial capability loss,
- and long-term margin impact.

If you cannot show that, then your closure logic is not proven — it is just asserted.

## Conclusion
The union will support a **shared-sacrifice restructuring**.  
We will **not** support workers bearing all the pain while management and owners escape structural responsibility.

If the company wants a durable settlement, it must be built on:
- burden-sharing,
- no forced redundancies,
- protection of the collective framework,
- and a future for German production.

If that is not on the table, then we will use every instrument available to defend jobs, wages, and the collective agreement.
