From the perspective of dispersed institutional shareholders, the central question is not who “wins” the industrial conflict. It is whether the eventual package **credibly restores competitive margins, protects long-term returns, and avoids a cosmetically negotiated outcome that leaves the company structurally impaired**.

### Where we agree
We agree with management, the family holding, and even the workforce representatives on one point: **this cannot be a symbolic settlement**. The company’s cost base, capacity footprint, and return-on-sales performance are not where they need to be. If the outcome merely preserves the status quo with softer language, that would be value-destructive.

We also agree that **prolonged conflict is itself costly**. Strikes, reputational damage, production disruption, and uncertainty all erode enterprise value. We do not want a drawn-out confrontation that burns cash and weakens the balance sheet.

### Where we disagree
We do **not** support any outcome that leaves the company uncompetitive in the medium term.

That means:
- no settlement that keeps the full cost base intact,
- no arrangement that preserves excess capacity without real offsetting savings,
- no “future investment” narrative that is not backed by measurable margin improvement,
- and no deal whose main effect is to defer restructuring pressure until the next crisis.

So to the works council and union: your insistence on **no closures, no forced redundancies, and a binding guarantee through 2030** is understandable, but it is not acceptable **unless** you can show how the same economic effect will be achieved. If the answer is simply “capacity reductions, redeployment, and temporary labour restraint,” that is not enough by itself. We need **durable, quantified cost relief**.

To management: we also do not accept a tactic of presenting plant closures as the only serious option before all credible alternatives are tested. If the company has not exhausted negotiated alternatives that produce equivalent savings, then pushing closures too early risks triggering the very blocking coalition that makes implementation impossible. In that case, you may be right on economics and still fail on execution.

To the state shareholder and family owners: we recognize the need for industrial stability, but stability is not value if it preserves a structurally weak earnings base. If the compromise protects every site but leaves margins structurally below target, then shareholders are simply underwriting inertia.

### What we require
Our support depends on three conditions:

1. **A credible and durable path to competitive margins**
   - measurable cost reduction
   - reduced structural overcapacity
   - better productivity
   - no vague promises

2. **Deal certainty**
   - a settlement that can actually be implemented
   - not one that invites immediate re-litigation, legal blockage, or another round of conflict in 12 months

3. **Fair burden-sharing, but not burden avoidance**
   - employees may contribute
   - management and owners may need to contribute too
   - but the total package must be large enough to matter economically

### Our position on the broad options
- **We would support deep structural cuts, including plant closures, if they are genuinely necessary to restore competitiveness and if no equivalent negotiated package exists.**
- **We would also support an alternative package without closures if, and only if, it delivers comparable and durable savings with clear execution certainty.**
- **We will not support a cosmetic compromise that claims social peace while leaving the cost base uncompetitive.**

### Bottom line
We are not attached to a particular instrument. We are attached to the outcome:
**a credible return to competitive margins, durable restructuring, and an implementable settlement.**

If management can prove that a negotiated alternative delivers the same economic effect as closures, we can support that. If not, then we will not oppose hard restructuring merely because it is politically difficult. What we cannot accept is delay dressed up as compromise.
