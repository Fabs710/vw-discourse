We are aligned with management, the family holding, and the state shareholder on the **diagnosis**: this is a real structural crisis, not a cosmetic one. The company needs a durable reset in cost and capacity. We also agree with the workforce-side parties that **a prolonged conflict would destroy value**. So the shared objective is clear.

But from the capital markets’ perspective, the only test that matters is this:

**Does the package credibly and durably restore margins, or does it merely postpone the problem?**

On that standard, we are not prepared to back any solution that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

## Where we agree
- **Management is right** that the current cost base is too high and that the company needs structural action.
- **The works council and union are right** that strike risk, lost output, and governance deadlock are economically real and must be counted.
- **The family and state shareholders are right** that a settlement must be durable and not just a temporary patch.

## Where we disagree
### 1) “No closures” as an absolute red line
We do **not** support a blanket no-closure position if the numbers do not work.  
If non-closure measures cannot close the cost gap on a durable basis, then refusing closures in principle is not responsible stewardship — it is value preservation at the expense of competitiveness.

### 2) Closures as the default opening move
At the same time, we do **not** support management treating plant closures as the automatic first answer. In this governance structure, that is likely to trigger blockage, strikes, and delay. If a negotiated package can achieve equivalent savings with less disruption, that is preferable.

### 3) Temporary contributions without structural effect
We also will not support a deal that leans on temporary wage restraint, voluntary exits, or one-off concessions while leaving the underlying fixed-cost problem intact. That is not restructuring; it is deferral.

## What we need to see
We need a **quantified comparison** of scenarios, not repeated assertions.

At minimum:
1. **Required annual savings**
2. **Duration of effect**
3. **One-time restructuring cost**
4. **Execution risk**
5. **Impact on return-on-sales**
6. **Probability of delay through conflict or veto**

The expert’s framing was useful: the real issue is **risk-adjusted NPV**, not slogans about toughness or social protection. On that basis, non-closure options are acceptable only if they deliver something close to the economic effect of closure-based restructuring.

## Our position on the main packages
### We could support:
- capacity reduction through internal measures if it is materially meaningful,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits and redeployment,
- productivity and overhead reduction,
- temporary but limited labor-cost moderation,
- management and owner contribution,
- and investment commitments in Germany,

**but only if** the package clearly restores competitive margins and is enforceable.

### We would not support:
- a cosmetic settlement,
- a freeze of the current cost structure,
- a guarantee that prevents any real adjustment,
- or a package that trades short-term peace for long-term decline.

## On the social and political arguments
We respect the concern for jobs, sites, and industrial capability. But we cannot endorse an outcome that protects every legacy structure while leaving shareholders with a permanently weak earnings base. That would be unfair to owners and ultimately harmful to the company’s future investment capacity.

## Bottom line
Our support is available for **deep, durable, implementable restructuring**.  
It is **not** available for a compromise that preserves uncompetitive costs or offers only temporary relief.

If management and the employee side can produce a quantified non-closure package with genuinely equivalent economic effect, we will consider it seriously. If not, then deeper structural measures, including closures, remain on the table because the company’s long-term value comes first.
