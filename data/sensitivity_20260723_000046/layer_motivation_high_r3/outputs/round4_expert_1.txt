Yes. From a turnaround finance/modeling perspective, the central question is not whether closures are “good” or “bad” in the abstract, but whether a non-closure package can deliver **comparable risk-adjusted economic value** after accounting for:

- annual cash savings,
- one-time restructuring charges,
- implementation timing,
- strike / veto / legal risk,
- and the probability that savings actually stick.

## Decision framework

The financially relevant comparison is:

### Closure-based scenario
Typically:
- larger gross fixed-cost reduction,
- higher one-time cash costs,
- higher governance and industrial conflict risk,
- longer implementation timeline if blocked,
- stronger structural certainty if executed.

### Non-closure scenario
Typically:
- lower one-time cash outlay,
- lower symbolic/political rupture,
- potentially faster to agree,
- but often smaller and less durable savings,
- and greater risk that the package erodes over time unless tightly enforced.

---

## What “equivalent economic effect” means

A non-closure package is financially equivalent only if it can deliver:

1. **Similar annual EBIT improvement**
2. **Similar cash cost reduction**
3. **Similar durability through 2030+**
4. **High probability of implementation**
5. **Low leakage risk from later reversals or renegotiation**

In practice, that means I would test it on a **risk-adjusted NPV** basis.

---

## Rule of thumb threshold

Without company-specific data, I would use this practical threshold:

A non-closure package is economically credible only if it can capture roughly **70–85% of the annual run-rate savings** of a closure-based plan, **and** do so with materially lower execution risk and materially lower one-time cost.

If it captures less than that, it usually becomes a delay mechanism rather than a true substitute.

Why that range?
- Because closures usually deliver the deepest structural savings.
- But closures also carry high failure risk in German co-determination settings.
- So a somewhat smaller savings package can still be rational if it is much easier to implement and much cheaper upfront.

---

## Simple break-even logic

A non-closure package can “beat” closures if:

\[
NPV_{non-closure} = PV(\text{savings}) - \text{restructuring cost} - \text{execution risk penalty}
\]

exceeds:

\[
NPV_{closure} = PV(\text{larger savings}) - \text{higher restructuring cost} - \text{delay / conflict penalty}
\]

So the key is not just savings size; it is:
- time to benefit,
- probability-weighted realization,
- and avoidance of conflict-induced delay.

---

## Illustrative model ranges

If we were screening this decision, I would think in rough bands like this:

### Closure scenario
- **Annual run-rate savings:** high
- **One-time cost:** high
- **Execution probability:** medium to low unless governance support is secured
- **Time to full benefit:** 1–3 years
- **NPV sensitivity:** very sensitive to delay

### Non-closure scenario
- **Annual run-rate savings:** medium to high, but usually less than closure
- **One-time cost:** medium
- **Execution probability:** higher if negotiated
- **Time to full benefit:** often faster
- **NPV sensitivity:** sensitive to slippage and partial dilution

If non-closure measures can produce savings quickly and reliably, they can outperform closures even with smaller nominal savings.

---

## What would make a non-closure package credible

It needs to be **quantified in four buckets**:

### 1. Labor cost
- temporary pay moderation,
- bonus/variable compensation reduction,
- working-time changes,
- removal or suspension of allowances/guarantees.

### 2. Capacity and footprint
- shift reductions,
- line consolidation,
- product specialization by site,
- reduced overtime,
- redeployment out of overstaffed areas.

### 3. Headcount
- voluntary exits,
- retirement bridging,
- redeployment,
- attrition.

### 4. Productivity / overhead
- indirect headcount cuts,
- procurement/SG&A reduction,
- process redesign,
- industrial utilization gains.

If the package does not visibly move all four levers, it is unlikely to close the structural gap.

---

## Main risks in a non-closure package

The biggest risks are:

- **savings leakage** over time,
- **reopening of concessions** after the first tranche,
- **insufficient headcount reduction**,
- **underestimated overhead rigidity**,
- **capacity remaining too dispersed**,
- **delayed EBIT impact** if implementation is staged too gently.

In my experience, non-closure packages often fail not because the arithmetic is impossible, but because the measures are too soft, too temporary, or too easily renegotiated.

---

## My modeler’s conclusion

### Financially, a non-closure package becomes acceptable if:
- it closes most of the gap to closure-based savings,
- it is front-loaded enough to matter before the next product cycle,
- and it is contractually durable enough to survive labor conflict and future renegotiation.

### If it cannot do that:
then closures remain the more credible structural reset, despite the higher execution risk.

---

## Practical bottom line

If management is asking whether a no-closure deal can substitute for closures, my answer is:

- **Yes, in principle**
- **Only if** the package delivers roughly **70–85% of the closure-based annual savings**
- **and** reduces execution risk enough to improve risk-adjusted NPV
- **and** contains enforceable capacity/headcount reductions, not just temporary labor concessions
