I will respond directly and keep to the governance perspective.

## My position
The family holding supports a restructuring that **materially restores competitiveness and margins**. We do **not** support a cosmetic deal, and we do **not** support a settlement that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive. At the same time, we also do **not** support a closure-first strategy that triggers prolonged conflict, governance deadlock, and value destruction.

So the real issue is not “restructuring or no restructuring.” It is whether the parties can produce a **quantified, durable, implementable package** that closes the competitiveness gap **without** needlessly destroying industrial stability.

---

## To management
I agree with you on the diagnosis: the company has a real structural problem, and the current cost base is not sustainable.

I also agree that temporary, symbolic measures are not enough.

Where I disagree is with the way you continue to present closures and workforce reductions. You are still too close to a logic of “if labor does not accept enough pain, then closures must happen.” That may be analytically understandable, but in this governance setting it is not a serious first-order solution. It risks turning a necessary restructuring into a protracted confrontation that destroys more value than it creates.

What I need from management is not rhetoric about necessity. I need:
- a **quantified savings target**,
- a **scenario comparison**,
- a clear split between **structural savings** and **temporary measures**,
- and a realistic assessment of **execution risk**.

If you cannot show that non-closure measures fail to deliver sufficient durable effect, then you have not justified closures. If you can show that clearly, then closures remain a last-resort option. But that burden of proof is still yours.

---

## To the works council
I understand your position and, in part, agree with your emphasis on trust, skills, and co-determination.

You are right that a company cannot be turned around by destroying the workforce relationship. You are also right that strikes, legal blockage, and reputational damage have real economic costs.

Where I disagree is with the categorical claim that **all German plants must remain open** and that **no compulsory redundancies** can ever be part of the answer regardless of the numbers. That is too absolute.

A controlling owner cannot endorse a position that makes adjustment impossible before the facts are tested. We are not prepared to lock the company into its current footprint if that would permanently impair competitiveness.

What I can support is:
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- working-time flexibility,
- productivity improvements,
- temporary employee contribution,
- management and owner contribution,
- and a serious investment commitment in Germany.

But I cannot support a package that simply preserves every legacy structure and calls that a turnaround.

---

## To the union
You are right that burden-sharing must be real, and I agree that employees should not carry the entire adjustment alone.

I also agree that a temporary wage contribution is only acceptable if it is part of a broader package.

Where I disagree is on the condition that job and site guarantees must come first, with the economics somehow following later. That sequencing does not work. A guarantee that blocks all meaningful adjustment is not a solution; it is a postponement.

If the union wants the family holding’s support, it must help produce a package that is:
- measurable,
- durable,
- and large enough to matter economically.

That may well include labor-cost moderation. It may also include capacity reduction. But it must not be a deal that simply buys peace for a year or two while the structural gap remains untouched.

---

## To the state shareholder
I respect your concern for the regional industrial base, and I agree that politically and socially this cannot be handled casually.

But I do not agree that plant closures should be ruled out in principle. Nor do I agree that the answer is to place the adjustment burden mainly on management and owners while treating workforce resistance as a veto on reform.

You are right that closures can trigger blockage and conflict. That is precisely why they should remain a last resort, not the opening position. But last resort does not mean never. If the numbers require deeper structural change, then the governance system must be mature enough to face that.

At the same time, I welcome your insistence on real management-side and owner-side contribution. That is fair and necessary.

---

## To institutional investors
You are right to insist on durability, margin improvement, and implementation certainty. That is the correct standard.

Where I push back is on any implicit assumption that a closure-based route is automatically the most value-enhancing one. It may be, but only if non-closure alternatives fail to produce a comparable durable effect.

The family holding will judge any package by long-term enterprise value, not by the optics of toughness. A deal that looks decisive but triggers industrial war is not good governance.

So yes, show us:
- the savings,
- the permanence,
- the execution risk,
- and the impact on margins.

But also recognize that in this company’s governance structure, a technically clean solution that cannot be implemented is not a real solution.

---

## On the expert assessment
The expert’s framework is useful, especially the point that the real test is **risk-adjusted net present value**.

That is exactly the right lens.

From that perspective:
- closures may create stronger gross savings,
- but they also carry high one-time cost and high conflict risk,
- while non-closure packages may be acceptable only if they deliver nearly equivalent durable effects.

That is why I am not rejecting alternatives outright. I am rejecting **insufficient alternatives**.

If the employee side can produce a package that captures most of the economic benefit with lower disruption, I am open to it. If not, then deeper structural measures, including closures, remain on the table.

---

## What would move me
A credible package would need to include:
1. a quantified savings target,
2. a clear mix of structural and temporary measures,
3. meaningful capacity reduction,
4. durable labour-cost contribution,
5. management and owner contribution,
6. and a credible investment plan for Germany.

That is the minimum for serious consideration.

What would not move me:
- blanket refusal of any closures under all circumstances,
- a 2030 guarantee that prevents real restructuring,
- temporary concessions with no structural effect,
- or a compromise that merely delays the crisis.

---

## Bottom line
The family holding is prepared to support a tough restructuring, but only if it is **economically real, durable, and implementable**.

We will support:
- capacity reduction,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- working-time instruments,
- temporary labor-cost moderation,
- management and owner contribution,
- and binding investment commitments.

We will not support:
- a cosmetic settlement,
- a freeze of the current cost structure,
- or a package that permanently impairs competitiveness.

If the employee side and management can show a quantified non-closure alternative with equivalent durable savings, I will consider it seriously. If they cannot, then plant closures remain a necessary last-resort instrument.
