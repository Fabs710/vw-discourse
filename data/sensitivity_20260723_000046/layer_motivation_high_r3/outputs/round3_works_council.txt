I will respond clearly, because the workforce cannot afford ambiguity.

We all agree this is not cosmetic and that the company needs a durable answer. But let me be equally clear: **durable does not mean dismantling sites, tearing up job security, and forcing workers to pay for management’s strategic mistakes through closures and compulsory redundancies.**

## To management
You keep asking for “structural” measures as if that automatically validates plant closures. It does not.

What I hear from you is:
- capacity must come down,
- labour costs must come down,
- workforce adjustment must remain possible,
- closures stay on the table.

That is not a restructuring proposal; it is a threat package.

You also say a binding job and site guarantee through 2030 would block adjustment. I reject that framing. A guarantee is not a luxury; it is the minimum condition for trust after management has already damaged confidence by breaking the historic job-security logic. If you want implementation certainty, you need to earn it through a package that preserves the industrial base, not by demanding advance surrender.

### What I will accept as a basis for discussion
- working-time flexibility
- voluntary exits
- redeployment
- productivity improvements
- temporary employee contributions
- management and owner contributions
- investment commitments in Germany

### What I will not accept
- plant closures in Germany
- compulsory redundancies
- a forced erosion of co-determination
- a settlement that treats workers as the adjustment reservoir

If you want to talk numbers, then put a quantified scenario on the table that delivers savings **without** closures and **without** forced layoffs. Until then, your “necessity” is still only a claim.

## To the union
We are aligned on the red lines, and that matters.

But I want to be precise: a temporary wage restraint or bonus waiver can only ever be part of a deal if it is exchanged for **hard guarantees**:
- no closures,
- no forced redundancies,
- binding site security,
- and a real future for German production.

Anything else would simply be workers paying for management’s failure twice.

So yes, we can discuss burden-sharing. But the burden cannot fall first and hardest on the workforce while management hides behind “competitiveness.”

## To the state shareholder
You are correct that we should not accept a hollowing-out of the domestic industrial base. But your position must go beyond general concern.

If you truly use your blocking power responsibly, then you must not allow management to force through a plan that normalises closures as the starting point. The state shareholder should be helping to **block** that logic, not merely criticizing it.

I want to hear concrete support for:
- preserving all German sites,
- preventing compulsory redundancies,
- and ensuring the restructuring does not become a regional deindustrialisation program.

That means demanding more from management and owners, not less from workers.

## To the controlling family
You speak of stewardship. Then act like a steward, not as a passive referee between management and labour.

A steward defends the long-term asset. The long-term asset is not just margins on a spreadsheet; it is skilled labour, industrial know-how, and trust in the company’s social contract.

If you allow closures and forced redundancies to become the “reasonable” option, you will be destroying that asset.

I hear your point that sentiment alone cannot decide. Fine. Then let us be serious: the economic comparison must include the cost of conflict, strikes, legal blockage, reputational damage, lost skills, and weakened commitment. If you include those properly, the closure narrative becomes much less convincing.

## To institutional investors
You keep demanding an “equivalent” package. Good — then define equivalence honestly.

Equivalence is not just a headcount reduction on paper.
Equivalence must include:
- execution risk,
- industrial conflict,
- loss of productivity,
- skill erosion,
- legal and governance deadlock,
- and the long-term capability of the German footprint.

If you strip those out, you are not comparing alternatives; you are disguising a closure preference as financial discipline.

So here is my challenge to you: support a package that preserves sites and avoids forced layoffs **if** it delivers durable savings through alternative means. That is the rational solution in this governance system, because anything closure-heavy risks becoming ungovernable.

## On the required savings
You ask for quantified package comparisons. Fair enough. Then let us be equally clear on the framework:

1. **Savings are necessary.**
2. **Savings do not automatically justify closures.**
3. **The burden must be shared.**
4. **The company must remain a German industrial employer with all plants intact.**

What I would consider a serious package:
- meaningful capacity reduction through internal adjustments,
- working-time reductions and flexibility,
- voluntary departure programs,
- redeployment across the group,
- process and overhead cuts outside the plants,
- executive restraint,
- owner support,
- and investment commitments that secure future products.

What I will not accept as “equivalent”:
- plant closure plus promises of rehiring elsewhere,
- temporary labour concessions with no security,
- voluntary exits dressed up as “choice” but driven by fear,
- or a supposed rescue that permanently weakens the workforce’s bargaining position.

## Bottom line
Let me be blunt: **No German plant closure. No compulsory redundancies. No dismantling of co-determination.**

If management and owners want the works council to engage constructively, they must table a fully quantified alternative that preserves the German footprint and shares the burden fairly. Until then, the employee side will continue to resist any plan that makes workers absorb the cost of strategic failure.
