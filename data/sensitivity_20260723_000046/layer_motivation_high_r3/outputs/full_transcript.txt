[Works Council (Betriebsrat) (Plant-level employee representation under co-determination) — Round 1]

1. **All German plants must remain open.**
2. **No compulsory layoffs.**
3. **A binding job and site guarantee through 2030.**
4. **Real co-determination in any restructuring plan**, not a fait accompli handed down from above.
5. **A credible future for investment, products, and technology in Germany**, not a hollowed-out footprint.

- capacity adjustments,
- working-time instruments,
- voluntary solutions,
- internal redeployment,
- efficiency measures,
- and a fair employee contribution **only if** it is tied to secure sites, secure jobs, and future investment.

But let me say this plainly: if management insists on closures, mass layoffs, or the dismantling of long-standing guarantees, then the works council will resist with all available means. We have the mandate of the workforce, and we will use it.

We are ready for a serious solution. We are **not** ready to sacrifice plants and livelihoods to make a restructuring easy for management.

### Risk-adjusted NPV of restructuring
\[
\text{Risk-adjusted NPV} = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{\mathbb{E}[\Delta EBIT_t \times P(\text{realization}_t)]}{(1+r)^t} - \text{PV(one-time costs)}

- \(\Delta EBIT_t\) = annual operating benefit,
- \(P(\text{realization}_t)\) = probability benefits actually materialize in period \(t\),
- \(r\) = discount rate / hurdle,
- one-time costs include severance, decommissioning, dual-running, legal, logistics, impairment, and transition costs.

### Key point
A plan with slightly lower gross savings can beat a “stronger” plan if it has:
- much lower execution risk,
- faster implementation,
- lower one-time cash burn,

## Decision framework

The financially relevant comparison is:

### Closure-based scenario
Typically:
- larger gross fixed-cost reduction,
- higher one-time cash costs,

## What “equivalent economic effect” means

A non-closure package is financially equivalent only if it can deliver:

1. **Similar annual EBIT improvement**
2. **Similar cash cost reduction**
3. **Similar durability through 2030+**
4. **High probability of implementation**
5. **Low leakage risk from later reversals or renegotiation**

In practice, that means I would test it on a **risk-adjusted NPV** basis.

## Rule of thumb threshold

Without company-specific data, I would use this practical threshold:

A non-closure package is economically credible only if it can capture roughly **70–85% of the annual run-rate savings** of a closure-based plan, **and** do so with materially lower execution risk and materially lower one-time cost.

If it captures less than that, it usually becomes a delay mechanism rather than a true substitute.

Why that range?
- Because closures usually deliver the deepest structural savings.
- But closures also carry high failure risk in German co-determination settings.
- So a somewhat smaller savings package can still be rational if it is much easier to implement and much cheaper upfront.

## Simple break-even logic

A non-closure package can “beat” closures if:

\[
NPV_{non-closure} = PV(\text{savings}) - \text{restructuring cost} - \text{execution risk penalty}
\]

exceeds:

\[
NPV_{closure} = PV(\text{larger savings}) - \text{higher restructuring cost} - \text{delay / conflict penalty}
\]

So the key is not just savings size; it is:
- time to benefit,
- probability-weighted realization,
- and avoidance of conflict-induced delay.
