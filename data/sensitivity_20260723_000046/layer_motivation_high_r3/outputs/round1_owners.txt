I appreciate the clarity from all sides. From the controlling family’s perspective, I want to be equally clear: **we will not support a deal that permanently weakens the company’s competitiveness, destroys margins, or leaves the group structurally unfit for the next investment cycle.** That would be a failure of stewardship.

### Where I agree
I agree with management on the core diagnosis: this is **not** a routine adjustment. The company has a real structural cost and capacity problem, and ignoring that would be irresponsible.

I also agree with the works council, the union, and the state shareholder on one essential point: **a destructive confrontation helps nobody**. Prolonged conflict, reputational damage, and instability would erode value far beyond any short-term gain.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the idea that the only credible answer is broad plant closure and a hard cut imposed from above.

Why? Because from an ownership and governance perspective, closures are not a slogan — they are a **last-resort instrument**. In a company with co-determination, a special statute, and blocking positions across the board, trying to force through a maximalist solution without a broader settlement is likely to produce:
- delay,
- legal and governance blockage,
- industrial action,
- reputational damage,
- and ultimately a weaker company.

That is not value creation. That is value leakage.

I also do **not** accept the position that employee guarantees are simply incompatible with restructuring. A credible turnaround can include security and still deliver savings — but only if the savings are real, measurable, and durable.

### What matters to us
The family holding’s priority is not to win a tactical fight. It is to protect the long-term value of the core asset. That means three things:

1. **Competitiveness must improve materially**
   - lower structural costs,
   - better productivity,
   - a leaner capacity footprint,
   - and room to invest in future products and technology.

2. **The solution must be durable**
   - not a temporary bridge that postpones the problem,
   - not a deal that expires before the structural issues are fixed.

3. **Governance control and enterprise stability must be preserved**
   - we cannot support outcomes that leave the company permanently impaired or open the door to chronic instability.

### What I would support
I am prepared to support a tougher restructuring if it is built around a serious negotiated package, for example:
- capacity reduction where unavoidable,
- voluntary departures and redeployment first,
- working-time and flexibility measures,
- temporary employee contributions,
- management and owner contribution as well,
- and binding investment commitments tied to the restructuring.

But I need to be blunt: **if the “alternative measures” do not produce equivalent economic effect, then they are not a solution.** They are just a delay.

### On the positions of the other stakeholders
To the works council and union: I respect the insistence on fairness and social responsibility. But “no closures, no compulsory redundancies, no meaningful cost change” is not a viable end state unless you can show a credible alternative that actually closes the competitiveness gap. If you cannot, then you are asking the owners to accept permanent underperformance.

To the state shareholder: I agree that the industrial base matters and that the German footprint should be protected wherever economically possible. But “protect all sites” cannot be treated as an absolute if the economics do not support it. The company must remain viable, not just locally present.

To management: you are right that cosmetic compromise is not enough. But I would caution against presenting plant closures as the default or starting point. If you frame this as a zero-sum showdown, you may get the worst of both worlds: no agreement and no improvement.

### My bottom line
The family holding will back a **durable competitiveness package**, not a symbolic peace deal and not a reckless confrontation.

So my answer is:
- **Yes** to structural reform
- **Yes** to real cost and capacity reduction
- **Yes** to negotiated burden-sharing
- **No** to a solution that permanently impairs the enterprise
- **No** to a drawn-out conflict that destroys value
- **No** to treating plant closures as the only serious instrument before alternatives are tested in good faith

If the parties can produce a package that restores margins, preserves future investment, and avoids instability, we can support it through governance. If not, then I will not endorse a course that weakens the company’s long-term value simply to avoid hard choices.
