I will be direct.

### Where I agree
I agree with all sides on one point: **a cosmetic settlement would be a mistake**. The company has a real structural competitiveness problem, and pretending otherwise would only deepen the crisis.

I also agree that **prolonged conflict is value-destructive**. Strikes, legal blockage, reputational damage, and supervisory-board deadlock would harm the company far beyond any single bargaining point.

### Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the works council’s and union’s central red line that **all German plants must remain open, no compulsory redundancies are possible, and a binding guarantee through 2030 can be given regardless of the economics**.

That is not stewardship. That is freezing the problem in place.

I also do **not** accept the state shareholder’s suggestion that plant closures are somehow to be ruled out from the start. If the economics require structural capacity reduction, then closures cannot be excluded as a matter of principle.

At the same time, I do **not** accept management’s tone that closures are simply the default answer. They are a **last-resort instrument**, not a slogan. In this governance structure, a maximalist closure strategy is likely to trigger exactly the blockage and conflict we all want to avoid.

### What I believe is required
The family holding will only support a package that meets three tests:

1. **It materially restores competitiveness and margins**
   - not symbolically, but measurably;
   - enough to fund future investment and protect the long-term asset.

2. **It is durable and implementable**
   - not a short-lived bridge;
   - not a deal that collapses under the first wave of conflict or re-litigation.

3. **It avoids value-destructive instability**
   - because a weak compromise plus prolonged confrontation is worse than a hard but workable settlement.

### On the alternatives
If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then it must show a package with **equivalent economic effect**:
- meaningful capacity reduction,
- real productivity gains,
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary departures and redeployment,
- temporary labor-cost restraint,
- and credible burden-sharing.

But I need to be blunt: **temporary measures alone are not enough** if the underlying structural cost gap remains. A short-term contribution that merely defers the issue is not an answer.

### On governance and fairness
I agree with the state shareholder that management and owners must contribute too. Fairness matters, and so does legitimacy. But fairness is not the same as preserving every legacy entitlement.

The family holding will not back:
- a deal that permanently impairs enterprise value,
- a plan that leaves the company uncompetitive,
- or a settlement that simply postpones restructuring and invites a worse crisis later.

### My bottom line
I can support:
- capacity reduction,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- working-time instruments,
- management and owner contribution,
- and binding investment commitments,
**if** they produce a real and durable improvement in competitiveness.

I cannot support:
- a blanket guarantee that forecloses necessary adjustment,
- a no-closure doctrine regardless of economics,
- or a weak compromise that preserves conflict and leaves margins unresolved.

So my message to all parties is this:
**Bring forward a package that actually closes the competitiveness gap.**
If you can do that without closures, fine — I will support it.  
If you cannot, then plant closures and deeper structural measures remain on the table, because the long-term value of the company must come first.
