I will be direct as well.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family holding, and the investors on one point: **this cannot be a cosmetic restructuring**.  
The company does have a structural competitiveness problem, and if we fail to address it, we will lose jobs and investment anyway.

I also agree that:
- conflict has costs,
- durable solutions matter,
- and the company must remain able to invest in future products and technology.

On those points, there is no real dispute.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the logic that plant closures and compulsory redundancies should remain the default answer, or that they can be treated as the inevitable benchmark before serious alternatives are exhausted.

For a regional anchor shareholder with a blocking minority, that is not acceptable as a starting point.  
The company is not just a line item. It is a regional industrial base, a skills ecosystem, and a central part of the state economy.

## To management
You have still not proven that closures are necessary rather than preferred.

You say:
- overcapacity must come down,
- labour costs must come down,
- and real adjustment must remain possible.

I hear that. But I still do not see why the same durable economic effect cannot be achieved through a serious package of:
- working-time instruments,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- productivity improvements,
- overhead cuts,
- management restraint,
- and owner contribution.

What you have not shown is that these measures are insufficient in quantified terms.  
Until that is shown, closures remain an assertion, not a demonstrated necessity.

## To the works council and union
I support your core red lines:
- no compulsory redundancies as a first instrument,
- no casual dismantling of co-determination,
- no settlement that turns workers into the sole adjustment reservoir.

That said, I also want to be precise: a durable compromise will need real economic substance.  
If there is to be an employee contribution, it must be:
- temporary,
- limited,
- and matched by binding commitments on jobs, sites, and investment.

I will not support a one-sided deal where workers pay and management merely keeps the right to come back later with the same threats.

## To the controlling family
You are right that sentiment alone cannot decide.  
But stewardship is not just margin protection; it is the protection of the long-term industrial asset.

That means:
- skilled labour,
- local production know-how,
- social peace,
- and trust in the company’s governance.

A closure-heavy strategy may look efficient on paper, but in this governance structure it risks destroying more value than it creates through conflict, deadlock, and lasting damage to the industrial base.

## To institutional investors
Your insistence on quantified savings is legitimate.  
But equivalence must include:
- execution risk,
- strike risk,
- legal and governance blockage,
- loss of capability,
- and the long-term damage from hollowing out the German footprint.

If you exclude those factors, you are not doing serious analysis.  
You are just preferring the harshest instrument.

## My position on the expert/modeler’s phased or hybrid ideas
I am open to a **phased non-closure restructuring** if it is real, enforceable, and front-loaded with substantial savings.

But I want to be clear: I do **not** accept a “review later, maybe closure later” logic if it is simply a delayed threat.  
A fallback mechanism only has value if it is genuinely last resort and only after serious, quantified alternatives have failed.

So the question is not whether there can be a later review. The question is whether the first-stage package is strong enough to justify excluding closures for a defined period.

That is the test.

## What would move me
I could support a settlement if it contains all of the following:
1. **A quantified savings target**
2. **Real capacity reduction through non-closure measures**
3. **No compulsory redundancies as the default path**
4. **Temporary and limited employee contribution**
5. **Meaningful management and owner contribution**
6. **Binding investment commitments in Germany**
7. **Protection of co-determination and the regional industrial base**

That would be a serious social-partnership solution.

## What will not move me
I will not support:
- closures as the opening assumption,
- a plan that guts regional employment,
- a settlement that sidelines the state shareholder,
- or a deal that weakens co-determination in practice.

## Bottom line
I am prepared to support a tough restructuring.  
I am **not** prepared to support a restructuring that starts by sacrificing German sites and jobs as the first answer.

If management can bring a quantified non-closure package that truly closes the gap, I will engage constructively.  
If not, then we are still not at a settlement.
