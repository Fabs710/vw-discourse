## 1) Short answer

**Yes, but only under a fairly demanding condition set.**

A **non-closure** package can be economically competitive **if and only if** it produces:
1. **large enough annual fixed-cost reduction**,  
2. **durable effect through 2030 or longer**,  
3. **high execution certainty**, and  
4. **limited “backsliding” risk** when temporary labor measures expire.

If the non-closure package is mainly:
- temporary wage restraint,
- voluntary exits without meaningful capacity reduction,
- or process improvements without real footprint shrinkage,

then it will usually **underperform a closure-based package on risk-adjusted EBIT/NPV**.

Given the governance features you described — co-determination vetoes, family control, state blocking position, and elevated strike risk — the biggest financial issue is not just the gross savings gap, but **implementation probability and delay**.

---

## 2) The right comparison framework

The correct decision metric is not announced headcount reduction. It is:

### Risk-adjusted NPV of restructuring
\[
\text{Risk-adjusted NPV} = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{\mathbb{E}[\Delta EBIT_t \times P(\text{realization}_t)]}{(1+r)^t} - \text{PV(one-time costs)}
\]

Where:
- \(\Delta EBIT_t\) = annual operating benefit,
- \(P(\text{realization}_t)\) = probability benefits actually materialize in period \(t\),
- \(r\) = discount rate / hurdle,
- one-time costs include severance, decommissioning, dual-running, legal, logistics, impairment, and transition costs.

### Key point
A plan with slightly lower gross savings can beat a “stronger” plan if it has:
- much lower execution risk,
- faster implementation,
- lower one-time cash burn,
- and fewer delay penalties.

---

## 3) What typically drives value in each package

## A. Closure-based restructuring
### Financial strengths
- Removes **structural fixed costs**:
  - plant overhead,
  - site support,
  - maintenance,
  - logistics,
  - duplicated management layers,
  - part of indirect labor.
- Usually gives the **largest durable annual EBIT improvement**.
- Can improve capacity utilization at remaining plants.

### Financial weaknesses
- High one-time cash outlay:
  - severance,
  - transition,
  - decommissioning,
  - contract termination,
  - possible impairment.
- High probability of:
  - strike delay,
  - legal challenge,
  - supervisory board deadlock,
  - political intervention.
- Long ramp period; savings may arrive only gradually.

### Typical financial profile
- **Year 0–1:** negative cash/EBIT due to restructuring charge
- **Years 2–5:** strong positive EBIT contribution if executed
- **Persistent benefit:** high, if the closure is real and irreversible

---

## B. Non-closure restructuring
### Financial strengths
- Lower headline disruption.
- Lower severance/exit cash.
- Faster to sign if negotiated.
- Better chance of preserving social peace and avoiding immediate production loss.

### Financial weaknesses
- Savings are often:
  - smaller,
  - more reversible,
  - more dependent on bargaining renewals,
  - and less effective at reducing fixed overhead.
- If capacity remains structurally excessive, the package may not fix margin pressure.
- Temporary concessions can roll off in 2–4 years and recreate the problem.

### Typical financial profile
- **Year 0:** lower restructuring cost
- **Years 1–3:** moderate savings, but only if enforceable
- **Years 4–5:** risk of benefit decay if measures expire

---

## C. Hybrid package
This is often the most realistic in a veto-constrained German setting.

A hybrid can combine:
- one plant closure or partial site rationalization,
- non-closure capacity reduction elsewhere,
- voluntary exits,
- working-time changes,
- temporary labor-cost moderation,
- management overhead cuts,
- and investment commitments.

Financially, a good hybrid often offers:
- most of the durable fixed-cost benefit of closure,
- less conflict than a full closure program,
- lower one-time cost than closing multiple sites,
- better probability of board approval than a pure closure strategy.

## 4) Probabilistic implementation risk matters a lot here

Because there are effective veto positions and strike capacity, the expected value of each package should be adjusted for:

- **strike probability**
- **strike duration**
- **veto / board delay probability**
- **legal challenge probability**
- **risk of dilution / renegotiation**
- **risk that temporary concessions expire and savings reverse**

### Practical modeling implication
A closure plan with **30–50% delay probability** and a long industrial dispute can lose a substantial share of its theoretical NPV.

Likewise, a non-closure plan can look attractive on paper but fail if:
- the employee contribution is too small,
- there is no enforceable exit of excess capacity,
- or savings are only temporary.

## 5) What savings level is likely needed?

You have not provided company financials, so I cannot give a precise euro target. But structurally, if management says the German cost base is materially above target and the brand misses ROS, then the required package usually needs to deliver:

- **meaningful annual fixed-cost reduction**
- enough to move operating margin by a visible step
- plus a confidence effect on future investment capacity

In industrial turnaround terms, the package must typically cover:
1. **run-rate EBIT lift**
2. **restructuring charge payback**
3. **buffer against implementation leakage**

If the company needs, for example, a mid-hundreds-of-millions annual EBIT improvement, then:
- small wage moderation alone is unlikely to be enough;
- a capacity rationalization without site closure may still be enough if it is deep;
- one closure may be enough if the plant is high-cost and subscale;
- multiple small concessions may not sum to a durable solution.

## 6) Decision rule I would use

### Non-closure package is financially preferable if:
- it can deliver at least **~80–90% of closure-equivalent annual savings**,
- with **substantially lower one-time cost**,
- and with **higher execution certainty**,
- and the savings are locked in contractually through 2030+.

### Closure-based package is financially preferable if:
- non-closure measures only produce **~40–70% of required structural savings**,
- or are mainly temporary,
- or the package requires repeated renegotiation,
- or governance deadlock makes implementation slow enough that NPV collapses.

### Hybrid is preferable if:
- one or more closures are needed to remove excess fixed cost,
- but a full closure program would trigger excessive delay or conflict,
- and a mixed package can preserve enough labor peace to realize savings sooner.

## 9) Concise conclusion

### Financial judgment
- **Closure**: highest gross structural savings, highest one-time cost, highest conflict risk.
- **Non-closure**: lower one-time cost, lower conflict risk, but often insufficient unless very deep and durable.
- **Hybrid**: usually the best risk-adjusted option in a veto-constrained governance environment.

### Bottom line
If the employee side can offer a **quantified, enforceable, long-duration package** that materially reduces the fixed-cost base and capacity, then **non-closure can be value-preserving**.

If not, then **closure becomes the economically credible route** to restore margin and protect long-term competitiveness.
