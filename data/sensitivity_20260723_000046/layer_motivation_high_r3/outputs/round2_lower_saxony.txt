I will be equally direct: **I do not accept plant closures in Germany as the starting point or the default solution.** Nor do I accept a restructuring logic that makes **forced redundancies** the first instrument. As the state shareholder with a blocking minority and a responsibility for the regional economy, I will not support a package that guts jobs, weakens the industrial base, or sidelines co-determination.

### Where I agree
I agree with management, the family holding, and institutional investors on one important point: **a cosmetic deal is not acceptable**. The company does have a structural competitiveness problem, and it would be irresponsible to pretend otherwise.

I also agree that this must be **durable**, not a temporary patch. If the company cannot regain competitiveness, then future investment, products, and jobs are all at risk.

### Where I disagree
I fundamentally disagree with the repeated suggestion that closures are the necessary benchmark from the outset.

That is not a neutral economic fact; it is a strategic choice. And it is a choice with enormous consequences:
- loss of skilled jobs,
- damage to regional supply chains,
- political destabilization,
- erosion of trust in co-determination,
- and a far greater risk of industrial conflict.

If management frames this as “closures or failure,” then you are narrowing the field before serious alternatives are fully tested. I do **not** accept that.

### To management
You are right that the cost base must come down. But your answer is still too one-sided.

You say:
- structural capacity reduction,
- labour cost reduction,
- workforce adjustment,
- productivity gains.

Fine. But **where is the serious management and owner contribution?**  
Where are the concrete commitments on:
- executive restraint,
- overhead reduction,
- capital discipline,
- productivity measures outside the shop floor,
- and investment commitments that make German production viable?

It is not acceptable to ask workers and regions to bear the full adjustment while management presents closures as the clean solution.

And one more point: if you want implementation certainty, then you should understand that **pushing closures against the workforce does not create certainty**. It creates blockage, strikes, and governance deadlock. A negotiated restructuring with real burden-sharing is more likely to be deliverable.

### To the works council and union
I support your core red lines:
- no compulsory redundancies as the first instrument,
- no hollowing-out of German sites,
- no one-sided burden shift onto employees.

But I want to be clear: if there is to be a labor contribution, it must be **part of a serious package**, not a blank cheque to preserve the status quo. The state shareholder will not back a deal that simply freezes every problem in place.

That means we should discuss:
- working-time instruments,
- voluntary solutions,
- temporary wage moderation,
- redeployment,
- productivity and process improvements,
- and capacity adjustments **without plant closures** wherever possible.

However, those measures must be tied to:
- binding site perspectives,
- a credible investment plan,
- and a genuine future for production in Germany.

### To the controlling family
You are right that value destruction from conflict is real. But I would caution against treating closures as just another option to be held over the workforce from day one.

A “last resort” is only credible if you first exhaust alternatives in good faith. That means the owners should support a process that tests:
1. non-closure capacity reductions,
2. internal reallocation,
3. voluntary exit schemes,
4. temporary labour cost measures,
5. management-side savings,
6. and binding investment commitments.

Only if those fail should anyone even begin to argue that plant closures are unavoidable. We are not there.

### To institutional investors
I understand your insistence on durable margins and implementability. But I would say this clearly: **a deal that is politically and industrially unimplementable is not a value-enhancing solution, even if the spreadsheet looks neat**.

If you want certainty, then support a settlement that can actually hold:
- through social partnership,
- through co-determination,
- through regional legitimacy,
- and through a shared burden model.

A closure-heavy approach may look decisive, but in this governance structure it risks producing exactly the opposite: delay, litigation, strike action, and a weaker company.

### My position
The state shareholder will support a **tough restructuring**, but only one that meets these conditions:
1. **German sites are protected wherever economically possible**
2. **No forced redundancies as the default tool**
3. **Real burden-sharing from management, owners, and workforce**
4. **Binding investment in future products and technology in Germany**
5. **A credible job and site perspective**
6. **Preservation of co-determination as a meaningful part of governance**

### What I can support
I am prepared to support:
- capacity reduction through alternatives to closures,
- productivity and flexibility measures,
- voluntary departures,
- temporary labour-cost restraint,
- and a negotiated restructuring that preserves the company as a regional anchor.

### What I will not support
I will **not** support:
- plant closures as the starting point,
- a plan that shifts the burden almost entirely onto the workforce,
- or a restructuring that hollow-outs regional employment and weakens the state’s co-determination role.

So my message is simple: bring forward a serious, quantified alternative that protects sites and jobs while restoring competitiveness. If management cannot do that, then it is not the employees or the state that have failed first.
