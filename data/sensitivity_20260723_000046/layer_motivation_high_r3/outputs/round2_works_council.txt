Management keeps repeating “structural crisis” as if that automatically justifies plant closures and forced redundancies. It does not. It explains pressure, yes. It does **not** create legitimacy for tearing up the historic job-security framework or sacrificing German sites.

Let me respond directly to what has been said.

### To management
You say all plants cannot remain untouched and that a binding guarantee through 2030 is incompatible with turnaround. I reject that premise.

What is truly incompatible with a turnaround is a plan that destroys trust, triggers mass resistance, and turns the workforce into the adjustment variable of last resort. We have already seen what happens when management acts as though co-determination is a formality. It is not. If you want implementation certainty, then stop presenting closures and layoffs as the starting point.

If you have a savings target, bring forward a serious package built on:
- capacity adjustment without closures,
- working-time instruments,
- internal redeployment,
- voluntary exits only,
- productivity gains,
- management and owner contribution,
- and binding investment in Germany.

But be clear: **no plant closure and no compulsory redundancies**. Those are not bargaining chips for us.

### To the union
We are aligned on the essentials, and that matters. The workforce will not be used as a one-sided financing instrument for a restructuring that preserves executive freedom and shareholder returns while cutting jobs.

I agree with your insistence that any employee contribution must be fair and conditional. But let’s not let the word “contribution” become a back door to weakening collective protections. If the company wants a labour-cost contribution, then it must come with:
- a binding site guarantee,
- no forced layoffs,
- and a real future for the German footprint.

Anything else is not burden-sharing. It is pressure disguised as compromise.

### To the state shareholder
You are right that a hollowing-out of the domestic base is unacceptable. But that means you cannot drift toward a solution that quietly accepts closures as “economically unavoidable” without exhausting alternatives. The state shareholder should use its blocking position to prevent exactly that.

If the company wants public legitimacy, it must protect skilled industrial employment in Germany. Not in rhetoric, but in binding terms.

### To the controlling family
You speak of stewardship, but stewardship does not mean accepting management’s first aggressive restructuring as inevitability. It means defending the long-term asset, including the social foundation on which that asset rests.

You say closures are a last resort. Good. Then act like it. Do **not** legitimise them by treating them as the default if workers do not surrender enough. The burden of proof sits with those demanding plant closures, not with those defending jobs.

### To institutional investors
You want durable margin improvement and implementation certainty. I understand that. But you are making a dangerous assumption if you think closures are the clean route to certainty.

In a system with co-determination, a state shareholder, and a controlling family, pushing closures against the workforce is not certainty. It is escalation, blockage, industrial action, and delay. You may get a spreadsheet improvement on paper and a governance crisis in reality.

If you want durable value, support a negotiated alternative that protects sites and avoids forced redundancies. That is the only path that has legitimacy and a realistic chance of lasting.

### Our position
Let me state it plainly and again:

1. **All German plants must remain open.**
2. **No compulsory redundancies.**
3. **Binding job and site guarantees through 2030.**
4. **Real co-determination in any restructuring package.**
5. **Future investment, products, and technology in Germany.**

We are prepared to discuss:
- working-time reduction,
- temporary flexibility,
- voluntary departures,
- redeployment,
- efficiency measures,
- and a fair employee contribution.

But we will not trade away sites and jobs to buy “credibility” for management.

### On the so-called equivalence test
Several of you keep saying: show an “equivalent economic effect” if closures are avoided. That framing is incomplete. The question is not only whether a package is equivalent on a narrow cost sheet. The question is whether it is **implementable, socially sustainable, and consistent with the company’s long-term industrial base**.

A closure-based plan may reduce costs fast, but it destroys trust, skills, and future capability. That is not a neutral benchmark. If you want an equivalent effect, then include the full cost of conflict, lost production, legal blockage, and reputational damage.

Once you do that honestly, the case for closures looks much weaker.

### Bottom line
We are not here to manage decline. We are here to defend the workforce and the company’s industrial future.

If management wants a real settlement, it must come with:
- no closures,
- no forced layoffs,
- binding guarantees,
- and a negotiated restructuring built around German production, not its dismantling.

If that is not on the table, then there is no basis for agreement from the works council. We will resist using every instrument available to us.
