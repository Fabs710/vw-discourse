I’ll respond directly.

### First: what I agree with
I agree with all sides on one point: **a cosmetic deal is worthless**.

- If we preserve the full cost base and call that a settlement, we fail.
- If we sign something that cannot actually be implemented, we fail.
- If we trigger a long conflict that destroys output and confidence, we also fail.

So yes: this must be **durable**, **implementable**, and **economically real**.

### Where I disagree sharply
But I do **not** accept the central claim from the works council and union that:

- all plants must remain open,
- there can be no compulsory redundancies,
- and a binding job and site guarantee through 2030 can be given
**without** deep structural concessions on cost and capacity.

That is not a turnaround. That is a freeze.

And let me be clear: **a freeze is not compatible with restoring competitiveness** in the current market. German factory costs are too high, capacity is too large for the market, and the brand must get back to a return-on-sales level that can fund future products and technology. That cannot be done by good intentions.

### To the works council
You argue that closures destroy trust and capability. I understand that concern. But trust does not pay the bills, and capability does not survive if the company remains structurally uncompetitive.

You say closures are not legitimate because management mismanaged the EV and China transition. Fair point on the past. But the company cannot now leave the cost structure untouched because the past was mishandled.

You also say “equivalent economic effect” must include the cost of conflict and lost production. It does. But the reverse is also true: **if you reject structural reductions, you are accepting an ongoing competitiveness gap that will itself destroy jobs over time**.

So I reject the idea that protecting every site is the same thing as protecting the industrial base. It is not. A weak company with all sites intact is still a weak company.

### To the union
You say employee contributions are possible if jobs and sites are secured. That is exactly the problem: you want the security first, then contributions later. We need the opposite logic:
- first, a package that delivers measurable structural savings,
- then, if appropriate, security elements that are credible because the economics work.

I am not asking for a disguised wage cut. I am asking for a **real labour-cost contribution** and a **real reduction in overcapacity**. If that cannot be achieved, then we are not solving the crisis.

### To the state shareholder
I appreciate your concern for the regional industrial base. I do not dismiss that. But it is not a credible policy to insist that all sites be protected regardless of economics.

If we preserve every site at any cost, we just postpone the adjustment and make the eventual correction harsher. That helps nobody — not workers, not the region, not the company.

### To the controlling family
I agree with you that value leakage from confrontation is unacceptable. I also agree that closures are not a slogan, they are a last-resort instrument.

But “last resort” does not mean “never on the table.” If the alternative measures do not deliver equivalent savings, then closures remain necessary to protect the long-term value of the core business.

### On the claim that closures are the easier option
No — they are not easier. They are difficult, politically and socially. But difficulty does not make them wrong. If the numbers do not work, then sentiment cannot decide the outcome.

### What management requires
We need a package that delivers all of the following:
1. **Structural capacity reduction**
2. **Durable labour-cost reduction**
3. **Real workforce adjustment**
4. **Productivity gains**
5. **Investment room for future products and technology**

If those outcomes can be achieved without closures, we are prepared to discuss that. But it must be more than promises:
- it must be quantified,
- enforceable,
- and large enough to restore margins.

### What is not acceptable
Let me be equally explicit about what we cannot accept:
- a binding 2030 guarantee that freezes the company into its current inefficiency,
- a “no closures” position that merely transfers the cost crisis into the future,
- a labour contribution that is temporary only and does not change the structure,
- or a deal whose only real achievement is avoiding confrontation for a few months.

### My position, plainly
Management’s position remains:
- **deep restructuring is necessary**
- **capacity must come down**
- **labour costs must come down**
- **real workforce reductions remain on the table**
- **alternative measures are acceptable only if they produce equivalent durable savings**

I am not moving from that core position.

If the employee side wants to avoid closures, then bring forward a package that genuinely closes the gap — not rhetorically, but economically. Until then, plant closures and deeper cuts remain part of the credible restructuring options.
