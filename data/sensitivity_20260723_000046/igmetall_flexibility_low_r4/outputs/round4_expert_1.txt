## What the expert view would be on the “proof” question
The key test is not whether a no-closure scenario is theoretically possible. It is whether it is:

1. **Durable** — savings persist over several years, not just one bargaining cycle.
2. **Large enough** — enough to close the cost gap, not merely reduce it.
3. **Fast enough** — savings arrive before the business loses further ground.
4. **Executable** — can be implemented within labor law, co-determination, and industrial-relations constraints.
5. **Credible** — both capital and labor believe the commitments will actually hold.

A site-preserving package can be credible only if it delivers:
- permanent capacity reduction,
- meaningful fixed-cost cuts,
- and verified productivity or working-time effects,
not just voluntary exits and short-term wage restraint.

## The main restructuring logic
In German automotive restructuring, there are usually four levers:

### 1) Capacity and footprint
- Plant closures
- Line eliminations
- Product consolidation
- Shift reductions

These produce the deepest structural savings, but they carry the highest political and industrial-relations cost.

### 2) Workforce adjustment
- Voluntary exits
- Early retirement
- Hiring freezes
- Redeployment
- Natural attrition

These are socially easier, but often too slow or too small to solve a large overcapacity problem alone.

### 3) Labor-cost adjustment
- Temporary wage restraint
- Bonus cuts
- Working-time reductions
- Partial compensation waivers

These help in the short run, but by themselves rarely create a lasting step-change unless embedded in a broader redesign.

### 4) Operational redesign
- Higher utilization
- Better shift logic
- Automation
- Overhead reduction
- Procurement and complexity reduction
- Product allocation changes

These can be material, but they depend on investment and strong execution discipline.

## My assessment of the three broad paths

### A. Zero-closure restructuring
This is **possible in principle**, but only under strict conditions:
- substantial voluntary workforce reduction,
- major working-time and flexibility concessions,
- real overhead elimination,
- product/investment commitments,
- and possibly permanent labor-cost moderation.

**Main risk:** the savings may be insufficient or too slow, leaving the company with the same footprint burden but a weakened industrial-relations settlement.

### B. Partial rationalization
This is often the **most realistic compromise** in German restructuring:
- preserve the strongest sites,
- rationalize weak or overlapping capacities,
- combine with workforce and working-time measures,
- avoid blanket confrontation.

**Main advantage:** it can close a meaningful part of the gap while reducing the number of closures.  
**Main risk:** everyone may see it as the “least acceptable middle,” so governance approval can still be hard.

### C. Closure-based restructuring
This is the **highest-impact cost reset** if overcapacity is severe and structurally persistent.

**Main advantage:** strongest fixed-cost relief and strongest signal to markets.  
**Main risks:** labor conflict, implementation delay, political backlash, supply-chain disruption, and value destruction if execution drags on.

## What would be the clearest quantitative trigger?
If I were specifying the trigger for deciding whether “zero-closure” is real or just postponement, I would look for this:

- Does the no-closure package reduce **annual fixed costs** enough to restore target returns after allowing for:
  - one-off restructuring charges,
  - lost production during transition,
  - productivity drag,
  - and the cost of any concessions granted to labor?

If the answer is no, then the package is not structurally sufficient.

More concretely, the model should show:
- savings by plant,
- utilization after the adjustment,
- payback period,
- and whether the remaining capacity is still over the breakeven threshold.

A plan that saves money only by postponing the cost problem to 2028 or 2030 is not a durable solution.

## On the “backstop” debate
From a restructuring governance perspective, a **proof-gated backstop** is actually a common compromise tool. It is not a Trojan horse by definition; it depends on design.

But for it to work, it must be:
- objectively triggered,
- time-limited,
- independently verified,
- and specific about what happens if the target is missed.

Otherwise it becomes either:
- a hollow reassurance for labor, or
- a permanent threat used in bargaining.

So the backstop only works if the trigger is tied to hard numbers and a jointly accepted review process.
