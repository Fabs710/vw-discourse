I will respond directly.

## First, what I agree with
I agree with nearly everyone on the core diagnosis:

- **Doing nothing is not an option.**
- The company needs a **durable** and **economically real** restructuring.
- A prolonged conflict would be **value-destructive** for the business, the workforce, and the owners.
- **Non-closure measures** can contribute materially and should absolutely be part of the analysis.

I also agree with the expert’s central point: a **zero-closure solution is possible in principle**, but whether it is sufficient in practice depends on the size of the savings gap and the durability of the measures.

That is the right frame.

## Where I disagree — clearly
I do **not** accept the employee side’s attempt to turn **“no closures”** and **“no compulsory redundancies”** into absolute governing principles.

That is not a restructuring solution. It is a constraint that may or may not be compatible with the economics. If the numbers show that the structural gap is large, then those red lines become a way of postponing the adjustment, not solving it.

I also do **not** accept binding site and job guarantees through 2030 as a starting point if they would lock in an uncompetitive structure. Value preservation is not achieved by freezing the cost base and hoping the market becomes more forgiving.

## To the Works Council
I understand your concern for jobs, sites, and trust. That matters. But I reject the claim that closures are merely a bargaining weapon and that management has no case unless it has exhausted every alternative.

You are right that management has not yet provided the quantified site-level proof. But that cuts both ways:
- it does **not** prove that closures are unnecessary;
- it means we need the numbers before ruling anything in or out.

What I cannot support is a position that says every German plant must remain open regardless of the economics. If a site is structurally non-competitive, preserving it indefinitely is not stewardship; it is deferred loss.

## To the union
I agree with you that employees should not bear the whole burden. A fair settlement must include shared sacrifice.

But I disagree with the idea that employee contributions must be only temporary if the underlying cost problem is permanent. Temporary concessions may buy time; they do not necessarily fix the business.

If you want to avoid closures, then the workforce package must be:
- deep enough,
- durable enough,
- and enforceable enough
to match the savings of structural change.

If it cannot do that, then it is not an alternative — it is a bridge.

## To management
You are right that the business needs structural improvement, not cosmetic compromise. I support that.

But you still owe us a quantified case. Repeating “structural problem” is not enough. We need:
- site-level cost data,
- utilization,
- product allocation through 2030,
- one-off restructuring costs,
- annual savings,
- and durability of each measure.

Without that, closure threats remain an assertion, not a demonstrated necessity.

## To the state government
Your role as anchor shareholder and political actor is legitimate, and I respect the regional responsibility you are carrying.

But I also have to be frank: a regional veto on hard decisions is not a strategy. If a site-preserving package can close the gap, fine — we should support it. If it cannot, then the state cannot simply block adjustment and expect long-term viability to follow.

## To investors
You are right to demand measurable, durable savings and a credible return to target margins. That is exactly what owners should insist on.

But you should not assume that the only credible route is immediate site rationalization. It may be the best route — but that must be proven, not presumed. If a site-preserving package can deliver equivalent durable savings, then that may preserve more value by reducing conflict and transition risk.

## My position
I am not committing to closures as the preferred outcome.

I am also not committing to a blanket no-closure rule.

What I am committing to is this:
- a **hard numbers-based comparison**
- of **zero-closure**, **partial rationalization**, and **closure-based** restructuring,
- with the burden on each side to show what is actually achievable.

## [SUMMON: independent site-level financial model]
We need a **site-by-site independent financial and operational model** that compares:
1. zero-closure restructuring,
2. partial site rationalization,
3. closure-based restructuring,

including:
- annual savings,
- one-off costs,
- payback period,
- utilization effects,
- workforce impact,
- and durability of savings.

That model should answer one question:  
**Can a site-preserving solution really restore competitiveness at the required scale and speed, or not?**

## Bottom line
I will support the solution that credibly restores margins and long-term competitiveness.

If that can be done without closures, excellent — show it.  
If it cannot, then I will not block structural measures just to avoid difficult decisions.

But I will not endorse a cosmetic compromise, and I will not support a settlement that permanently weakens the enterprise’s value or governance control.
