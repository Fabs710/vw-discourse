I’ll respond directly.

## To the Works Council
You keep asking management to prove that closures are necessary, as if the burden of proof alone somehow removes the underlying economics. It doesn’t.

I agree with you on one point: **non-closure measures can contribute materially**. They absolutely should be part of the package. Redeployment, qualification, voluntary exits, working-time flexibility, and overhead reductions are all on the table.

Where I **do not** agree is your attempt to turn “no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” into absolute red lines before the numbers are even tested. That is not a restructuring position. That is a political veto.

The expert has already said the crucial thing: **a zero-closure solution is possible in principle, but not proven sufficient in practice**. That means exactly what management has been saying all along: if the savings gap is large and structural, then site rationalisation may be required. You have not demonstrated that the gap can be closed without it.

So let me be equally clear:
- I am **not** accepting a blanket no-closure commitment.
- I am **not** accepting a blanket no-redundancy commitment.
- I am **not** accepting guarantees through 2030 that lock in an uncompetitive structure.

If you want to keep every site open, then show how you achieve the same **durable fixed-cost reduction** and **capacity correction**. Not slogans. Numbers.

## To the Union
You say the workforce is prepared to contribute, but only if the package is shared and limited. I welcome that language. What I do **not** accept is the idea that employee contributions must be only temporary while the structural imbalance remains permanent.

That is the central problem with your position.

Temporary wage restraint, bonus sacrifices, and voluntary exits can help bridge a gap. They do not automatically solve a structural cost problem. If the company needs permanent relief, then the solution must be permanent enough to deliver it. Otherwise we are just postponing the crisis.

I also reject the claim that management is using closures only as leverage. No — closures remain on the table because they are a real restructuring instrument. If the economics require it, it would be irresponsible for management to rule them out in advance.

So yes:
- employee contribution,
- yes to working-time changes,
- yes to redeployment and qualification,
- yes to voluntary separation tools.

But no:
- to pretending those alone are sufficient without proof,
- to turning labor protections into a no-risk shield against structural adjustment,
- and to a package that leaves the brand’s return target unreachable.

## To the State Government
I appreciate your insistence on regional responsibility. I also understand why you do not want closures as the default. But you are not being honest with the company if you treat “site-preserving” as a goal regardless of economics.

I agree with you that closure should not be a reflex.

Where I disagree is your apparent assumption that you can support a solution that preserves the footprint first and fixes competitiveness second. That order does not work.

If the state wants to help shape a durable outcome, then it needs to support a package that:
- reduces cost structurally,
- improves utilization,
- and restores competitiveness.

If that can be done without closures, excellent. If not, then the state cannot simply block the adjustment and expect the company to remain viable.

## To the Controlling Family Holding
You are right to focus on long-term enterprise value. I agree with that.

Where I disagree is any suggestion that waiting for a perfect alternative is better than acting now. Delay also destroys value. Every month we carry excess cost base and weak utilization, we lose margin, we lose flexibility, and we weaken the investment case for 2030.

So yes, we need durable value preservation. But durability means structural change. It does **not** mean preserving every legacy arrangement and hoping for the best.

I support a hard restructuring because the brand cannot fund the future on the current cost base.

## To Investors
You are right to demand quantified savings, implementation certainty, and a clear path back to target margins. That is exactly what management is pushing for.

What I do not accept is the idea that a non-closure deal is automatically the safer value path. It may be, but only if it produces **equivalent permanent savings**. So far, nobody has shown that.

The expert’s assessment is clear:
- zero-closure can work only if labor concessions are unusually deep and durable;
- partial rationalization is the most credible middle case;
- closures are the most reliable tool for large structural correction.

That is why I will not commit the company to a cosmetic no-closure outcome just because it sounds more acceptable politically.

## On the expert request
I support the call for a site-level model, but let’s be precise about why it is needed: not to create a delay tactic, and not to search for an excuse to avoid hard decisions, but to compare options in a disciplined way.

[SUMMON: independent site-level financial model to quantify by plant the annual savings, one-off restructuring costs, payback period, capacity reduction, utilization effects, workforce impact, and durability of savings for zero-closure, partial rationalization, and closure-based scenarios.]

That model should answer one question:
**Can a site-preserving solution close the competitiveness gap at the speed and scale required, or not?**

If yes, I will consider it. If no, then the discussion must move to site rationalisation.

## My position, plainly stated
I am prepared to discuss:
- capacity reduction,
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- voluntary exits,
- qualification,
- temporary employee contributions,
- and targeted cost measures.

I am **not** prepared to accept:
- a blanket no-closure rule,
- a blanket no-compulsory-redundancy rule,
- binding job guarantees through 2030 regardless of economics,
- or a settlement that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

## Bottom line
We need a restructuring that is:
- durable,
- financially sufficient,
- and capable of restoring returns.

If the numbers show that this can be done without closures, then fine — show it.  
If they do not, then closures and broader workforce reductions remain legitimate instruments, and I will not pretend otherwise.

That is the reality.
