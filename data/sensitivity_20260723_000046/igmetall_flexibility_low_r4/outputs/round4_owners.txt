I will be direct.

## To management
I agree with your diagnosis: the company needs durable structural savings, not cosmetic measures. I also agree that non-closure measures can contribute materially and should be part of the package.

Where I do **not** agree is with the implication that closures and compulsory redundancies should remain an essentially open-ended threat unless labor first accepts a broad surrender. That is not governance discipline; that is negotiation by pressure.

You still owe us the **site-by-site proof**. If you want the family holding to support a hard restructuring, then show:
- plant-level costs,
- utilization,
- one-off restructuring costs,
- annual savings,
- payback period,
- and durability of each measure.

Without that, closure remains a possibility, not a case.

## To the works council and union
I understand your insistence on trust, sites, and jobs. I also agree that temporary concessions alone are not enough if the business problem is permanent.

But I do **not** accept your position that **no closures** and **no compulsory redundancies** can be absolute starting principles regardless of economics. If the numbers show a large structural gap, then that position becomes a way to freeze the problem and shift the burden to owners, future investment, and ultimately the company’s viability.

That said, you are right on one point: **closures should not be the first reflex without proof**. They must be justified, not merely threatened.

## To the state government
I respect your role as regional anchor shareholder and your responsibility for employment. But I do not support turning that into a blanket veto on hard decisions.

A regional anchor only remains credible if the business remains competitive. If a site-preserving package can close the gap, I will support it. If it cannot, then blocking adjustment in the name of regional stability only delays the damage and makes the eventual outcome worse.

## To investors
You are right to demand permanent cost relief and a believable path to target margins. I share that concern.

But I do not accept a simplistic conclusion that the biggest immediate footprint cut is automatically the best value answer. Value also depends on execution risk, labor peace, regional legitimacy, and the ability to keep investing in future products. That is why I want the numbers before any final judgment.

## My position
I am **not** committing to closures as the preferred outcome.

I am **not** committing to a blanket no-closure rule either.

What I am committing to is a hard, quantified comparison of:
1. zero-closure restructuring,
2. partial rationalization,
3. closure-based restructuring.

That comparison must answer one question: **Can a site-preserving package deliver sufficient durable savings at the required speed, or not?**

[SUMMON: independent site-level financial and operational model comparing the three scenarios by plant, including annual savings, one-off costs, payback period, capacity reduction, utilization effects, workforce impact, and durability of savings.]

## Bottom line
I will back the solution that credibly restores competitiveness and margins while preserving long-term enterprise value.

If that can be done without closures, fine — prove it.
If it cannot, then structural measures must remain on the table.

What I will **not** support is a cosmetic compromise that preserves every legacy arrangement, avoids hard choices, and leaves the company structurally uncompetitive. That would be value-destructive, and I will not endorse it.
