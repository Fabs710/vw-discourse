## Bottom line
Based on the discussion so far, the economically relevant question is not whether closures are “good” or “bad,” but whether a **site-preserving package can deliver enough durable fixed-cost reduction, capacity correction, and workforce reduction** to restore margins at acceptable speed and risk.

From an industrial-restructuring perspective:

- **Zero-closure restructuring** is **possible in principle**, but only if the measures are deep, durable, and enforceable.
- **Partial site rationalization** is usually the **most plausible compromise** if the savings gap is material and closures are politically constrained.
- **Closure-based restructuring** is the **strongest tool** for removing excess capacity and fixed cost, but it carries the highest execution, labor-relations, and governance risk.

## How I would structure the analysis
A proper comparison should be done by plant and by year for three scenarios:

### 1) Zero-closure restructuring
Typical levers:
- voluntary exits
- early retirement
- hiring freeze
- working-time reductions / shift redesign
- temporary wage moderation
- overhead cuts
- redeployment and qualification

Key risk:
- savings may be too temporary unless a large share of the labor contribution becomes permanent.

### 2) Partial site rationalization
Typical levers:
- closing selected lines, modules, or support functions
- consolidating volume into fewer sites
- removing duplicate capabilities
- reducing fixed overhead and logistics duplication

Key advantage:
- better chance of producing durable savings without a full footprint exit.

Key risk:
- transition cost and network disruption can be significant.

### 3) Closure-based restructuring
Typical levers:
- full site closure
- transfer of volume
- elimination of fixed site overhead
- workforce reduction tied to asset exit

Key advantage:
- strongest permanent cost reduction.

Key risk:
- highest industrial conflict and implementation delay.

## What the likely hierarchy is
Absent hard numbers, the most defensible hierarchy is:

1. **If the gap is modest:** zero-closure may work.  
2. **If the gap is substantial but not extreme:** partial rationalization is usually the best compromise.  
3. **If the gap is very large and persistent:** closures may be economically justified, though still difficult to execute.
