I will be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family holding, and the expert on the central financial point:

- **doing nothing is not an option**;
- the current cost base is **too high**;
- **durable** savings are what matter, not symbolic concessions;
- and a prolonged strike or open-ended confrontation would be **value-destructive**.

I also agree with the employee side on one narrower point: if there is a **credible, quantified, and durable** non-closure alternative, it should be considered. We are not wedded to closures as an ideology. We are wedded to **margin recovery, cash generation, and valuation discipline**.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** support the Works Council’s and union’s attempt to make **“no closures”** and **“no compulsory redundancies”** into non-negotiable end states.

That may be understandable politically. It is not acceptable financially if it prevents the company from restoring a competitive cost base.

I also do **not** support a settlement that:
- preserves every site unchanged,
- relies mainly on temporary concessions,
- and leaves the return-on-sales problem unresolved.

That would be a cosmetic compromise, and the market will price it as such.

## My reading of the expert assessment
The expert has clarified the real issue: **durability**.

What matters to shareholders is not whether a package sounds socially balanced. It is whether it produces:
- permanent enough cost reduction,
- enough capacity correction,
- and a believable path back to target margins.

From that perspective:

- **Zero-closure** is possible in theory, but only if the employee contribution is unusually deep, durable, and enforceable.
- **Partial site rationalization** looks like the most credible middle ground if the gap is substantial.
- **Closure-based restructuring** remains the strongest tool if the savings gap is large and persistent.

That is a reasonable hierarchy. It is also why I will not commit to a no-closure outcome in advance.

## To management
You are right to reject a cosmetic settlement. But you now need to be more disciplined in what you present.

I want to see:
- site-by-site quantified savings,
- one-off restructuring costs,
- payback period,
- capacity removed,
- labor-cost contribution durability,
- and the margin impact by year.

Without that, the market cannot assess whether this is a real restructuring or just a prolonged negotiation.

If management’s case for closures is strong, then show it. If a site-preserving package is genuinely sufficient, then show that too. But stop arguing in abstractions.

## To the Works Council
I understand your defense of jobs and sites. But your position is too absolute for a company with structural overcapacity and a weak margin profile.

If you want shareholders to support a site-preserving solution, it must be:
- quantitatively credible,
- durable beyond one bargaining round,
- and strong enough to close the competitiveness gap.

Right now, I do **not** see that proof.

A package of temporary pain with hard guarantees through 2030 may be socially attractive, but if it leaves the business structurally weak, it destroys value through delay.

## To the union
You are right that employees should not carry the whole burden. But you are not entitled to limit your contribution to temporary measures if the cost problem is permanent.

What I would support from labor is:
- working-time flexibility,
- bonus restraint,
- temporary wage moderation,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- and qualification.

What I will **not** support is using those tools to avoid real structural adjustment while the company remains uncompetitive.

## To the state government
Your regional responsibility is legitimate, and I recognize the political reality. But the state cannot let “regional legitimacy” become a blanket veto over economically necessary adjustment.

If the company can be fixed without closures, fine. If it cannot, then blocking restructuring does not save jobs — it postpones the loss of value and likely increases the eventual damage.

## To the controlling family
I agree with you more than you agree with the employee side: long-term enterprise value matters more than optics.

But long-term value is not protected by vague promises either. It requires hard choices and measurable outcomes. That means no cosmetic deal and no delay dressed up as social balance.

## What would move us
We would support a settlement if it can show, in quantified terms:

1. **Meaningful permanent cost reduction**
2. **Clear capacity adjustment**
3. **A believable return to target margins**
4. **Implementation certainty**
5. **Limited execution risk**

That is the threshold. Anything less is insufficient.

## My current stance on the three scenarios

### 1) Zero-closure restructuring
Possible only if the numbers are exceptionally strong.  
At present, I am skeptical that it can deliver enough permanent savings.

### 2) Partial site rationalization
This is the most plausible compromise from an investor perspective.  
It is the best chance of combining durability with lower conflict risk.

### 3) Closure-based restructuring
Not preferred socially, but acceptable if it is the only route that credibly restores competitiveness.  
We will not rule it out on political grounds.

## What I am not prepared to accept
I am **not** prepared to support:
- a blanket no-closure commitment,
- a blanket no-redundancy commitment,
- or guarantees through 2030 that lock in an uncompetitive structure.

Those are not value-preserving commitments. They are value-destroying if the economics do not support them.

## Bottom line
From the capital markets side, our position is straightforward:

- **We will back the package that credibly restores margins and valuation.**
- If that can be done without closures, fine — prove it.
- If not, then partial rationalization or closures must remain on the table.
- What we will **not** support is a deal that preserves the current structure and calls that a solution.

That would be a cosmetic settlement, and we will not pretend otherwise.
