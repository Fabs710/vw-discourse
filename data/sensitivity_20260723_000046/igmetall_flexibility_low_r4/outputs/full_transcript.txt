[Works Council (Betriebsrat) (Plant-level employee representation under co-determination) — Round 1]
As Works Council, I will be very clear from the outset:

We will not accept plant closures in Germany, and we will not accept compulsory redundancies.

The workforce has already carried a great deal of the burden of transformation. What management has now put on the table is not a balanced restructuring, but a unilateral attempt to shift the crisis onto employees and entire regions. That is unacceptable.

Our position is this:

- Keep every German plant open.
- Secure jobs, with binding guarantees through 2030.
- Avoid forced layoffs; if there must be adjustments, they must be through socially responsible, voluntary, and reversible measures.
- Preserve co-determination and the existing job-security framework.
- Any contribution from employees must be matched by real commitments from management on investment, product allocation, and site security.

We are prepared to discuss alternatives that genuinely protect sites and employment: capacity adjustments, working-time solutions, qualification, internal redeployment, and negotiated cost contributions. But these must be part of a fair package, not a pretext for closures or breaking long-standing commitments.

Management needs to understand: if this is presented as a fait accompli, there will be fierce resistance. We have the mandate, the legitimacy, and the means to defend the workforce.

## Scenario comparison

### 1) Zero-closure restructuring

**Definition:** all German plants remain open; savings come from working-time adjustments, voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, temporary wage restraint, bonus reductions, overhead cuts, and productivity improvements.

### Likely savings profile
- **Short term:** meaningful.
- **Medium term:** moderate.
- **Long term:** uncertain unless concessions are made durable.

This scenario can generate savings from:
- lower payroll cost via attrition and voluntary exits,
- reduced bonuses and allowances,
- working-time reductions,
- internal mobility and redeployment,
- selective process simplification,
- overhead and procurement cuts.

### Strengths
- Avoids immediate closure costs.
- Preserves industrial footprint and regional legitimacy.
- Lower strike and political escalation risk than closure.
- Faster to implement if negotiated.

### Weaknesses
- Savings may be **insufficient in scale** if the structural gap is large.
- Many measures are **reversible** or **time-limited**.
- Fixed costs remain if all sites stay open.
- Overcapacity is not fully removed.
- Utilization may improve, but not enough to change the economics materially.

### Employment impact
- No plant closure jobs lost.
- Still likely substantial workforce reduction through voluntary exits and attrition if savings are real.
- Depending on the package, workforce reductions of a meaningful size can still occur, but typically without the full fixed-cost relief of a shutdown.

### Bottom line
A zero-closure package is credible only if the required savings are **within reach of labor-cost and overhead measures alone**. If the restructuring target is large and permanent, this scenario often underdelivers.

---

### 2) Partial site rationalization

**Definition:** one or more sites or site functions are reduced, consolidated, or partially closed; capacity is removed selectively; some production is shifted; the company keeps most, but not all, German footprint.

### Likely savings profile
- **Short term:** moderate, with some transition costs.
- **Medium to long term:** strong and durable.

This scenario usually delivers the best compromise between:
- permanent fixed-cost reduction,
- capacity correction,
- and political/social feasibility.

### Strengths
- Removes part of the fixed-cost base.
- Improves utilization at remaining sites.
- Can be aligned with product allocation and capex concentration.
- More durable than labor concessions alone.
- Usually more feasible than full closure if the workforce has blocking power.

### Weaknesses
- Requires difficult negotiations.
- Some one-off restructuring costs remain high.
- Implementation complexity can be substantial if production is moved between sites.
- Savings depend on whether the consolidated sites are actually competitive.

### Employment impact
- Job losses are significant but below a full closure case.
- Some redeployment possible.
- Can preserve more core jobs than closure-based restructuring.
- Often the most realistic way to reduce headcount and capacity while avoiding a total rupture.

### Bottom line
If management’s problem is structurally real but not catastrophic, partial rationalization is usually the **most economically defensible and operationally credible** solution.

---

### 3) Closure-based restructuring

**Definition:** one or more German plants are fully closed; capacity is removed permanently; associated functions and headcount are eliminated or relocated.

### Likely savings profile
- **Short term:** high costs, because severance, transfer, decommissioning, and transition expenses are large.
- **Medium to long term:** highest permanent savings.

This scenario most effectively eliminates:
- fixed site overhead,
- duplicated maintenance and support functions,
- low-utilization production lines,
- and legacy operating complexity.

### Strengths
- Strongest permanent capacity reduction.
- Best way to fix a persistent overcapacity problem.
- Highest certainty of structural savings.
- Can materially improve utilization at surviving sites.

### Weaknesses
- Very high social and political resistance.
- Industrial action risk is high.
- One-off costs are substantial.
- Supply chain and ramp-up risks at receiving sites can be large.
- If done poorly, may damage morale, retention, and execution elsewhere.

### Employment impact
- Highest direct job loss.
- Secondary losses through suppliers and regional ecosystem effects.
- Most disruptive to labor relations.

### Bottom line
This is the most powerful instrument if the business needs **large, durable, non-reversible cost and capacity reduction**. It is also the most contentious and costly to implement.

- Plant closures
- Line eliminations
- Product consolidation

- Hiring freezes
- Redeployment

- Temporary wage restraint
- Bonus cuts
- Working-time reductions

- Higher utilization
- Better shift logic
- Automation
- Overhead reduction

- Product allocation changes

The model should quantify annual savings, one-off costs, payback period, capacity removed, utilization impact, workforce impact, and durability of savings, and should be paired with a time-limited no-closure negotiation phase while results are validated.

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction
- The real dispute is not “closure vs. no closure” in the abstract; it is the **proof standard** required before closure becomes legitimate.
- The positions hardened around **trust and sequencing**: labor views closure backstops as coercive, while capital views unconditional no-closure as economically unserious.
- A zero-closure solution was acknowledged as **possible in principle** even by the expert, but only if it is deep, durable, and quantitatively sufficient.
- The most plausible compromise that emerged is **partial rationalization after a proof-gated first phase**, not an all-or-nothing settlement.
- The state and family owner are not aligned with either extreme; they function as **bridge actors** seeking a solution that avoids both a politically explosive closure campaign and a value-destructive stalemate.
