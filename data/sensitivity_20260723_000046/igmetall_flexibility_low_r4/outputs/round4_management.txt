## To the Works Council
You keep framing this as if management is asking for closures because we enjoy confrontation. That is false.

We are asking for **durable structural cost relief** because the current footprint is **not competitive**. The brand misses its return target, the European market is shrinking, and German factory costs are far above target. If you want every site preserved, then you must explain how the company gets to a **permanently lower cost base** and **higher utilization** without structural footprint change.

You still have not done that.

I agree with you on one point: **non-closure measures can contribute materially**. Voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, working-time flexibility, and overhead cuts are real levers. We should use them.

Where I disagree is your attempt to turn **“no closures” and “no compulsory redundancies” into absolute starting rules**. That is not a restructuring position. That is a veto. And a veto is not enough when the business model is under pressure.

If you want to defend every German plant, then show me:
- the annual savings by site,
- the utilization effects,
- the one-off cost,
- and, most importantly, the **permanent** reduction in fixed costs.

Until then, closures remain a legitimate instrument.

## To the Union
I welcome your willingness to discuss employee contributions. That is the first constructive thing in this exchange.

But let’s not pretend that **temporary wage restraint, bonus sacrifices, and voluntary exits** automatically solve a structural problem. They do not. If the cost gap is permanent, then the response must be permanent enough to fix it.

So yes:
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary exits,
- limited labor-cost contribution.

No:
- compulsory redundancies as the default,
- cosmetic “shared sacrifice” that leaves the structure intact,
- or a deal that protects the old cost base and shifts the burden onto shareholders and future investment.

If you want a serious package, then your side has to accept that **durable competitiveness** matters more than preserving every legacy guarantee.

## To the State Government
I respect your regional responsibility. I also understand why closures are politically difficult.

But I will be clear: the state cannot ask the company to remain a regional anchor **and** block the structural adjustment needed to make that possible. That is not stewardship; that is wishful thinking.

I agree that closures should not be the first reflex. They are **not** the first reflex. That is why we asked for the independent model.

What I do not accept is your attempt to treat a site-preserving alternative as the default and closures as somehow illegitimate before the numbers are even tested. The company cannot be governed by political comfort.

If the site-level model shows that a no-closure package closes the gap, I will support it. If it does not, then the state cannot simply say “no closures” and expect the economics to obey.

## To the Controlling Family Holding
You are right to focus on long-term enterprise value. That is exactly my point.

But value is not preserved by delaying hard decisions until the cost base becomes even more uncompetitive. Every month we postpone structural adjustment, we burn margin and reduce our room to invest in future products and technology to 2030.

So I agree with you on:
- durability,
- execution certainty,
- and the need for measurable savings.

I disagree with any suggestion that we should wait for a perfect non-closure solution before acting. If the model proves that the gap cannot be closed without site rationalization, then preserving every site becomes value-destructive. We should not confuse sentiment with strategy.

## To Institutional Investors
You are right to demand quantified, durable savings and a believable path to target margins. That is exactly why management is insisting on structural measures.

But I do **not** accept your implication that the only serious path is the one with the biggest immediate footprint reduction. The right path is the one that actually restores competitiveness with acceptable execution risk.

That may be:
- zero-closure, if it truly delivers permanent savings;
- partial rationalization, if it is the most credible compromise;
- or closures, if that is what the numbers ultimately demand.

What we cannot accept is a deal that sounds cooperative but leaves us with the same cost problem in a different wrapper.

## My position on the “bridge” ideas
### 1) Phased restructuring with a proof-gated backstop
This is not a trap. It is a disciplined approach.

You call it a Trojan horse. I call it responsible governance:
- Phase 1: serious non-closure measures and employee contribution.
- Phase 2: if the independent model shows the gap remains, then further structural action.

That is exactly how you avoid both a cosmetic deal and a premature closure.

### 2) No compulsory redundancies, but conditional structural rationalization
This is workable **only if** the rationalization is real and the savings are durable.

I am not interested in semantic games. If “conditional rationalization” means actual footprint and cost reduction, fine. If it means nothing beyond delay, then no.

### 3) Site guarantees tied to measurable contributions and review clauses
This can be part of a settlement, but only if the guarantees are **credible**, not absolute.
A binding 2030 guarantee regardless of economics is not credible. A review clause is not a betrayal; it is what keeps the deal honest.

## What I am prepared to support
I remain prepared to discuss:
- capacity reduction,
- working-time flexibility,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- voluntary exits,
- temporary employee contributions,
- overhead cuts,
- and product/investment commitments.

What I am **not** prepared to accept:
- a blanket no-closure rule,
- a blanket no-redundancy rule,
- guarantees through 2030 that freeze an uncompetitive structure,
- or a settlement that fails to restore the return-on-sales target and free funds for future products and technology.

## Bottom line
I am not retreating.

If the numbers show that a site-preserving package can deliver **equivalent permanent savings**, then it deserves serious consideration.  
If they do not, then closure and broader workforce reduction remain legitimate instruments, and I will not pretend otherwise.

The company needs a restructuring that is:
- durable,
- economically sufficient,
- and fast enough to protect competitiveness.

Anything less is not a solution.
