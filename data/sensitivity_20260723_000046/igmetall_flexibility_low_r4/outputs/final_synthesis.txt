## 1. Decision context
The company faces a structural competitiveness problem in its German footprint: high fixed and labor costs, weak European demand, and pressure from lower-cost competitors. Management seeks durable cost and capacity reductions, including possible plant closures and workforce reductions; employee-side actors seek restructuring without closures or compulsory redundancies. Because of co-determination, family control, and state shareholding, the decision requires broad legitimacy and is not solvable by management alone.

## 2. Stakeholder assembly
- **Group & Brand Management**: initiates restructuring and holds operational diagnosis.
- **Works Council**: plant-level employee representation with co-determination power and effective veto capacity.
- **Industrial Union**: bargaining counterpart with strike capacity.
- **Regional State Government**: anchor shareholder and political actor with blocking leverage.
- **Controlling Family Holding**: majority voting-rights controller through governance.
- **Institutional Investors**: capital-markets shareholders seeking value recovery.
- **Independent Expert**: technical advisor on restructuring feasibility and durability.

## 3. Final positions
### Group & Brand Management
Management insists the current structure is not competitive and that savings must be durable, sufficiently large, and fast. It will not accept a blanket no-closure or no-redundancy rule, but is open to non-closure measures if they can be proven to deliver equivalent permanent fixed-cost relief.

### Works Council
The works council rejects German plant closures and compulsory redundancies as bargaining starting points. It accepts capacity adjustment, redeployment, qualification, voluntary exits, working-time changes, and temporary employee contributions, but only if sites and jobs are protected through binding guarantees.

### Industrial Union
The union aligns with the works council on refusing forced layoffs, closures as leverage, and dismantling of the collective framework. It is willing to discuss shared sacrifice, but only in a package that preserves jobs/sites and avoids workers bearing the full burden.

### Regional State Government
The state supports restructuring in principle but resists closures and forced layoffs as first-line instruments. It wants a site-preserving path if possible, insists on plant-by-plant proof, and is cautious about becoming complicit in politically damaging shutdowns.

### Controlling Family Holding
The family owner agrees the company needs hard restructuring and durable savings, but also demands quantified proof before endorsing closures. It rejects a permanent no-closure veto and favors a disciplined, evidence-based process with closure retained as a legitimate last-resort option.

### Institutional Investors
Investors prioritize durable cost reduction and margin restoration. They are open to site-preserving solutions only if those deliver equivalent permanent savings; otherwise they want partial rationalization or closures left available.

### Independent Expert
The expert concludes the key issue is not whether the company has a problem, but whether any package can deliver durable fixed-cost relief at sufficient scale and speed. The expert sees zero-closure restructuring as possible in principle, but likely only if savings are deep and lasting; partial rationalization appears the most realistic compromise if no-closure proves insufficient.

## 4. Genuine conflicts (structurally incompatible positions)
- **Closure legitimacy:** employee and state actors treat closures as unacceptable absent proof; management, owners, and investors treat them as a necessary available instrument.
- **Forced redundancies:** labor rejects them categorically; capital-side actors refuse a blanket prohibition.
- **Guarantee horizon:** employee side wants binding site/job security through 2030; management and owners say such guarantees are not credible without restored competitiveness.
- **Proof standard:** no shared threshold exists for when site-preserving measures are “insufficient” and closure may be activated.
- **Trust deficit:** labor does not trust a closure backstop; capital does not trust temporary concessions to be durable.

## 5. Areas of convergence
- Doing nothing is not an option.
- The restructuring must be durable, credible, and economically real.
- Purely cosmetic compromise will fail.
- Non-closure measures can contribute materially.
- An independent site-level model is necessary.
- Investment/product commitments and employee contributions should be part of any settlement.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker
- **Jobs vs. competitiveness:** how much employment protection can be preserved without locking in uncompetitive costs.
- **Site preservation vs. structural savings:** whether all German sites can stay open while still achieving sufficient permanent savings.
- **Speed vs. social legitimacy:** faster restructuring through closures versus slower but socially managed adjustment.
- **Certainty vs. flexibility:** hard guarantees for labor versus management’s need to retain fallback options.
- **Political legitimacy vs. governance discipline:** the state’s regional role constraining economically necessary change.

## 7. Integrative pathways
### A. Time-limited, proof-gated no-closure phase
Implement a defined non-closure package first: voluntary exits, redeployment, qualification, working-time flexibility, temporary labor contribution, overhead cuts, and product/investment commitments. Closures and compulsory redundancies are suspended during a fixed proof period while the independent model is completed.

### B. Plant-specific thresholds and staged escalation
Assign each plant target metrics for savings, utilization, and contribution. If a site fails the agreed metrics after the first phase, only narrowly defined structural rationalization is reopened, rather than immediate closure.

### C. Conditional guarantees with jointly audited review
Offer time-bound site/job guarantees in exchange for measurable employee contributions and scheduled review points. This preserves a planning horizon for labor and the state while keeping a controlled adjustment mechanism for capital.

## 8. Open questions
- What exact site-level savings threshold would make a zero-closure plan credible?
- Are the savings from working-time and wage contributions durable enough, or only temporary?
- Which plants, if any, are structurally underutilized beyond recovery?
- How large is the one-off cost and execution risk of closure compared with partial rationalization?
- What independent trigger should activate any fallback?
- Can management, labor, and owners agree on a common model and audit process?

## 9. Recommended next step
Commission and jointly review an independent plant-by-plant restructuring model comparing:
1. zero-closure restructuring,
2. partial rationalization,
3. closure-based restructuring.

The model should quantify annual savings, one-off costs, payback period, capacity removed, utilization impact, workforce impact, and durability of savings, and should be paired with a time-limited no-closure negotiation phase while results are validated.

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction
- The real dispute is not “closure vs. no closure” in the abstract; it is the **proof standard** required before closure becomes legitimate.
- The positions hardened around **trust and sequencing**: labor views closure backstops as coercive, while capital views unconditional no-closure as economically unserious.
- A zero-closure solution was acknowledged as **possible in principle** even by the expert, but only if it is deep, durable, and quantitatively sufficient.
- The most plausible compromise that emerged is **partial rationalization after a proof-gated first phase**, not an all-or-nothing settlement.
- The state and family owner are not aligned with either extreme; they function as **bridge actors** seeking a solution that avoids both a politically explosive closure campaign and a value-destructive stalemate.
