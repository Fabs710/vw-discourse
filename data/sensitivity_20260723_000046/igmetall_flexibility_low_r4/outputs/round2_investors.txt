From the capital markets side, the discussion is now finally at the right level: **not slogans, but whether the savings are durable enough to restore margins**.

## Where we agree
I agree with management, the family holding, and the expert on the basic diagnosis:

- **Doing nothing is not an option.**
- The current cost base is **too high**.
- **Temporary or cosmetic measures are not sufficient** if they leave the business structurally uncompetitive.
- A prolonged strike or open conflict would be **value-destructive**.

I also agree with the Works Council, union, and state on one practical point: **if there is a credible, quantified, durable alternative to closures, it should be considered**. We are not attached to plant closures as an ideology. We are attached to **returns, free cash flow, and a credible path back to target margins**.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** support the employee side’s attempt to turn “no closures” into a hard principle.

That is not acceptable to shareholders if it prevents the company from reaching a competitive cost base.

Likewise, I do **not** accept a settlement that:
- preserves all sites,
- relies mainly on temporary concessions,
- and leaves the return-on-sales problem unresolved.

If the result is simply deferred pain, then it is not a solution. It is value erosion by delay.

## On the expert assessment
The expert’s analysis is helpful, and it points to the key issue: **durability**.

What I take from it is:

- **Zero-closure can work in theory**, but only if labor concessions are deep, durable, and enforceable.
- **Partial site rationalization is likely the most credible compromise** if the savings gap is substantial.
- **Closure-based restructuring is the strongest instrument** if the gap is large and persistent.

That is consistent with our financial view. We do not need the harshest instrument for its own sake. But we do need the instrument that actually closes the margin gap.

## What would move us
We would support a settlement if it can show, in quantified terms:

1. **Meaningful permanent cost reduction**
2. **A clear effect on return on sales**
3. **Implementation certainty**
4. **Limited execution risk**
5. **A credible timeline to restored competitiveness**

That means the proposal must spell out:
- savings by year,
- capacity removed,
- headcount impact,
- one-off costs,
- payback period,
- and how durable the labor contribution really is.

## Our current position on the options

### 1) Zero-closure package
We are **not opposed in principle**, but only if it is:
- quantified,
- durable,
- and large enough to restore margins.

Right now, based on what has been said, I do **not** yet see evidence that a zero-closure package can reliably match the structural savings of site rationalization. So at this stage, we are skeptical.

### 2) Partial site rationalization
This is currently the **most plausible middle ground** from a shareholder perspective.

Why?
- It can deliver durable fixed-cost relief.
- It is more credible than temporary concessions alone.
- It may preserve more value than a full closure-heavy plan by reducing conflict and transition risk.

If the numbers support it, this is the kind of package we would likely back.

### 3) Closure-based restructuring
We do not prefer this socially, but we will support it if it is the only way to restore competitiveness.

We will not rule it out because of political discomfort. What matters is whether it materially improves the enterprise value case.

## To management
Management is right not to accept a cosmetic settlement. But management now needs to be disciplined and specific:

- Show the **quantified gap**.
- Show which sites or functions are truly non-competitive.
- Show why a non-closure package closes or does not close the gap.
- Show the **timing** of savings and the impact on margins.

Without that, the market will continue to doubt whether this restructuring is serious.

## To the Works Council and union
Your insistence on protecting jobs and sites is understandable, but it is not sufficient if it prevents the cost base from being fixed.

If you want shareholders to support a site-preserving solution, then it has to be:
- bigger than symbolic,
- durable beyond a short bargaining cycle,
- and enforceable enough to matter to valuation.

A package of temporary sacrifices with hard guarantees through 2030 may be socially attractive, but if it leaves the company structurally weak, we will not support it.

## To the state government
Your role matters, and your push for a socially sustainable solution is noted. But the state should not encourage a false compromise that delays adjustment. Regional employment is important; so is the long-term viability of the company that supports it.

A competitive company is the best protection for jobs over time.

## To the controlling family
We agree on long-term enterprise value, but that value is not protected by ambiguity. The market needs clarity that this is a **real restructuring**, not a political holding pattern.

## Bottom line
From the capital markets side, the condition for support is straightforward:

- **We will back the package that credibly restores margins.**
- If that can be done with non-closure measures, fine.
- If not, then partial rationalization or closures must remain on the table.
- What we will **not** support is a deal that preserves the current structure and calls that compromise.
