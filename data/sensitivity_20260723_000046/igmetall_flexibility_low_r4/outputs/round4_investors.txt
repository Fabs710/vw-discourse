I’ll be direct.

## Where I agree
I agree with management, the family holding, and the expert on the central point: **doing nothing is not an option**. The company’s German cost base is too high, margins are under pressure, and the market will not reward a cosmetic settlement. I also agree that **durable** savings matter more than symbolic concessions or a short-lived truce.

I further agree that:
- working-time flexibility,
- voluntary exits,
- redeployment,
- qualification,
- overhead reduction,
- and some employee contribution

should all be on the table.

## Where I disagree
I do **not** accept the employee side’s attempt to make **“no closures”** and **“no compulsory redundancies”** absolute starting rules. From a shareholder perspective, that is only acceptable if it is shown to deliver **equivalent permanent savings** and a believable return to target margins. So far, that proof has not been provided.

I also do **not** support a settlement that:
- preserves every site unchanged,
- relies mainly on temporary labor concessions,
- and leaves the structural cost problem unresolved.

That would be a cosmetic compromise, and the market will price it accordingly.

## To the Works Council and union
I understand the desire to protect jobs and sites. But you are treating closure risk as illegitimate before the numbers are tested. That is not enough.

The expert’s point is the right one:
- a **zero-closure** solution is possible in principle,
- but it must be **deep and durable**,
- and we still do not have the plant-level proof that it can close the gap.

So if you want investors to back a site-preserving solution, show us:
- site-by-site savings,
- utilization effects,
- one-off costs,
- payback period,
- and, most importantly, whether the savings are **permanent**.

Until then, I will not endorse a blanket no-closure position.

## To management
You are right to reject a cosmetic settlement. But you still need to discipline the case you present. Repeating “structural problem” is not enough.

We need a proper comparison of:
1. **zero-closure restructuring**
2. **partial rationalization**
3. **closure-based restructuring**

with:
- annual savings,
- one-off restructuring costs,
- payback period,
- capacity removed,
- workforce impact,
- utilization effects,
- and durability of savings.

Without that, closure talk remains an assertion, not a demonstrated necessity.

## To the state government
I respect the regional responsibility, but I do not accept any implicit veto on economically necessary adjustment. If a site-preserving package can deliver the numbers, fine. If it cannot, then blocking structural change just delays the pain and worsens value destruction.

## To the controlling family
I agree that long-term enterprise value matters more than optics. But value is not protected by keeping every legacy arrangement intact. Delay is costly too. A deal that postpones the adjustment while pretending to solve it is not stewardship.

## My position
From the capital markets side, the line is straightforward:

- We will support the package that **credibly restores margins and competitiveness**.
- We are **open** to a no-closure solution **only if** it delivers durable, quantified savings at the required scale.
- Otherwise, **partial rationalization or closures must remain on the table**.
- What we will **not** support is a cosmetic compromise that leaves the company structurally uncompetitive.

## Bottom line
We back a hard restructuring if it restores competitive margins and valuation.

If that can be done without closures, prove it.
If it cannot, then closure and broader workforce reduction must remain available.
What we will not fund is a deal that preserves the current cost structure and calls that a solution.
