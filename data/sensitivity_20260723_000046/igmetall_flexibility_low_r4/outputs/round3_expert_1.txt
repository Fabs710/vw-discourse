From an industrial restructuring perspective, the conversation has now reached the right fault line: **positions are clear, but the economic proof is still missing**. The decisive issue is not rhetoric about “closures” versus “no closures,” but whether a **site-preserving package can deliver durable, company-wide fixed-cost reduction and capacity correction at sufficient scale and speed**.

## My assessment, within restructuring expertise

### 1) What is factually consistent with the situation
- A large German OEM with **overcapacity, high domestic labor and conversion costs, and margin pressure** is structurally exposed.
- In Germany, **co-determination, collective bargaining, and political-shareholder constraints** make plant closures and forced redundancies hard to execute.
- A **credible restructuring** in this setting usually needs some combination of:
  - capacity reduction,
  - workforce reduction,
  - labor-cost moderation,
  - working-time flexibility,
  - product reallocation,
  - and investment commitments.
- If closures are politically and governance-wise blocked, the burden shifts to **non-closure measures** to produce equivalent savings.

### 2) What is plausible inference
- The employee side is right that **closure is not the only imaginable restructuring instrument**.
- Management is also right that **temporary concessions alone are usually insufficient** if the problem is structural and permanent.
- The expert’s framing is the key one:  
  - **zero-closure is possible in principle**,  
  - but it must be **deep and durable**,  
  - and **partial rationalization** is often the most realistic compromise if the savings gap is material.

### 3) What remains unproven
No one in the discussion has yet shown, in plant-level numbers, whether:
- a **zero-closure deal** can close the cost gap,
- a **partial rationalization** scenario is enough,
- or **full site closures** are economically necessary.

That means the current dispute is still **under-modeled**. In restructuring practice, that is often where negotiations stall: each side argues from principle before there is a shared bridge model.

## What an independent site-level model must answer
To move this forward, the model should quantify by site and by year:

- current fixed and variable cost base,
- labor cost per vehicle,
- utilization rates,
- volume allocation,
- one-off restructuring costs,
- annual savings,
- payback period,
- impact on logistics and launch capacity,
- workforce impact,
- and whether savings are **permanent, contingent, or one-off**.

The critical question is not “can we save money?” but:
**Can we save enough, durably, without destroying operational coherence or deferring the problem?**

## Restructuring logic of the three scenarios

### A) Zero-closure restructuring
This is feasible only if the package includes several durable levers at once:
- headcount reduction mainly via voluntary exits,
- significant working-time and shift-pattern changes,
- permanent overhead reduction,
- product-line simplification,
- redeployment,
- and a labor contribution that does not expire too quickly.

**Risk:** if the measures are mostly temporary, the savings may look good in year 1 but fail to fix the structural cost base.

### B) Partial site rationalization
This is often the most credible industrial compromise when full closure is politically blocked.
It can achieve:
- capacity consolidation,
- better utilization at remaining sites,
- lower fixed costs,
- and a more durable cost step-down than temporary concessions alone.

**Risk:** transitional disruption can be high, and if the selected sites are operationally important, the network can become more fragile if done badly.

### C) Closure-based restructuring
This is the strongest instrument for large fixed-cost correction, because it removes capacity and overhead decisively.

**Risk:** it creates the highest labor conflict, political resistance, execution delay, reputational damage, and potential legal/industrial-relations complications in the German context.

## Likely industrial-relations reality
Given the governance described:
- a pure closure strategy is **hardest to execute**;
- a pure no-closure strategy is **hardest to make economically convincing** if the gap is large;
- the most negotiable outcome is usually a **hybrid package**:
  - one or more limited site rationalizations,
  - strong voluntary tools,
  - investment commitments,
  - and a time-bound employee contribution.

That is not a value judgment; it is the usual bargaining geometry in German industrial restructuring.

## Workforce implications
If the company wants to avoid forced redundancies, it still needs a credible mechanism for reducing jobs through:
- voluntary separation,
- early retirement,
- internal transfers,
- training into growing areas,
- reduced hiring,
- working-time changes.

But there is a hard rule in restructuring:  
**if headcount reduction is required and voluntary levers are insufficient, the gap does not disappear just because compulsory redundancies are politically unpopular.** It reappears as underperformance, delayed savings, or a later harsher adjustment.

## Governance constraint
The co-determination and shareholder veto structure means management cannot treat plant closure as a normal unilateral operational decision. In practice this means:
- the restructuring must be **negotiated as a package**,
- the package needs **mutual concessions**,
- and any settlement has to be **credible to the board, the workforce, and the capital markets simultaneously**.

That is why binding commitments on one side and binding savings on the other become central.

## Bottom line
My neutral restructuring view is:

- **Doing nothing is not viable.**
- **A zero-closure solution is not ruled out in principle.**
- **But it is not yet proven sufficient.**
- **Partial rationalization is the most plausible compromise if the savings gap is substantial.**
- **Closure-based restructuring remains the strongest economic tool, but also the most disruptive and least governable in this setting.**
