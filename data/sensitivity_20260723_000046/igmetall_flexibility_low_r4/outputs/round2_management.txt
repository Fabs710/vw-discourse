I’ll respond directly.

## First: where I agree
I agree with everyone on one basic point: **doing nothing is not an option**.

I also agree that any solution must be:
- **durable**
- **credible**
- **quantified**
- and capable of restoring the company’s competitiveness, not merely postponing the problem.

I further agree that a prolonged conflict would be damaging. A strike, supplier disruption, and reputational damage would be bad for the company and for everyone here.

## Where I disagree — and this is the core issue
I do **not** accept the employee side’s attempt to make **“no closures”** and **“no compulsory redundancies”** the starting and ending point of the discussion.

That is not a restructuring position. That is a demand to preserve the current structure regardless of economics.

And I will be equally blunt about the idea of **binding site and job guarantees through 2030**:  
I cannot commit to locking in an uncompetitive structure for that long while the brand is under pressure and our investment needs are urgent. That would be irresponsible to the company, to the owners, and to the future of the German operations themselves.

## To the Works Council
You say all German plants must remain open, and that any adjustment must be voluntary and reversible.

I understand the intent, but I reject the conclusion.

- **Voluntary-only measures are not enough** if they do not create the required structural savings.
- **Reversible measures** are not enough if we need permanent cost relief.
- **Keeping every plant open** is not a viable objective if it prevents the business from regaining competitiveness.

If we accept that the cost base is too high, then the adjustment must be real. That means some combination of:
- capacity reduction,
- workforce reduction,
- labour-cost contribution,
- working-time changes,
- and potentially site rationalisation.

If your position is that all German plants must stay open no matter what, then you are not offering a solution; you are defining away the problem.

## To the union
You say closures and compulsory redundancies are off the table, and that any contribution from employees must be temporary.

I disagree on both counts.

- A **temporary contribution alone** does not solve a structural cost problem.
- A settlement that only delays the burden and leaves the cost base intact is not enough.
- If the economics require permanent change, then the measures must be capable of delivering permanent effect.

You are right that employees should not be the only ones carrying the burden. But that does not mean the workforce contribution can be limited to temporary concessions while the structural issue remains untouched.

If you want to avoid closures, then show me a package that matches the savings, timing, and durability of closure-based restructuring. Not a slogan. Not a promise. A quantified package.

## To the state government
I welcome your acknowledgment that doing nothing is not an option.

But I have to be direct: saying closures should be a last resort is not the same as offering a durable alternative. If the state wants to prevent closures, then it needs to support a package that actually fixes the economics. Political discomfort does not change the cost curve.

A social partnership framework is fine. A framework that preserves employment while failing to restore competitiveness is not.

## To the controlling family holding
I agree with your core point that **long-term enterprise value matters more than short-term optics**.

Where I would sharpen it is this: value destruction also comes from delay. A compromise that protects appearances but leaves the business structurally weak is not stewardship; it is procrastination.

So yes, I support a durable restructuring. But durability means more than industrial peace. It means enough cost relief and capacity correction to restore returns and fund the next technology cycle.

## To investors
I agree with the insistence on measurable, durable savings.

But let me add: if the supervisory board wants to preserve value, it must not settle for a cosmetic arrangement that looks socially balanced but leaves the brand’s return-on-sales target out of reach. The market will not reward that. Neither will the capital allocation needs for 2030.

## What management is prepared to discuss
I am prepared to discuss a package built from:
- capacity reduction
- working-time flexibility
- internal redeployment
- voluntary separation tools
- qualification and transformation measures
- targeted labour-cost contributions

But I am **not** prepared to accept a package that:
- keeps every site untouched,
- guarantees every job unchanged,
- and merely shifts costs temporarily.

That is not a restructuring.

## The real test
The question is not whether we can all declare support for “responsible change.” The question is whether the employee side is prepared to accept **structural adjustment**.

If you want to rule out closures, then bring a credible alternative that delivers:
1. **durable cost reduction**
2. **material capacity reduction**
3. **real labour-cost relief**
4. **implementation at speed**
5. **a path back to target margins**

If that cannot be done without some site rationalisation, then I will not pretend otherwise.

## Bottom line
Management will not endorse a cosmetic compromise.

We need a settlement that is:
- structurally effective,
- financially sufficient,
- and durable enough to restore competitiveness through 2030.

If the proposal on the table cannot do that, then I will say so plainly: **it is not acceptable**.
