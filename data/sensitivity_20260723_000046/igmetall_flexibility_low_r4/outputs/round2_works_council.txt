I will respond just as clearly in round 2:

### First: what we agree on
Yes, doing nothing is not an option. Yes, the company needs a viable future. Yes, any settlement must be durable, credible, and economically real.

But those generalities do **not** justify plant closures or compulsory redundancies. They do **not** justify turning workers and regions into the shock absorber for management’s strategic failures. And they do **not** justify destroying the job-security framework that has underpinned this company for years.

### Where I draw the line
Let me be explicit:

- **No German plant closures.**
- **No compulsory redundancies.**
- **No unilateral dismantling of long-standing job guarantees.**
- **No “structural adjustment” that is just a polite label for firing people and closing sites.**

That is my position, and it has not changed.

### On management’s argument
Management keeps saying the cost base is uncompetitive and therefore closures must remain on the table. I hear the diagnosis. I reject the conclusion.

If the business needs savings, then management must first show that it has exhausted **all alternatives that protect sites and employment**. That means a serious package built from:
- capacity reductions,
- working-time adjustments,
- voluntary exits only,
- redeployment across sites,
- qualification and transformation measures,
- and a fair, negotiated employee contribution.

But if management wants us to consider any contribution from the workforce, then the exchange must be real:
- **binding site guarantees to 2030,**
- **binding job security,**
- **clear product allocation,**
- **investment commitments for German plants,**
- and a co-determination role in shaping implementation.

Without that, there is nothing to negotiate.

### On the ownership side
To the family holding and investors: I understand your demand for durable savings. But durability is not the same as plant closure. It is not the same as mass layoffs. And it is certainly not the same as demanding that the workforce surrender its protections while others preserve strategic freedom.

If you want value preservation, then preserving the industrial base matters. A company that tears up trust, provokes strikes, destroys regional employment, and weakens labour relations does not become stronger.

### On the state government
I welcome the clear resistance to closures and forced redundancies. But I will also say this: if the state really means what it says, then it should use its position to defend a genuine no-closure solution, not just express concern while management keeps closure threats alive as leverage.

### What would actually be acceptable to us
We are prepared to discuss a tough package, but only one that meets these conditions:

1. **Every German plant remains open**
2. **No compulsory redundancies**
3. **Adjustments are voluntary, socially responsible, and reversible**
4. **Any workforce contribution is temporary and limited**
5. **Management and owners give binding commitments on investment, products, and site security**
6. **Co-determination is preserved in shaping the restructuring**

If management can put forward quantified savings that come from these measures, then we can discuss them. But do not ask us to sign off on closures and job destruction as the price of “realism.”

### The real issue
The key question is not whether savings are needed. The key question is **who pays for the adjustment**.

Our answer is: not by closing plants, not by forced layoffs, and not by breaking trust with the workforce.

If management insists that those options remain “open,” then it is not negotiating in good faith. It is keeping the threat of destruction on the table to pressure employees into surrender. That will not work.

If you want a settlement, bring forward a package that keeps German plants open, protects jobs, and shares the burden fairly. Otherwise, expect resistance to remain fierce.
