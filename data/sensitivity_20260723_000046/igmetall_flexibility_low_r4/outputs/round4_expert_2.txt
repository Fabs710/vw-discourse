From a restructuring and site-economics perspective, the key question is not whether closures are politically preferable, but whether the company can achieve **durable fixed-cost relief at sufficient scale and speed** without them.

## My assessment of the three scenarios

### 1) Zero-closure restructuring
**Definition:** all German plants remain open; savings come from voluntary exits, working-time changes, redeployment, productivity gains, overhead cuts, and limited employee contributions.

**Likely strengths**
- Lowest political and social disruption
- Lowest immediate execution risk from labor conflict if negotiated well
- Preserves footprint and optionality
- Can work if the cost gap is moderate and there is meaningful overcapacity only in labor, not in sites

**Likely weaknesses**
- Savings may be too small or too slow if the problem is structural overcapacity
- Risk that “temporary” labor measures do not become permanent enough to restore competitiveness
- Often depends on a large employee contribution and significant productivity gains
- May preserve too much fixed cost if all sites and most capacities remain intact

**Economic test**
A zero-closure package is credible only if it can deliver:
- permanent annual savings large enough to close the gap,
- after one-off costs,
- with no material erosion of utilization or future product allocation.

If it mainly shifts pain into wages and voluntary exits while leaving the footprint unchanged, it is usually **not structurally sufficient**.

---

### 2) Partial site rationalization
**Definition:** some plant or line-level consolidation, but not a full closure-led reset; weaker or overlapping capacities are removed, strongest sites preserved.

**Likely strengths**
- Usually the best compromise between savings and implementability
- Produces more durable cost relief than a pure no-closure plan
- Can improve utilization at retained sites
- Reduces the number of “sacred cow” sites that absorb low utilization
- Often more credible to capital markets than a zero-closure solution

**Likely weaknesses**
- Still politically difficult
- Can trigger significant conflict if viewed as a wedge leading to wider closures
- Savings can be diluted if too little capacity is actually removed
- Requires careful sequencing to avoid underutilized survivors

**Economic test**
This is usually the most plausible route if the company has:
- some but not massive overcapacity,
- multiple overlapping German footprints,
- and a need to restore utilization without a total restructuring shock.

It is often the **best balance** if the zero-closure case fails but a full closure program is judged too costly or too risky.

---

### 3) Closure-based restructuring
**Definition:** one or more German plants are closed, capacity is removed structurally, and workforce reductions follow.

**Likely strengths**
- Highest potential for permanent fixed-cost reduction
- Strongest improvement in utilization at the remaining sites
- Fastest way to force a major step-change in the cost base
- Most convincing signal to investors if execution is clean

**Likely weaknesses**
- Highest one-off cost: severance, transfer, dismantling, environmental, logistics, and transition costs
- Highest implementation risk in a co-determined German setting
- High probability of conflict, delay, and indirect cost
- Can destroy value if litigation, strikes, or political intervention slow execution
- May reduce future flexibility if product allocation is misjudged

**Economic test**
Closure-based restructuring is economically justified only if:
- the savings are large,
- persistent,
- and the alternative scenarios fail to close the gap credibly.

Closures are not automatically superior; they are superior only if the **net present value** after transition costs and disruption is clearly better.

---

## What the model should show to distinguish the scenarios

The decision should be based on the following plant-level outputs:

1. **Annual recurring savings**
2. **One-off restructuring costs**
3. **Payback period**
4. **Capacity removed**
5. **Utilization after restructuring**
6. **Workforce impact**
7. **Durability of savings**
8. **Execution risk**
9. **Effect on future product allocation and investment efficiency**

## Practical trigger logic

A site-preserving package is credible only if it meets all three:
- **saves enough**
- **saves permanently**
- **saves quickly enough**

If it fails on any of those, then partial rationalization becomes more plausible. If even that fails, then closures become economically hard to avoid.

## Bottom-line view

- **Zero-closure restructuring** is possible in principle, but only if the cost gap is modest or the employee contribution plus productivity gains are unusually large.
- **Partial site rationalization** is often the most realistic and financially balanced compromise.
- **Closure-based restructuring** offers the strongest fixed-cost reset, but it also carries the highest one-off cost and execution risk.
