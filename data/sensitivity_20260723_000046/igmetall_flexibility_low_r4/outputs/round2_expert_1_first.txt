From an industrial-restructuring perspective, the key question is **not** whether savings are needed — they clearly are — but **whether the needed savings and capacity correction can be achieved credibly without plant closures**.

## My assessment, in plain terms

### 1) What is factually plausible
For a large German automotive manufacturer with:
- a high domestic cost base,
- overcapacity in Europe,
- weak utilization at some sites,
- and pressure to fund the EV transition,

it is **plausible** that management is facing a **structural** rather than purely cyclical problem. In that situation, measures limited to voluntary exits, temporary wage restraint, and working-time adjustments can help, but they often **do not fully close the gap** if the required savings are large and durable.

### 2) What non-closure measures can realistically deliver
A package without closures can still deliver meaningful savings through:
- reduced working hours / short-time-like arrangements,
- elimination or reduction of bonuses and premiums,
- temporary wage concessions,
- accelerated natural attrition,
- voluntary severance,
- redeployment,
- productivity improvements,
- procurement and overhead reduction,
- and tighter portfolio allocation.

But the critical issue is **durability**.  
Many of those measures are:
- time-limited,
- politically fragile,
- or dependent on continued labor cooperation.

That means they are good at buying time, and sometimes good at stabilizing a turnaround, but **less reliable for producing permanent cost relief at scale** than a genuine reduction in fixed industrial footprint.

### 3) When closures become economically credible
Plant closures become more credible when one or more of the following apply:
- the plant is structurally underutilized,
- its product pipeline is weak or end-of-life,
- investment would not restore competitiveness,
- logistics or labor costs are materially above benchmark,
- or the site duplicates capacity available elsewhere.

In that case, keeping the plant open can mean preserving jobs today but locking in a cost problem for years. That is usually the management logic behind closure proposals.

### 4) Are closures “unavoidable”?
I would **not** say closures are automatically unavoidable from the facts given.  
I would say:

- **They may be avoidable if** the required savings are moderate, if there is spare room in other cost categories, and if labor is willing to make a large and durable contribution.
- **They become increasingly likely if** the required savings are large, utilization is weak, and management needs permanent structural relief rather than a temporary bridge.

So the honest answer is: **possible, but not proven**.

### 5) What would be needed to judge this properly
To determine whether non-closure measures can replace site rationalization, you would need a quantified restructuring model with at least:
- current and target cost per vehicle by site,
- fixed vs variable cost breakdown,
- utilization rates by plant and line,
- product allocation through 2030,
- expected demand scenarios,
- redundancy and redeployment assumptions,
- labor-cost contribution parameters,
- capex requirements,
- and the timing profile of savings.

Without that, any claim that “closures are unavoidable” is **an assertion, not a demonstrated conclusion**.

## My bottom-line expert view
- **Non-closure measures can contribute materially.**
- **Whether they are sufficient depends on the magnitude of the savings gap.**
- **If management needs permanent structural savings on the scale implied here, a package with no closures and no compulsory redundancies is possible in theory, but it is usually hard to make credible in practice unless labor accepts unusually deep and durable concessions.**
- **So closures are not proven unavoidable, but they are a credible restructuring instrument if the quantified gap is large and persistent.**
