[Government Task Force (Lead Funder) (Executive-branch task force controlling rescue financing) — Round 1]
The government task force’s position is straightforward:

We will support a restructuring only if it is deep enough, fast enough, and credible enough to produce a viable company without recurring taxpayer support.

That means:

- **No open-ended subsidy** for an unrestructured carmaker.
- **Sacrifices from all private claimants**: management, labour, and unsecured creditors.
- **A meaningful balance-sheet reset**, not just operating cuts.
- **A plan that can be completed within the 60-day window**, or, if necessary, through an expedited court-supervised process that can impose terms.

Our priorities are:

1. **Viability**
   - The company must come out with a cost structure and debt burden it can actually sustain.

2. **Fair burden-sharing**
   - Labour has to accept real concessions.
   - The retiree health obligation cannot remain untouched in its current form if it makes the company unworkable.
   - Unsecured bondholders must take a meaningful haircut. They cannot be protected at taxpayer expense.

3. **Speed and finality**

### Bottom line
We are prepared to support a fast restructuring if, and only if:
- unsecured bondholders receive **meaningful** recovery,
- treatment is **not inferior** to comparable unsecured claims without compensation,
- and any conversion into equity or warrants gives us a real stake in the reorganized company.

If the current direction is to protect the retiree trust and labour first while asking bondholders to absorb the largest losses, then we are opposed. That is not a fair compromise, and we will not endorse it simply because the deadline is tight.

=== SYNTHESIS ===

## 1. Decision context
A collapsing major carmaker is being forced through a rapid restructuring under severe liquidity pressure, with only a short working-capital bridge and an explicit fallback to an expedited court-supervised process. The central decision is not simply whether to rescue the company, but how to distribute losses among unsecured bondholders, the retiree health trust, labour, public funders, and domestic industrial stakeholders while preserving a viable ongoing enterprise.

The multi-turn discussion clarified that all parties now accept the need for a real balance-sheet reset, deep operational change, and material sacrifice. The unresolved issue is the hierarchy of sacrifice: whether the retiree trust is treated as a special obligation or as ordinary unsecured debt, how deep bondholder losses must be, how much labour and plant consolidation is required, and what public funding should purchase in governance and domestic-production terms.

## 2. Stakeholder assembly
- **Government task force / lead funder**: highest decisional power; controls bridge financing and court-supervised fallback.
- **Company management under new leadership**: high operational legitimacy; must present a viable restructuring.
- **Industrial union & retiree health trust**: high consent power; labour peace and trust treatment are pivotal.
- **Unsecured bondholders’ committee**: significant legal leverage through creditor consent and court outcomes.
- **Neighbouring governments / co-funders**: material leverage through cross-border funding and domestic plant protection.

## 3. Final positions
### Government task force
The task force insists on a true balance-sheet reset, large bondholder losses, material labour concessions, and substantial plant/brand rationalization. It rejects any structure that preserves the retiree trust intact or protects domestic plants regardless of viability, and is prepared to impose terms through expedited court process if necessary.

### Company management
Management supports deep restructuring and agrees the current capital structure is unsustainable. It wants the retiree obligation reduced into a durable but financeable instrument, bondholder debt deeply cut and partly converted to equity/warrants, and plant/brand closures focused on preserving the profitable core rather than the entire footprint.

### Industrial union & retiree health trust
The union accepts that concessions are necessary, including wage moderation, work-rule changes, and some workforce reductions. It refuses, however, to let the retiree trust be treated as a mere ordinary unsecured claim; it seeks a reduced but durable claim with equity-like value and rejects any settlement that makes workers and retirees the principal shock absorber.

### Unsecured bondholders
Bondholders accept a real haircut and conversion into new equity and warrants, but reject near-wipeout or token recovery. Their central objection is relative treatment: they will not consent if the retiree trust receives meaningfully better treatment without offset, and they insist on parity or at least non-inferior treatment among unsecured claims.

### Neighbouring governments
The neighbouring governments are willing to co-fund only if the deal protects domestic production and employment through binding commitments and gives them meaningful governance or equity rights. They oppose plant closures that shift production away from their jurisdiction and will not finance a settlement that weakens their industrial base without a direct stake.

## 4. Genuine conflicts
1. **Retiree trust status vs. unsecured creditor parity**:  
   The union frames the trust as deferred compensation deserving special protection; bondholders and the task force treat it as an unsecured enterprise obligation that cannot be insulated without offset. This is structurally incompatible unless one side yields on relative priority or receives compensating value.

2. **Depth of bondholder haircut**:  
   The task force wants a deep reduction and large conversion to new equity; bondholders say anything approaching a wipeout is unacceptable. This is not just a valuation dispute but a conflict over whether creditor recovery remains meaningful at all.

3. **Domestic plant protection vs. viability-driven consolidation**:  
   Neighbouring governments want binding commitments to keep domestic plants and jobs; the task force and management want closure authority based on economic viability. These positions conflict whenever a viable footprint requires shutting a politically important facility.

4. **Burden allocation between labour/trust and financial creditors**:  
   Labour rejects being the residual shock absorber, while bondholders reject being overused to subsidize retiree protections and public objectives. The parties agree on shared sacrifice in principle, but not on the distribution.

5. **Public capital as bridge vs. public capital as control**:  
   The task force wants oversight and strong governance protection; neighbouring governments want ownership/governance commensurate with contribution; others want public money limited to temporary support. The scope of public control remains unresolved.

## 5. Areas of convergence
- The company must survive as a going concern, not be cosmetically adjusted.
- Delay destroys value; speed and finality are widely accepted.
- All parties accept that everyone must take some pain.
- Bondholders will not be paid in full.
- The retiree health obligation cannot remain unchanged.
- Labour concessions are unavoidable.
- Plant and brand consolidation is necessary.
- Public financing, if any, must be bridge financing with oversight.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker
- **Viability vs. fairness**: a stronger balance-sheet reset improves survival odds but increases perceived unfairness to whichever constituency takes the deepest hit.
- **Labour peace vs. creditor recovery**: preserving trust and morale may require more protection for workers/retirees, but this can compress bondholder recoveries.
- **Domestic industrial policy vs. operating efficiency**: keeping more plants protects jobs and political support, but may make the company unfinanceable.
- **Public support vs. governance dilution**: more public money can stabilize the restructuring, but only if funders receive enforceable control or protection.
- **Voluntary deal vs. court-imposed finality**: an out-of-court agreement can preserve legitimacy, but the court route may be the only way to prevent deadlock.

## 7. Integrative pathways
1. **Deep bondholder haircut with meaningful equity/warrants**  
   Make bondholders take large losses, but preserve real upside in the reorganized company so the recovery is not symbolic.

2. **Retiree trust converted into a durable equity-linked instrument**  
   Recast the trust as a reduced, enforceable claim tied to company performance, likely with equity-like participation rather than cash-dominated obligations.

3. **Bounded labour concessions linked to plant consolidation**  
   Tie wage/work-rule/staffing changes to a clearly specified footprint reduction and viability model, rather than open-ended labour sacrifice.

4. **Selective plant rationalization with domestic core preservation**  
   Close redundant or unsustainable facilities, but identify a protected domestic core to satisfy viability and political constraints.

5. **Bridge financing with conditional governance**  
   Allow public and neighbouring-government financing to buy milestone-based oversight and some equity/governance rights, but not a veto over economically necessary restructuring.

6. **Use court-supervised process as backstop, not first choice**  
   Keep the expedited court route credible to discipline negotiations, while still aiming for a negotiated term sheet if parity questions can be bridged.

## 8. Open questions
- What exact exchange ratio will bondholders accept for equity/warrants?
- How large is the reduction to the retiree trust, and what form does its new instrument take?
- What specific wage, work-rule, and staffing reductions are sufficient for viability?
- Which plants and brands are core, redundant, or politically protected?
- What equity/governance share do public and neighbouring-government funders receive?
- Can production commitments to neighbouring governments be written without impairing operational flexibility?
- Does the final package preserve parity among unsecured claims, or deliberately create a special treatment for the retiree trust?

## 9. Recommended next step
Produce a single integrated term sheet with quantified proposals on: bondholder recovery, retiree trust conversion, labour concessions, plant/brand closures, and public/government equity-governance rights. The term sheet should be tested against three criteria simultaneously: company viability, acceptable burden-sharing, and political feasibility for public funders.

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction
- The key fault line is not simply “creditors vs. labour,” but **parity among unsecured claims**: the dispute over whether the retiree trust is morally and politically distinct from bond debt became the central blocker.
- The discussion revealed a shared acceptance of **court-supervised restructuring as a credible discipline mechanism**, not merely a threat; this changed the negotiation from “avoid court” to “court is acceptable if the package is unfairly blocked.”
- Neighbouring governments are not passive observers; they effectively function as **quasi-veto holders** on plant footprint because their co-funding is conditional on domestic production and governance.
- Management’s role is more than technical: it is trying to define the boundary between a viable company and politically motivated overreach, especially on plant closures and consumer-franchise preservation.
- The parties have moved from abstract fairness claims to a concrete competition over **who bears the residual burden** after viability constraints are imposed. That residual burden allocation is now the actual decision problem.
