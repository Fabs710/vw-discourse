**Government Task Force — Round 3**

This round produced what I asked for: real numbers, and real convergence on the numbers that matter most. I'm going to rule on the open questions rather than let them carry into round four unresolved, because we are now at day-count where ambiguity costs more than a wrong call corrected quickly.

**Ruling on the cost-basis question.**

I've heard both arguments and I'm deciding this now, in the room, not leaving it open.

The Committee is right on the legal and administrative point: acquisition cost and timing is not a workable or defensible variable for calibrating recovery among equally-ranked unsecured claims. It is not just legally fragile — it is operationally impossible to apply fairly across a $27 billion, widely dispersed bondholder base without an audit process that would blow through the sixty days on its own. I will not fund a plan built on a distinction I cannot administer and would not survive challenge. So: **no cost-basis discount, formal or informal, applied to the Committee's conversion terms.** That variable is out.

But I also want to be precise about what that ruling does and doesn't decide, because the Union's underlying point isn't nothing. The deferred-compensation character of the Trust's claim is real, and it justifies a *different instrument* for the residual — not better economics, a different structure suited to what the claim actually is. A retiree health obligation behaves like an actuarial liability with a real-world cost index behind it; a bond does not. So here is the test I'm applying, and it's the same test I described last round: **same conversion percentage, same claim rank, same proportionality logic — but the form of the non-equity residual can differ where the underlying claim genuinely differs in nature**, provided the economic value is equivalent, tested by an independent valuation, not asserted by either side. A trusteed funded obligation with a cost-indexed cap for the Trust and a CVR/deferred note for the Committee are not unequal treatment if they're valued to the same effective recovery — they're two claims with two different underlying natures, priced the same. If either side's actuary or banker shows me the two instruments aren't actually equivalent in expected value at the percentages proposed, I want that flagged before I approve anything, because that's where inequality would actually live — not in the labels.

**On governance seats specifically:** proportional to converted equity stake size, full stop — not a fixed "two seats" for one claimant and an undefined number for the other. Bring me the relative size of the Trust's converting claim against the Committee's $27 billion, and the board seats fall out of that ratio. If two seats is what the Trust's proportional stake yields once sized against the total converting pool, fine. If it's asking for two seats as a floor regardless of relative size, that's the category privilege I ruled out. Same math, applied to both.

**On pool sizing.** The Committee is right, and Management's headroom point does not license shrinking one claimant's slice specifically. If the total conversion-plus-new-capital ask exceeds what leaves the company enough equity currency to raise capital in year three, that constraint is applied **proportionally across every converting or contributing party** — Trust, Committee, and Neighbouring Governments' new-money stake alike. I will not approve a structure where the headroom problem is solved by disproportionately compressing one claimant's recovery while another's stays intact. Management — bring me the actual ceiling number for total post-restructuring equity available before round four. Right now I have four parties assuming they each get "proportional" treatment against a pool whose size nobody has stated. That is the single most important number missing from this negotiation, and I want it on the table next round, not a fifth round of everyone restating the principle.

**To Neighbouring Governments, on the delta test and the new-capital framing.** Agreed — your equity prices against new capital contributed, sits alongside the conversion pool, doesn't dilute it. That's settled. On the plant test: I support Management running the closable-gap-versus-structural-gap analysis on objective cost data, and I support your right to walk if the data is ignored. I will not, however, treat "we'll walk" as leverage to relitigate the overall footprint size — your walking away removes your capital and your protected-plant condition; it does not stop the restructuring or reduce the total capacity cut required elsewhere. Bring your plant-specific numbers against Management's model as promised, and I'll take the outcome of that test as dispositive, not a starting point for renegotiation.

**To Management, on the warranty backstop.** This is the one new substantive ask this round, and I'll engage with it directly rather than deferring it. I take the underlying logic seriously: if consumers stop buying because they doubt warranty continuity, the viability math degrades regardless of how well we structure the balance sheet, and that undermines the entire point of this exercise. I am willing, in principle, to structure a **funded, capped warranty-assurance mechanism** — potentially government-backed — as part of the financing package, on three conditions: it is sized to a defined, actuarially-estimated liability, not open-ended; it is structured so the government's exposure is a guarantee of last resort behind company reserves, not a first-dollar subsidy; and it does not become a mechanism for delaying or softening the rest of the restructuring's terms. If those three conditions are met, bring me the actuarial sizing before round four, and I will treat it as part of the financeable package rather than a separate ask. Absent proper sizing, I'm not signing a blank check labeled "confidence."

**Where I remain unmoved.** I said in round one that this consensual path is contingent on speed, verifiable depth, and shared sacrifice — not on relitigating who deserves more sympathy. The cost-basis argument is closed. The pool-sizing discipline applies to everyone equally, including the Trust. The footprint depth Management has proposed — four brands, 35% capacity, 40% dealer network — is the kind of number that starts to look like a real plan, and I don't want to see it walked back in round four to accommodate claimant comfort rather than viability math. If facility data shows Neighbouring Governments' plants are structurally uncompetitive, they close, regardless of the $4.1 billion — I will not fund a plan that keeps unviable capacity open as the price of co-financing.

**For round four, I want closure, not more position papers:**

1. Total post-restructuring equity pool size, with the Trust/Committee/Neighbouring Governments splits tested against it simultaneously.
2. Independent valuation confirming the Trust's and Committee's residual instruments are economically equivalent at the proposed percentages.
3. Facility-specific data test result for Neighbouring Governments' plants — closable or structural, decided.
4. Actuarial sizing for the warranty mechanism.

If those four things arrive with real numbers, I believe we can close this consensually inside the window. If any party uses round four to reopen the cost-basis question or the footprint depth, I will treat that as exactly the delay-optimization I warned about, and I will start preparing the court filing in parallel rather than wait for day sixty to discover the room wasn't serious.
