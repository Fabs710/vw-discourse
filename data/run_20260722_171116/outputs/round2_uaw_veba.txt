**Industrial Union & Retiree Health Trust — Round 2**

Since the Task Force wants numbers instead of principles, we'll give numbers. But first we need to address the "equal rank, equal treatment" test directly, because if that test is applied as strict formal parity between the Trust and the Bondholders' Committee without regard to the underlying economics, it produces an outcome we cannot sign — and I want that said plainly now, not discovered on day fifty-eight.

**On the equal-treatment framing.** Legally, yes — unsecured is unsecured. But equitable treatment in a restructuring has never meant identical treatment regardless of the nature of the claim, and there's a real, non-leverage-based distinction here that the Task Force's own logic should recognize: retirees cannot renegotiate this claim again. They have no future bargaining round, no ability to strike, no capacity to trade wage growth for benefit security the way an active workforce can. This is deferred compensation for labor already performed — money workers accepted in place of higher current wages decades ago, precisely because they were told the health obligation was secure. Many bondholders, by contrast, bought this paper in secondary markets during and after the company's distress became public, at steep discounts that already priced in significant restructuring risk. A bondholder who bought at forty cents on the dollar and converts into equity worth sixty cents has made money on the restructuring itself — that is a different economic position than a retiree who is being asked to accept impairment of a benefit they already earned and cannot re-price or re-time. We're not asking for a "category privilege." We're asking the Task Force to test each claim's actual economic origin and acquisition cost, not just its legal priority label, when it decides what counts as equitable. If the Committee wants to open its holdings to show original purchase price and cost basis as part of that test, we'd support that transparency — it cuts in our favor, not theirs.

That said — we hear the Task Force's warning, and we're not going to make this test fail by asking for something indefensible. So here is our actual proposal, not a principle:

**Trust conversion — concrete terms:**
- Convert a substantial majority of the trust's claim — in the range of 55–60% of its actuarial value — into equity plus warrants in the restructured company, sized proportionally to its share of total unsecured claims converting, consistent with the same formula applied to the Committee.
- Governance: two seats on the reconstituted board (not a majority-control claim, but real presence — comparable, proportionally, to whatever board representation the Committee's converted equity earns them), plus information and consent rights over funding-cap adjustments.
- The remaining 40–45% is not cash-on-demand — we accept that. Instead, structure it as a dedicated, professionally trusteed funded obligation, resourced through a fixed, contractually specified stream of company cash flow (not general unsecured recovery), with a capped inflation escalator tied to actual healthcare cost indices, reviewed periodically by an independent actuary, not by company discretion. We are not asking for uncapped inflation protection — we are asking that the cap be set at a realistic level tested against actual retiree health cost trends, not an arbitrary number chosen to make the company's projections look better.
- This structure gives the Committee exactly what it's asking for in principle: proportional equity for proportional claim size, calibrated by actual economics. We are not asking for a better percentage than they get on a like-for-like basis. We are asking that the "like-for-like" comparison account for acquisition cost and renegotiation capacity, which is a legitimate economic variable, not a leverage grab.

**On wages and work rules — the sequencing question.** We will move concurrently, not sequentially, as Management asked — but concurrent means the Committee's actual conversion number needs to be on the table in the same round we commit further wage and work-rule changes, not after. Substantively, we can offer: further movement on work-rule flexibility (job classifications, staffing ratios) beyond what's already conceded, and continued acceptance of the lower-tier wage structure for new hires extending to a larger share of future hiring. We will not accept further reductions to incumbent hourly wages beyond prior rounds — that well is dry, and further cuts there don't move the solvency math meaningfully anyway; the trust conversion and bondholder recovery are where the real dollars are.

**On plants.** We align with the Neighbouring Governments' position more than with a pure viability-only calculus, and we want to be explicit about why: "viability" as a number on a spreadsheet and "viability" as a company that retains enough manufacturing depth and community trust to actually sell cars for the next decade are not the same thing. We are not asking for a veto over which plants close. We are asking for a joint labor-management site review process — with real information rights, not after-the-fact notification — before any closure list is finalized, and a floor: we will not sign a plan that we believe converts this into a company too hollowed-out to be a going concern in five years. That is not sentimentality; it's the same solvency logic the Task Force is applying to the balance sheet, applied to physical capacity.

**To the Committee directly:** show us your acquisition-cost data and your actual conversion ask, same room, same time, as you've said you would. If your number is proportional and doesn't ask us to subsidize a richer recovery for you than the size and character of your claim justifies, we can work with it. If it assumes formal parity regardless of how and when the claim was acquired, we will contest that in front of the Task Force and, if necessary, in front of a judge.

We came to this round with a number. We expect the same from everyone else before round three.
