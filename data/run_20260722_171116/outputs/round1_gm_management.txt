We accept the frame both of you just laid out, and I want to be honest about why: we're not in a position to litigate the fairness of who moves first. We're weeks from a cash-out date. So let me put the operational reality on the table, because both of you are negotiating around a company whose viability depends on decisions that have to be made in parallel, not sequentially.

To the Task Force: understood, and agreed on the core architecture — deeper cuts, real balance-sheet restructuring, equity calibrated to sacrifice. We're not going to relitigate the rejected plan. It wasn't aggressive enough and we own that. What I need from you is financing certainty through the negotiation itself. If working capital is at risk of being pulled mid-negotiation because talks are running past day forty or day fifty, every stakeholder in this room will negotiate defensively instead of constructively, and you'll get a worse deal, not a better one. Tell us now what happens if we need a short technical extension to close a deal that's substantively done — because "sixty days, no exceptions" as a hard wall, applied literally, could force us into court not because the terms are wrong but because the calendar ran out on paperwork. That's a different failure mode than the one you're trying to prevent, and I want it on the record that we distinguish between them.

To the Union: I'm not going to pretend your prior concessions weren't real — they were, and management said so at the time. But I have to be equally blunt about the math. The retiree trust obligation, as currently structured, is not something this company can carry as a fixed cash claim under any footprint we can realistically sustain. That's not a negotiating position, it's a solvency fact. Converting a meaningful share to equity with governance weight is something we can work with and, frankly, prefer to a court imposing worse terms on you. Where I'll push back is on the idea that your further wage and work-rule concessions should wait on bondholders committing first. Operationally, we need line-of-sight on total labour cost concurrent with brand and plant decisions — you can't finalize footprint without knowing what the assembly cost structure looks like, and you can't finalize the trust conversion without knowing what equity pool exists after bondholder dilution. These aren't sequential, they're simultaneous unknowns. I'd rather we work them together, in the room, than each wait for the other to move first while the clock burns.

On plant and brand cuts specifically: I want to be candid that we are prepared to go deeper than our rejected plan proposed, including brand eliminations we previously resisted. But every cut has to be sized against one hard constraint from our side: we cannot execute cuts so severe, or so publicly chaotic, that they trigger a consumer flight before the restructuring even closes. Warranty confidence and dealer network continuity are not sentimental attachments — they are the revenue base that makes every other number in this plan real. A footprint decision that saves cost on paper but empties showrooms in practice doesn't serve anyone at this table.

On bondholders: I'll let the committee speak for itself, but from where we sit, the trust's point stands — if unsecured creditors expect near-par recovery while retirees take structural risk, that imbalance isn't just unfair, it's not financeable. We need their numbers on the table now, same room, same time, as the Union asked.

We'll come back with a revised footprint and cost structure that reflects deeper cuts than before. But we need the two things I named — financing certainty through the process, and a structure that protects the consumer-facing franchise through the transition — or we're negotiating a plan that dies of consumer flight before it ever reaches viability.
