We're going to be direct about where this stands, because the clock doesn't stop for anyone's comfort.

The plan submitted so far is inadequate. It cuts brands and plants but leaves the fundamental cost structure and balance sheet essentially intact, which means we'd be financing the same failure at a slower pace. That is not something we will fund. Taxpayers are not in the business of subsidizing an unrestructured company indefinitely, and I want to be explicit about that now so no one mistakes the sixty-day window for a negotiating tactic. It isn't. It's the actual limit of what we're prepared to bridge without a credible path to viability.

Here is what I require, and what I'll be testing every proposal against:

First, shared sacrifice, verifiable and deep, from every stakeholder — not just the ones with the least leverage. Labour's cost structure, including the retiree health trust obligation, has to be restructured into something the company can actually carry long-term, likely converting a meaningful share of that claim into equity or a funded, capped instrument rather than a legacy cash obligation. I understand the trust was negotiated in good faith, but a claim against an insolvent company is a claim against nothing — better a real stake in a viable one.

Second, the unsecured bondholders need to recognize what their instrument actually is: unsecured debt in a company that, absent our support, would be liquidated with them recovering far less than what's on offer. A significant debt-for-equity conversion is the baseline expectation. If they hold out for near-par recovery at public expense, they will get court-supervised treatment instead, where I have more control over outcomes, not less.

Third, plant, brand and dealer cuts need to go as deep as viability requires, not as shallow as politically comfortable. I will not fund a network sized for a market that no longer exists.

Fourth, on ownership: whoever bears the sacrifice should hold the equity upside, calibrated to what they give up — not as a reward, but as the mechanism that makes the sacrifice rational for them to accept.

Fifth, on the neighbouring government's co-funding: welcome if it comes with proportional conditions tied to its own plants, not as a blank check riding on our restructuring terms.

I'd rather reach this consensually. It's faster and less legally uncertain for everyone, including us. But I am entirely prepared to fund the company through the expedited court process if consensual terms aren't deep enough or aren't reached in time. That is not a bluff — we've already forced out a CEO and rejected one plan. I will not hesitate to reject a second.

So: bring me a plan where labour, the trust, and the bondholders have each taken a real hit, where the surviving footprint is sized to a viable company, and where the taxpayer is positioned to exit, not to keep writing checks. Anything short of that, we go to court.
