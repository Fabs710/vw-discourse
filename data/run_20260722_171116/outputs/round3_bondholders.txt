**Unsecured Bondholders' Committee — Round 3**

We'll take the Task Force's ruling for what it is: a win on the principle we said we would not compromise on, and we intend to hold the room to it as it moves into implementation. But there are three things in this round's record we need to correct before they harden into assumptions we didn't agree to.

**On the cost-basis ruling — we note, precisely, what closed and what didn't.** The Task Force ruled no cost-basis discount, formal or informal. Good. That is the floor we said we would defend and it held. But we want to flag something in the Union's response that tries to walk the same argument back in through the instrument-equivalence door: their statement that "if our CVR is shown to carry more expected value than their funded obligation at the same nominal percentage, that inequality runs the other way." We agree with that in principle — genuinely equal treatment cuts both ways — but we want to be clear about the burden of proof. The Trust proposed the differential-instrument structure; the Trust is the one asking the actuary to validate that its cap and its discount rate produce equivalence. We are not going to accept a framing where our straightforward CVR is treated with suspicion by default while their capped, trusteed, actuarially-managed obligation gets the benefit of the doubt because it sounds more conservative. The actuary flagged the trend-rate assumption as the single largest lever and the most gameable one — we support that scrutiny fully, and we expect it applied with the same rigor to the healthcare trend assumption the Trust proposes as to our enterprise-value forecast. Neither side gets to be the default "reasonable" instrument.

**On governance seats.** We accept the Task Force's math: proportional to converted equity stake, full stop. We will bring our proportional claim size against the Trust's next round, as instructed. We note the Union has now conceded the "two seats regardless of size" ask was a floor demand, not a proportional one — that concession matters, and we expect it to hold when the actual ratio is calculated, not renegotiated back upward if the ratio comes in lower than they'd like.

**On pool sizing — this is where we need to push back hard on Management's numbers just presented.**

Management has now put a ceiling on the table: $10–12 billion in post-emergence equity value, with 12% reserved for future capital headroom and 3% for a management incentive plan — 15% off the top before a single converting claimant sees a share. We are not going to wave this through as a neutral technical allocation. A few points:

First, the Task Force ruled that pool-sizing constraints apply *proportionally across every converting or contributing party* — Trust, Committee, Neighbouring Governments. That ruling was about how the *shrinkage* is distributed among claimants, not a license for Management to carve off 15% for its own priorities before the proportionality test even begins. A management incentive plan is a legitimate cost of running the business — we don't dispute that in principle — but it is being sized and proposed by the party that will benefit from it, presented as a fait accompli in the same breath as the two creditor claims that are actually taking the haircut here. We want the Task Force to test that 3% the same way it's testing our conversion percentage and the Trust's cap: independently, against comparable restructurings, not accepted because Management says it's "standard."

Second, on the 12% capital-raise reserve: we understand the logic — a company with zero equity currency repeats this crisis. But "authorized but unissued" reserved equity is not free; it is dilution risk sitting over our converted stake and the Trust's, waiting to be issued on terms neither of us will have consented to at the time. If that reserve gets issued in year three at a price that dilutes converted claimholders without our consent rights attached, the 55–60% we're being asked to accept today is worth less than it looks on this page. We want either: (a) our proportional governance rights to include consent or anti-dilution protection specifically over how and when that reserve is issued, or (b) the reserve sized smaller and the balance tested again once actual post-emergence capital needs are clearer, rather than reserved against a hypothetical need on day one of a sixty-day process.

**Our number, restated against Management's ceiling.** At a $10–12 billion equity pool, before any reserve carve-outs, our proportional share of a 55–60% conversion on a $27 billion claim, sized against the Trust's converting claim and whatever Neighbouring Governments' new capital dilutes into the cap table, needs to be run explicitly — Management's presentation stopped mid-sentence on its own allocation table, so we don't yet have the actual split. We want that completed before round four closes, not left as an unfinished slide. We will not sign off on a pool ceiling that nets us a materially lower effective percentage once reserves are subtracted than the headline 55–60% implies. If the reserves compress our real recovery below what the headline number promised, that is exactly the kind of quiet asymmetry the Task Force said it would not fund.

**On the warranty backstop.** No objection to the mechanism in principle — a functioning company is a better outcome for our recovery than a collapsing one, and we recognize that consumer confidence protects the enterprise value our equity conversion depends on. Our only interest here: if it's structured as a government-backed guarantee sitting ahead of company reserves, we want confirmation it does not create a senior claim on the estate's assets that primes our converted equity position or the residual instrument. Cheap consumer confidence bought by quietly subordinating creditor recovery is not a free good.

**Where we stand heading into round four.** The cost-basis fight is closed, and we're not reopening it — agreed, on record. The proportional governance formula and pool-sizing discipline are ones we support, provided the pool is actually tested transparently rather than pre-loaded with management and reserve carve-outs before proportionality is calculated. Our floor has not moved: same conversion percentage as the Trust, same rank, no quiet erosion through reserves or incentive-plan sizing that lands more heavily on us than on any other converting claimant. If round four produces a completed allocation table that holds to that standard, we can close quickly. If it produces a structure where our effective recovery comes in materially below the Trust's once all carve-outs are netted, we will contest it in this room first and in court if we have to — exactly as we said in round one.
