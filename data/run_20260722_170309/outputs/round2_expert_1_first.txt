   - Unsecured debt must be cut deeply enough that the post-restructuring cash interest and amortization burden is sustainable under stressed volume.

3. **Adequate bridge liquidity with finality**

## Specific restructuring implications

### 1) Unsecured bondholders
They are clearly in the money only if the post-restructuring enterprise value supports them after:
- senior/bridge claims,
- working capital needs,
- pension/retiree legacy costs,
- and retooling capex.

Given the facts, unsecured bondholders should expect **substantial haircuts**. A token exchange would not be enough. If bond debt remains too high, the company will fail again.

### 2) Retiree health trust
This is a major viability issue. If the trust’s claim is treated as economically equivalent to a large fixed obligation of the operating company, the company likely remains cash-flow constrained.

For viability, the trust likely needs one or more of:
- reduction in nominal claim,
- deferral/stretch-out,
- conversion into a contingent or equity-linked instrument,
- partial external funding support,
- or transfer of some burden away from the operating company.

What will not work is a structure that leaves the company paying the trust in full while also carrying meaningful unsecured debt.

### 3) Plants, brands, dealer network
The footprint likely must be **materially reduced**.
- Automotive manufacturing is capital intensive and highly sensitive to utilization.
- Excess plant capacity destroys margin and cash.
- Brands that do not generate meaningful positive contribution should be discontinued.
- Dealer overcapacity, if contractually hard to unwind, is a continuing drag on economics.

If the company preserves too much of its historical footprint, it will probably not reach break-even at realistic sales levels.

### 4) Public funding
Public bridge financing can stabilize liquidity, but it cannot create solvency by itself.
- It should be viewed as **time-buying capital**, not permanent support.
- Any public support should be conditioned on hard restructuring milestones.
- Public money should not be used to preserve a capital structure that is still unsustainable.

## Main failure modes

The company likely fails if any of the following occur:

- **Creditor sacrifice is too shallow**
  - Post-restructuring leverage remains too high.

- **Retiree obligation remains too heavy**
  - Cash flow is consumed by legacy claims.

- **Operational cuts are too modest**
  - Too much fixed capacity remains.

- **The process takes too long**
  - Supplier failures, inventory liquidation, and customer confidence erosion accelerate value destruction.

- **The company is forced to carry politically protected excess capacity**
  - This is a classic path to a “restructured but still insolvent” outcome.

## Conditions required for viability

A standalone outcome would require:

- a **deep deleveraging** of unsecured claims,
- a **material modification** of the retiree trust obligation,
- closure/consolidation of non-economic plants and brands,
- a smaller, more disciplined dealer footprint,
- sufficient post-restructuring liquidity,
- and an operating plan that assumes lower, not pre-crisis, vehicle volumes.

## Recovery and ownership expectations

If this is done properly, the post-restructuring equity will likely need to go primarily to:
- the new money / public bridge providers if they are truly taking risk,
- and/or the impaired creditor and legacy claim constituencies through conversion.

That said, I cannot assign precise percentages from the facts given. What I can say is that **existing common equity is likely wiped out**, and the new equity will reflect the parties that absorb the economic losses and provide exit financing.

**Standalone survival is possible, but only after a severe restructuring.**
