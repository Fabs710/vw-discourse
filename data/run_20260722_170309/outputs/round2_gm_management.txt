We appreciate the expert’s confirmation of the central point: **this company cannot survive with a cosmetic deal**. On that, we are aligned. If the balance sheet is not repaired and the footprint is not reduced, then we are simply burning the bridge financing and heading back to failure.

Where I agree with the others:
- **Management history does not deserve protection.** The old leadership is gone for a reason.
- **Some plants, brands, and dealer capacity must go.** We cannot preserve everything.
- **Unsecured bondholders must take meaningful haircuts.**
- **The retiree trust cannot remain in its current form if that makes the company unviable.**
- **A fast, binding solution is essential.**

Where I disagree, and strongly:
## 1) The trust cannot be treated as untouchable
To the union and trust: I understand this is earned deferred compensation, not speculative capital. But if you insist on keeping the trust economically equivalent to a senior operating burden, then you are asking the company to carry a legacy liability it cannot afford. That is not protection; it is a path to collapse.

We can support:
- a real instrument for the trust,
- equity, warrants, or another enforceable security,
- and a structure that preserves some genuine value for retirees.

We cannot support:
- full economic preservation,
- a structure that drains operating cash,
- or a solution that makes the company insolvent in all but name.

## 2) Labour cannot be insulated from the required adjustment
We are not asking workers to bear everything. But the company does need:
- wage and work-rule changes,
- lower legacy costs,
- and a smaller operating footprint.

If every plant closure is treated as unacceptable, then there is no restructuring — only delay. We will prioritize the strongest brands and the plants that can actually support a profitable product plan. That means some facilities will not survive. I do not say that lightly, but I will not pretend otherwise.

## 3) Bondholders are right that they need real recovery, but not at the expense of viability
To the bondholders: you are correct that conversion into token equity would be meaningless. If debt is being impaired, the recovery package must be economically real. But the company cannot be capitalized in a way that leaves it overlevered just to soften your haircut. That would preserve paper claims and destroy the business.

So the test is not whether bondholders like the recovery. It is whether the resulting capital structure lets the company survive after the dust settles. If not, it is not acceptable.

## 4) Public co-funding cannot force us to keep excess capacity
To the neighbouring governments: we understand your domestic political obligations. We also understand that your co-funding should come with a real seat at the table. But if the price of your funding is retaining uncompetitive capacity that the company cannot support, then you are not helping the rescue — you are narrowing it into failure.

We can accept binding commitments where they fit a viable plan. We cannot accept a geography-driven solution that preserves politically favored plants while weakening the whole enterprise.

## Where we stand now
The restructuring must do all of the following:
1. **Deeply reduce unsecured debt**
2. **Materially modify the retiree trust**
3. **Cut capacity enough to match realistic demand**
4. **Preserve the strongest consumer-facing brands and the most efficient plants**
5. **Provide bridge financing with finality**
6. **Avoid an outcome that destroys the going concern**

That last point is critical: an uncontrolled liquidation is not an acceptable outcome. If the choice is between hard restructuring and collapse, we will support hard restructuring. But if any stakeholder insists on protections that make the restructured company nonviable, then they are not negotiating in good faith.

## Minimum direction we need next
I want the parties to come back with actual parameters, not just principles:
- **Trust treatment:** what real instrument or claim do you want, and what burden can the company carry?
- **Creditor haircut:** how deep can the unsecured impairment go while still being enough to restore solvency?
- **Footprint reduction:** which plants, brands, and dealer capacity are genuinely essential, and which are not?
- **Funding structure:** what public funding is available, on what milestones, and with what exit path?

If we get a package that protects the consumer franchise, preserves the best assets, and leaves the company financeable, we will engage constructively. If not, we will not pretend a weak deal is a solution.
