From a restructuring and standalone-viability standpoint, the limits are now fairly clear.

## My core judgment
A rescue is viable **only if** it delivers all four of these at once:

1. **Deep unsecured debt reduction**
2. **Material modification of the retiree health obligation**
3. **Meaningful footprint reduction**
4. **Enough exit liquidity to survive a weak market after closing**

If any one of those is materially watered down, the company is likely just postponing insolvency.

---

## Where the viability limit likely sits

### 1) Retiree trust burden
The trust **cannot remain cash-heavy in its current form**.

A viable structure likely requires:
- a **material nominal reduction**, and/or
- **long deferral**, and
- conversion of part of the claim into an instrument with value but limited near-term cash drain.

If the trust stays close to full cash burden, the operating company likely cannot fund:
- capex,
- product development,
- supplier support,
- restructuring costs,
- and normal cyclical working capital.

**Bottom line:** the trust must be reduced enough that it no longer behaves like a senior cash claim on the business.

---

### 2) Unsecured bondholders
Unsecureds are the main balance-sheet repair lever.

A viable standalone deal likely requires a **deep haircut**—not token impairment. The post-restructuring company cannot carry near-par unsecured debt and still be financeable under weak-sales conditions.

In practical terms:
- unsecured bondholders should expect to bear the **largest single financial haircut**,
- with recovery mainly through **new equity and warrants**, not residual debt.

**Bottom line:** if unsecured recovery is too generous, leverage stays too high and the turnaround fails.

---

### 3) Footprint rationalization
This is not optional. The company needs:
- fewer plants,
- fewer brands,
- reduced dealer burden,
- lower break-even volume.

If domestic plant protection prevents closure of uneconomic sites, fixed costs stay above what the market can support. That is a classic path to repeat distress.

**Viability limit:** you can protect only a **core, objectively economic domestic base**. A blanket freeze on domestic capacity is inconsistent with standalone survival.

---

### 4) Liquidity
Even after deleveraging, this kind of business needs a **large liquidity cushion**.

The reason is simple:
- automaking is working-capital intensive,
- demand is cyclical,
- inventory and supplier timing can consume cash quickly.

If the rescue only funds the transaction and leaves little post-close buffer, the company remains fragile.

**Bottom line:** the business needs not just bridge funding, but exit liquidity sufficient to absorb a prolonged weak-sales period.

---

## Trade-offs that are unavoidable

### You cannot have all of these at once:
- high trust protection,
- shallow unsecured haircuts,
- broad domestic plant protection,
- and a viable post-restructuring balance sheet.

Something has to give. Economically, the burden has to fall where the cash-flow capacity is weakest:
- the legacy unsecured debt,
- and the excess operating footprint.

The retiree trust also has to be modified, but it cannot be the only adjustment if you want a consensual deal. If you try to preserve both the trust and too much bond value, the company stays overlevered.

---

## Consensual deal vs court-supervised route

### Out of court
Possible only if:
- the trust accepts real economic modification,
- unsecureds accept deep impairment,
- and plant rationalization is sufficiently aggressive.

That is hard because of holdout risk and stakeholder asymmetry.

### Court-supervised
Mechanically, this is the more credible path if:
- the trust refuses enough modification,
- unsecureds resist deep cuts,
- or plant protection demands become too rigid.

The court route is better suited to impose:
- depth,
- finality,
- and elimination of holdout leverage.

---

## My practical conclusion
If you ask where the line sits:

- **Retiree trust:** must be materially reduced/deferrable; cannot stay cash-heavy.
- **Unsecureds:** must take deep impairment, likely the largest financial haircut.
- **Plants/brands/dealers:** must be cut meaningfully; only economic core can remain.
- **Liquidity:** must be sufficient for a stressed post-close period, not just the transaction.

If those conditions are not met, the company is not being rescued; it is being propped up.
