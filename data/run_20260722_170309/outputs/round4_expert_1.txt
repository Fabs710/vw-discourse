From a restructuring and insolvency standpoint, the parties are converging on the right diagnosis: the current capital structure is not viable, and a “bridge-only” solution without deep balance-sheet repair would fail.

## My judgment
A standalone restructured carmaker **can** be viable only if the package includes:

1. **Very substantial unsecured debt reduction**
2. **Material modification of the retiree health obligation**
3. **Meaningful footprint reduction**
4. **Working-capital and exit liquidity sufficient to absorb a prolonged weak-sales period**
5. **A clean post-restructuring leverage profile**

Absent that, the company likely survives only briefly before re-entering distress.

---

## Key viability constraints

### 1) Legacy claims are too heavy for the operating cash flow
The largest issue is not just total debt; it is the combination of:
- unsecured bond debt,
- retiree health obligations,
- and a bloated fixed-cost base.

If the retiree trust remains cash-heavy and the bondholders are not deeply impaired, the company will not generate enough free cash flow to fund:
- capex,
- product development,
- restructuring implementation,
- pension/benefit obligations,
- and normal working capital.

That is an insolvency problem, not just a liquidity problem.

### 2) Footprint reduction is not optional
A viable standalone manufacturer at a weaker demand point must have:
- fewer plants,
- fewer brands,
- reduced dealer burden,
- and lower break-even volume.

If plant closures are constrained mainly by politics rather than economics, the company will preserve fixed costs that the market cannot support. That usually leads to another restructuring later.

### 3) Working capital remains a serious risk
Even after restructuring, an automaker is highly exposed to:
- inventory build,
- supplier payment timing,
- dealer financing stress,
- and cyclical demand swings.

So the company needs not just “a deal” but a **large liquidity buffer**. If the bridge financing merely gets it through the transaction, but leaves no cushion for a depressed market, it remains fragile.

---

## What a financeable package likely requires

### Retiree trust
The trust cannot stay in its current economic form if the company is to survive.

A viable structure would usually require:
- **nominal reduction and/or long deferral**, and
- conversion of part of the claim into an instrument with value but limited near-term cash drain.

If the trust is effectively preferred over unsecureds, the debt burden likely stays too high. If it is wiped out entirely, the deal may fail politically, but that is a stakeholder issue rather than a solvency one.

### Unsecured bondholders
The unsecured bonds are the primary balance-sheet repair lever.

A viable standalone deal likely requires a **deep haircut**, with recovery mainly through:
- new equity,
- warrants,
- or a small residual debt instrument.

If recoveries are only modestly impaired, the post-restructuring leverage will still be too high. Based on the facts given, I would expect the company needs unsecured creditors to absorb the majority of enterprise value erosion.

### Plant and dealer cuts
The rationalization must be meaningful enough to produce:
- lower breakeven volume,
- lower labor and overhead cost,
- and less capital tied up in unproductive assets.

If the closure list is shallow, the plan is probably not viable unless there is very large external subsidy.

### Public funding
Public funding can bridge liquidity, but it cannot replace restructuring.

It should be treated as:
- temporary working capital,
- transition financing,
- or backstop funding for the court or out-of-court process.

It should **not** be relied on to preserve an overlevered balance sheet. If public funds are asked to support a structurally unviable capital structure, the company likely just postpones failure.

---

## Out-of-court versus court-supervised
### Out-of-court
Possible only if:
- all major creditor groups accept substantial impairment,
- the retiree trust agrees to material modification,
- and there is enough liquidity support to close quickly.

The problem is holdouts. With dispersed unsecured bondholders and a highly sensitive retiree trust, a consensual deal is hard unless the numbers are compelling.

### Court-supervised
From a restructuring feasibility perspective, the court path is likely the more credible route if:
- the trust will not accept enough modification,
- bondholders resist a deep haircut,
- or domestic plant commitments become too rigid.

The court process is the better mechanism for imposing depth and finality when stakeholder consent is unlikely.

---

## Failure modes
The plan fails if any of the following occur:

1. **Trust burden remains too large**
   - cash drain survives the restructuring.

2. **Bondholder impairment is too shallow**
   - balance sheet remains overlevered.

3. **Plant cuts are too limited**
   - fixed costs stay too high.

4. **Public co-funding is too conditional**
   - financing comes with operational constraints that block economic restructuring.

5. **Liquidity is underfunded**
   - the business runs out of cash before the turnaround takes hold.

---

## Bottom line
### Can it survive as a standalone carmaker?
**Yes, but only under a deep restructuring and with a materially smaller operating footprint.**

### Is the currently described compromise enough?
**Probably not yet.**  
The positions described still appear too politically constrained and not fully severe enough on legacy claims and capacity reduction.

### What is required for viability?
- a large reduction in unsecured debt,
- real modification of the retiree trust,
- closure/consolidation of uneconomic plants and dealer capacity,
- sufficient exit liquidity,
- and a post-restructuring capital structure that is plainly sustainable under stressed sales assumptions.
