We are at the point where the numbers have to govern the politics, not the other way around.

## Where I agree
I agree with the expert on the essential point: **this is not financeable unless the restructuring is deep**. A viable outcome requires:
- substantial unsecured debt reduction,
- material modification of the retiree trust,
- meaningful footprint reduction,
- and a balance sheet that is plainly sustainable after the rescue.

I also agree with management that the company cannot carry its current legacy burden and that delay destroys value. And I agree with labour on one narrow but important point: a cosmetic deal would fail and would not be worth funding.

## Where I disagree

### 1) The retiree trust cannot be preserved in its current economic form
To the union and trust: I respect that these obligations were negotiated and earned. But I am not here to preserve every legacy promise at the expense of viability and taxpayers.

If the trust remains a cash-heavy burden on the operating company, the company fails again. That is the end of the story.

So I reject any structure that:
- leaves the trust effectively senior in cash terms,
- preserves full economic value as if nothing has changed,
- or turns public financing into a backdoor guarantee of the old liability.

What I can support is a **real instrument with real value**, but only if it materially reduces the operating company’s burden. That means a large modification, not a cosmetic one. If that requires a significant nominal reduction, so be it.

### 2) Bondholders will take deep impairment, and they will not be made whole by making labour absorb the gap
To the bondholders: you are correct that you should not be the only shock absorber. I am not asking you to be.

But you also do not get to insist on a recovery that forces the trust and the workforce to carry the most pain while you preserve value. This company is overlevered. Unsecured creditors will be impaired. That is unavoidable.

What I will not accept is a package that:
- protects the trust more than unsecureds,
- preserves excess domestic capacity,
- and still claims the bondholders are being treated fairly because they get some token upside.

If the company is to survive, unsecured debt must be cut deeply enough to repair the balance sheet. Anything less is just a delay.

### 3) Domestic plant protection has a hard economic limit
To the neighbouring governments: I understand the political and industrial pressure. But domestic plant protection is not a substitute for competitiveness.

If you want public co-funding, that money must go toward a viable business, not toward freezing inefficient capacity. I will not fund a restructuring that preserves plants because they are domestic while forcing taxpayers to cover the cost of non-economic production.

I am prepared to consider protection only for a **core domestic base** that is objectively efficient and strategically necessary. Not all domestic sites can be protected. Not if we are serious about solvency.

And I will not accept veto rights over the necessary rationalization. Public capital does not buy the right to block restructuring.

### 4) Co-funding is welcome only if it supports restructuring, not substitutes for it
To the neighbouring governments: if you want to co-fund, fine. But then:
- your contribution must be real,
- your equity must be proportional to real money at risk,
- and your governance rights must be limited to protecting the integrity of your funding, not controlling the operating map.

I will not trade taxpayer leverage for an arrangement that entangles us in permanent political management of plant decisions.

## My position on the package
The company must emerge with:

1. **Deep unsecured impairment**
   - not symbolic, not near-par,
   - with meaningful equity/warrant participation only after proper deleveraging.

2. **Material trust modification**
   - a real, enforceable instrument,
   - but one that removes the cash choke point from the operating company.

3. **Selective plant, brand, and dealer rationalization**
   - close the non-economic assets,
   - keep only the core industrial footprint.

4. **Limited domestic protection**
   - only where the economics support it,
   - no blanket geographic guarantees.

5. **Fast finality**
   - if we cannot land a binding voluntary deal quickly, we move to the court-supervised route.

## On the court-supervised fallback
I want to be explicit: the fallback is not a bluff, and it is not a bargaining chip to be worn down by persistence.

If parties continue to hold out for better treatment, or try to protect politically sensitive claims at taxpayer expense, we will use the court-supervised process to impose a structure that actually works. That is what it is for: depth, finality, and no holdout premium.

If your condition is blanket protection of domestic capacity, that is a nonstarter.

I will not finance:

- or a deal that preserves the full cash burden of legacy liabilities while pretending it is a rescue.

That remains the limit.
