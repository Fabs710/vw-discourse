We agree on the core reality: **the current capital structure is broken and cannot survive untouched**. We also agree that **time is short** and that a cosmetic “extend and pretend” deal would destroy value for everyone, including unsecured creditors.

But let me be equally clear: **we will not endorse a restructuring that uses unsecured bondholders as the default dumping ground while the retiree trust is given better economics and political protection.** That is exactly the kind of unequal treatment we are here to resist.

## Where we agree
- The company needs **real deleveraging**.
- Some **operating footprint reduction** is unavoidable.
- Existing equity is effectively **worthless**.
- A **court-supervised process** is a credible fallback if voluntary terms are not disciplined enough.
- A token exchange into trivial equity would be **insufficient**.

## Where we disagree strongly
### 1) The retiree trust cannot be treated as untouchable
We understand the trust is politically sensitive and tied to earned retiree benefits. But it is still a **financial claim on the enterprise**. If it is given a more favorable structure than unsecured bondholders, that has to be justified by real legal priority or offsetting value.

We will not accept a structure where:
- the trust gets a materially better recovery,
- bondholders absorb the deeper haircut,
- and everyone is told that this is “fair” because the trust is socially important.

That is not fairness. That is political preference.

If the trust receives:
- equity,
- warrants,
- a secured or priority-like instrument,
- or some other enhanced recovery,

then unsecured bondholders must receive **comparable value** or the deal is unacceptable.

### 2) “Real recovery” must mean meaningful recovery for us too
The task force says bondholders cannot expect ordinary refinancing treatment. Fine. We are not asking for par. We are asking for **a recovery that is not confiscatory**.

If the balance sheet can only be fixed by wiping unsecured creditors close to zero, then the proposed operating plan has not been aligned to the enterprise’s actual economics. The remedy is not to crush the unsecured class more; it is to make the restructuring **more credible and more even-handed**.

### 3) Public funding cannot be used to subordinate us politically
To the task force and neighbouring governments: public money is not a license to impose a political hierarchy on creditor claims.

We are not opposed to bridge financing. We are opposed to a structure where taxpayer support:
- props up a deal that overprotects labour or domestic plant interests,
- while unsecured creditors take disproportionate losses,
- and the trust is shielded from the same discipline.

If public funding is available, then its purpose is to support a **viable, balanced restructuring**, not to let other stakeholder groups avoid economic reality.

### 4) Plant and brand cuts must be driven by economics, not politics
We agree with management and the expert that excess capacity must go. But we are not going to endorse an industrial map designed to protect politically favored constituencies while bondholders are asked to finance the shortfall.

If domestic governments want to co-fund, that is their decision. But that cannot come at the expense of creditor recoveries through suboptimal capacity retention. The company must cut to what is viable, not to what is locally convenient.

## Our position on the deal architecture
We are prepared to consider a restructuring only if it includes:

1. **Deep but not confiscatory unsecured impairment**  
   The haircut must be meaningful, but not a wipe-out.

2. **Equal treatment across similarly situated unsecured claims**  
   If the retiree trust is treated more favorably, that must be offset.

3. **Real upside participation if debt is converted**  
   If bondholders are forced into equity or warrants, the stake must be large enough to reflect the sacrifice.

4. **A credible, smaller operating company**  
   We accept rationalization, but not politically engineered overcapacity.

5. **A fast, binding path to finality**  
   Delay destroys value. If consensus cannot produce an equitable package quickly, we will not hesitate to let the court-supervised route test everyone’s claims.

## On the “court process” threat
We hear the implied warning: accept now or face an imposed deal later. That cuts both ways.

If a court-supervised process can impose terms, then the question is not whether bondholders can be bullied into accepting unfair treatment; it is whether the proposed consensual package is actually better than what the court would impose.

We believe a court process may well force:
- deeper transparency,
- more disciplined valuation,
- and a harder look at who is really being overprotected.

So no, we are not intimidated by the fallback route. If the voluntary proposal is materially worse than a fair court outcome, we will reject it.

## Minimum acceptable direction from us
Before we can support any package, we need actual numbers and relative treatment, not generalities:

- **Unsecured bondholder recovery range**
- **Retiree trust recovery range**
- **Whether the trust is getting priority or enhanced security**
- **How much equity/warrants bondholders receive**
- **Which plants/brands are closed**
- **What public funding is coming and on what terms**

If the answer is still “bondholders take the biggest hit because they are easiest to cut,” then there is no deal from our side.

## Bottom line
We are willing to take losses. We are **not** willing to be singled out for the deepest losses while the retiree trust receives political protection.

If the restructuring respects parity among unsecureds, gives bondholders a real recovery path, and produces a genuinely viable post-restructuring company, we can engage seriously.

If not, we will oppose it.
