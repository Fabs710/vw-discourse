We are at the point where a real package must be put on the table, not just competing claims. I will be direct.

## Where I agree

I agree with the task force, the specialist, the bondholders, and the union on the central facts:

- The current structure is **not viable**.
- **Existing equity is effectively worthless**.
- We need **deep deleveraging**, not a token exchange.
- We need **meaningful footprint reduction**.
- We need **finality fast**; delay will destroy value.
- A **court-supervised route** must remain available if we cannot reach a binding deal quickly.

That is the operational reality.

## Where I disagree

### 1) The retiree trust cannot remain in its current economic form
To the union/trust: I respect that this is earned compensation, not speculative debt. But I cannot support any structure that leaves the operating company carrying the full cash burden of the trust as if the crisis never happened.

That is not protecting retirees. It is creating a claim the company will fail to service.

So I agree with the specialist and the task force that the trust must be **materially modified**. But I also agree it must remain **real**. The answer is not to preserve it in full, and it is not to hollow it out.

What I can support:
- a reduced nominal claim,
- stretch-out of payments,
- equity or warrants,
- or another enforceable instrument with genuine value.

What I cannot support:
- full economic preservation,
- a structure that drains operating cash,
- or a “protected” trust that makes the company unfinanceable.

### 2) Bondholders cannot expect ordinary refinancing treatment
To the bondholders: I understand your objection to being the default dumping ground. That concern is legitimate. But it does not follow that the trust or labour can be insulated while unsecured debt only takes a modest haircut.

The company cannot survive with a balance sheet that is still too heavy. If unsecured debt remains excessive, the rescue is just a delay.

So yes, bondholders will take a **substantial impairment**. That is unavoidable. In return, any recovery must be economically real, not symbolic. But the recovery has to fit a viable capital structure, not the other way around.

### 3) Domestic plant protection has a hard economic limit
To the neighbouring governments: I understand why you want binding protection for domestic plants. If you are co-funding, you need a real stake and you want to protect domestic industry.

But I cannot accept a plan that freezes capacity for geography alone. If we keep too much fixed cost in the system, the company will remain structurally loss-making.

The specialist is right: we can protect a **core domestic base** if those plants are efficient and essential. We cannot protect all domestic capacity, and we cannot accept veto rights over necessary closures.

### 4) Labour cannot be insulated from the adjustment
To the union: I accept that workers should not be the sole shock absorber. But there is no viable restructuring that avoids wage, work-rule, and footprint changes. If we preserve too much of the old cost structure, the company will fail again.

That said, I am not asking for wholesale destruction of the workforce or the wage framework. I am asking for **bounded concessions** that match the realities of a smaller company.

## My position now

The company needs a plan with these elements:

1. **Deep unsecured impairment**
   - Not a token haircut.
   - Real conversion value, but only after proper deleveraging.

2. **Material modification of the retiree trust**
   - Real value preserved.
   - Cash burden reduced enough for the operating company to survive.

3. **Selective, economically justified plant and brand rationalization**
   - Close or consolidate the weak assets.
   - Preserve the strongest brands and the most efficient plants.

4. **Limited domestic protection**
   - A core domestic manufacturing base can be protected.
   - Blanket protection is not acceptable.

5. **A fast binding process**
   - If we cannot land this voluntarily, the court-supervised path becomes the serious route.

## On the specific arguments made

### To the union/trust
You say the trust must not be treated like a speculative claim. I agree. But neither can it remain a fixed operating burden the company cannot carry. The trust needs real value, but in a form the reorganized company can afford.

### To bondholders
You say you should not be singled out. Fair enough. But “not singled out” does not mean “made comfortable.” Everyone here is taking losses. The only question is whether those losses are arranged so the company survives.

### To neighbouring governments
You say domestic plants need binding protection. I am open to protecting the plants that are truly core and competitive. I am not open to preserving every domestic site regardless of economics.

### To the task force
You are right that the company must not be preserved through public subsidy alone. But the package must still protect the consumer-facing franchise: warranties, dealers, and the strongest brands. A rescue that destroys customer confidence is not a rescue.

## My non-negotiable line
I will not support:
- an unrestructured balance sheet,
- a plan that leaves the trust economically untouched,
- or a footprint that is too large for the new market reality.

I also will not support an outcome that destroys the going concern. An uncontrolled liquidation is unacceptable.

## What we need next
We need actual numbers, not generalities:
- the haircut range for unsecured bondholders,
- the form and burden of the trust instrument,
- the specific plants/brands/dealers to be cut,
- the amount and conditions of any co-funding,
- and the ownership split in the restructured company.

If those numbers do not add up to a financeable company, then we should stop pretending a weak deal is a solution and move to the court-supervised path.
