We agree on the central point: this must be a real restructuring, not a delay that merely buys a few more weeks of cash burn. If the company exits this process with the same cost base, the same obligations, and no credible path to profitability, then we all fail — including workers, retirees, creditors, and the public.

Where I differ is on the **priority order** and the **minimum conditions for survival**.

### Where we agree
- **The old structure is not viable.**  
  The company cannot carry its current burden of debt, legacy obligations, and excess capacity.
- **Shared sacrifice is unavoidable.**  
  Management has already changed. Labour and creditors will both need to take losses.
- **We need speed and finality.**  
  There is no room for prolonged bargaining that drains the company further.

### Where I disagree
- **We cannot protect every existing claim and preserve every job, plant, and brand.**  
  That is not restructuring; that is slow-motion insolvency. Some closures, consolidations, and eliminations are going to be necessary if there is to be a going concern at all.
- **The balance sheet has to be genuinely repaired.**  
  A plan that leaves the company burdened with obligations it cannot service is not acceptable, even if it sounds fair in distributional terms.
- **Creditor sacrifice cannot be treated as optional.**  
  Unsecured bondholders will need to take substantial haircuts. But equally, the company cannot survive if all the burden is pushed onto taxpayers or if operational cuts are too shallow to matter.

### On the retiree health trust
I understand the point that this obligation was earned and cannot simply be waved away. But I have to be direct: **if the structure of that claim makes the restructured company unviable, it must be modified materially**.

What we can support is a structure that:
- preserves value for retirees in a real form,
- is durable and enforceable,
- but does **not** cripple the reorganized company’s cash flow.

That likely means some combination of:
- reduced nominal burden,
- deferred payments,
- equity or other securities,
- and a claim structure tied to the company’s recovery.

What we cannot support is a version that preserves the full economic burden while expecting the operating company to somehow absorb it.

### On plants, brands, and jobs
We are prepared to preserve the strongest parts of the franchise, especially where they support near-term sales and long-term competitiveness. But I want to be clear: **not every plant or brand can be saved**.

If we are forced to choose, we will prioritize:
- the strongest consumer-facing brands,
- the most efficient plants,
- the dealer network that keeps the franchise alive,
- and the parts of the business that can actually earn returns.

That means some cuts will be deeper than anyone would like. But the alternative is uncontrolled collapse, which would destroy far more jobs and value than a hard restructuring.

### On process
We are open to either route if it gets us to a viable result in time. That said, if voluntary agreement cannot deliver the needed depth of sacrifice quickly, then a court-supervised process is not only acceptable — it may be necessary.

### Our bottom line
We will support a deal only if it delivers:
1. **credible financing certainty**,  
2. **meaningful debt reduction**,  
3. **operational cuts deep enough to restore viability**, and  
4. **a structure that protects the consumer franchise through transition**.

If the proposal tries to preserve too much of the old company at the expense of the business itself, we will not back it. That would imperil the enterprise, not save it.
