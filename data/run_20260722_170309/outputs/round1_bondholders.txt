We agree on one thing: this cannot be a cosmetic restructuring. The company is broken, and time is not on anyone’s side.

But I need to be equally direct about where we differ.

### What we agree with
- **Real balance-sheet repair is necessary.**
- **Management already changed, and it should not be protected.**
- **Some operational cuts are unavoidable.**
- **A court-supervised process is a real fallback if consensus fails.**

### Where we draw the line
The unsecured bondholders will not accept being treated as the residual dumping ground while others are insulated.

That means:

1. **No confiscatory treatment of unsecured creditors**
   We are not here to underwrite the rescue for free. If the company needs debt relief, fine — but the haircut must be proportionate, not punitive.

2. **Equal treatment among unsecureds**
   If the retiree health trust is being given a materially better package than the unsecured bondholders, that is not a fair restructuring. If one unsecured class receives better economics, there must be a real and justified basis, or compensation for the disparity.

3. **No equity without meaningful upside**
   “Some equity” is not automatically value. If bondholders are being converted into equity, the stake must be large enough to reflect the sacrifice being imposed. Small crumbs of stock in an overlevered company are not a fair exchange.

4. **No forced bailout of labour commitments at bondholder expense**
   We recognize retiree claims are politically sensitive, but that does not make them superior to contractual debt. If the trust is to receive a special structure, then unsecured creditors cannot be left materially worse without offsetting consideration.

### On the retiree health trust
We are prepared to accept that the trust may not be paid in full in cash. But if the trust is to receive equity, warrants, or a secured/priority structure that improves its recovery relative to unsecured bondholders, then we will object unless the bondholder class receives comparable value.

The current direction of travel looks like: labour gets political protection, the trust is treated as quasi-sacrosanct, and unsecured creditors are asked to absorb the deepest losses. That is exactly the kind of subordinating treatment we will resist.

### On process
We are not emotionally attached to out-of-court versus court-supervised restructuring. What matters is the result.

But let’s be honest: if the task force is prepared to use a court process to impose terms, then bondholders need to be clear-eyed about our leverage. We are dispersed, yes, but we have a very large claim and real standing. If the forced deal is materially worse than a fair voluntary exchange, we will not endorse it just because the clock is ticking.

### Bottom line
We are open to a deal only if it delivers:
- **meaningful recovery for unsecured bondholders,**
- **equal treatment relative to similarly situated unsecured claims,**
- **real upside participation if principal is being impaired,**
- and **no political preference disguised as restructuring fairness**.

If the proposal is basically “bondholders take the biggest hit so the trust and labour can look protected,” then let me be clear: we will oppose it.
