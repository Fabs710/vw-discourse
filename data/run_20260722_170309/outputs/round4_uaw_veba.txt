We have reached the point where everyone is finally putting hard lines on the table. So I will do the same.

## Where I agree
I agree with the task force, management, and the specialist on the central facts:

- the company in its current form is not viable,
- the old equity is gone,
- some plant and brand reduction is unavoidable,
- unsecured creditors must take meaningful impairment,
- and a cosmetic deal would only burn time and taxpayer support.

I also agree that a deal must be **real** and **fast**. We are not here to preserve a dead structure.

## Where I disagree

### 1) The retiree trust is not just another unsecured claim
This is the core issue, and I will not back off it.

The trust represents earned retiree health promises. It cannot be treated as a convenient pool of losses to be stripped out so that bondholders and public funders can call the restructuring “clean.”

What I will **not** accept:
- hollowing out the trust,
- reducing it to a meaningless deferred promise,
- or forcing retirees to absorb the largest economic loss while financial creditors preserve a better recovery.

What I **can** consider:
- a reduced nominal claim,
- a longer payment schedule,
- and a real instrument with enforceable value, such as equity, warrants, or a priority-backed funding vehicle.

But it must remain **durable and real**. A mere paper promise is not enough.

### 2) Workers cannot be the default adjustment variable
We have already accepted concessions. So let me be explicit:

- no wholesale sacrifice of the workforce,
- no destruction of the wage framework,
- no closure program built primarily to satisfy creditor recoveries,
- no treatment of labour as junior to financial creditors.

Yes, labour cost changes may be necessary. Yes, some footprint reduction may be necessary. But if the adjustment lands disproportionately on workers and retirees, this union will oppose it.

### 3) Unsecured bondholders cannot demand protection from the very burden they took on
I hear the bondholders saying they should not be the dumping ground. On that narrow point, I agree: they should not be singled out if others are protected.

But they are still unsecured creditors in a failed company. They took the risk of yield. They cannot demand a restructuring where the trust is hollowed out and workers are cut deepest so they can preserve a “meaningful” recovery.

So yes:
- they must take substantial haircuts,
- they should receive upside only if the company survives,
- and they do not get to define fairness as “everyone else gives more than us.”

### 4) Domestic plants are not expendable political decorations
To the neighbouring governments: we understand why you want binding protection. We also understand the task force’s warning that blanket protection breaks viability.

The answer cannot be to simply sacrifice domestic plants because they are politically easier to cut. We need a plan that protects a **core industrial base** and preserves as many jobs and plants as can be justified economically.

That means:
- objective criteria,
- selective closures only where the business case is clear,
- and binding commitments for the plants that are genuinely core.

## Our minimum position
Any deal we could support must include all of the following:

1. **A real retiree trust instrument**
   - real value,
   - enforceable,
   - not a hollow unsecured remainder.

2. **Bounded labour concessions**
   - some changes may be necessary,
   - but no wage-framework destruction and no wholesale layoffs.

3. **Selective, justified footprint reduction**
   - closure only where economics require it,
   - preservation of core plants and jobs.

4. **Deep but proportionate creditor impairment**
   - unsecured bondholders take substantial losses,
   - but not by dumping the largest burden onto labour and retirees.

5. **A viable operating company**
   - smaller, yes,
   - but still an industrial company with a future.

## On the task force’s “non-negotiable line”
You say you will not finance an unrestructured company or one carrying the full cash burden of legacy liabilities. Understood.

But I will be equally direct: we will not sign off on a deal that preserves the company only by destroying the trust or making workers carry the heaviest load.

If public money is being used to stabilize this company, then the public responsibility includes preserving industrial jobs and honoring retiree obligations in a durable form. Not fully, perhaps, but in a real way.

## On the court-supervised fallback
We are not afraid of it. But do not assume a court process automatically gives creditors and funders their preferred outcome.

If the court route is used to impose a structure that overprotects bondholders while stripping the trust and workforce, we will oppose it there too.

## What we need next
If this is serious, then stop asking for generalities and put forward actual terms:

- What exact form of security or equity will the retiree trust receive?
- What nominal reduction or deferral is being asked of the trust?
- Which plants are genuinely uneconomic and why?
- What binding job and plant commitments are being offered for the core industrial base?
- What haircut range are unsecured bondholders actually prepared to accept?

If the answer remains “the trust must absorb whatever makes the math easiest,” then there is no agreement from us.
