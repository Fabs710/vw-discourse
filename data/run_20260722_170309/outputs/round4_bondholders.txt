We are prepared to be constructive, but not to be used as the residual dumping ground for everyone else’s protected interests.

## Where we agree
We agree on the fundamentals:
- the company in its current form is not viable,
- the old equity is effectively worthless,
- some footprint reduction is unavoidable,
- and a cosmetic deal will fail.

We also agree that speed matters and that finality is important. A stalled process burns value for everyone, including us.

## Where we disagree

### 1) We will not accept unsecured bondholders being singled out while the retiree trust is given superior economics
This is the central issue.

Multiple parties keep talking about “shared sacrifice,” but the actual direction of the proposals still points the wrong way:
- the trust wants a durable, real claim,
- labour wants protected value and job preservation,
- neighbouring governments want protected domestic plants and governance rights,
- and unsecured bondholders are expected to absorb the deepest cut.

That is not balanced restructuring. That is political triage.

If the retiree trust is given a materially better instrument than unsecured bonds, then there must be clear offsetting value for unsecured creditors. Equal treatment among similarly situated unsecured claimants is not optional. We will not consent to a structure where the trust is insulated while the bondholders are left to eat the gap.

### 2) The retiree trust is not sacrosanct, but it is also not entitled to better treatment than us
We recognize that the trust reflects earned retiree health promises. Fine. That does not make it superior to unsecured bond claims in economic terms.

If the trust receives:
- equity,
- warrants,
- a priority instrument,
- or a materially better recovery structure,

then unsecured bondholders must receive comparable economic value. Not slogans. Not moral appeals. Comparable value.

We reject any package where the trust is politically shielded and the bondholders are quietly written down further because we are dispersed and therefore easier to cut.

### 3) “Deep impairment” cannot mean a near-wipeout of unsecured debt
The task force and management keep saying unsecureds will take “substantial impairment.” We understand that. But there is a line between a real restructuring and a confiscatory one.

We will not accept:
- token recoveries,
- a recovery that is materially worse than the retiree trust’s economics,
- or a conversion into equity that is so diluted it is effectively a wipe-out.

If the company needs a cleaner balance sheet, that does not justify treating us as the default shock absorber while protected stakeholders keep better economics.

### 4) Domestic plant protection cannot be funded by unsecured creditors
We understand the neighbouring governments’ political position. But they do not get to preserve domestic capacity by pushing the adjustment burden onto unsecured bondholders.

The specialist has already said it plainly: only a limited number of core, efficient plants can be protected without breaking viability. Anything beyond that is a hidden subsidy financed by the balance sheet and by creditors.

We will not support a restructuring where:
- domestic plant protection becomes a veto right over rationalization,
- co-funding is used to freeze uneconomic capacity,
- or creditors are made to pay for industrial geography.

### 5) Public funding is not a license to subordinate financial creditors
To the task force: public money may stabilize liquidity, but it cannot be used to force unsecured bondholders into an inequitable outcome while other legacy claimants secure carve-outs.

If taxpayers are backstopping the rescue, then the deal should be economically disciplined and evenly shared. Not one where public support props up protected labour claims and protected plants while bondholders are told to accept scraps.

## What we will support
We are prepared to support a restructuring only if it includes all of the following:

1. **Meaningful recovery for unsecured bondholders**
   - not par,
   - not token value,
   - not a near-wipeout.

2. **Comparable economic treatment among unsecured claimants**
   - if the trust gets better economics, unsecureds must receive offsetting value.

3. **Real upside participation**
   - if our debt is converted, the equity and warrants must be meaningful, not symbolic.

4. **A genuinely viable footprint**
   - closures of uneconomic plants, brands, and dealer capacity are acceptable;
   - blanket domestic protection is not.

5. **Fast finality**
   - if there is no voluntary package that respects these constraints, the court-supervised route may be preferable to a bad deal.

## On the court-supervised fallback
We are not afraid of the fallback route. In fact, if the voluntary package tries to:
- privilege the retiree trust,
- protect domestic plants for political reasons,
- and leave unsecured bondholders as the residual shock absorber,

then we would rather let the court-supervised process impose a fairer outcome.

The court route is not automatically the bondholders’ defeat. It can also expose whether the current deal architecture is simply too favorable to protected stakeholders and too harsh on dispersed unsecured creditors.

## Our non-negotiable line
We will not accept:
- materially worse treatment than comparable unsecured claims without compensation,
- a near-wipeout dressed up as “shared sacrifice,”
- or a deal where the trust is effectively preserved ahead of unsecured creditors.

If that is the proposal, we will oppose it.

## What we need next
If this is serious, then put forward actual numbers:
- the unsecured recovery range,
- the retiree trust recovery range,
- the precise basis for any differential treatment,
- the plant closure list,
- and the funding terms.

If the answer is still “bondholders take the biggest hit because they are easiest to cut,” then there is no deal.
