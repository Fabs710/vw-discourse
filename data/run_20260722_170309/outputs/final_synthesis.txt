## 1. Decision context
The company is in acute distress: sales have collapsed, liquidity is exhausted, and the existing capital structure is not supportable without deep restructuring. The executive task force controls the only financing and has made clear that support is conditional on material sacrifice by labor, bondholders, and public co-funders. The central decision is not whether to restructure, but how deeply to cut legacy claims, how much of the footprint to retain, how to allocate ownership, and whether to complete the deal consensually or through a court-supervised process.

## 2. Stakeholder assembly
- **Government Task Force (highest leverage and institutional standing):** controls bridge financing and sets the viability threshold.
- **Company Management:** responsible for operational plan and implementation.
- **Industrial Union & Retiree Health Trust:** represents labor and the retiree obligation with strong legitimacy and consent power.
- **Unsecured Bondholders’ Committee:** dispersed financial creditors with legal claims and blocking potential.
- **Neighbouring Governments (co-funders):** politically important, but their leverage is conditional on providing new money and protecting domestic plants.
- **Restructuring Expert:** not a party, but a high-credibility analytical voice shaping the feasible boundary.

## 3. Final positions
### Government Task Force
The task force’s position is the clearest and most authoritative: the company is only financeable if unsecured debt is cut deeply, the retiree trust is materially modified, and the footprint is reduced. It rejects preserving the trust in its current cash-heavy form and will not accept blanket domestic plant protection or public money used to freeze uneconomic capacity.

### Company Management
Management aligns closely with the task force on the need for a deep reset. It supports substantial unsecured impairment, material trust modification, selective plant and dealer rationalization, and limited protection for only a core domestic base, but insists the company cannot survive if legacy claims remain cash-heavy or the footprint remains politically frozen.

### Industrial Union & Retiree Health Trust
The union accepts that the company must be resized and that some concessions are inevitable, but it insists the retiree trust must remain a real, durable, enforceable claim rather than a hollowed-out promise. It rejects a deal that makes workers and retirees the default adjustment variable or uses restructuring to preserve creditor economics at their expense.

### Unsecured Bondholders’ Committee
Bondholders accept impairment but reject being singled out as the residual loss bucket while the retiree trust receives superior economics. They want meaningful recovery through equity/warrants, equal treatment among unsecureds, and a restructuring that is deep but not confiscatory.

### Neighbouring Governments
The co-funders are prepared to contribute money only if a core domestic industrial base is protected with binding commitments and they receive proportional equity and governance rights. They oppose a rescue that finances domestic industrial decline or leaves them with money at risk but no real seat at the table.

### Restructuring Expert
The expert’s conclusion is that viability requires all four of the following at once: deep unsecured haircut, material trust modification, meaningful footprint reduction, and enough exit liquidity to survive a weak market. It strongly suggests the current compromise is probably not yet severe enough and that a court-supervised route may be more credible if stakeholder consent cannot reach the required depth.

## 4. Genuine conflicts
1. **Retiree trust vs. unsecured bondholders:** both claim unfair treatment if the other is protected. The trust wants real value and durability; bondholders reject being subordinated to a politically sensitive labor claim.
2. **Domestic plant protection vs. economic rationalization:** neighboring governments want binding protection for domestic plants, while the task force, management, and expert say the company cannot carry blanket geographic guarantees.
3. **Public funding vs. operational control:** co-funders want governance rights and binding commitments, but the task force refuses veto rights that would block necessary plant closures and restructuring.
4. **Depth of impairment vs. political acceptability:** the economically necessary haircut/deleveraging may be too severe for a consensual deal, especially with a protected trust and dispersed bondholders.

## 5. Areas of convergence
- The current structure is not viable.
- A cosmetic or bridge-only solution will fail.
- Management change is accepted.
- All major stakeholders accept that sacrifice must be shared.
- Some plant/brand/dealer rationalization is unavoidable.
- A real equity or contingent-value component is likely needed for at least some claimants.
- Court-supervised restructuring is a credible fallback if consensus cannot achieve sufficient depth.

## 6. Key trade-offs for the decision-maker
- **Viability vs. fairness:** the deeper the haircut and footprint reduction, the more likely the company survives; but the less politically tolerable the deal becomes.
- **Labor legitimacy vs. creditor confidence:** preserving a real retiree claim helps labor legitimacy, but bondholders will resist any differential treatment without offsetting value.
- **Domestic industrial policy vs. operational economics:** protecting local plants may satisfy regional political goals but can destroy the standalone business case.
- **Consensual finality vs. imposed finality:** out-of-court agreement is faster and less adversarial, but court-supervised restructuring may be the only path capable of forcing sufficient sacrifice.
- **Public support vs. control:** co-funding can help liquidity, but governance concessions must not block rationalization.

## 7. Integrative pathways
1. **Split retiree trust into base + contingent upside**
   - Reduce the cash burden materially.
   - Preserve a real enforceable base instrument.
   - Add warrants/contingent value rights tied to performance.

2. **Tiered unsecured recovery**
   - Give bondholders equity/warrants rather than near-par debt.
   - Ensure recoveries are meaningful but subordinate to balance-sheet repair.
   - Use upside participation to soften nominal haircut politics.

3. **Core domestic footprint schedule**
   - Protect only objectively efficient domestic plants.
   - Pre-agree closure/consolidation of non-core sites.
   - Tie co-funder rights to monitoring and funding integrity, not operational vetoes.

4. **Limited governance rights for co-funders**
   - Proportional equity and board visibility.
   - No veto over economically necessary closures.
   - Clear enforcement of any production/job commitments tied to public money.

## 8. Open questions
- What is the maximum annual cash burden the reorganized company can sustain for retiree obligations?
- What precise nominal reduction, deferral, or conversion of the trust is acceptable to labor?
- What haircut range and equity/warrant package would unsecured bondholders accept as commercially meaningful?
- Which specific domestic plants are economically defensible as “core” and what binding commitments can reasonably attach to them?
- How much public co-funding is available, and what governance rights would come with it?
- Is the aggregate sacrifice package deep enough to satisfy the task force’s financeability test without forcing the process into court?

## 9. Recommended next step
Move immediately to a **single term-sheet grid** with numeric minimums and fallback positions for each stakeholder on:
- retiree trust treatment,
- unsecured creditor recovery,
- plant closures/core plant protection,
- co-funding amount and rights,
- equity split,
- and the preferred path: voluntary deal vs. court-supervised fallback.

If the numbers do not close within that grid, the decision-maker should prepare to activate the court-supervised process rather than prolong an unfinanceable voluntary negotiation.

## 10. Emergent insights that only appeared through multi-turn interaction
- The real impasse is not “restructuring or not,” but **who takes the deepest economic loss and in what legal form**.
- The retiree trust is the pivotal political and financial obstacle: it cannot remain cash-heavy, yet it also cannot be hollowed out without breaking labor consent.
- Bondholders’ central fear is not simply haircut size; it is being made the **default residual shock absorber** while protected claims receive better economics.
- Domestic plant protection becomes viable only if narrowed to a **core economically justified footprint**; blanket protection is inconsistent with solvency.
- The court-supervised route is increasingly not just a threat but a **credible coordination mechanism** if consent cannot overcome holdout and political constraints.
- Public co-funding is useful only as **transition liquidity**, not as a substitute for balance-sheet repair; otherwise it merely postpones failure.
- The parties have converged on the necessary ingredients of a deal, but not on the **minimum severity threshold** required to make the post-restructuring company financeable.
