We are at the point where everyone is finally speaking plainly, so I will do the same.

## Where we agree
We agree on the facts that matter:
- the company in its current form is not viable,
- the old equity is effectively gone,
- some footprint reduction is unavoidable,
- unsecured creditors must take real impairment,
- and a cosmetic “extend and pretend” deal would fail and waste the bridge financing.

We also agree with the specialist that **mere liability stretching is not enough**. If the company keeps too much debt, too much legacy burden, and too much unused capacity, it will fail again. We are not trying to preserve a dead balance sheet.

## Where we disagree
### 1) The retiree trust cannot be treated as just another unsecured claim to be crushed
This is the central issue.

The trust represents negotiated retiree health promises earned over decades of work. It is not a speculative instrument and it is not merely a line item to be liquidated for convenience.

I have heard the task force say the trust must be materially modified. I have heard management say the company cannot carry it in its present form. I understand the economics. But there is a line, and we will not cross it:

- We cannot accept a structure that destroys the trust as a real claim.
- We cannot accept a deal that turns retiree health protection into a hollow promise.
- We cannot accept being told that “viability” means the workforce and retirees absorb the largest losses while financial creditors preserve meaningful recovery.

If the trust is to be modified, it must be converted into something **durable and enforceable**:
- real equity,
- warrants,
- or a secured / priority instrument with actual value,
- plus a payment structure the company can carry.

A claim that is merely deferred, diluted, or made contingent without real economic value is not acceptable.

### 2) Workers cannot be the default adjustment variable
We accept that the company must be smaller and more efficient. We do **not** accept that this becomes a license for disproportionate job destruction, wholesale plant closures, or a reset of the wage framework at workers’ expense.

We have already accepted concessions in earlier rounds. So let me be explicit:
- no workforce wholesale sacrifice,
- no deep destruction of the wage framework,
- no closure list built primarily to satisfy creditor recoveries or political optics.

If plants are to close, they must close because there is a defensible economic case, not because workers are the easiest constituency to cut.

### 3) Bondholders are not entitled to shift the deepest losses onto labour and retirees
I hear the bondholders saying they should not be the default dumping ground. On that narrow point, I agree: they should not be singled out if others are protected. But that cuts both ways.

They invested for yield and took credit risk. They do not get to insist on a recovery structure that leaves the trust hollowed out and the workforce carrying the largest burden.

So yes:
- they must take substantial haircuts,
- they should receive only what a viable capital structure can support,
- and if they convert to equity, the upside must reflect real sacrifice.

What they do **not** get is a restructuring where the union/trust is cut hardest so unsecured creditors can call their recovery “commercially meaningful.”

### 4) Domestic plant protection cannot be reduced to “politics is expensive”
We understand the neighbouring governments’ concerns. We understand the task force’s warning that excessive protection breaks viability. Both are true.

But we will not support a plan that treats domestic plants as expendable while preserving other capacity simply because it is more convenient for the numbers on paper. The company is an industrial enterprise with communities, supply chains, and real employment. Viability has to include industrial continuity, not just a cleaner spreadsheet.

That means:
- preserve as many plants as can be justified,
- close only what is clearly uneconomic,
- and protect core industrial capability in a binding way.

## Our minimum position
If there is going to be a deal, it must include all of the following:

1. **A real, durable retiree trust instrument**
   - equity, warrants, or secured/priority value,
   - not a symbolic unsecured promise.

2. **Bounded labour concessions**
   - some wage/work-rule change may be necessary,
   - but no wholesale destruction of the wage framework.

3. **Selective, justified footprint reduction**
   - closures only where the economic case is clear,
   - preservation of core plants and jobs.

4. **Deep but proportionate creditor impairment**
   - unsecured bondholders take substantial losses,
   - but not by forcing retirees and workers to absorb the deepest pain.

5. **A viable company, not a stripped shell**
   - the restructuring must leave a real industrial future,
   - not a company that survives only as a smaller platform for creditor recovery.

## On the task force’s “non-negotiable line”
You say you will not finance an unrestructured company or one that preserves the full cash burden of legacy liabilities. Understood.

But let me be just as clear: we will not sign off on a plan that preserves the company only by destroying the trust or making workers the shock absorber for everyone else’s losses.

If the public purse is being used to stabilize this company, then the public responsibility includes preserving industrial jobs and honoring earned retiree obligations in a durable form. Not fully, maybe not in the old form — but in a real form.

## On the court-supervised fallback
We are not afraid of the court route. But do not assume it gives the task force an automatic victory over labour and retirees. If the court process is used to impose a structure that overprotects bondholders while stripping the trust and workforce, we will oppose it just as firmly there.

If anything, the fallback route makes one thing clearer: the deal must be better than an imposed outcome for it to be worth signing voluntarily. That means the current proposal still needs:
- a real trust instrument,
- protection for core jobs and plants,
- and losses distributed fairly.

## What we need next
If this is serious, then stop asking for slogans and give us actual terms.

I want the next round to answer these directly:
- What exact form of security or equity will the retiree trust receive?
- What nominal reduction or deferral is being asked of the trust?
- Which plants are genuinely uneconomic and why?
- What plant/job commitments are being offered for the core industrial base?
- What haircut range are unsecured bondholders actually prepared to take?

If the answer is still “the trust must take whatever is necessary and workers should accept it because the company is weak,” then there is no agreement from us.
