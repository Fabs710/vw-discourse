We are hearing the right acknowledgments, but we are still not yet hearing a package that is deep enough, fast enough, or balanced enough to justify continued public financing.

Let me be explicit.

### Where we agree
- The company is **not viable in its current form**.
- Management has already changed, and that was necessary.
- Labour, retirees, creditors, and public funders must all take losses or concessions.
- A court-supervised process remains a real fallback if voluntary agreement cannot be reached quickly.

### Where I disagree, sharply
#### 1) The retiree health trust cannot be treated as sacrosanct
I recognize the claim is politically and morally sensitive. But sensitivity does not make it payable in full, and it does not make it superior to the viability of the enterprise.

If the trust is structured in a way that leaves the restructured company cash-flow impaired, then that is not a solution. It is just postponing collapse.

So let me be clear:  
- I will not finance a deal that **preserves the full economic burden** of the retiree obligation on the operating company.  
- I will not finance a deal that uses the public purse to backfill an unrepaired legacy liability.  
- I will not accept any structure where unsecured creditors are forced to absorb the deepest losses while the trust is insulated through political pressure.

#### 2) Bondholders cannot expect to be paid as if this were ordinary refinancing
The unsecured bondholders are not being singled out for punishment. They are being asked to absorb the losses required to make the company viable.

That said, I do agree with one point: if debt is being impaired, the recovery must be real, not symbolic. But “real” does not mean generous. It means consistent with a viable capital structure.

What I will not accept:
- a balance sheet that is still overlevered after restructuring,
- a recovery package that leaves too much debt in place,
- or a voluntary deal in which bondholders hold out for better terms while the public keeps the company alive.

#### 3) On plants and jobs, sentiment cannot outrun arithmetic
Management is right that not every plant, brand, or dealer can survive. The union and the neighbouring governments are right that indiscriminate cuts would destroy value.

But we are not going to preserve capacity simply because it is politically difficult to close. The test is whether the plant network fits a viable post-restructuring business.

So the rule is simple:
- preserve what is efficient and strategically necessary,
- close what is structurally uncompetitive,
- and stop pretending that every site can be saved through hope and delay.

Binding domestic commitments may be possible where they are economically justified. They are not acceptable if they merely export the burden to the rest of the restructuring.

#### 4) Public co-funding is not a veto right over viability
To the neighbouring governments: if you want co-funding, that is welcome. But your participation does not change the central requirement that the company must be viable. Domestic production guarantees cannot be allowed to undermine the restructuring’s economics.

I will consider co-funding only if:
- it comes with enforceable commitments,
- it supports a viable operating footprint,
- and it does not force the company to keep excess capacity simply to satisfy local politics.

If the price of your money is an unviable industrial map, then we do not have a solution.

---

## The minimum contours I will support
This is the level of seriousness we are looking for:

1. **Deep creditor impairment**  
   Unsecured bondholders must take substantial haircuts. A token exchange is not enough.

2. **Material modification of the retiree obligation**  
   The trust may receive a real instrument or participation, but not one that cripples cash flow. If that requires reducing nominal claims or stretching payments materially, so be it.

3. **Operational downsizing that matches the new business**  
   Plants, brands, and dealer capacity must be cut enough to create a sustainable company, not a politically convenient one.

4. **No open-ended public subsidy**  
   Our financing is a bridge to a viable end-state, not a standing commitment to keep every claimant whole.

5. **A fast route to finality**  
   If a consensual package matching these terms is not available quickly, we will move to the court-supervised path. I am not bluffing about that.

## To each stakeholder
### To labour / retiree trust
If you want the trust to survive as a real claim, then it must survive in a form the company can actually carry. We are not asking you to take nothing; we are telling you that the old structure cannot simply be preserved under a new name.

### To bondholders
You will get a better outcome by engaging seriously now than by forcing the matter into a court process where we can impose the necessary depth of impairment. Do not confuse leverage with entitlement.

### To management
You have our support only to the extent you present a plan that is genuinely enforceable, not aspirational. We need hard numbers, hard closures, and hard savings.

### To neighbouring governments
Bring concrete co-funding terms tied to verifiable production commitments, not political statements. If your support helps stabilize the enterprise without breaking viability, we will consider it. If it becomes a mechanism to preserve excess capacity, we will reject it.

## What I need in the next round
I want each party to come back with **actual minimum numbers and terms**, not general principles:
- creditor haircut range,
- retiree trust treatment,
- plant closures / capacity reduction,
- public funding amount and conditions,
- equity split in the restructured company.

If the proposals still do not add up to a viable balance sheet and operating model, then we are wasting time—and the fallback process becomes the serious path.
