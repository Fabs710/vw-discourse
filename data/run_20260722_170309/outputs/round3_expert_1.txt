From a restructuring finance and insolvency perspective, the package as described is **not yet financeable on a standalone basis** unless the numbers behind the concessions are materially deeper than the language suggests.

## Bottom line
A viable standalone plan likely requires all of the following:
1. **Very substantial unsecured debt reduction**
2. **Material modification of the retiree health trust obligation**
3. **Meaningful plant/brand/dealer rationalization**
4. **Enough public/co-funding to bridge liquidity and working capital**

## My assessment of each disputed area

### 1) Retiree trust
The task force and management are correct on the core point: **the trust cannot remain in its present economic form** if the company is to survive.

From a viability standpoint, the trust must be converted into a structure that:
- **reduces near-term cash burden materially**
- preserves **some real value** for retirees
- and does **not operate like senior debt on the operating company**

The most workable forms would be:
- a reduced claim with long deferral,
- a contingent value instrument,
- a combination of cash, equity, and warrants,
- or a separable funding vehicle backed by limited support.

What will not work is a claim that remains economically equivalent to the current burden.

**Judgment:** the trust must take a **large economic haircut** or extended deferral. If it does not, the company likely fails again.

---

### 2) Unsecured bondholders
The bondholders are also correct that they should not be the only shock absorber. But in an enterprise this distressed, unsecured bondholders are almost certainly the **primary loss-bearing financial class**.

For a company in this condition, meaningful viability usually requires:
- a **deep haircut**
- debt-for-equity conversion
- and perhaps warrants for upside

A token exchange will not repair the balance sheet.

**Judgment:** unsecureds likely need to absorb a **very large impairment**. If the trust is given materially better economics than unsecureds, bondholders’ recovery must still be sufficient to avoid litigation or holdout risk, or the deal may fail to bind consensually.

---

### 3) Plants, brands, dealer network
The specialist’s position is the economically right one: **the footprint must shrink materially**.

For a distressed automaker, viability usually depends on:
- eliminating structurally underutilized plants,
- cutting brands that do not earn their cost of capital,
- reducing dealer excess capacity,
- and lowering fixed overhead to match post-crisis demand.

If domestic governments insist on broad plant protection, that is a direct threat to viability unless those plants are demonstrably efficient and required in the future operating base.

**Judgment:** the company needs **selective closures and consolidation**, not a cosmetic trim. Blanket preservation of domestic sites is not compatible with a financeable standalone case.

---

### 4) Public funding / co-funding
Public support can bridge liquidity, but it cannot substitute for restructuring.

The public money is viable only if it is:
- time-limited,
- conditional,
- senior enough to protect the funder,
- and not used to keep excess capacity alive.

If neighbouring governments co-fund, they should get a real economic return and some governance rights. But **governance rights must not amount to a veto over necessary rationalization**.

**Judgment:** public funding can help complete the rescue, but it does not solve solvency. It is only additive if the underlying capital structure is repaired.

## Likely viability threshold
Without the actual balance sheet I cannot give a precise haircut percentage, but structurally the company needs to emerge with:

- **substantially reduced funded debt**
- **legacy obligations converted into non-cash or contingent claims**
- **liquidity sufficient for 12–18 months post-restructuring**
- **a break-even volume aligned to a smaller footprint**
- **working capital headroom for a highly cyclical industry**

For an auto manufacturer in distress, the danger zone is when legacy claims still consume too much of:
- operating cash,
- capex,
- and restructuring savings.

If the plan leaves the company with:
- a heavy fixed-cost base,
- unresolved pension/retiree obligations,
- and too much bond debt,
then the restructuring becomes only a delay.

## Court-supervised vs consensual
### Consensual deal
A consensual deal is preferable if it can be done quickly, but only if it delivers:
- real deleveraging,
- real claim modification,
- and finality.

### Court-supervised process
If consensus stalls, court supervision becomes the more credible path because it can:
- impose haircuts on holdouts,
- force breadth of sacrifice,
- and deliver finality within the deadline.

**Judgment:** if any one stakeholder class is demanding protection that breaks the economics, the court route may be the only way to achieve a viable result.
