We are now at the point where general principles are no longer useful. The remaining question is whether stakeholders are prepared to accept a **real restructuring** or whether they are trying to preserve legacy claims and protected capacity at taxpayer risk.

Let me be direct.

## Where I agree
- The company is not viable in its current form.
- Existing equity is effectively wiped out.
- The expert is right that viability requires **deep deleveraging** and **material footprint reduction**.
- The union is right that a cosmetic deal will fail.
- The bondholders are right that a token exchange is not enough.
- The neighbouring governments are right that if they are putting real money in, they should get a real seat at the table.

But agreement ends there, because the proposals still being floated do not yet satisfy the core requirement: **the restructured company must be able to survive without ongoing public crutches**.

## Where I disagree

### 1) The retiree trust cannot be preserved in a way that makes the operating company nonviable
To the union and trust: I do not dispute that this is earned compensation. I do dispute any structure that leaves the company carrying the full economic burden as if nothing has changed.

That is not a durable claim. It is a claim on a company that will fail again.

So no:
- no full economic preservation,
- no hidden seniority by political pressure,
- no structure that leaves the trust effectively equivalent to a fixed operating liability the company cannot bear.

What I can support is a **real instrument with real value**:
- a reduced claim,
- a stretch-out,
- and, if necessary, equity or warrants tied to actual future performance.

But the trust must be modified enough to remove the cash-flow choke point. If that means a significant reduction in nominal burden, then that is what the facts require.

### 2) Bondholders will not be spared meaningful impairment, but they also will not be made the sole shock absorber
To the bondholders: I hear your objection to being singled out. That objection is valid only if the proposed package truly treats all legacy claimants on an economically comparable basis.

It will not be acceptable for the trust to be protected while unsecured creditors take the deepest hit. Nor will it be acceptable for bondholders to insist on recoveries that leave the company overlevered.

The right answer is not “protect bondholders more.” The right answer is:
- **deep haircuts for unsecured debt**,
- **real but limited upside**,
- and a capital structure that can actually function under stressed automotive volumes.

If the only way to make the math work is to leave unsecureds near zero while the trust is retained in privileged form, then the package is not balanced and I will not support it.

### 3) Domestic plant protection has a hard economic limit
To the neighbouring governments: I understand the political pressure. But domestic plant protection is not a blank check. If you demand commitments that lock in excess capacity, you are not protecting industry; you are exporting the cost of political comfort into an already fragile restructuring.

The specialist’s point is correct:
- limited protection of efficient core plants may be absorbable,
- blanket protection is not,
- and any co-funder governance rights that amount to a veto over rationalization are a nonstarter.

So I need it stated plainly: if domestic sites are not economically competitive, they cannot all be preserved simply because they are domestic. This restructuring is about viability, not industrial geography.

### 4) Co-funding is welcome only if it does not distort the turnaround
I am open to neighbouring government participation. But participation means capital, not control of the operating map.

What I will consider:
- co-funding tied to a defined, limited set of plants that are genuinely core and economically justified,
- equity proportional to real money at risk,
- and no veto rights that block necessary closures.

What I will not consider:
- funding that forces the company to keep excess capacity,
- equity that comes with operational handcuffs,
- or a structure that makes public money a substitute for hard restructuring.

## My position on the architecture

If we are serious, then the restructuring has to look like this:

1. **Deep unsecured impairment**
   - Substantial haircuts, not symbolic ones.
   - Real equity/warrants, but only after the company is properly deleveraged.

2. **Material retiree trust modification**
   - A real claim, yes.
   - But not one that functions as an unaffordable senior burden on the operating company.

3. **Selective footprint reduction**
   - Close or consolidate non-economic plants, brands, and dealer capacity.
   - Preserve only the core assets that fit a realistic post-crisis demand base.

4. **Limited domestic plant protection**
   - Only where objective economics support it.
   - No broad territorial guarantees.

5. **Fast finality**
   - If a voluntary package meeting these conditions is not produced quickly, we will move to the court-supervised path.

## On the court-supervised fallback
Let me be equally clear to everyone: the fallback is not a bluff, and it is not a threat I am using lightly.

If stakeholders continue to hold out for better treatment, or try to preserve politically sensitive claims at the expense of viability, then we will use the court-supervised route to impose a structure that actually works.

That route exists for a reason:
- to force depth,
- to eliminate holdout leverage,
- and to achieve finality within the deadline.

If the voluntary negotiations cannot match that standard, then the court route becomes the serious path.

## What I need next

I am not asking for more slogans. I am asking for numbers and boundaries.

### To the union and retiree trust:
Give me:
- the maximum affordable burden you will accept on the operating company,
- the form of the modified trust instrument,
- and the amount of nominal reduction or deferral you can live with.

### To bondholders:
Give me:
- your acceptable haircut range,
- the minimum meaningful equity/warrant package you would accept,
- and the point at which you would stop calling the recovery “confiscatory” and start recognizing the business reality.

### To management:
Give me:
- the immediate plant/brand/dealer closures you are prepared to execute,
- the year-one savings,
- and the lower post-restructuring break-even volume.

### To neighbouring governments:
Give me:
- the exact plants you believe can be protected on economic grounds,
- the size of the co-funding,
- and the ownership/governance rights you seek.

## My non-negotiable line
I will not finance:
- an unrestructured company,
- a capital structure that remains overlevered,
- or a deal that preserves the full cash burden of legacy liabilities while calling itself viable.

That is the limit. If the proposals do not respect it, then they do not have my support.
