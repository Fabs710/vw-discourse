We appreciate the specialist’s confirmation of what we have been saying from the start: **a cosmetic deal will fail**. The company needs real deleveraging and real industrial downsizing. We do **not** dispute that.

But we are going to be equally direct about the parts of this discussion that are still being pushed too far in the direction of labour and retirees absorbing the pain while financial claimants and public actors define “viability” on their terms.

## Where we agree
- The current structure is unsustainable.
- Some plant, brand, and dealer rationalization is unavoidable.
- A fast, binding solution is necessary.
- Existing equity is effectively gone.
- The trust cannot simply remain in its old form if that makes the company unviable.

## Where we disagree
### 1) The retiree health trust is not just another unsecured claim
The expert says the trust must be materially modified. We agree with that **in principle**, but not with the implication that it can be casually subordinated or hollowed out.

This obligation was earned by workers and retirees over decades. It is not a speculative investment. If the restructuring converts it into something contingent, weak, or purely symbolic, then you are not “preserving viability” — you are destroying a negotiated retirement promise and shifting the burden onto the people who built the company.

What we can accept:
- reduced nominal burden,
- stretched payments,
- conversion into **real, enforceable equity or securities**,
- and a structure that gives retirees an actual stake in the restructured company.

What we cannot accept:
- an empty promise,
- a claim so diluted that it has no durable value,
- or a structure where the trust is treated as a convenient place to absorb losses while others are protected.

### 2) Workers cannot be the primary adjustment variable
The expert is right that the company needs a leaner footprint. But if “leaner” becomes a code word for disproportionate job destruction, wage rollback, and plant closures concentrated on workers while creditors still expect meaningful recovery, then this is not shared sacrifice.

We have already accepted concessions in earlier rounds. We will not accept:
- wholesale plant shutdowns,
- deep wage framework destruction,
- or a restructuring that leaves the workforce carrying the heaviest burden after management has already been replaced.

If the company survives, it must survive as an industrial enterprise with a future workforce, not as a shell that maximizes creditor recovery by liquidating communities.

### 3) Bondholders are not entitled to be made comfortable
I want to address the bondholder point directly. They keep arguing that the trust should not be “special” if unsecured creditors are impaired. That misses the point.

The retiree trust is not a trading claim; it is deferred compensation and healthcare protection for retirees. Bondholders invested for yield and took credit risk. They absolutely must take haircuts, and if they are converted into equity, that equity must reflect real sacrifice — not a token piece meant to preserve face.

We will not support a structure where:
- workers and retirees take deep cuts,
- plants close,
- and bondholders still emerge with a recovery package that treats them as the primary constituency.

That is not fairness. That is creditor precedence dressed up as restructuring discipline.

### 4) Public funding must not be used to impose one-sided industrial shrinkage
To the task force and neighbouring governments: we understand taxpayer protection and the need for a viable business. But public money should not become leverage to force labour and the trust into accepting the worst of the losses while public actors secure political wins through selective plant protection.

We can support:
- bridge financing tied to hard milestones,
- co-funding tied to enforceable domestic production commitments,
- and a smaller but viable company.

We cannot support:
- public support that subsidizes bondholder recoveries,
- or a restructuring map that sacrifices one industrial base to protect another.

## Our minimum conditions
If there is going to be a deal we can sign, it must include:

1. **A durable retiree trust instrument**
   - real equity, warrants, or a secured claim with actual value,
   - not a hollow unsecured promise.

2. **Meaningful but bounded labour concessions**
   - wage/work-rule adjustments only to the extent necessary for viability,
   - no wholesale destruction of the wage framework.

3. **Selective, justified plant cuts**
   - closures only where there is a clear economic case,
   - preservation of the core industrial base and as many jobs as possible.

4. **Deep but proportionate creditor haircuts**
   - bondholders must absorb substantial losses,
   - any upside they receive must reflect real impairment.

5. **A viable company, not a cosmetically smaller one**
   - if the proposed footprint is so small that it cannot support the remaining workforce, retirees, and local supplier network, then the restructuring is not acceptable.

## On the process
We remain open to either route, but we are not open to delay tactics.

If voluntary negotiations can produce:
- real trust securities,
- concrete employment commitments,
- and a credible deleveraging package,

then fine.

If not, then the court-supervised route may be necessary — but let me be clear: we will use our consent power to ensure the court process does not become a mechanism for forcing labour and retirees to take the deepest losses while bondholders hide behind “market discipline.”

## Bottom line
We accept that **the company must be smaller and financially repaired**.

We reject the idea that this means:
- sacrificing the retiree trust,
- using workers as the shock absorber,
- or preserving creditor recovery at the expense of industrial communities.

If the next package includes a **real, durable claim for the trust**, **protected core employment**, and **shared sacrifice that is actually shared**, we can continue. If it is still a plan to make workers and retirees pay first so everyone else can call the outcome “viable,” then we will oppose it plainly.
