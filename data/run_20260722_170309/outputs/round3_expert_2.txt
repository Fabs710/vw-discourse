## Where the viability limit likely sits
The hard limit is wherever the legacy burden still consumes too much of:
- operating cash,
- restructuring savings,
- capex,
- and working capital.

For a distressed automaker, that usually means:
- the retiree obligation cannot remain economically equivalent to its current form,
- unsecured debt cannot remain anywhere near current levels,
- and the footprint must be sized to a much lower sustained demand base.

If the reorganized company still carries a heavy fixed-cost base plus large legacy claims, it will likely fail again.

## Specific judgments by issue

### 1) Retiree trust
The trust cannot remain in its present economic form if the operating company is to survive.

What works:
- reduced claim,
- long deferral,
- contingent value instrument,
- or a mix of cash, equity, and warrants.

What does not work:
- keeping it functionally senior and cash-heavy on the operating company.

**Viability conclusion:** the trust needs a **large economic haircut or long deferral**.

### 2) Unsecured bondholders
Unsecureds will almost certainly be the **primary loss-bearing financial class**, but not the only one.

What works:
- deep haircut,
- debt-for-equity conversion,
- warrants for upside.

What does not work:
- token exchange,
- recovery that leaves the company still overlevered.

**Viability conclusion:** unsecureds likely need **very large impairment**.

### 3) Plants, brands, dealer network
The footprint must shrink materially.

What works:
- closing structurally underutilized plants,
- cutting brands that do not earn their capital,
- reducing dealer excess capacity,
- lowering fixed overhead.

What does not work:
- blanket domestic plant protection,
- politically frozen capacity,
- preserving geography instead of economics.

**Viability conclusion:** the company needs **selective closures and consolidation**, not cosmetic cuts.

### 4) Public funding / co-funding
Public support can bridge liquidity, but it cannot substitute for restructuring.

What works:
- time-limited,
- conditional,
- senior enough to protect the funder,
- paired with real deleveraging.

What does not work:
- using public money to keep excess capacity alive,
- governance rights that become a veto over rationalization.

**Viability conclusion:** public funding is additive only if the underlying capital structure is repaired.

## Consensual vs court-supervised
A consensual deal is preferable only if it delivers:
- real deleveraging,
- real claim modification,
- and finality.

If any stakeholder class demands protection that breaks the economics, the **court-supervised route** becomes the more credible path because it can:
- force breadth of sacrifice,
- impose haircuts on holdouts,
- and deliver finality within the deadline.

## Bottom line
A viable outcome requires:
- **substantial trust modification**
- **deep unsecured impairment**
- **material footprint reduction**
- **limited, economics-based plant protection only**
- **public funding as a bridge, not a substitute**
- **equity allocation reflecting real sacrifice**

If the final compromise does not materially exceed the current positions, then the most likely outcome is **continued insolvency pressure and a court-imposed restructuring**.
