[Regional State Government — Round 2]

This round moved from positions to numbers, and I want to credit that before I get to where I still disagree, because credit where it's due matters if we want this process to keep producing disclosure rather than retreat into posturing.

**On the €4 billion figure and the external verification mechanism: this is real progress, and I support the mechanism as proposed — with one addition.** Management disclosing the gap, and conceding a jointly-mandated external party rather than self-graded modelling, addresses exactly what the Works Council and Union flagged as the core procedural problem: whoever controls the measurement controls the outcome. I want to be explicit that the state expects a seat in designing the terms of reference for that external verification — not as a fourth arbiter competing with the union and works council's role, but because as a shareholder with board seats and a public mandate, I will be asked by this region's citizens whether the number is real, and I need to be able to answer that with more than "management told me so." That is not distrust of management specifically; it is the same discipline I'd apply to any figure this consequential. I'd ask the union and works council whether they can live with a three-way design of terms of reference — you, management, and us as a shareholder — rather than a two-party negotiation over methodology that leaves the state simply told the outcome afterward.

**On the top-level contribution: I will say plainly that 10% and a distribution freeze is a real number, not a gesture, and I welcome it — but I also hear the union's warning, and I take it seriously.** Tens of millions against a €4 billion gap is not going to close anything structurally, nor did management claim it would. Its function is legitimacy, and legitimacy is not a soft consideration to me — it is the difference between a settlement that holds through 2027 and one that reignites the moment the next earnings dip arrives. So here is my honest assessment: this figure is sufficient to be taken seriously as a good-faith opening, but I would push management and the Family Holding to go further before this is finalised, specifically on the dividend side. A freeze at current levels, with surplus redirected to the restructuring fund, is a start; given the scale of what you're asking the workforce to absorb, I would want to see whether that freeze can extend meaningfully into any special distributions or share buybacks during the same horizon, not just the ordinary dividend line. I'll raise that directly with the Family Holding at board level, because management has said this requires their engagement, not just management's.

**On the 2028-versus-2030 guarantee horizon: this is the point where I part from management's position, and I want to be specific about why, not just restate the works council's objection.** I understand management's underlying concern — a fixed commitment that ignores a further market contraction or a 2027 cost shock is a real risk, and I won't pretend it isn't. But I don't accept that the answer is shortening the horizon to 2028. The answer is what management itself proposed for the reopening mechanism: narrow, objective, jointly-verified triggers. If those triggers are genuinely narrow and objective — a specified market-volume threshold, a specified savings-delivery shortfall, verified by the same external party rather than management's discretion — then the horizon can run to 2030 without becoming a "blank guarantee," because the protection against unforeseen shocks lives in the trigger design, not in truncating the guarantee itself. Shortening to 2028 does the opposite of what management says it wants: it turns a conditional, narrowly-triggered guarantee into a short guarantee that simply reopens the whole fight two years sooner regardless of whether any trigger condition is met. I would ask management to reconsider: keep 2030, keep the narrow objective triggers, and you get durability for the workforce without the rigidity you're actually worried about.

**On the "if it lands materially short, we are looking at a smaller, negotiated closure or capacity footprint" comment: I want to flag this now, directly, because I said in Round 1 I wouldn't let vague framing slide, and I meant it.** I understand why management raised it — transparency about what happens if the arithmetic falls short is better than a surprise later, and I won't pretend otherwise. But I need to be equally transparent in return: my veto does not soften proportionally to how "small" a closure is dressed up as being. A smaller closure is still a closure, still a community losing an anchor employer, still the precedent the works council named as unacceptable. Before any closure — of any size — reaches this board, I expect the record to show that every non-closure lever was genuinely exhausted: extended attrition timelines, further working-time reduction, inter-plant transfers and load-balancing across the German footprint, additional voluntary severance incentives, and a harder look at whether the remaining shortfall could be closed through capacity reduction without a site closing entirely — reduced shifts, reduced lines, partial repurposing. If, after all of that, verified independently, a genuine and unclosable gap remains, I will engage with that reality rather than pretend it away — I said in Round 1 I would not defend a cosmetic package, and I hold myself to the same standard on the other side. But "materially short" cannot become the trigger for reaching for closure as the convenient next step. It has to be the trigger for exhausting the next tier of alternatives first.

**On the works council and union's numbers: the direction is right, but I'd push you, gently, the same way I pushed management.** A mid-single-digit wage contribution, attrition acceleration, front-loaded working-time reduction, a solidarity fund — that is a real package, and I don't think you're posturing. But you have not yet run it against €4 billion, and until someone does, on a year-by-year basis through 2027 as management requested, we are still arguing about adequacy in the abstract. I'd ask you to commit, this round, to doing that arithmetic jointly with management and the external verifier as soon as the terms of reference are set — not as a concession to management's framing, but because I think your package is stronger with numbers behind it than without them, and because the state's veto is more defensible to the public if I can point to a verified calculation, not just a mandate.

To summarise where I actually stand after this round: I support the disclosure and the verification mechanism; I want a state role in designing it; I think the top-level contribution is real but should go further on distributions; I disagree with shortening the guarantee horizon and will argue for 2030 with narrow triggers instead; and I will not accept "smaller closure" as a fallback until every non-closure alternative has been jointly verified as exhausted. My veto stands on that basis — not as a blank refusal, but as a conditional one that requires the process just outlined to actually happen, transparently, before any closure proposal reaches this board.
