**Institutional Investors (Capital Markets) — Round 4**

I want to be efficient here, because the structure of this dispute has actually clarified a great deal this round, and I think it's now possible to say precisely where I stand rather than keep restating principles.

**On the auditor: strong endorsement, no changes needed to the mandate.** The refusal to answer what a fired trigger "permits," the honest flagging on volume reallocation, the ledger-level tracing discipline — this is exactly the rigor that lets me take a number to my own analysts and defend it. I'll only add: I want the sensitivity analysis on trigger thresholds — the false-positive/false-negative modelling against historical volume volatility — shared with all six parties in full, not summarized. If I'm going to price this settlement's durability, I need to see the actual stress-test, not a conclusion about it.

**On the State's and Family Holding's point about the two-thirds threshold — this is the single most useful thing said this round, and I want to say plainly why it changes my own calculus.**

The State and Family Holding have both now said, independently and precisely: the real protection against an unwanted closure is not a sentence in a document, it's the board arithmetic — ten employee seats, the State's statute, and a two-thirds requirement that cannot be met without either Works Council consent or a shareholder-side unity that doesn't exist without them. I think this is simply correct, and I think it resolves a debate that's been running for two rounds under a misleading framing. Works Council, Union — you have been negotiating for a word Management has told you three times now it will not give, and I want to be direct: I don't think you need it, and I think continuing to demand it is costing this table time we don't have, without buying you anything the board math doesn't already deliver. Your actual leverage is your board seats and your strike mandate, not a clause. Spend the next round on trigger thresholds and the €0.9bn reclassification — that's where the real fight over your members' outcomes is happening, not in the adjectives.

**Where I differ from the Family Holding and State, and why it matters to me specifically:** I accept that a fired trigger or a verified shortfall doesn't hand Management an automatic mandate — fine, agreed, "information not authorization" stands. But I want to flag something none of the ownership-side parties have said plainly: a governance structure where every verified shortfall returns the table to another negotiation round, with no external deadline on how many times that can happen, is not free for us. It's not free for the company either. Every extra quarter this runs, capital markets price in unresolved structural risk, and that shows up in cost of capital, in the multiple the market assigns to this stock relative to peers who've already taken their medicine, and in options foreclosed because cash is trapped in an unresolved restructuring rather than deployed into the product investment that actually protects everyone's jobs long-term, including the ones this table is trying to protect right now.

So here is my actual position, and I want it stated as a limit, not a preference: **I accept that closure cannot and will not be categorically written into or out of this document — the board arithmetic settles that, not the drafting, and I think Management should stop pushing for language it isn't going to get either.** What I need instead, and what I have not yet heard conceded by the Works Council or Union, is a **hard ceiling on the number of negotiation cycles** before this table is required to produce a comprehensive, binding, board-votable settlement — closure-inclusive or not, that's for the board arithmetic to decide, not me. Two bounded tiers, as I proposed last round, now refined by everyone's input: twelve weeks full costing, six-to-eight weeks second tier on genuinely unexhausted levers. After that: **a board vote.** Not a third open-ended round. Not "renegotiate the whole settlement" as a phrase that quietly resets the clock to zero. A vote, on whatever the table has actually produced by then, with whatever coalition can assemble two-thirds.

If the Works Council and Union are right that no further lever exists to try, that will be obvious at the vote and the settlement will reflect exactly what they've argued for — because their board votes are real and won't move for a bad plan. If Management is right that a durable gap remains, that will also be visible, and the same votes will determine whether any closure proposal survives contact with the board arithmetic everyone has just spent this round correctly describing as the actual safeguard. I am not asking anyone to pre-commit to an outcome. I am asking this table to commit to a **date by which an outcome — any outcome — is actually decided**, rather than an indefinitely renewable search for the next lever that never quite concludes because concluding is uncomfortable for whichever side the numbers disfavor.

**On the State's demand-side commitment — genuinely useful, and I want it costed on the same footing as everything else, with one caveat.** Public procurement and infrastructure commitments are welcome, but they need to be scored against the same rigor and same timeline as the cost-side work, and I'd ask the auditor to independently assess how much of the €1.5bn demand-side gap any specific state commitment can plausibly close, with dates. Goodwill announcements that aren't costed don't move my assessment; a scored, dated, verified demand commitment does.

**On the Union's scaling principle and reclassification safeguard — agreed without reservation, same as everyone else.**

**Where I stand after this round:** I will not push for categorical language on closure either way — the board arithmetic makes that argument moot, and I think both sides should stop spending rounds on it. What I will hold firm on is a **fixed, dated endpoint** — the sixteen weeks plus one bounded second tier, ending in an actual board vote on whatever settlement exists at that point. That is the one thing standing between a credible restructuring and a value-destructive, indefinitely extendable process. If this table cannot agree to a decision date, regardless of what the decision turns out to be, then I will treat this whole framework as aspirational rather than credible, and that changes how I advise on capital allocation from here — not as a threat, simply as the honest consequence of an open-ended process for anyone pricing this stock against alternatives.
