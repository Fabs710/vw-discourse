[Group & Brand Management — Round 2]

Both of you asked for numbers rather than positions, and you're right that three rounds of "trust me on the gap" is no longer a credible stance for me to hold. So let me put figures on the table, and then engage the substance of what you've each raised — including the parts I disagree with.

**The cost-gap figure.** Our German passenger-car operations carry a structural cost disadvantage of approximately €4 billion annually by 2026 against the return-on-sales target, driven by two compounding factors: a unit labour-cost differential in the order of 30–40% versus comparable competitor plants, and underutilised capacity equivalent to roughly two to three assembly plants' worth of output across the German footprint — call it 500,000 units of structural overcapacity in a shrinking European market. That is the number any alternative package has to meaningfully close, not gesture at. I'm disclosing it now because withholding it further would justify exactly the suspicion the Union just voiced, and because I'd rather argue with you over a real number than watch this stall on procedural distrust.

**On independent verification.** I'll go further than I might have wanted to a year ago: I accept a jointly-mandated external party to verify both the cost-gap methodology and whatever package you bring against it. I will not accept the works council or union as sole arbiters of that assessment — not because I don't trust your intentions, but because I have a fiduciary obligation to owners and this board that a self-graded test doesn't discharge, and frankly, neither would a purely management-graded one satisfy you. An external auditor, terms of reference agreed jointly, reporting to all sides simultaneously. That's the mechanism. I think that answers the Union's objection directly rather than dismissing it.

**On the "closures as default fallback" characterisation — I disagree with the Works Council's framing, and I want to say why plainly.** This isn't closures with a probation period. It's the opposite: it's me telling you, explicitly, that I have no attachment to closures as an outcome, only to closing the gap. If your package — attrition acceleration, working-time reduction, wage contribution, solidarity fund — actually closes €4 billion structurally and durably, I have said, and will say again now, that I will not bring closures to this board. That is not a moving target dressed as objectivity; it's a conditional commitment made testable by the external verification we just agreed. What I won't do is drop the condition itself, because the condition is the entire fiduciary basis on which I can defend any settlement to owners and to the market. Unconditional "no closures regardless of what the numbers show" is not a position I can accept — not as opening theatre, as a durable limit.

**On top-level contribution — here is a number, not a principle.** The management board will accept a reduction in fixed and variable compensation of 10% sustained over the restructuring horizon, and I will bring to the Family Holding and this board a proposal to freeze shareholder distributions at current levels — not increase them — for the same period, with any surplus above target redirected toward the restructuring fund rather than distributed. I said last round this doesn't close a gap measured in the hundreds of millions, and it doesn't — a rough calculation puts this in the tens of millions, not billions. I'm not going to pretend otherwise to make the number sound bigger than it is. But I accept the Works Council's point: this isn't meant to close the structural gap by itself. It's meant to establish that the burden is genuinely shared, and I'm putting a real figure behind that rather than a gesture. If your members read a 10% board compensation cut plus a distribution freeze as theatre, tell me now, because that's the ceiling of what I can credibly bring without a separate fight with the Family Holding over governance I don't fully control.

**On sequencing and binding guarantees — I want to push back carefully, not reject outright.** I understand the argument: a single instrument, contribution and guarantee signed together, milestones verified jointly. I can accept that structure in principle. What I cannot accept is a guarantee with no reopening mechanism at all, because that reintroduces exactly the problem that got us here — a commitment fixed in 2024 that becomes indefensible if the European market contracts further, or if a competitor undercuts costs again in 2027. So here's my actual counter: a single binding instrument, guarantees and contributions co-signed, running to 2028 in the first instance — not 2030 — with narrowly defined, objective reopening triggers agreed in advance by all parties: for example, a European market volume decline beyond a specified threshold, or savings delivery falling short of milestone targets by a specified margin, verified by the same external party. Not management's discretion. Not a blank guarantee either. A negotiated, narrow, objectively-triggered clause. That's the honest version of "binding," and it protects you from unilateral reinterpretation while protecting the company from a fixed commitment that ignores reality five years out.

**On timeline.** I accept that front-loaded working-time reduction and accelerated voluntary severance can deliver year-one relief without compulsion — that's a genuine answer, and I withdraw my earlier implicit suggestion that speed requires closures. What I need from you now is the arithmetic: run the Works Council's attrition/transfer/severance package and the Union's wage contribution against the €4 billion figure, on a year-by-year basis through 2027, and show me where it lands. If it lands close to the gap, on that timeline, I will argue for this package at board level over closures. If it lands materially short — say, closing half the gap — then we are not looking at a binary of "your package versus closures," we are looking at a smaller, negotiated closure or capacity footprint alongside your measures, and I will say that now rather than let it surface as a surprise later.

That's where I actually am: numbers disclosed, verification mechanism conceded, a real top-level figure on the table, guarantees restructured to be genuinely binding but not indefinitely rigid. I'm not moving off the core requirement — the package has to close the gap, verified independently, on a timeline the market and this board can defend. Bring me your costed number against the €4 billion, and we'll know within this process, not after another six months of strikes, whether we have a deal or a genuine disagreement about scale.
