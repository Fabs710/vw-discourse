Colleague on the Works Council has said much of what needs saying, and the union stands with the workforce on the fundamentals: no plant closures, no compulsory redundancies. I want to be precise about why, and about what we bring to this table beyond a veto.

Our position is not obstruction for its own sake. We have already put a counter-proposal on the table — one that offers real movement: a negotiated labour-cost contribution, capacity reduction through attrition and time-limited working-time models, and openness to a solidarity fund financed in part by employees. That offer is genuine, it is substantial, and it stands. We led warning strikes not because we reject dialogue, but because the peace obligation has ended, our members are angry, and management needs to understand that this offer has a shelf life and is not the opening bid in a war of attrition.

Here is where I differ, in emphasis, from a pure veto posture: I am not simply saying no and waiting. I am naming the price of yes. If management wants a durable settlement rather than a permanent state of confrontation and legal deadlock at the supervisory board, here is what has to be true:

First, any labour-cost contribution from our side is conditional, not unconditional. It buys something specific: binding site guarantees and no compulsory redundancies through a defined horizon, not a vague promise revisited at the next earnings dip. We are not selling goodwill for goodwill's sake.

Second, shared sacrifice has to be real, not rhetorical. Before we finalise any wage concession, I want to see what management board compensation, dividend policy, and shareholder distributions are contributing. If the answer is "nothing changes at the top while the shop floor absorbs the entire adjustment," that is not a shared-sacrifice package, it is a wage cut with better branding, and our members will read it exactly that way. I am not asking for symbolic gestures — I am asking for a number, in the same restructuring package, that shows the burden is distributed.

Third, on timing: this cannot be an open-ended negotiation while warning strikes and counter-strikes escalate indefinitely. We are prepared to intensify — full strikes are on the table if management treats our counter-proposal as a negotiating tactic to be waited out rather than a serious offer. But we are also prepared to move fast toward a settlement if management moves seriously toward us. I would rather close this in weeks with a durable agreement than win a symbolic victory in a war of attrition that damages the company we and our members depend on.

So, to be concrete: closures are off the table for us, full stop, for the reasons the works council gave. Within that constraint, we will negotiate hard and constructively on capacity, working time, and a wage contribution — but only against binding guarantees running to 2030, and only as part of a package where owners and management visibly contribute too. Absent that, don't expect the two-thirds majority, and don't expect labour peace.
