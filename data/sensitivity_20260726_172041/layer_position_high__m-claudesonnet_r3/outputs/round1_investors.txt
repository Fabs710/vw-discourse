I'll speak plainly, because that's the only thing that gets us anywhere useful in a negotiation this consequential.

We have watched this cost gap widen for several quarters now, and it shows up exactly where it should: in the share price, in analyst notes, in the widening valuation discount against peers who have already taken the harder decisions. So let me be clear about what we actually want, because it is narrower than what most of you have been arguing about. We do not have a strong preference between closures and an equivalent alternative package. We have a strong preference for closing the structural cost gap, credibly, on a timeline that shows up in results within a small number of years, not a decade of deferred adjustment.

On that basis, here is where I land on what's been said.

Management's test — a fully costed alternative that closes the gap on a comparable timeline, or closures if it doesn't — is the right framework, and I support it explicitly. I want to be equally direct about why: the works council's own admission that "we've used these instruments before, in past downturns" is not the reassurance it was offered as. It is precisely the pattern that has produced a company sitting below its return target while competitors do not. Attrition and voluntary programmes are slow and partial by design. If that is the entirety of the counter-proposal, it does not meet our bar, and we will say so to the market, not just at this table.

The family holding and the state are also right that a "win" for management that cannot clear the two-thirds board threshold is not a win — it is a value-destroying stalemate. I have no appetite for prolonged strikes, litigation, and governance deadlock. That destroys value faster than the cost gap itself, and it destroys it in a way that is far harder to reverse: customers defect, capital gets allocated elsewhere in the group, credit spreads widen. So I am not asking anyone to "win" this confrontation. I am asking for a credible, board-passable settlement that actually restores competitive margins — whichever mechanism achieves that.

On shared sacrifice: I won't pretend to be enthusiastic about this, but I'll be honest rather than defensive — a visible contribution from management compensation and dividend policy is a reasonable and probably necessary precondition for getting labour peace and a two-thirds majority. What I will not accept is a shared-sacrifice framing used to dilute the scale of the actual cost measure. A token executive pay cut paired with an inadequate capacity reduction is not a package I will treat as durable, regardless of how it's presented politically. Show me the numbers, not the symbolism.

So, concretely, what would move us: a costed plan — whichever combination of capacity cuts, working-time reduction, wage contribution, and closures — that a credible third party (or clearly disclosed management modelling) shows closes the structural margin gap within, say, two to three years, with enforceable milestones rather than open-ended promises. Guarantees to 2030 are fine in principle, but only if tied to delivered savings, as management said — unconditional guarantees decoupled from performance would tell us this is deferral, not resolution, and we would treat it as a negative signal for capital allocation.

What we will not support: a settlement that trades a strike for a permanently impaired cost base. If the eventual package leaves return-on-sales structurally below target with no credible path to closing it, that is worse for us than a difficult few months of industrial action followed by a real fix. We are patient about mechanism, not about outcome.
