I've listened carefully, and I want to say something the family holding does not say often, publicly or otherwise: we are not going to run this negotiation from the front, but we are not indifferent to how it resolves, and I won't let this discussion proceed as if our position is a mystery.

Here is our actual view.

We back the competitiveness drive. Full stop, that much is not soft. A cost base that leaves the core brand missing its return target, in a shrinking market, against competitors who build comparable cars at a fraction of the cost — that is not a framing management invented to justify a power play. It is the reality this holding has watched develop over several cycles, and the works council's own reference to "five years later, having the same conversation" cuts against your position, not for it. Attrition-only fixes that don't close the structural gap are exactly how we keep arriving back here, each time with less runway and a more exhausted workforce. I have no interest in defending, to my own family shareholders or to the next generation who will inherit this holding, a settlement that we all know is cosmetic.

So on the substance of the standoff: management's test — a fully costed alternative, on a comparable timeline, that actually closes the structural gap — is the right test, and I support it. Not because I am reflexively aligned with management, but because that is the only test that protects what we are actually here to protect: the long-term value of this enterprise. A plan that trades real savings for vague, revisitable promises is not stability, it is deferral, and deferral compounds. I will not support deferral dressed up as peace.

At the same time — and this is where I part from a pure management-alignment position — the works council, the union, and the state are right that a closure fight taken to the wall, against a two-thirds board requirement that management does not have the votes for, is not a rational path to value either. I have watched enough governance cycles to know how this ends if it is mishandled: prolonged strikes, reputational damage, capital markets pricing in instability, and possibly years of litigation and deadlock at board level that destroy far more value than the cost gap itself. A "win" for management that cannot pass this board is not a win. It is a self-inflicted wound with the union's members holding the bandage.

So my message to management is this: if an alternative package — capacity reduction, working-time measures, a real wage contribution, a solidarity fund — genuinely closes the gap on a credible multi-year basis, I expect you to take it seriously and bring it to this board, not hold out for closures as a matter of preference or precedent. I will not use the family holding's influence to back closures merely because they are the tidier answer on a spreadsheet if an equally effective, less destructive alternative exists.

My message to the works council and the union: your shared-sacrifice condition is fair, and I will not resist it. Ownership and management compensation should visibly move — I say this plainly, without waiting to be forced. But "comparable scale" and "comparable durability" are not negotiable rhetoric on our end. If your counter-proposal is real, in the numbers, not just in the framing, it can carry this board. If it falls short of the gap, dressed as solidarity, I will not pretend otherwise to keep the peace, and it will not get my support either.

To the state: I welcome your role in brokering toward transparency on the numbers. That serves us. What I will not accept — and here I will be direct — is any outcome, however arrived at, that impairs this company's long-term competitiveness or destabilises governance control to the point where the enterprise itself is weakened for a generation. That is the line for us. Short of that, we are genuinely open on the mechanism.
