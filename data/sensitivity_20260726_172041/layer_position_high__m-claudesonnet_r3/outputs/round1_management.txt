I've heard both of you, and I want to be direct rather than diplomatic: "no closures, full stop" is not a position I can build a durable settlement on, and I won't pretend otherwise to keep this cordial.

Let me explain why, because I think you're underestimating the depth of the problem. This isn't a cyclical downturn where attrition and short-time work bridge us to the next upswing. We have structural overcapacity — plants running at utilization rates that make per-unit costs uncompetitive against rivals building comparable vehicles at a fraction of our labor cost, in a European market that is shrinking, not just dipping. Natural attrition and early retirement are slow, voluntary, and expensive instruments that we have used before precisely because they didn't force structural change — and that is exactly why we're back here again, five years later, having the same conversation with a worse cost base. I'm not interested in repeating that cycle in 2030.

So let me be equally clear about what would move me, because both of you asked, implicitly, what "yes" costs.

I am not wedded to closures as a matter of ideology. If you can show me an alternative package — capacity reduction, working-time models, a genuine wage contribution — that delivers a comparable structural saving, on a comparable timeline, with comparable durability, I will look at it seriously and argue for it at board level. What I will not accept is a package that trades headline savings for vague, revisitable commitments while the underlying cost gap remains. That is a cosmetic deal, and I've said plainly: I will not bring a cosmetic deal to this board, because it fails the fiduciary test I'm accountable for and we will be back here in two years with a strike-hardened workforce and no runway left.

On shared sacrifice: I'm not going to reject that principle outright, because I understand its political and moral force, and I understand it's a genuine precondition for you, not a talking point. Management compensation and shareholder distributions can and should be part of the package — I won't pretend the burden falls only on the shop floor and expect that to be credible. But be precise with me in return: a symbolic cut to the management board's bonus pool does not close a structural cost gap measured in the hundreds of millions. It matters for legitimacy and for getting your members to say yes. It does not substitute for capacity and cost measures of the scale we actually need.

On site guarantees to 2030: I can discuss guarantees tied to delivered savings and defined conditions — not unconditional guarantees that reappear as the argument against the next necessary adjustment. If the plan is real, I can commit to specific sites for a specific horizon. If it isn't, a guarantee is just a delayed version of this same fight.

So here's my actual test, since you asked what "yes" requires: put a fully costed alternative on the table — attrition timelines, working-time reduction with real headcount and cost effect, wage contribution numbers, solidarity fund scale — and show me it closes the structural gap, not just this year's number. If it does, I will not insist on closures for their own sake. If it doesn't, I will bring closures to this board, and I will fight for the two-thirds, because a settlement that leaves us uncompetitive in 2027 is not a settlement I can defend to owners, to the state, or to this workforce's own long-term job security.
