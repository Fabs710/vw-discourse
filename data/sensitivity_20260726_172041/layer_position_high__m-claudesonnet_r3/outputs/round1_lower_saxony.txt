Let me set out where the state stands, plainly.

We share the works council's and the union's bottom line: no plant closures in this state, no compulsory redundancies. That is not a rhetorical position — it is the condition under which we use the blocking minority we hold. We did not take two supervisory-board seats and a special-law veto to be a passive shareholder watching sites disappear from the map. If a plan with closures reaches this board, we will not provide the votes for it, and neither will the employee side. Management should do that arithmetic now, not after months of confrontation.

But I want to be equally direct with the works council and the union: my position is not "no, forever, regardless of the numbers." It is "no closures, no forced layoffs, within a settlement that actually restores competitiveness." Those two things have to go together, or the jobs we're protecting today are gone in the next downturn anyway, and worse, taken with less goodwill and less time to manage it well. I have watched this company go through cycles before. Attrition-only fixes that don't touch the underlying cost gap are exactly how we ended up back here five years later, as management rightly points out. I won't defend a package to the public and to this board that I know is cosmetic.

So here is what I need from each side, concretely.

From management: you say you'll look seriously at a comparable alternative package. Prove it. Put the actual cost-gap number on the table, openly, so this isn't a negotiation conducted through press briefings and vague framing. If capacity reduction, working-time measures, and a wage contribution genuinely close that gap on a credible multi-year timeline, you have no principled basis left for closures — you'd be choosing closures over an equally effective alternative for reasons other than competitiveness, and I will not support that. Conversely, if the union and works council proposal falls meaningfully short of the gap, don't expect me to wave it through on solidarity grounds alone — that would be its own kind of bad faith, and it would not survive contact with reality in two years.

From the works council and union: your shared-sacrifice condition is right, and I will personally push it at board level — management compensation, dividend policy, and shareholder distributions all need to visibly move, not as a symbolic gesture but as a real signal that this is a shared adjustment. I will use my board seats to insist on that. But I'd ask you to be equally rigorous on your side: a wage contribution and capacity reduction that is real in scale, not a minimal offer dressed as solidarity. Fair treatment cuts both ways.

On site guarantees to 2030: I support them, tied to a plan that is actually funded and credible — guarantees anchored in a real restructuring, not guarantees as a substitute for one. That protects workers without asking the company, and by extension the region, to sign a blank cheque.

One more thing, addressed to management specifically. You framed closures as a fiduciary necessity. I'd remind you that this company's value to its owners, including this state, is not measured only in next year's return-on-sales figure. It is measured in whether it remains a stable regional and industrial anchor that can attract investment, skilled labour, and political support over decades. A short-term win that fractures co-determination trust and detonates strikes across German sites is not fiduciary prudence — it is a strategic risk you'd be importing into the company's future. I am prepared to back a tough but fair restructuring. I am not prepared to back one that treats site closures as the default answer because it is administratively simpler than negotiating a real alternative.

So: get the numbers on the table, all sides, transparently. I will use this board seat to broker toward a negotiated settlement that saves jobs and sites and genuinely restores competitiveness. I will use the veto against anything less.
