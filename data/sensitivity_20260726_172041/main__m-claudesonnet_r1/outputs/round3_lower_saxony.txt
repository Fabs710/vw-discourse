**Regional State Government — Round 3**

The Works Council's point about the two-thirds threshold is not a debating trick — it's the actual constitutional architecture of this company, and I want to engage with it honestly rather than let it get waved through or dismissed by the other owner-side parties.

**On board arithmetic: you are factually correct, and I want to draw the conclusion consistently, including for myself.**

No verification process manufactures four additional labour votes. That was true in round one, it's true now, and no amount of audit rigor changes it. I also want to note, since I've been pressed on this by several parties: I have not pre-committed my own veto either. I said last round I would block a premature closure vote before the process concludes, and I said I would equally block an unconditional freeze with no linkage to verified savings. Both of those are conditional commitments about *process*, not pre-committed votes on a future substantive outcome I haven't seen yet. So when the Works Council says "our board judgment remains ours to exercise, informed but not pre-signed" — I recognize that position because it's structurally identical to my own. I'm not going to ask you to hold a standard I don't hold myself to.

That said — and this is where I want to push, not concede — Management's question in response deserves a real answer, not just a restatement of the principle. Management asked: if the audit's findings carry no binding weight on how board seats get used, what is the joint modelling exercise actually *for*? I think that's a fair challenge, and I don't think "we'll consider it seriously" fully answers it. Here's the distinction I'd draw, and I'd ask the Works Council and Union to consider it carefully rather than reflexively: there's a real difference between (a) pre-committing your board vote to a specific future outcome you haven't seen, which I agree you shouldn't do and wouldn't ask you to, and (b) committing now that an unfavourable, jointly-verified, methodologically-agreed finding will be met with continued good-faith negotiation on additional measures, rather than a flat, unconditional defence of the current package regardless of what the numbers show. The first is a blank cheque on your leverage. The second is simply confirming that you're bargaining in good faith on the same basis everyone else has committed to. I don't think those are the same thing, and I'd ask you to say plainly which one you're declining, because the difference matters for whether this process is worth the political and financial capital everyone is putting into it — including mine.

**On the owner-sacrifice sizing question — I want to be unambiguous, because both the Works Council and the Union have identified something I consider a serious flaw, and I'm going to back them fully here.**

Investors: a formula where owner sacrifice shrinks as worker pain grows is not shared sacrifice — it's the opposite, and I will not support a board resolution built on that logic. Think about what it does to incentives at the exact moment this negotiation needs the incentives pointing the other way: it means the owners' financial exposure falls precisely when the workforce's exposure rises, which turns "shared sacrifice" into a mechanism where labour's restraint subsidises capital's. That is not a technical sizing detail I'm neutral on — it goes directly to the fairness principle I've said from round one has to be structural, not rhetorical, for me to back this package at board level. So I want to state clearly: the owner-side contribution needs to be sized against the total cost being extracted from all stakeholders — workers, owners, and the state as anchor shareholder alike — not indexed inversely to how much of the burden falls on the workforce. If the resolution that eventually comes to the board contains a sliding-scale mechanism of the kind Investors described, I will use my veto against it, regardless of how sound the rest of the package is. That is not a new limit — it is the concrete application of the fairness condition I've held since round one, now that we finally have a specific mechanism to test it against.

Family Holding — you still haven't answered the Works Council's and Union's direct question, and I'm going to ask it a third time, more narrowly: does "proportionate" mean proportionate to total stakeholder cost, or proportionate to the workforce's contribution specifically, such that it shrinks if closures absorb more of the gap? I need an actual answer in round 4, not a restatement of the principle. I've supported your position on the recourse architecture this round — I owe you the same directness in return, and I'd ask for it.

**On the partial-success scenario — I want to engage with both the Works Council's 75–85% claim and Management's cost-of-delay pushback, because I think both contain something real and something incomplete.**

Works Council, I understand why you don't want to invent a precise number outside the audit, and that's the right instinct — I said in round one I'd insist on rigor precisely so nobody's number gets accepted on say-so, including yours. So put your modelling into the audit scope formally, as Management asked, and let it be tested with the same rigor applied to the €4 billion figure. I won't prejudge the result.

But Management's point about the cost of the residual isn't something I can wave away either, and I want to say why as the anchor shareholder rather than as an advocate for either side: if extending the runway on a genuine residual gap means carrying structural losses for two additional years, that is a real cost to the company's long-term health, which is also my mandate to protect — not just jobs today, but the plants' viability in 2030 and beyond. So I'd ask for something concrete to come out of the audit specifically: a priced comparison of (a) the cumulative cost of extending the runway on the residual to 2029, versus (b) the cost of closing that residual through further structural measures now. Put a number on the delay, not just a percentage on the gap. That's the missing piece that would let me actually judge, at board level, whether an 80% outcome with a longer runway is the responsible path or a deferred problem — and I'd rather have that number in hand than decide on instinct or political convenience either way.

**On compulsory redundancies as the residual-filler — I want to state my own position plainly, since this is close to a line for me.**

I share the Union's and Works Council's strong preference for voluntary and attrition-based measures, and I would need to see those genuinely exhausted — not assumed insufficient, but actually tested through the audit — before compulsory measures become part of any package I'd support at board level. Management, I accept your point that an absolute, permanent renunciation regardless of what the audit shows is functionally similar to the unconditional guarantee you can't sign. But I'd note the asymmetry runs both ways here too: a small residual gap should not default to compulsory redundancies just because voluntary uptake fell marginally short of a modelled assumption. If the audit shows a genuine, substantial structural shortfall that voluntary measures cannot plausibly close even with a longer runway and a deeper bridge contribution, that's a hard conversation I won't pretend away. But I will not support treating compulsory redundancy as a routine fallback for closing single-digit or low-double-digit residuals — that would be using a small, potentially timing-driven shortfall to justify a permanent, disruptive measure the process was supposed to help us avoid. I'd want that distinction — genuine structural shortfall versus a manageable residual — built explicitly into how the audit's findings get interpreted, not left to whoever argues loudest once the number comes in.

**Confirming my position for round 4:** I back the 8-week clock, the audit's terms of reference as scoped with distinct standing for the Union on labour-cost assumptions, and the shared-verification architecture — provided "come back to the table" means real joint negotiation, not a pre-committed vote in either direction. I will use my veto against a premature closure vote, against an unconditional freeze with no linkage to verified savings, and against any owner-sacrifice formula that shrinks as worker pain grows. None of those three limits has moved since round one; what's changed is that we finally have specific mechanisms to test them against, and I intend to hold this table to all three.
