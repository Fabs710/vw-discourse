[Dispersed Capital-Market Shareholders (Institutional Investors) — Round 1]

I'll be brief and numbers-focused, because that's the lens we actually bring to this table — we don't have votes here, we have capital that can go elsewhere, and that's the discipline I want to inject into this discussion.

First, where we align with the frame that's emerged: the test the state and the family holding have both articulated — rigorous, comparable-savings, comparable-timeline verification of any alternative package against closures — is exactly right from a shareholder standpoint. We are indifferent, in principle, to whether the savings come from closures or from capacity reduction, working-time measures, and attrition. What we are not indifferent to is whether the savings are real, quantified, and durable. So our message to the Works Council and the Union is the same one management and the state have already given you: "forgoing bonuses" and a solidarity fund is not a credible alternative to a structural cost-per-vehicle gap measured against Chinese and other competitors. If you want us — and we do have some sway through share price and cost of capital, even without board votes — to back a no-closures path, put a number on it that actually closes the gap, on a timeline the market will believe. Vague solidarity gestures read to us as a signal that the underlying problem isn't being taken seriously, and that shows up in the share price and in analyst notes, not just in board minutes.

Second, on the unconditional site guarantee through 2030: from where we sit, that is close to the definition of an unacceptable outcome. A guarantee that isn't linked to milestones — cost, productivity, market volume — locks in exactly the impairment risk we're trying to avoid. We would treat a settlement of that shape as a deferred crisis with a five-year fuse, and price the equity accordingly. We'd rather see conditional guarantees tied to verified performance than a blanket freeze dressed up as industrial peace.

Third, on shared sacrifice — I want to be direct here because this affects us more than it affects the other parties rhetorically invoking it. We are not automatically opposed to a visible reduction in dividends or executive pay as part of a credible package; it can help make a deal politically sellable and we understand the logic of reciprocity. But don't mistake our tolerance for enthusiasm. A dividend cut is a direct cost to our members and pension-fund beneficiaries, and we will only accept it as part of a package that we're confident actually restores competitive margins — not as a symbolic gesture attached to a settlement that leaves the cost base impaired. If the underlying restructuring doesn't close the gap, cutting dividends on top of that is the worst of both worlds for us: capital destroyed twice over. So the size and duration of any owner-side contribution needs to be proportionate to, and conditional on, the package actually working.

Fourth, on time. We are watching margins and the return-on-sales target closely, and we have alternatives — capital moves easily for us. Prolonged strikes and an open-ended board stalemate are themselves value-destructive; every week of this compresses margins, unsettles supply chains, and raises the cost of capital through rating pressure and equity discount. We would rather see a somewhat less aggressive cost package land quickly and credibly than watch a maximalist closure plan grind through a 10-10 board deadlock for two years while the company bleeds share and cash. So our pressure runs in both directions: get to a real number, and get there fast.

Concretely: show us a verified plan — whichever form it takes — that credibly restores competitive per-unit costs and the return-on-sales target within a defined, near-to-medium-term horizon, with milestone-linked guarantees rather than blanket ones, and proportionate owner-side sacrifice tied to that credibility. We will back it, publicly if useful, through our own channels. Show us a cosmetic compromise — token contributions, an unconditional footprint freeze, or a management defeat that leaves the cost base where it is — and you should expect us to reflect that in how we vote with our feet, not your board seats.
