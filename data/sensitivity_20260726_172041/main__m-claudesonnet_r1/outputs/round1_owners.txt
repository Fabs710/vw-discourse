I've listened to all three positions, and I'll be candid about where the family holding stands, though you should not expect us to conduct this negotiation in public or through this forum — that is what the supervisory board exists for, and we intend to use it.

First, a general observation: there is more convergence in this room than the rhetoric suggests. Management concedes an alternative path is possible if the numbers close the gap. The Works Council and Union concede a real, quantified contribution is on the table. The state has framed the correct test — comparable savings, comparable timeline, rigorously verified rather than asserted. That is the right frame, and I associate the holding with it. We are not interested in closures as a matter of principle, nor are we interested in a settlement that merely postpones the competitiveness problem to the next supervisory board cycle. Both outcomes damage the asset we are long-term stewards of.

To management: I do not dispute your arithmetic on cost and capacity, and the family holding has no appetite for a sentimental attachment to a footprint the market no longer supports. But I would remind you that "I will take this to a board vote and to the public" is not, in practice, a path to a decision — it is a path to a 10-10 deadlock, given the two-thirds threshold for closures, and a public conflict that destroys value for everyone, including the shareholders I represent. A board fight you cannot win is not leverage; it is a self-inflicted wound on enterprise value, and I will not support forcing that vote prematurely, before every alternative has been rigorously tested and shown genuinely insufficient. Bring me a comparison, not an ultimatum.

To the Works Council and Union: your conditions are not unreasonable in structure, but an unconditional, board-ratified site guarantee through 2030 with no linkage to demonstrated cost savings is not something the holding can back either. We have seen what happens when guarantees are made that the underlying economics don't support — it produces exactly the trust-destroying reckoning we are having today, one cycle later and with less room to manoeuvre. If you want durability, guarantees have to be earned by a credible, quantified contribution and tied to real productivity and cost outcomes, reviewed against milestones rather than frozen for six years regardless of what happens to the market.

On shared sacrifice: I will not pretend this is a peripheral point. If the workforce is asked to bear a real, quantified contribution, the owners' side has to be seen to carry a comparable, visible burden — on dividends and on executive compensation. I say this not as a concession extracted from me but because it is the only basis on which any settlement here is durable rather than a temporary truce. I will raise this with the board directly.

Where I actually am: I support pursuing the alternative-path comparison the state has proposed, rigorously and on a tight timeline — not open-ended, because prolonged conflict and strikes are themselves a value-destroying outcome I will not tolerate indefinitely. If that comparison shows a negotiated package of capacity reduction, working-time measures, attrition, and a serious financial contribution genuinely closes the competitiveness gap on a comparable timeline, I will support it over closures, and will back matching owner-side sacrifice to make it credible. If it does not close the gap, I will not support a guarantee that locks in an uncompetitive footprint — that outcome permanently impairs the asset, and protecting that asset is the one thing I will not trade away, regardless of how this negotiation is framed publicly. I would ask everyone in this room to get to that comparison quickly, rather than litigate positions further.
