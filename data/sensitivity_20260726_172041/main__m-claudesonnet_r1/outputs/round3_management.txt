**Group & Brand Management — Round 3**

I want to engage the board-arithmetic point directly, because the Works Council is right about the legal fact and wrong about its implication, and I think that distinction matters enough to spell out carefully.

**On the two-thirds threshold: you're correct about the mechanics, but I don't accept the conclusion you're drawing from them.**

Yes — I cannot force a closure vote through the board without labour consent. I have never claimed otherwise; it's precisely why I've spent three rounds negotiating rather than announcing. But here is what follows from that fact, and it cuts the opposite direction from where you took it: if your board representatives are, in the end, going to exercise independent judgment regardless of what the audit finds — which is what "we will engage seriously but not pre-commit" actually means in practice — then I need to ask plainly what the 8-week joint modelling exercise is actually *for*. I am proposing to open our cost models, our volume assumptions, our benchmark set to external audit and to your own engineers and actuaries. That is a real, costly, reputationally exposed commitment on my side. I am doing it because I believe a rigorous, jointly-verified number should be capable of moving the outcome — not simply informing a decision you've already reserved the right to make on other grounds. If the audit's findings carry no binding weight on how your board seats get used, then the process isn't joint decision-making test — it's a very expensive fact-finding mission with no mechanism attached to its conclusions. I'm not asking you to sign a blank voting instruction today. I am asking for something less than that and more than what you've offered: a commitment that if the audit — jointly scoped, methodology agreed by all sides in advance, your own experts in the room — concludes the alternative package leaves a substantial, structural shortfall, your representatives will negotiate additional measures in good faith on the basis of that finding, rather than defend an unconditional freeze regardless of what the numbers show. That is a meaningfully different ask than "vote for closures in advance," and I think you know that. If you won't give me even that much, then say so plainly, because it changes what I can responsibly ask my own board to invest in this process.

**On the Union's parallel point — the same logic applies to strike restraint, and I'll be equally direct back.** You're right that nobody in this room is signing a blank cheque on their own leverage. Fine. But then don't ask me to treat "we'll hold current action for 8 weeks" as a durable concession while simultaneously reserving full discretion to escalate the moment the audit's findings displease you. Reciprocity has to run both ways: I am putting a testable number on the table, accepting external verification, and committing to bring a concrete owner-sacrifice proposal within the same window. If the response to an unfavourable finding is simply "we return to industrial action, unconstrained," then the process is asymmetric in exactly the way I flagged to the Works Council above.

**On the 75–85% claim — I want to test it, not accept or reject it on your say-so, and I want to be honest about why that number alone doesn't settle the question even if it's accurate.**

I'll take you at your word that your internal modelling suggests something in that range. Put it into the audit scope formally — I want your assumptions on attrition uptake rates, voluntary programme participation, and the working-time reduction's actual output impact tested with the same rigour you're applying to my €4 billion figure. That's not scepticism for its own sake; it's the same standard the Investors and the State have both insisted on for my number, and I'm holding you to it, not exempting you from it.

But here's the substantive point I need on the record regardless of what the audit ultimately shows: **a shortfall isn't just a percentage, it's a timing and cash-flow question, and "extend the runway" is not free.** Every additional year we carry overcapacity costs real money — fixed cost absorption on underutilised lines, continued capital tied up in facilities that should be generating return, and a competitive gap that widens while European and Chinese rivals aren't waiting for our internal negotiation to conclude. If the audit shows your package closes 80% of the gap by 2027 and the remaining 20% only closes by extending to 2029, I need that residual two years priced explicitly — what does the deferred shortfall cost the company in cumulative terms, not just in the year it's finally closed. If that number is manageable, I can work with a longer runway on the residual. If it's not, "80% is a good outcome" isn't good enough, however hard-won it was politically. I'm not conceding the framing that a large majority automatically qualifies as sufficient — it depends entirely on what the remaining fraction actually costs the business while it persists.

**On compulsory redundancies as the residual-gap-filler — Union, I hear your red line, and I need to push back on it as an absolute, not as a starting point for negotiation.**

I have consistently preferred voluntary and attrition-based measures — they're less disruptive and often cheaper to execute. But I cannot accept, in advance, a permanent renunciation of compulsory measures regardless of what the audit shows about the achievable scale of voluntary uptake. If voluntary programmes and attrition genuinely cannot deliver the required headcount reduction within a credible timeframe — and that's an empirical question the audit should test, not something either of us should assume — then ruling out compulsory measures entirely, no matter how small the residual, is functionally identical to the unconditional guarantee I've already said I can't sign. I'm not proposing compulsory redundancy as a default; I'm refusing to pre-commit to zero recourse if voluntary measures fall structurally short. Same principle you're both applying to your own leverage — I'm applying it to mine.

**On owner-sacrifice sizing — I'll weigh in here because it affects whether I can actually deliver a package, even though it's not primarily my call.**

I think the Works Council and Union have identified a real design flaw in a sliding-scale formula that shrinks owner contribution as worker pain increases — that inverts the entire logic of shared sacrifice and I would not want to sell that structure to my own workforce either, because it makes labour's restraint the thing that lets capital off the hook. Family Holding, I'd ask you to respond to that directly rather than let "proportionate" remain undefined — it materially affects whether I can present this as a genuinely shared package rather than one where labour absorbs the downside risk and ownership absorbs none of it.

**On the Union's request for distinct standing on labour-cost assumptions in the audit — granted, without reservation.** That's a reasonable and narrow ask; I'll confirm it explicitly in the terms of reference. Capacity and site assumptions are the Works Council's technical territory; wage, working-time, and headcount-cost assumptions get separate standing for your bargaining economists. I don't see a reason to resist that, and conflating the two would be a mistake on my part, not a concession.

**To close this round:** I've held the line on recourse because a permanent, no-conditions guarantee still isn't something I can sign — that hasn't moved. I've held the line on compulsory measures as a genuine, if last-resort, tool if voluntary measures structurally underdeliver — that hasn't moved either. What I need from the Works Council and Union, concretely, going into round 4: a commitment that an unfavourable, jointly-verified audit finding leads to further good-faith negotiation on additional measures, not a flat defence of the status quo regardless of the number. Without that, I don't think the 8-week process is worth what everyone is being asked to put into it — including me.
