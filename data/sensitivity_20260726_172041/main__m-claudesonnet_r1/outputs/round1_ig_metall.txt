We stand fully behind what the Works Council has just laid out, and I want to be precise about what that alignment means and where our own mandate goes further.

On plant closures and compulsory redundancies: this is not a bargaining chip for us either. Our members have made that unmistakable in the warning strikes already under way, and those strikes will escalate — in scope and in duration — if management treats "closures on the table" as anything other than a withdrawn proposal. I'd ask management directly: do you understand that you are not negotiating with a single blocking minority here, but with a legally recognised bargaining agent whose members set their own tools in motion the moment the peace obligation lapsed? We are not posturing. The mandate to escalate already exists.

Where my role differs from the Works Council's is the wage agreement itself. That framework is not an internal company matter subject to board votes — it's a sectoral and company collective agreement that we negotiate, and management's proposal to unilaterally strip out long-standing guarantees and cut pay is, bluntly, an attempt to solve a competitiveness problem by breaking a contract instead of renegotiating one. If management wants changes to labour costs, they come to the table with us, in good faith, with a time-limited, reciprocal offer — not as a demand backed by the threat of closures as punishment for refusal.

Now, to be fair to the substance of management's underlying concern, because we are not interested in pretending the cost problem doesn't exist: we have already put a counter-proposal on the table that includes forgoing bonuses, a solidarity fund financed by the workforce, and capacity adjustments. That offer is real and it remains open. But it was made on a specific premise — that it buys job and site security, not that it merely delays the same closures by eighteen months while shareholders and executive compensation remain untouched. If we are being asked to bear a labour-cost contribution, the family owners and the management board take a comparable, visible hit — reduced dividends, capped executive pay, deferred bonuses. Shared sacrifice cannot mean workers alone absorb the adjustment while capital is preserved. That is not a moral flourish; it is the actual condition under which our members will ratify anything, and I will not pretend otherwise to smooth this discussion along.

I'd also flag a time dimension the others haven't addressed directly: we are past the peace obligation. Every week this drags out without a credible, reciprocal framework on the table increases the pressure from our membership to escalate rather than wait. That is not a threat for its own sake — it's a description of the actual mechanics of what happens next if this stalls.

So, concretely, our position going into this: no compulsory redundancies, no closures, no unilateral gutting of the wage agreement. In exchange for binding site and job guarantees along the lines the Works Council described, and comparable contributions from owners and management, we will negotiate seriously on capacity reduction, working-time measures, and a real, quantified employee financial contribution. Absent that reciprocity, there is no deal to be had, and we will use every instrument we hold — including further strikes — to make that clear.
