**Institutional Investors — Round 3**

Three things happened this round that matter to how we price this, and I want to take them in order of importance to us, not in the order they were raised.

**First, and most consequential: the board-arithmetic point, and what the Works Council and Union have now explicitly declined to commit to.**

I want to be blunt about how this reads from where we sit, because I think it's being treated as a procedural nuance when it's actually a material risk disclosure. Management asked directly: if the audit's findings carry no binding weight on how board seats get used, what is the 8-week process actually for? The Works Council and Union's answer, as I read it, is: we'll engage seriously, we won't pre-judge, but our board discretion and our strike mandate remain fully ours regardless of what the audit concludes. I understand the logic — nobody signs a blank cheque on their own leverage, and the State and Family Holding made the same point about their own vetoes. Fair enough as a matter of principle.

But here is the asymmetry that matters to us specifically: Management has put a number on the table, opened its models to external audit, and committed to a concrete owner-sacrifice proposal on a fixed clock. The Family Holding has committed to a sizing principle. The State has committed process discipline. Those are all commitments that constrain the capital and management side in verifiable ways. What we have from the labour side, so far, is a commitment to *participate* — not a commitment to accept a specific class of finding as grounds for further concession. That doesn't make the Works Council or Union wrong to hold that position; it makes the process, as currently structured, one where the audit can bind everyone except the party whose consent actually determines whether closures happen. For us, that goes directly to deal certainty, which is one of the few things that reliably moves our support. An 8-week audit that produces a rigorously verified number neither side is bound to act on is not a resolution mechanism — it's an expensive delay with a strike-truce attached, and the truce itself, as the Union has said plainly, expires with the window.

So I'll answer the Family Holding's question the way I'd want the Works Council and Union to answer it: we would treat "we commit to good-faith negotiation on further measures if the audit shows a genuine structural shortfall" as the minimum threshold for treating this process as more than theatre. If that commitment isn't forthcoming in round four, we will read the 8-week window as a de-escalation tactic rather than a resolution mechanism, and we will price the ongoing uncertainty accordingly — which means no re-rating of the stock on the strength of this process alone, regardless of how good the modelling looks on paper.

**Second — on the sizing critique. I want to correct the record, because I think three parties characterised our position less charitably than intended, though I accept responsibility for the ambiguity.**

We did not propose, and do not support, a formula where owner sacrifice shrinks as worker pain grows. That's a fair objection to a poorly specified version of what we said, and I'll take the correction. What we actually care about is that the owner-side number is tied to something *real and audited*, not to whatever figure makes the room comfortable on the day of signing. The Family Holding's answer this round — sized to total value extracted from all stakeholders to close the gap, not indexed inversely to workforce contribution — is compatible with what we actually need, and we'll take that as the operative definition going forward. To be clear about our interest here: we are not pushing for the largest possible dividend cut for its own sake, nor are we indifferent to it — we simply want the number to survive contact with the audited numbers rather than be a political gesture calibrated to close the deal in this room. On that basis, we're aligned with the Works Council, the Union, the State, and the Family Holding on this specific point, and I'd rather state that plainly than manufacture disagreement where there isn't one.

**Third — on the partial-success scenario, since the Works Council pushed back on our round-two framing directly.**

You're right that I shouldn't pre-judge an 80% outcome as equivalent to Management's "a third of the way there" illustrative failure case — those aren't the same thing, and I was making a point about our floor, not pre-scoring your package. Let me be precise about where we actually land: we don't have a fixed percentage threshold. What we care about is whether the *residual* — whatever it turns out to be — leaves the cost base structurally uncompetitive on a timeline that matters. That's exactly why we support the State's and Management's request for a priced comparison: cumulative cost of extending the runway on a genuine residual versus closing it through further structural measures now. If that pricing shows an 80% outcome plus a longer runway is cheaper, and genuinely converges on competitiveness by, say, 2029 rather than drifting indefinitely, we can live with that — a credible multi-year path to the €4bn target, even if not fully closed by 2027, is still a credible path. If the pricing shows the residual compounds into continuing margin erosion with no defined end, that's the scenario we flagged as unacceptable, regardless of how hard-won the 80% was politically. We're not going to pre-commit to a number now any more than anyone else in this room is pre-committing their vote — but we will judge the actual priced outcome on its merits, not on procedural goodwill.

**Where we stand going into round four:** we back the audit, the 8-week clock, the total-stakeholder-cost sizing principle for owner sacrifice, and the priced cost-of-delay analysis on any residual gap. We need clarity — not vague reassurance — from the Works Council and Union on whether an unfavourable, jointly-verified finding leads to further good-faith negotiation or a flat defence of the status quo, because that answer determines whether we treat this process as substantive or as a costly pause before the same argument resumes in eight weeks. Deal certainty is the thing we actually price; right now, it's the one piece still missing from an otherwise credible structure.
