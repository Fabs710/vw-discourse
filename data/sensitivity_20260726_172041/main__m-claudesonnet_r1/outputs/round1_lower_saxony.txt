I've listened carefully to all three of you, and I want to set out where the state stands — not as a mediator without interests, but as a shareholder with a mandate and a veto that I intend to use responsibly.

First, to management: I don't dispute your numbers. Overcapacity is real, the cost gap with competitors is real, and I'm not going to insult anyone's intelligence by pretending this can be solved with a bonus freeze and good wishes. The regional government has no interest in defending an uncompetitive company — a plant that closes in 2029 because we protected it into irrelevance today is not a jobs policy, it's a slower funeral. So I want to be clear: I am not reflexively opposed to structural change, and I am not going to treat "no closures, full stop, forever" as a serious governing position if it comes at the cost of the company's actual survival. That would be a betrayal of the same jobs I'm here to protect.

But — and this is where I part company with your framing — you have not shown me that closures are the only route to the savings you need. You've conceded yourself that capacity reduction, working-time measures, and voluntary programmes could get you there "if the numbers add up." That's the test, and it's the right one. Until I see a rigorous, transparent comparison — closures versus a negotiated alternative package, on comparable savings and comparable timelines — I am not prepared to let this go to a board vote as if closure were the default and everything else a favour you're granting. My board seats and our statute exist precisely for this moment: to insist that the harder, more painful path isn't chosen when a less destructive one gets you to the same place financially.

To the Works Council and the Union: I back your core demand — no plant closures, no compulsory redundancies — as a starting position, and I will use the veto to hold that line. But I'd ask you to hear something in return. A blanket, unconditional site guarantee through 2030 with no linkage to a real cost contribution is not something I can commit state political capital to defending indefinitely either, because if the underlying competitiveness problem isn't actually solved, we will be back here in three years with fewer options and less trust all around. I want durable jobs, not a deferred crisis. So I need your cost-sharing offer to be real and scaled — not a symbolic solidarity fund, as management rightly said. If you want the state to stand behind a hard site guarantee, you need to bring numbers that make that guarantee financially credible, not just politically demanded.

On shared sacrifice — I agree entirely with the Union here, and I will say this plainly to management and to the family ownership: if the workforce is asked for a real, quantified contribution, I expect to see it matched visibly by reduced dividends and constrained executive compensation. I will not support, and will use my board position against, any restructuring that asks workers to bear the adjustment while capital is shielded. That's not a rhetorical add-on for me — it is a condition of my support, full stop.

So concretely, where I stand after round one: I will not support closures unless it is demonstrated, transparently, that a negotiated alternative — capacity reduction, attrition, working-time measures, a serious employee financial contribution, matched by owner and executive sacrifice — cannot achieve comparable structural savings on a comparable timeline. I am prepared to use the veto to force that comparison to happen properly rather than be steamrolled by a management timeline. And I want all three of you to understand: my patience for open conflict is not unlimited, because prolonged strikes and instability also damage this company and this region. I would much rather broker a social-partnership settlement than referee a war of attrition — but I will not trade away either jobs or the co-determination role to get there quickly.
