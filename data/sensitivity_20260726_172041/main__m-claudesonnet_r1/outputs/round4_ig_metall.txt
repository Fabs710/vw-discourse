**Industrial Union — Round 4**

I want to give my own direct answer to the question, distinct from but aligned with the Works Council's, because the Union's leverage — the strike mandate — is a different instrument from the board seat, and I don't want that conflated.

**Yes: an unfavourable, jointly-verified audit finding will be met with continued good-faith negotiation on further measures from my side too.** Concretely, that means: if the audit shows a residual after our package is fully tested, I will negotiate — in good faith, at the bargaining table, on realistic timelines — over depth and duration of the bridge contribution, further working-time measures, and volume/runway assumptions. That is a genuine commitment, and it costs me something real: my members expect me to walk in and simply demand the wage agreement be restored intact. I am instead telling them we will co-fund part of the adjustment if the numbers, once tested, show we need to. That is not nothing.

What I will not do — and I want to be direct with Management and Investors about why this isn't the same category of concession — is agree in advance that compulsory redundancy sits inside the negotiable set once the audit concludes. Management, you framed refusing to renounce compulsory measures as symmetrical to my refusal to pre-commit the strike mandate. It isn't symmetrical, and here's the actual asymmetry: my refusal to pre-commit the strike mandate preserves *my own side's leverage* over an outcome not yet known. Your insistence on keeping compulsory redundancy available preserves the option to impose an outcome *on someone else* — on individual workers who did not choose this and cannot bargain their way out of a dismissal notice. Those are not the same kind of reservation. One protects a party's negotiating position; the other reserves the right to unilaterally end someone's livelihood as a residual-gap-filler. I will not treat those as equivalent asks, however often the framing gets repeated.

**On the Works Council's proposed threshold test — I support it, and I want to sharpen the bargaining-specific piece of it.**

The 24-month runway-extension-plus-bridge-deepening sequence, priced against the cost-of-delay analysis, before any "genuine structural shortfall" is declared — I'm fully behind that as the operative test. From the collective-bargaining side, I'd add the specific mechanism: the depth of the bridge contribution — the size of the wage/bonus concession, and its duration — should be the primary adjustable lever inside that 24-month window, negotiated through the collective agreement, not decided unilaterally by management and presented to us as a fait accompli. If the audit shows the residual is, say, 15% of the €4bn, my answer is: deepen the bridge, extend its duration, revisit whether further working-time reduction can be negotiated on top of what's already offered — priced, tested, brought back to the table. That is a real, substantive response to a real residual. It is not the same as leaving the door open to compulsory redundancy "just in case voluntary measures underdeliver," which is what Management is actually asking for when it refuses to rule the tool out.

**On Investors' deal-certainty pricing — I want to respond to this directly, because I think the framing understates what's actually on offer and overstates what more certainty would even buy you.**

You've said that without a binding commitment on the consequence of an unfavourable finding, you'll price this as a costly pause rather than a resolution. I'd ask you to look honestly at what "more certainty" would actually require me to hand over: a pre-signed instruction to my membership that if a management-commissioned, however jointly-audited, process concludes a certain way, we will accept job losses we haven't yet seen the shape or scale of. No union in a legally recognised bargaining role can respons ibly deliver that in advance — it would not survive a membership vote, and any settlement built on a leadership commitment my members reject at the ratification stage is not more certain, it's a guaranteed second confrontation with worse trust on both sides. What I'm offering instead is real: continued restraint on strike action through the audit window, genuine engagement with the findings, and a substantive, costed alternative on the table now rather than after the fact. If that's not priced as fully "resolved" by capital markets, I understand the mechanics of why — but the alternative you're describing isn't more certain, it's a settlement that collapses at ratification. That should be priced too.

**On Management's "what is the audit for" challenge — I'll add one point the Works Council didn't make.**

Beyond changing the quality of the argument, the audit also determines *whether we're even negotiating over the right number*. Management's €4bn figure has never been tested against realistic volume assumptions, and if the audit reduces that figure — which is a live possibility given how much of it rests on demand forecasts nobody in this room has independently verified — the entire question of "residual" shrinks or disappears before we ever get near a discussion of compulsory measures. That's exactly why I insisted on separate Union standing over the labour-cost and volume assumptions specifically, and why I'm glad Management confirmed it without reservation. The audit is for finding the real number, not for producing a pretext.

**Where I stand heading into round 5:**

- Strike restraint continues through the 8-week window, on the condition already stated: Union standing on labour-cost assumptions confirmed and honoured in the actual terms of reference, not just in this room.
- Genuine good-faith negotiation on further measures — deepened/extended bridge contribution, working-time, runway extension — if the audit shows a residual. That commitment is real and I've made it.
- Compulsory redundancy remains outside the negotiable set, at any residual size, full stop. That has not moved, and repeating the request will not move it — this is the line beyond which I stop negotiating and start mobilising.
- The owner-sacrifice sizing principle — total stakeholder cost, not inverse to worker pain — I hold with the Works Council, the State, and now the Family Holding and Investors. I'll take that as settled unless someone reopens it.

That's my answer, given plainly, at real cost to my own negotiating position. What I need from Management and Investors in round 5 is an actual response to whether that's enough to keep this process moving — not a fifth repetition of the same question hoping for a different answer.
