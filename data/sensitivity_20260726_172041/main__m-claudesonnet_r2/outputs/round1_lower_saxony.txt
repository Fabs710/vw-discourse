[State Government — Round 1]

Let me set out where the state stands, because I think all three of you need to hear this plainly before positions harden further.

We hold twenty percent under the special statute, two board seats, and a veto over exactly the kind of decision being discussed. I want to be clear that this is not a passive shareholding we intend to sit on quietly while this is fought out between the other three of you. We were elected by the people who live in these plant towns, and our mandate is to protect the regional economic base — jobs, suppliers, communities — while making sure this company remains viable for the long term. Those two things are not, in our view, in tension. A company that guts its German core to satisfy a quarterly return-on-sales target is not a company we consider "saved."

So let me respond to each of you in turn.

To the Works Council and the Union: I am broadly aligned with the substance of what you've laid out — no compulsory redundancies, site guarantees, real co-determination in shaping the plan rather than rubber-stamping it afterward. That reflects the social-partnership model this state has backed for decades, and I will use our board seats and our veto to support it. But I want to be honest with you as well: "no closures, full stop, forever" as an unconditional principle is not something I will defend as a matter of ideology if the alternative numbers genuinely don't add up over the long run. My interest is jobs and sites preserved durably — not a symbolic freeze that collapses in three years because the cost base was never actually fixed. If your counter-proposal is real, costed, and closes the gap, I will fight for it at board level against closures. If it's thin, I will tell you so directly, because a deal that isn't durable serves nobody.

To Management: I take seriously that the cost base is a real problem, not a fiction invented to extract concessions. But I want to push back hard on the framing that closures are simply "the largest lever" to be assessed on efficiency grounds alone. Plant closures in this state are not a line item — they are the economic anchor of entire regions, and the political and social cost of that is not something I will treat as a rounding error against a spreadsheet. You asked for a costed alternative on a credible timeline. Fair. But I expect the same rigor applied to your side: show me, with equal transparency, what management compensation and shareholder distributions are contributing to this adjustment before you ask a workforce to accept pay reductions and lost guarantees. If sacrifice is not visibly shared across the family ownership, executive pay, and the workforce, I will not consider this a legitimate restructuring — and I will use our veto to say so at the board.

I'll also say this on process: I do not want to find out about site-specific closure decisions through a press announcement, as apparently happened here. That is precisely the kind of unilateral move that damages trust and forces all sides into public confrontation rather than negotiation. If management wants our support in the board room, we expect to be inside the design process from here forward, not informed after the fact alongside the trade union and works council.

Where I can genuinely move: a negotiated restructuring — capacity reduction via attrition and voluntary measures, working-time adjustments, a bounded and time-limited labour-cost contribution, in exchange for binding site and job guarantees to 2030 and real investment commitments in future products at German plants. That is a deal I would defend at the board and publicly. What will not move me: closures dressed up as inevitable before alternatives have been seriously costed and negotiated, or a settlement that asks the workforce to bear the adjustment while capital and management sacrifice is vague or deferred.

We are not in a rush to force an outcome this week. We can play a longer game here, and we will, because getting this durably right matters more to us than getting it fast. But I want everyone at this table to understand: two-thirds is a high bar, and it will not be cleared over our objection if what's proposed guts jobs in this state without a fair, transparent, and shared plan behind it.
