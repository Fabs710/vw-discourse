[Institutional Investors (Capital Markets) — Round 2]

I'll take the moderator's structure and go point by point, because this round actually produced something we can evaluate rather than just position statements. Let me say clearly what moved us and what didn't, since that's the only signal we're offering.

**What moved us: the joint task force with an independent auditor, and the twelve-week deadline to a board proposal.** Management, this is the first concrete process commitment from any party that addresses our core concern — that "testing the alternative" doesn't become an open-ended negotiation with no terminal date. Six weeks to a costed model, twelve weeks to a board decision, is a timeline we can hold you to and hold the market's patience against. I'll be blunt about what that means practically: we will treat the twelve-week mark as a real deadline in our own assessment, and if it slips without a credible reason, we will treat the slippage itself as a negative signal, independent of what the eventual package contains. Discipline on process is itself part of what we're pricing.

**On the State Government's request to fold in "the full regional cost of closure" into the task force's model — I want to push back on this directly, because I think it risks corrupting the one thing that made this round's progress credible.** I understand the political logic: a closure has real costs beyond the plant gate, and I don't dispute that suppliers, regional tax bases, and communities bear consequences that don't show up on the company's own P&L. But I'd draw a hard line here: those costs are not costs to this company's shareholders, and folding them into the task force's comparison as if they were symmetrical with the structural savings being measured is a way of pre-loading the model to favor the alternative regardless of whether it actually closes the competitiveness gap. If the state wants those externalities weighed, that's a legitimate input to the state's own board vote and to public policy — subsidies, transition funding, regional investment are all instruments available to a government that a private company's cost model is not obligated to internalize. Don't ask the joint task force to produce a number that answers a political question by disguising it as a financial one. Keep the model clean — comparable, audited savings, on comparable timelines — and let each stakeholder weigh the non-financial factors through their own vote. That's the only way this process retains the credibility everyone spent Round 1 asking for.

**On the Family Holding's "mechanism and date, not a number" — I'll take that as a real step, provisionally.** I understand the argument that manufacturing a number under discussion-round pressure is worse than a disciplined process producing a durable one. Fine. But I want to be precise about what we'll be watching for: the mechanism has to be *material* relative to the size of the workforce contribution being asked, not a token gesture with a sunset clause designed to expire quietly once the headlines move on. A dividend restraint and executive-pay reduction that is proportionate — even if smaller in absolute euros than the labour-cost contribution, given the relative size of the pools — is defensible. A restraint that reads as symbolic against a multi-hundred-million-euro cost gap will not satisfy us any more than it will satisfy the Union, and we will say so publicly once the actual figures are disclosed at the twelve-week mark, not before.

**On the sunset clause / recovery trigger being discussed by the Union and accepted in principle by Management — I want to flag a risk here that nobody has addressed yet.** A contribution that scales back once margins recover to target is sound in principle — it makes the package sellable and it's fair. But the mechanics matter enormously to us: what exactly triggers the reversal, over what averaging period, and does it apply only to the labour contribution or symmetrically to the dividend/exec-pay restraint too? If the trigger is loosely specified, this becomes a mechanism that quietly unwinds the cost discipline the moment there's one good quarter, and we're back to the exact pattern I flagged in Round 1 — a settlement that looks durable on signing day and erodes within a few years. I'd ask the joint task force to specify this precisely, not leave it as a general principle to be worked out later.

**On the Union's argument that strike risk should be priced into our own assessment of "quarters versus years."** I accept the logic and I'll say so plainly: a negotiated path that avoids a multi-year industrial conflict is very plausibly faster to margin recovery than a closure path that triggers sustained action across multiple sites, and we are capable of pricing that risk rather than ignoring it. That is precisely why we've said from Round 1 that we're indifferent to method. But don't read that acknowledgment as an invitation to slow the process down under the assumption that any negotiated outcome, however thin, beats a contested one. The risk-adjustment cuts both ways: a negotiated package that doesn't close the gap is not "safer" than a contested closure — it's a deferred version of the same problem, now with less capital and less credibility to fix it the second time. We will judge the twelve-week package on whether it closes the gap, not on whether it avoided a fight.

**On irreversibility — Works Council and Family Holding both raised versions of this, and I want to respond once, directly.** I don't dismiss the point that a closure is irreversible in a way a working-time reduction isn't. But an unresolved structural cost gap is also, functionally, irreversible — it compounds, it erodes the balance sheet's capacity to fund the EV transition every single quarter it persists, and by the time it becomes undeniable to everyone at this table it will have destroyed more capital than an early, decisive fix would have cost. Irreversibility is not an argument that belongs to one side of this comparison. Both paths carry a form of it. That's exactly why the joint costing needs to be rigorous and time-bound rather than qualitative.

**Where we stand, restated with this round's specifics in view:**

We support the twelve-week process as structured by Management and endorsed, with conditions, by the State Government and the Family Holding. Our conditions for continuing to support it, unchanged from Round 1 but now more specific:

1. The joint task force's model must be a clean, comparable, audited comparison of structural savings — not diluted by unquantifiable externalities that predetermine the answer in either direction.
2. The twelve-week deadline holds. Slippage is itself a negative signal we will price.
3. Any shared-sacrifice mechanism on dividend/executive pay must be proportionate and specific by the twelve-week mark, not indefinitely deferred.
4. Any sunset/recovery trigger on the labour contribution must be precisely specified, with a defined trigger and averaging period, not a vague principle.
5. We can live with no compulsory redundancies and no closures, provided the package genuinely closes the structural gap on this timeline. We cannot live with a guarantee — of sites, jobs, or anything else — that exists independent of whether that arithmetic closes. On that point, we are aligned with Management and the Family Holding, and nothing said this round moves us off it.

That's the test. Twelve weeks from now, we're either looking at a credible, costed path back to competitive margins, or we're not. Everything else discussed this round — process, embedding, governance — matters only insofar as it gets us a real answer to that question on schedule.
