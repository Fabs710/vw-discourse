I stand fully behind what the Works Council has just laid out, and I want to add the union's own weight to it — because the Works Council can shape the supervisory-board vote, but I control whether this company runs at all in the meantime.

Let me be direct about where we are. The peace obligation has ended. That is not a technicality — it means the members who authorised warning strikes over the past weeks are asking me a very simple question: do we escalate? And the honest answer right now is: yes, if management continues to treat closures and forced layoffs as a starting assumption rather than a red line that gets management nowhere.

So let me state our position with the same clarity. No compulsory redundancies. No plant closures. Not because we refuse to negotiate — we have already put a counter-proposal on the table that management cannot say it wasn't offered — but because these two outcomes shift the entire cost of a crisis management helped create onto the people least responsible for it and least able to absorb it. A worker who loses a job at fifty, in a single-industry region, does not have "market flexibility" as a fallback. That risk is not being shared. It is being dumped.

Here is what we have already signalled, and I will repeat it on the record: we are prepared to discuss a genuine labour-cost contribution — foregone bonuses, a solidarity fund, working-time adjustments, capacity reductions achieved through attrition and voluntary programmes. That is a real, costed offer, not a rhetorical one. But it is conditional, and I want to be precise about the conditions because I don't think management has yet grasped how conditional it is:

First, shared sacrifice has to be visible and real, not asserted. If the workforce is being asked to give up pay elements, management compensation and shareholder distributions are on the table too, in the same conversation, at the same time. I will not bring my members a package where the factory floor takes a cut and the executive floor and dividend do not. That is not a negotiating tactic — it is a fairness threshold, and if it isn't met I cannot and will not sell any contribution to the membership. It would not survive a ratification vote.

Second, any employee contribution is exchanged for binding security — site guarantees, no compulsory redundancies, and investment commitments that give people a reason to believe there's a company here in 2030. A contribution without a durable guarantee is just a first instalment on more cuts next year. We've seen that pattern before in this industry, and we're not signing up for it blind twice.

Third, timing matters. My members are not going to sit quietly for months while this is litigated in the press as a foregone conclusion. If management wants to de-escalate the strike posture, the fastest way is to visibly move off the closure announcement and into a real negotiation on capacity and cost-sharing. Every week management spends defending closures publicly is a week I spend mobilising, and that mobilisation gets harder to unwind the longer it runs, not easier.

I'll close with what should be obvious but bears saying plainly: I am not dependent on any single outcome here in the way the company is. A company that shuts plants and breaks pledges gets its cars built somewhere else eventually, or gets no labour peace to build them here. We have tariff autonomy, we have the strike weapon, and we have just come out of a peace obligation — meaning our leverage is at its highest point in years. I would rather use it to get a durable, fair settlement than to prove a point. But make no mistake: if the only thing on offer is closures dressed up as inevitability, we escalate, and we escalate hard.
