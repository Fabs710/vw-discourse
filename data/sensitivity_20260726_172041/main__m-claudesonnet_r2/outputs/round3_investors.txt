# Institutional Investors — Round 3

Let me be precise about what advanced our position this round, what remains a genuine gap, and where I want to correct the record on one point where I think my Round 2 statement was read more generously than I intended.

**On the Family Holding's proportionality standard — this is real progress, and I'll say so.** Committing to be judged against the Works Council's formulation — proportional sacrifice measured against each side's own pool — is a falsifiable commitment, which is what I asked for. I want to be precise about what "falsifiable" means in practice: at week twelve, we will do that arithmetic ourselves, independent of the Holding's own characterization of it, and if the dividend restraint and executive-pay reduction land materially below that proportional benchmark, we will say so publicly and we will price it as a governance-credibility problem, not just a labour-relations one. A shareholder base that watches its own controlling owner fail a standard it explicitly accepted does not read that as a minor miss. Holding — you've made the right move by committing to a standard rather than staying vague. Don't let the actual figure undercut the credibility you just built.

**On the State Government's transition-funding proposal — I want to respond carefully, because this cuts closer to my own position than the Works Council's regional-cost argument did, and I don't want to be inconsistent.** I drew a hard line against folding unquantifiable regional externalities into the task force's savings model. The State's proposal this round is different in kind, and I want to acknowledge that difference explicitly: this is not an externality estimate, it's a real, committed euro input — state co-financing and investment incentives attached to specific sites — that can legitimately enter the model as a cost offset, the same way any other third-party financing commitment would. I don't object to that in principle. I have three conditions before I'll treat it as load-bearing in the twelve-week arithmetic, and I'd ask the task force to apply them rigorously:

1. **Durability and legal certainty.** State co-financing carries political risk — a change of government, an EU state-aid challenge, or a budget cycle can unwind a commitment that looked solid on paper. Any euro figure from this source should be discounted for that risk in the model, not counted at full face value alongside the company's own structural savings, which don't carry the same reversal risk.
2. **Milestone-conditionality, as the Holding rightly flagged.** If this funding is tied to delivery milestones on the alternative package rather than paid upfront and unconditionally, that's the right structure — and it should be specified with the same precision as the sunset trigger, not left as a general pledge.
3. **It should reduce the size of the labour and shareholder contribution needed, not simply pad the comparison to make an otherwise-insufficient package look adequate.** If the state funding is doing most of the work of closing the gap while the underlying cost structure remains uncompetitive once that funding lapses, we haven't fixed anything — we've deferred the same problem to whenever the funding cycle ends. I want to see what the package looks like with and without the state contribution, so the durability of the underlying fix is visible, not obscured by a one-time subsidy.

With those conditions, I welcome this — it's a legitimate lever the State is entitled to use, and it materially changes my earlier objection because it's real money, not a re-labelled externality.

**On the wage-bargaining track running in parallel with the task force, and Management's condition on it — I support Management's position here without reservation.** The Union is right that sequencing the wage agreement after the task force wastes time. Management is equally right that the wage agreement's entry into force has to be contingent on the capacity question closing too — otherwise we get exactly the outcome I flagged in Round 1: labour-cost savings banked while the overcapacity that's driving a large share of the structural gap remains unresolved. That is not a durable fix; it's a partial one dressed as complete. I'd add one thing: I want the same contingency applied symmetrically to the shareholder/executive contribution. If the wage agreement's benefits are conditional on capacity resolution, so should the dividend restraint be — nobody's piece of this should lock in independently of whether the whole package actually closes the gap.

**On the board-vote question — I side fully with Management and the Family Holding, and I want to say directly to the Works Council and Union why I think your framing, however understandable, is the wrong ask.** You want the board vote to "follow" a task force finding as a matter of obligation. I understand the anxiety — you've been burned by a unilateral announcement once already, and I don't dismiss that history. But a board that pre-commits to ratify a working group's output regardless of final content is not exercising judgment, it's rubber-stamping in advance, and frankly, if that were the structure on offer, I would trust this whole process less, not more. A genuine, adjudicated, jointly-authored costing exercise that the board then actually evaluates on its merits is worth something specific: credibility with people like me, who are watching whether this company's governance can make a hard decision on the evidence rather than on whoever shouted loudest or threatened longest. Management's and the Holding's formulation — advocate for the negotiated path if the numbers close, and be accountable if they don't advocate despite the numbers closing — is the right balance. I'd add: if the numbers close and the board still reintroduces closures anyway, that is precisely the scenario where I would expect the State Government to exercise its veto, and I would not view that veto as obstruction. It would be the system working as designed.

**On Management's site-by-site breakdown commitment — good, and necessary.** An aggregate figure was never going to be credible to us either; we've seen enough restructuring announcements built on network-level totals that dissolve once you look at individual sites. I want that breakdown, with the same distribution transparency, available to us as observers of this process even though we don't sit in the task force — not the raw negotiating detail, but enough to judge whether the eventual twelve-week package is closing a real, site-specific gap or producing a network-average number that conceals continued weakness at the worst-performing plants.

**On the Works Council's specific volumes and Management's follow-up questions — I want to underline Management's point, not soften it.** Fifteen to eighteen thousand retirement-eligible positions is a real number, but "positions eligible for retirement" and "cost and capacity actually removed" are not the same thing until the task force specifies backfill policy and line consolidation, plant by plant. I'm not disputing the Works Council's good faith — I'm saying this is exactly the kind of input that has to survive audit before it counts as closing anything. The same discipline applies to whatever figure the Family Holding eventually brings. I don't grade generously based on who proposed a number; I grade based on whether the auditor's output shows the gap closed.

**On the sunset/recovery trigger — accepted as specified: two consecutive full fiscal years at the ROS target, audited, symmetric across labour and shareholder contributions.** This is the right level of precision and closes the concern I raised last round. I'd only add: the averaging methodology and the specific ROS measure (brand-level, as referenced in the original scenario) should be locked into the task force mandate document itself, not left as a verbal understanding, so there's no room for dispute about what "recovery" means when the two years arrive.

**Where I stand, unchanged in substance, sharpened in specifics:**

1. Twelve-week deadline holds. Nothing this round changes that, and the State's funding proposal, while welcome, must not become an excuse to extend the timeline "to get the numbers right" — durability discounting can be done within twelve weeks.
2. The task force's core savings model stays clean of unquantifiable externalities. Real, committed third-party financing — like the State's proposal — can enter it, properly discounted and milestone-conditioned. Qualitative regional-impact arguments belong in each stakeholder's own vote, as the State itself has now agreed.
3. The Holding's proportionality standard is accepted in principle; the actual figure will be judged against it publicly and independently by us, not taken on trust.
4. No unconditional site or job guarantee, regardless of process quality. I've heard three rounds of increasingly sophisticated process-building, and it's genuinely improved the credibility of this exercise — but a guarantee that exists independent of whether the audited numbers close the gap remains something we will not support. That has not moved, and nothing said this round gives me reason to move it.
5. Wage-agreement and capacity contingency, and dividend-restraint and capacity contingency, both apply symmetrically — no piece of this locks in ahead of the whole.

Twelve weeks from today, we are either looking at an audited package that closes the structural gap with proportionate shared sacrifice on both sides, or we are not. Everything constructive that happened this round — the standard, the funding proposal, the trigger mechanics, the site-level transparency — matters because it makes that final test more rigorous, not because it changes what the test is.
