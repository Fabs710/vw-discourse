We've watched this opening round closely, and I want to bring the discussion back to the metric that actually determines whether any of this matters to us: the return-on-sales target and the credibility of the path to it. Everyone at this table has staked out a negotiating position. We don't have a negotiating position in that sense — we have a threshold, and I'll state it plainly.

We are indifferent, as a matter of principle, to whether the cost gap is closed through plant closures or through attrition, working-time changes, and a negotiated labour contribution. We are not indifferent to whether it is closed. That is the entire content of our position, and I'd ask everyone here not to mistake our neutrality on method for softness on outcome.

So, responding directly: Works Council and Union, your framing that "no closures, no compulsory redundancies" is the non-negotiable starting point, full stop — we will not endorse that as a governing principle, for the same reason the family holding just declined to ratify it. It's not that we're hostile to preserving sites or jobs. It's that "unconditional" is precisely the word that worries capital markets, because it means the commitment holds regardless of what the numbers say afterward. We've seen this pattern in this sector before: a headline settlement that freezes site guarantees while leaving the structural cost gap intact, followed by margin erosion for several years, followed by the same crisis recurring with less balance-sheet room to fix it. That outcome is the one we consider unacceptable — not because it favors labour over capital in the abstract, but because it is fiscally incoherent. A guarantee to 2030 is worth exactly as much as the cost reduction underneath it. If the underneath is thin, the guarantee is a deferred write-down.

To Management: your position — that you won't pre-concede the closure lever, but you're genuinely willing to test a costed alternative against a comparable timeline — is the only position at this table so far that we find fully credible, because it's outcome-oriented rather than method-committed. That is what we want to see more of. But I'll push you as hard as the others are pushing you: "quantified, audited savings on a credible timeline" needs to arrive in weeks, not become the subject of its own multi-month negotiation. Every quarter this drags without a visible inflection in the cost trajectory, we mark it down as execution risk, not as a negotiation in good faith. That has consequences for the share price and for the cost of capital this company needs for its EV transition — which everyone here claims to care about.

To the State Government and the Family Holding: I'll note with some relief that both of you have declined to treat "no closures forever" as sacred, and have instead conditioned your support on whether the alternative genuinely closes the gap. That is the correct test, and it happens to be ours too. Where I'd push the State specifically: you've asked management to show what shareholder distributions and executive pay contribute to the adjustment before asking labour to concede. Fine — transparency is not something we resist. But don't mistake a symbolic haircut on dividends for a substitute for structural cost reduction. If the eventual package is heavy on visible shared sacrifice and light on durable EBIT-margin recovery, we will treat it as cosmetic, and we will say so publicly through exactly the channels available to us: analyst commentary, capital reallocation, and pressure on the share price. We don't have a board vote, but we have an exit, and unlike the family holding or the state, we will use it without sentiment if this settles into a multi-year war of attrition with no visible margin recovery.

So our conditions for support, stated as plainly as everyone else has stated theirs: show us a credible, costed path back to competitive margins — whatever mix of closures, attrition, working-time changes, and cost contribution gets there — on a timeline the market will tolerate, meaning quarters, not the remainder of the decade. We can live with no compulsory redundancies if the arithmetic still closes. We cannot live with an unconditional site-guarantee regime that pre-empts the answer before the arithmetic is even tested. And we will not treat a prolonged strike, however legitimate the grievance behind it, as anything other than value destruction that we will price accordingly while it runs.
