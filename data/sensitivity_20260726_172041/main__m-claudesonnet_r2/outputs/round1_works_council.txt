Let me be absolutely clear from the outset: plant closures are off the table, and compulsory redundancies are off the table. That is not an opening bid to be traded away over the coming weeks — it is the line the workforce will not accept, and it is the line I am mandated and structurally positioned to enforce.

I want to state the facts of leverage plainly, because they should inform how the rest of this discussion proceeds. No closure or relocation of a German plant passes this supervisory board without a two-thirds majority. Labour holds ten of twenty seats. That means this restructuring does not happen on management's terms alone, no matter how it is framed in the press or to capital markets. I am not saying this to posture. I am saying it because management appears to have opened this process by announcing closures as though they were a foregone conclusion, and that is precisely the kind of unilateral fait accompli that guarantees confrontation rather than resolution. We have already responded with warning strikes across multiple sites, and I will escalate that mobilisation further if this is handled as an ultimatum rather than a negotiation.

Let me also say plainly: trust is already damaged. Long-standing job-security pledges — pledges the workforce built their lives and their communities around — are being treated by management as disposable the moment the return-on-sales target is missed. That is a breach, and it colours everything I hear from the other side of this table.

Now, what I will engage with seriously. I am not blind to the competitive and cost pressures the company faces, and I am not interested in running this company into the ground out of pure obstinacy — a company with no future helps no worker either. I have said before, and I will repeat it: we are prepared to discuss capacity reductions, working-time models, shift changes, and even a genuine, bounded labour-cost contribution — including on pay elements — if, and only if, two conditions are met.

First: binding site and job guarantees running to 2030, covering every German plant, with no compulsory redundancies. Attrition, early retirement, voluntary programmes, redeployment — all negotiable. Forced layoffs and closures are not.

Second: a genuine co-determination role in shaping the restructuring plan itself, not consultation after the fact on a plan management has already decided. If we are being asked to accept sacrifice, the workforce's representatives sit at the table designing the path, not receiving a memo about it.

Outside of those two conditions, there is no deal I can bring back to the workforce, and there is no deal that survives the supervisory board vote. I would rather this be understood now, at the start, than discovered after weeks of posturing: management can pursue closures if it wishes, but it will not get them past this board. The faster we move to discussing real alternatives — capacity, cost-sharing, investment commitments in exchange for guarantees — the faster we get out of a strike posture and into a negotiated solution that keeps this company competitive without breaking the people who built it.
