**On capacity-consolidation specificity — since Management and Investors are right to ask, and I said I'd bring answers, not just volumes.**

Let me be concrete rather than defensive about this. The 15,000–18,000 retirement-eligible positions are concentrated disproportionately at three of the plants carrying the heaviest overcapacity — the same plants that, per Management's own site-by-site breakdown commitment, likely show the worst structural gap. That is not a coincidence; it reflects workforce age profile at older, more established sites. My proposal is that natural attrition at these specific plants is paired with **actual shift consolidation** — moving from three-shift to two-shift patterns where volume no longer justifies three, not simply thinning headcount across an unchanged shift structure. That is precisely the difference between attrition that removes capacity and attrition that just raises everyone else's overtime bill, which Management rightly flagged as the risk. I am not proposing the second version. On the working-time reduction: the 5–8% range should be weighted toward the plants where consolidation is happening, not applied as a flat network-wide number, so it reinforces the capacity reduction rather than sitting alongside it as a separate, weaker lever. I will bring line-level detail to week one of the task force — which lines, which shift patterns, what output consequence — because I agree with Management that "several thousand voluntary exits" is an input, not a model, and I'd rather be the party that turns it into a model than the party accused of withholding the detail that would let it be tested.

**On the board vote — I want to correct something before it hardens into an unexamined shared assumption across this table.**

Management, the Holding, and Investors have now all converged on the same formulation: the task force informs, it doesn't bind, and the real accountability is personal advocacy plus the State's veto. I've listened to that argument made three times this round, more carefully each time, and I still don't accept the framing — not because I'm unmoved by good-faith argument, but because it misdescribes what's actually happening in this room.

I don't need any of you to *promise* the vote will follow the task force. I already control ten of twenty board seats, and closure requires fourteen. That is not a negotiating position I am asking you to grant me — it is the legal structure this company already operates under, the same structure the Family Holding correctly cited when it said the task force doesn't substitute for the vote. Fine. Let's be precise about what that actually means in practice: **if the joint-costed package genuinely closes the gap, I will vote to keep every plant open, and my ten votes plus however many others agree are enough to block any attempt to reintroduce closures regardless of what management "advocates" for or against.** I am not asking Management to bind itself to an outcome it doesn't control. I am telling this table plainly that I already control the outcome that matters to me, and the only open question is whether the process between now and week twelve is honest enough that I don't have to exercise that veto in a way that looks, and functions, exactly like the fait accompli I've already lived through once.

So when Management says "a guaranteed vote outcome before the work is done isn't a process, it's a pre-negotiated result with extra steps" — I'd say: you have it backwards. The pre-negotiated result is already built into this company's governance, by design, specifically so that capital cannot outvote labour on this question. What I am offering, by engaging constructively in the task force rather than simply invoking the veto now and refusing to negotiate at all, is the chance to reach an outcome that doesn't require me to use structural power adversarially. That is generosity on my part, not a concession I owe you. I'd ask everyone at this table to stop describing my position as a demand for a guarantee I haven't earned, and start recognizing it as a description of a veto I already hold, being exercised through patience rather than immediate refusal.

**On the unconditional guarantee question, restated once more because it keeps being treated as unmoved rather than examined.**

I've now heard three different versions of the same argument — from Management, the Holding, and Investors — that an unconditional guarantee "regardless of what the numbers show" is not something any of you can sign, and that a structural gap left unresolved is itself a slower form of irreversibility. I said last round this argument is incomplete, not wrong, and I'll be precise about why it remains incomplete after this round's restatements.

The asymmetry isn't just about speed of harm — it's about who bears the risk of being wrong. If the joint-costed package underperforms and the gap reopens in three years, that is revisitable: we renegotiate, we adjust the working-time measures, we extend the sunset trigger, we come back to this exact table. If a plant closes and the underlying assumption behind that closure turns out to have been wrong — because the market shifted, because a competitor stumbled, because the EV transition timeline moved — there is no renegotiation. The capital is gone, the workforce is dispersed, the supplier relationships are severed, and no future board, however well-intentioned, can undo it. Management's "slow leak sinks a ship" framing is evocative, but a slow leak can be pumped, patched, and monitored. A closure is not a slow leak. It's scuttling the ship because pumping it is inconvenient this quarter. I still haven't heard anyone address that the *cost of being wrong* is wildly asymmetric between the two paths, not just the visibility of the harm.

That said — I recognize I have veto power over closure regardless of whether anyone signs a guarantee document. So what I actually need from this table isn't philosophical agreement on irreversibility. It's a **credible, jointly-costed package that makes the guarantee moot because closure becomes structurally unnecessary** — which is exactly what the task force is for. I'll keep engaging on that basis. But don't mistake my willingness to negotiate specifics for willingness to accept closures if the numbers come in short. If they come in short, we go back to the model, the trigger mechanics, the shareholder contribution, and the state funding — not to closures. That door stays shut regardless of how this round's arguments are dressed up.

**On the State's transition-funding proposal and Investors' three conditions — I support the State fully here, and I want to push back on one part of Investors' framing.**

The durability discount and milestone-conditionality are reasonable — I don't dispute that political risk is real and should be priced. But I want to flag a risk in how that discount gets applied: if the discount rate is set unilaterally by the task force's financial modelers without State input, it can become a backdoor way of shrinking a real commitment until it's convenient to dismiss. I'd ask that the discount methodology itself be jointly agreed, not imposed, given how much weight it will carry in whether this closes the gap without requiring deeper workforce concessions. State Government — I'd like your funding proposal treated with the same rigor everyone's asking of my attrition numbers, but not with more skepticism than Management's own cost projections, which nobody has yet subjected to the same discounting logic. If uncertainty discounts apply to third-party commitments, structural cost assumptions on Management's side should be pressure-tested with equivalent scrutiny — including whether "well above target" cost figures account for one-off transition costs that will not recur.
