**On the board vote — I stand fully with the Works Council's clarification, and I want to add the industrial-relations dimension that makes it more than a governance argument.**

Management, Investors, Holding — you've each now made a version of "the task force informs, it doesn't bind," and each time you've made it sound like a principled defense of governance integrity rather than what it actually is: an acknowledgment that ten board seats already sit with the workforce, and that closure requires fourteen. The Works Council is right that this isn't a demand for something unearned — it's a description of the arithmetic you all already operate under. I'd only add what happens on my side of the table if that arithmetic gets tested adversarially instead of resolved constructively. If this task force does honest work, produces an audited package that closes the gap, and the board — through some combination of shareholder-side maneuvering or a split employee bench — nonetheless reintroduces closures, I will not treat that as a governance dispute to be litigated in board minutes. I will treat it as the moment the peace obligation's absence stops being a background fact and becomes the operative one. That is not a threat deployed for effect; it is what "the union holds the strike weapon" means in practice, and I'd rather you hear it plainly now than discover it in week thirteen. I say this not because I expect it to happen — the process this round has been more honest than I expected after Round 1 — but because Management's repeated formulation ("I can't promise the vote, only my own advocacy") keeps leaving a gap between what management controls and what actually protects jobs. The Works Council's ten votes fill part of that gap through governance. My strike calendar fills the rest of it through leverage. Both are real, and neither should need to be exercised if the twelve-week process is what everyone at this table says it is.

**On capacity-consolidation specificity — the Works Council has now done what Management and Investors asked, and I want to do the equivalent on my side rather than leave my numbers as unaudited inputs.**

Last round I offered a two-year bonus suspension above a protected floor and a solidarity fund topping up reduced-hours income. Here is the specification that should have come with it: the bonus suspension, applied progressively above the floor, generates a company-wide figure in the mid-to-high hundreds of millions over the two-year window — I will bring the exact modeled range, by pay grade, to week one, calculated from our own wage data as I said. The solidarity fund is smaller by design — it is a redistribution mechanism, not a savings mechanism — and its purpose is narrow and specific: it exists so that the Works Council's shift consolidation at the three plants they've now identified doesn't quietly become an unstated pay cut for exactly the workers least able to absorb it. I want to be explicit about why this matters to the capacity-consolidation model Management asked for: if the working-time reduction at those plants isn't income-protected at the lower grades, you will get resistance to the shift consolidation itself, and the capacity relief Management needs will not materialize on the shop floor no matter what the spreadsheet says. The fund is not a cost item competing with the savings target — it is what makes the savings target deliverable without a walkout at the exact plants carrying the heaviest restructuring load. I'd ask the task force to model it that way: not as a subtraction from net savings, but as the price of consolidation actually happening on schedule rather than being fought line by line.

**On the parallel wage-bargaining track and Management's capacity-contingency condition — I accept the condition, but I need the mechanism specified now, not left as a general principle that gets argued over in week eleven.**

Here is what I propose: the collective agreement is negotiated and drafted in parallel, on the timeline I asked for, with a full signed text ready by week twelve. Its entry into force is conditioned on a single, defined trigger — certification by the task force's auditor that the combined package (labour contribution, working-time consolidation, shareholder contribution, state funding, and whatever capacity measures are agreed) closes the structural gap as defined in the mandate. That is a clean, auditable condition, not an open-ended "when capacity is resolved" that could mean anything from twelve weeks to two years. If the auditor certifies the package closes the gap, the agreement enters into force automatically on that date — no further negotiation, no re-opening. If it doesn't certify, we return to the table on the specific shortfall, not to a blank page. I want that written into the task force mandate itself, exactly as Investors asked for the averaging methodology to be. Vague contingency is what turns into "another three months of separate wage negotiations," which I said in Round 3 my members would read as drift. A named trigger, tied to the same auditor and the same certification everyone else is relying on, is not drift — it's the same discipline applied consistently.

**On the Family Holding's proportionality figure — I'm adding my voice to the Works Council's request for a directional signal, and I want to be specific about why silence here carries more weight than it might elsewhere.**

You've accepted a standard I respect as a real concession. But I'd note: every other party at this table has now attached either a number, a range, or a mechanism to their commitment this round — Management with the site-by-site breakdown, the Works Council with line-level detail, the State with a funding structure, even Investors with named discount conditions. The Holding is the only party that has moved from "engaging" to "accepting a standard" without moving from "standard" to "any indication of scale." I'm not asking for the figure — I accept your argument that manufacturing one under pressure is worse than a disciplined process producing a durable one. I'm asking whether, in directional terms, the eventual number is likely to land near the proportional benchmark or well short of it. If you can't answer that even directionally, I'll draw my own conclusion about which it is, and so will my members — and that conclusion will shape how I read "twelve weeks" from here forward.

**On Investors' conditions for the State's transition funding — I support them, with the same caveat the Works Council raised.**

Durability discounting and milestone-conditionality are reasonable disciplines, and I don't object to them being applied to the State's commitment. But discipline has to run in both directions. Management's "high hundreds of millions to low billions" gap figure has not yet been subjected to any equivalent scrutiny — how much of it reflects one-off transition and integration costs from the EV investment cycle that won't recur, versus a genuinely structural, permanent cost disadvantage. I'm not alleging the figure is inflated. I'm saying it hasn't been pressure-tested with the same rigor being applied to a regional government's funding pledge, and if the standard is "real, audited, discounted for risk," that standard has to apply to the baseline everyone is trying to close, not only to the offsets brought by the workforce, the State, and the Holding. I'd ask the task force to apply the same discipline to Management's own numbers in week one, alongside the site-by-site breakdown already promised.

I am holding the current warning-strike posture, not escalating it, on the understanding that next round brings a directional answer from the Holding, a jointly-agreed discount methodology for the State's funding, and equivalent scrutiny of Management's own baseline figure.
