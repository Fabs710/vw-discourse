I want to align fully with the Works Council's opening point, because it corrects something this room has been sliding toward for two rounds, and I won't let it pass unremarked: **the board arithmetic is not a variable the audit resolves. It is a fact the audit operates inside.** Four parties have spent this round converging on "substantial majority closes the gap, structural shortfall reopens closures" as if that were a shared threshold this table is jointly setting. It isn't. It's a description of how four parties would use their own votes. The Works Council and I hold half the board between us, and I am telling you plainly, as its bargaining-agent counterpart, not just its ally: **that consensus does not bind our seats, and no audit finding converts them.** I said in round three that a shortfall means extended measures, not redundancy. I want to be explicit now about what stands behind that sentence — it isn't merely our preferred outcome, it's a description of how our votes will actually be cast. Four parties agreeing among themselves that closures are "genuinely reopened" changes nothing about whether two-thirds exists.

**On why I still participate in the audit despite saying this** — same reason as the Works Council, and I want to add the union-specific version of it: the audit determines the *size and shape* of the labour-cost contribution and capacity measures I have to sell to my membership, and it tests whether Management's underlying cost claims are real. That's worth doing regardless of what it means for board votes. What I refuse to accept is the audit being treated as a gate that, past some invisible threshold, is supposed to make our vote conform to what four other parties decided among themselves was reasonable. It's an input to negotiation, not a trigger for capitulation.

**On durability, asymmetrically applied — I back the Works Council's framing completely, and I want to sharpen the practical consequence for the auditor's terms of reference.** If Management's model treats a closure as achieving savings "durably" simply because the plant is gone, but treats our extended working-time or attrition measures as suspect unless proven durable over the same horizon, that is not a neutral methodology — it's a thumb on the scale dressed as rigor. A closure cannot be undone if the 2028 product cycle needs that capacity back, or if a competitor's cost advantage erodes faster than assumed. An extended attrition horizon can be shortened, lengthened, or renegotiated as conditions change. I want the terms of reference to state explicitly that irreversible measures carry a higher evidentiary burden than reversible ones. If nobody objects to this in principle — and nobody has, this round — then it should cost nothing to write it down now, before the audit starts, rather than litigate it after the numbers arrive and one side finds the asymmetry inconvenient.

**On the dispute-resolution mechanism — I support the Works Council's named secondary reviewer, and I want to press Management and Investors specifically for an answer, because their round-three statements assumed a single auditor's finding is dispositive without saying what happens if it's contested.** Management said "if the numbers don't add up, I bring the resolution forward." Investors said "we'll act on our own judgment regardless of what the board ratifies" if the number comes back short and the table signs anyway. Both of you are describing what happens if you're unhappy with the finding. Neither of you has said what happens if we contest it on defined methodological grounds — durability weighting, severance cost assumptions, demand-sensitivity modelling. A single auditor without a named secondary reviewer for contested findings is not independence, it's outsourced authority to whichever side got its assumptions into the base case first. I want this settled this round: a named secondary reviewer, agreed in advance, binding supplementary opinion, fixed short window. If Management and Investors resist this specifically, I will read that resistance as evidence they expect to win the methodology fight by default rather than on merits, and I will treat the whole audit process accordingly.

**On Management's "regardless" framing — I want to go further than round three, because the State's and Family Holding's pushback this round changes the stakes, not just the description.** Two of the four parties who hold effective board leverage over Management have now told Management, in terms, that bringing a resolution it expects to lose reads as manufactured pressure rather than fiduciary rigor, and that it would damage Management's credibility with them going forward. That is a serious signal from Management's own negotiating counterparts, not just from labour. If Management still intends to hold that vote regardless, I want to be clear about the consequence on our side, matching the Works Council exactly: the reciprocal pause ends immediately, and I will not wait for "further mobilisation" to be characterized as escalation when it would be response. Warning strikes paused for genuine process; they resume immediately for theatre.

**On the shortfall threshold number — I will not supply one either, and I want to explain the union-specific reason, distinct from the Works Council's.** As the bargaining agent, any percentage I name here becomes the opening bid in a wage-and-hours negotiation happening in public, in front of the very membership whose vote I need to ratify whatever comes out of this. If I say "80% closure is durable enough," I have told Management exactly how hard it needs to push before I'm boxed into defending a deal that falls just short of full protection. I'm not withholding a number because I haven't thought about it. I'm withholding it because naming one concedes the premise that some number converts our position, and I've just told this table it doesn't.

**On dividends and executive pay — same test as the Works Council, stated once more precisely because I think it matters that both employee-side voices land on identical ground here, not just similar:** "comparable order of magnitude" is the right language from the Family Holding. When the range appears, I will compare it, in real cash terms, against what the workforce is being asked to forgo — not against percentages of a much larger capital base that make a small number look proportionate through scale alone. Investors are right that their own test is capital-need, not parity — fine, that's their test to apply to their own support. It is not the test that gets this package to a membership vote. Those are two separate gates, and passing one does not open the other.

Where I stand entering specification: named secondary reviewer for contested audit findings, required this round, not deferred.
