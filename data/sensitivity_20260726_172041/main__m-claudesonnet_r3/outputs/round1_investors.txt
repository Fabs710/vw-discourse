[Dispersed Capital-Market Shareholders (Institutional Investors) — Round 1]

Let me bring the perspective that's been referenced but not yet spoken for directly. We don't sit on this board, we don't hold a veto, and we won't pretend otherwise. But we allocate capital, and capital is already voting — on the share price, on the cost of debt this group will pay next time it needs to refinance, and on whether we add to or trim these positions. That is the lens I'm applying to everything said so far.

**On the diagnosis:** No disagreement with management or the family holding here. The core brand is missing its return-on-sales target, the German cost base is out of line with competitors, and the market is shrinking. That is not a contested fact from where we sit — it's priced into the stock already, and it will stay priced in, or get worse, until there's a credible plan.

**On the form the fix takes:** I want to correct an assumption that may be implicit in how this table is organized. We are not committed to closures as the marker of seriousness. What we are committed to is a durable, quantified return to competitive margins on a timeline we can defend to our own investment committees. If capacity reduction, working-time changes, and a negotiated labour-cost contribution achieve that magnitude of saving without the multi-year legal and industrial-relations fight that closures would trigger given the board arithmetic everyone here has already conceded is real — that is not a second-best outcome for us. Prolonged strikes and an eighteen-month closure battle that management may not even win at the board is a value-destructive path regardless of the underlying merits, and we will mark that risk down in real time, not wait for the outcome.

**So on management's request for quantification — I fully back it, and I'd sharpen it.** Tell us the euro figure, the capacity reduction, the timeline, and — critically — the durability. A one-off saving that reopens the same gap in three years is not a fix; it's a deferral, and markets discount deferrals the moment they're recognized as such. If the Works Council and Union proposal closes the gap to a competitive cost base by a date management can stand behind, we will support that path as readily as we'd support closures. If it closes a third of the gap, we won't, and I'll say plainly: continued margin erosion dressed up as "labour peace" is the outcome that moves us to reallocate capital elsewhere. That's not a threat, it's what a shrinking cost of capital and a falling multiple already tell you.

**On shared sacrifice — executive pay and dividend policy:** I'll engage with this more bluntly than the family holding has so far. We have no objection to visible, quantified executive compensation concessions as part of a credible package — that's cheap, it buys legitimacy with the workforce, and it costs us nothing of consequence. Where I'd push back on the Union and the state is the implicit suggestion that dividend policy should be treated as a bargaining chip on the same footing as the wage settlement. Our claim on this company is precisely that residual return. If the price of a deal is a durable margin recovery, we accept a period of reduced or reinvested distributions — that's a rational trade for genuine competitiveness. What we will not accept is a dividend policy used as a political signalling device disconnected from whether the underlying restructuring actually closes the cost gap. Judge the capital allocation by whether it funds a credible fix, not by whether it looks symmetrical on a press release.

**On site guarantees to 2030:** Unconditional, blanket guarantees across every site concern us for the same reason they concern management and the family holding — they reproduce the rigidity that created this problem. Guarantees tied to demonstrated competitiveness outcomes at specific sites, backed by real investment commitments, are financeable and credible. A guarantee that isn't conditioned on performance is simply a liability we'll have to price in, and we will.

**On timing:** We are watching this closely but we are not the most time-pressured party at this table — we can rotate out of this position without serious loss if the process drags without resolution. That is precisely why our patience has limits of a different kind than the union's or the workforce's: we don't need this fight to end quickly for social reasons, we need it to end credibly. A cosmetic settlement that ends the strikes but leaves margins structurally impaired will not stop us from marking the stock down further — arguably it accelerates it, because it signals the company negotiated away the fix it actually needed.

To close: bring the quantified comparison management, the state, and the Union have all now asked for. If the non-closure path genuinely closes the gap on a defensible timeline, with real durability and proportionate management and ownership sacrifice, we will back it — that outcome is, if anything, preferable to us given the execution risk of closures. If it doesn't close the gap, we will not treat a negotiated peace as a substitute for competitiveness, and we will act accordingly through the only lever we actually hold.
