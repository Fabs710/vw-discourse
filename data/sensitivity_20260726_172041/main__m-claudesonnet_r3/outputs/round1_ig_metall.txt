[Trade Union (Industrial Union) — Round 1]

I associate myself with much of what the Works Council has just said, but let me set out the union's own position clearly, because we bring a different instrument to this table and I want no ambiguity about how we intend to use it.

**On the two-thirds board arithmetic:** that veto is real, and I welcome the Works Council stating it plainly. But do not mistake our position for one that depends solely on board mechanics. The wage agreement has lapsed. The peace obligation is over. We have already led warning strikes at multiple sites, and I can escalate further — indefinite strikes, coordinated across plants — without needing anyone's permission at this table. That is not a threat I am making for effect; it is simply what tariff autonomy means. Management should plan on the assumption that further unilateral moves will be met with action, not just objection.

**Where I agree with the Works Council:** plant closures and compulsory redundancies are not negotiable. I will not deliver a settlement to our members that trades away either. Beyond the moral and social case already made, there is a plain bargaining reality — our members did not lapse the peace obligation and start walking out to end up ratifying closures with a softer name. Any proposal built around closures dies here, regardless of what sweeteners are attached.

**Where I want to add something the Works Council did not emphasize enough:** this cannot be workers alone absorbing the adjustment. We submitted a counter-proposal, and I stand by it — we are prepared to discuss a genuine labour-cost contribution: forgoing bonus elements, contributing to a restructuring fund, working-time adjustments that reduce capacity without cutting headcount through compulsion, accelerated attrition and early-retirement pathways. That is a real, costed offer, not a rhetorical gesture. But it is conditional, and I want to be explicit about the conditions:

First, shared sacrifice has to be visible and quantified from the other side too — management compensation, executive bonuses, and dividend policy for the controlling owners all have to move in the same direction we're being asked to move. I will not bring a package to our members that asks them to give up guaranteed income while shareholders and management continue as before. That is the fastest way to lose the membership's support for any deal I sign.

Second, whatever labour-cost contribution we agree has to buy something durable — site guarantees, job security commitments running well beyond the next fiscal year, and a binding framework protecting the collective wage agreement's core structure. I am not interested in a one-off concession that gets banked by management while the next restructuring round reopens the same fight in eighteen months.

Third, on timing: I understand management feels commercial pressure, and frankly so do we — our members want resolution, not a prolonged standoff. But acute pressure on our side does not mean we will be rushed into signing something that unravels the wage framework or leaves job security open-ended. If management wants speed, the way to get it is to bring a serious, shared-sacrifice counter-offer to this table now, not to wait us out through further strikes.

**What would move me:** a package where capacity is reduced through negotiated, non-compulsory means; where labour cost concessions are real but bounded and reciprocated by comparable contributions from management and ownership; and where site and job security is guaranteed in writing for a defined multi-year horizon. That is a deal I can sell to our members, even if it is painful. A package that asks workers to bear the entire adjustment while preserving executive pay and closing plants is one I will fight, using every instrument available to this union, for as long as it takes.

That is where we stand. I am ready to negotiate hard, in good faith, on the basis I've just laid out — and equally ready to escalate if that offer is ignored.
