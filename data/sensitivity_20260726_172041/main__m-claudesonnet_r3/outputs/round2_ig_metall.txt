[Trade Union (Industrial Union) — Round 2]

I want to build on the Works Council's position — we are largely aligned, but I'll add the union's own weight to specific points and sharpen a few things that matter to how our members will read this.

**On the "closures aren't actually live" point:** The Works Council is right, and I want to say it in my own words because it matters for how I calibrate escalation. Management, the family holding, and the investors have all now said, on the record, that they are not committed to closures as such and would accept a non-closure path if it closes the gap. The state has said it will not supply votes for closures. We hold half the board. Add that up and the honest read is: closures are not a realistic outcome of this process, they are a pressure tool being used to shape the size of our concessions. I understand why management deploys it — it's a legitimate negotiating tactic up to a point — but I want management to hear this plainly: it is not moving me. I do not bargain against a threat I can count votes against. What moves me is the actual content of a shared-sacrifice package. So let's stop spending rounds on the closure threat's credibility and spend them on the number.

**On quantification — I will match the Works Council's commitment with my own.** We are prepared to formalize, in writing, a labour-cost contribution package: capped bonus forgoing across defined job grades, a contribution to a restructuring fund, working-time reduction models tied to capacity, and support for attrition-based headcount reduction over a four-to-five year horizon. I will not read out our internal cost modelling into this multiparty session either, for the same reason the Works Council gave — we are not negotiating against ourselves before we've agreed what it's being measured against. But I will commit to a joint costing exercise, with independent verification if necessary, comparing our package against management's closure case on a like-for-like basis — including the externalities the Works Council flagged: severance, legacy pension obligations, litigation risk, reputational cost, and the cost of the industrial action itself. If management wants credibility on "the gap isn't closed," that claim has to survive an audit that includes those costs, not just headcount arithmetic.

**On conditional guarantees — I want to add a bargaining-specific point the Works Council didn't fully spell out.** A guarantee "conditioned on competitiveness" that management alone monitors and interprets is not a guarantee I can bring to a membership vote. Our members have just lived through a decades-old job-security pledge being unilaterally set aside. Asking them to trade real income now for a new promise defined and policed by the same party that broke the last one is not a serious offer — it's a request for trust we have no basis to extend yet. If conditionality is going to exist, I want three things: jointly agreed metrics, agreed in advance and not redefinable unilaterally; a joint monitoring body with co-determination and, if needed, external audit; and a defined remedy — if targets are met, the guarantee is irrevocable for its term, full stop, not subject to re-litigation.

**On sequencing — I agree with simultaneous signing, and I'll go further on the interim period.** Between now and a signed instrument, our warning strikes continue. That's not new. But I want to register something explicitly: if management wants us to move from warning strikes toward a negotiating posture that allows this to be resolved in weeks rather than months, the single fastest de-escalating step available to them is a public statement — board-minuted, not just said at this table — that closures are suspended for the duration of good-faith negotiation on the alternative package. That costs management nothing if they're serious about the non-closure path being genuinely preferable, which three of the five other parties at this table have now said it is. If they decline to say that plainly, I will read it as confirmation that the threat is being kept alive as leverage rather than as a genuine option, and I will calibrate our action accordingly — which may mean broader, longer strikes, not narrower ones.

**On dividend policy — I want to respond directly to the investor position, because I think it deserves a sharper answer than "noted."** You said dividend policy shouldn't be "a political signalling device disconnected from whether the restructuring actually closes the cost gap," and that you'd accept reduced distributions "if the price of a deal is a durable margin recovery." I'll take you at your word on that, because it's actually closer to my position than your framing suggests. I am not asking for dividends to be cut as theater. I am asking for the same test you're applying to our labour-cost contribution to apply to distributions: if the restructuring is funded partly by workers accepting bounded income losses, and the plan is credible and durable, then a comparable, quantified, time-bound reduction in distributions during the restructuring period is not a symbolic gesture — it is the same shared-sacrifice logic you're applying to executive pay, just at a different address. You said executive pay concessions are "cheap" and you'll accept them because they buy legitimacy at no real cost to you. I'd ask you to notice that this argument proves my point rather than undermining it: if it's cheap and buys legitimacy, do it, on both fronts, and don't draw the line exactly at the point where it would cost your side something.

**On the family holding's approach:** I noted that you said you're not going to negotiate dividend policy "in public" and that it's a board matter. I understand the governance point, but I want to be clear that from where I sit, this cannot be a matter that gets settled invisibly after the workforce has already signed away income and job flexibility. If ownership sacrifice is address at the board after the fact, unquantified, that is not shared sacrifice — that is workers moving first and hoping. I will not recommend a package to our members on that basis. I need at least a framework — a range, a mechanism, a timeframe for the board addressing it — visible before final signature, even if the precise figure is settled at board level.

**Where I stand entering round three:** I will produce a costed labour-contribution package for joint, audited comparison against management's closure case. I require closures publicly suspended for the duration of good-faith negotiation, mutually monitored and irrevocable guarantees tied to jointly-defined metrics, and a visible framework — not a vague promise — for executive and ownership contribution before any final package is signed. Absent movement on the closure-suspension point specifically, I will treat continued ambiguity as evidence that management intends to use the threat as leverage indefinitely, and I will escalate accordingly. That is not posturing — it is what tariff autonomy is for, and I will use it if this table will not move past assertion to instrument.
