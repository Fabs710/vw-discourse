[Controlling Family Holding (Shareholder acting through the Supervisory Board) — Round 1]

I'll keep this shorter than the previous contributions, in keeping with how the holding company generally operates here — through the supervisory board rather than through public argument. But since the question has been put to all parties directly, let me be clear about where the family holding stands.

**On the framing that this is "capital versus labour with the state and family as background vetoes":** I understand why it's tempting to describe it that way, but it isn't accurate from where I sit. The holding's interest is not in winning a particular round of this negotiation. It is in the value of this enterprise across decades, not quarters. We have sat through downturns and restructurings before this one and will be here after this one is resolved. That perspective shapes how I'm going to engage, and it means I am less interested in who "wins" this confrontation than in whether the outcome leaves the company competitive and stable five and ten years from now.

**On the substance of the crisis:** I do not dispute management's diagnosis. Margins on the core brand are below target, the cost base is out of line with competitors, and pretending otherwise would be a disservice to the very stewardship function I'm supposed to perform. So on the basic premise — that something structural has to change — I am aligned with management, and I want to say that plainly rather than let it be assumed.

**On method — closures versus alternatives:** Here I am genuinely open, and I want to correct any impression that the family holding is committed to closures as such. We are not. What we are committed to is a durable improvement in competitiveness. If that can be achieved through capacity reduction, working-time measures, and a negotiated labour-cost contribution without the disruption, reputational cost, and — frankly — the multi-year legal and political fight that plant closures would trigger given the board arithmetic everyone has already described, that is not a second-best outcome from our standpoint. It may well be the more prudent one. A closure fight that consumes eighteen months of management attention, provokes indefinite strikes, and still ends in the same two-thirds wall the Works Council has described is not a path I will encourage the board to pursue for its own sake. Value destroyed in the fighting is value destroyed, regardless of who is technically right.

**Where I will be direct about a limit:** I will not support, and will use the board to resist, any outcome that leaves the underlying cost and competitiveness problem unresolved. A settlement that buys labour peace at the price of deferring the same crisis eighteen months from now is not stability — it is a slower-moving version of the value erosion we are trying to avoid. So when management asks the union and works council to quantify their counter-proposal, I consider that a reasonable and necessary test, not a negotiating tactic. I would want to see that modelling myself before the board is asked to bless anything.

**On shared sacrifice from management and ownership:** I won't pretend this doesn't touch the family holding directly, since dividend policy has been raised. I'm not going to negotiate that in public, and I'd ask that it not be treated as something settled by rhetorical pressure at this table — it is a supervisory board matter, and it will be addressed there, seriously, if a credible overall package is on the table. What I will say now is that I recognise the fairness argument the Union and the state government have made. A restructuring that visibly asks only the workforce to bear cost while ownership and management are untouched is not one I regard as durable — not on moral grounds alone, but because it will not hold, and a deal that does not hold is not a solution, it is a delay.

**On site guarantees running to 2030:** I would caution against a blanket, unconditional commitment of that length from either side. Six-year guarantees with no link to competitiveness outcomes are exactly the kind of rigidity that contributed to this cost position. Guarantees tied to demonstrated cost and product competitiveness at specific sites are a different matter, and one I could support the board endorsing.

To close: the family holding wants a negotiated, durable outcome achieved without a prolonged, value-destroying conflict. We are not wedded to closures. We are not going to accept a deal that leaves the competitiveness problem unresolved. And we expect this to be worked through by management, the works council, the union, and the state in good faith, with the board exercising its proper oversight role rather than this becoming a public battle of positions. I would rather this take a few more weeks to get right through the board than be settled quickly in a way that has to be revisited in two years.
