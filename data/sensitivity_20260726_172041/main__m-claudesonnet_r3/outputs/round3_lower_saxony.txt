I want to address several things directly, because this round has sharpened real disagreements that my round-two position needs to engage with precisely, not paper over.

**On Management's fiduciary framing — I want to push back, carefully.** Management has now said twice: if the audited package is "insufficient, not marginally short," a closure resolution goes to the board "regardless" of expected outcome, because losing on the record forces the state and workforce to "own the alternative in public." I understand the institutional logic, but I want to name what's actually being proposed here: using a lost board vote as a political instrument aimed at me and at labour, not as a genuine attempt to close plants. If that's what "bringing it forward regardless" means, I'd rather Management said so plainly than dressed it as fiduciary duty. My veto is not a prop for management's internal accountability theatre. If a resolution comes to the board that both arithmetic and substance say will fail, and its purpose is optics rather than outcome, I will treat that as a bad-faith use of the closure threat — precisely the dynamic the Works Council flagged in round two — and it will cost Management credibility with me for everything that follows, including how generously I read "genuinely exhausted alternatives" going forward.

**On the shortfall question — I find myself between the Works Council/Union position and Management's, and I want to be exact about where I actually sit, not just gesture at a middle.** I will not accept, in advance, an open-ended principle that *any* shortfall, however large, must be resolved through indefinite extension of measures rather than ever considering closures. That would hollow out my own "genuinely exhausted alternatives" test into something meaningless — if extension is infinite, alternatives are never exhausted, and my conditionality becomes a permanent veto in disguise, which I have not signed up to hold. But I equally will not accept Management's implicit reservation that a large shortfall automatically reopens closures as the fallback. My actual test, stated plainly for the first time in concrete terms: if the audited package closes the substantial majority of the gap on a durable, multi-year trajectory — not a headline figure that thins by year three — then a residual shortfall should be closed through deepened working-time measures, extended attrition horizons, or a longer contribution period, and I will not supply votes for closure on that basis. If the audited package falls materially and structurally short — not a modest residual gap but a fundamental failure to address the cost base — then I have to be honest that my "exhausted alternatives" test may in fact be met, and I am not going to pre-commit today to blocking closures regardless of that outcome. I said this in round two and I'll repeat it because it matters: my conditionality is real in both directions, not just as leverage against management.

What I will insist on, though, is that "materially and structurally short" versus "substantial majority closed" is not Management's call alone to make, which is exactly why the independent auditor point matters so much to me.

**On the independent auditor — I fully back the Union's sharpened version, and I want to add my own stake in it.** A jointly appointed, jointly funded auditor, reporting to all six parties simultaneously, with binding terms of reference fixed before either side submits numbers, is the only structure under which I can credibly apply my own threshold test. I am not going to take Management's characterization of "insufficient" at face value, and I am not going to take the Works Council's characterization of "closes most of the gap" at face value either. I need the same independent finding both sides are bound by. I'll use my board seats to insist the terms of reference are agreed this week, inside Management's own proposed sub-deadline, and that the state has visibility into the auditor's brief before it's finalized, not just the output.

**On the numerical trigger for exceptional circumstances — I welcome Management naming actual criteria, finally, and I want it locked down properly.** A "sustained multi-quarter European market decline beyond the base case" and a "verified, materially widening cost-competitiveness gap" are the right categories. I will insist the specific percentages and time horizons are set by the same independent process, not asserted unilaterally by Management inside the audit it's also submitting numbers to. If Management brings figures to the audit process "alongside the closure-case model," fine — but the state gets to review and effectively co-sign those figures before they're treated as agreed, given that my monitoring-body seat depends on trusting this exact mechanism years from now.

**On dividends — I want to be blunt with the Family Holding and Investors both.** The Family Holding has confirmed sequencing — framework before ratification — and I take that as a genuine, not cosmetic, concession; I said in round two I'd treat sequencing failure as a fairness breach serious enough to withhold support, and that specific risk is now addressed. But sequencing without magnitude is only half of what I asked for. Management has now said it will put the magnitude question on the board agenda "explicitly, inside the three weeks." I want to be clear: I will treat the range that emerges from that agenda item as a direct test of whether shared sacrifice is real. If the range is trivial relative to what the workforce is being asked to absorb, I will say so on the record at the board, and it will affect whether I lend my votes to the rest of the package, guarantees and all. This is not a threat for its own sake — it's the plain consequence of what I said fairness requires, and I don't intend to soften it now that it's close to being tested.

**On the pause and joint communication to the workforce — I support the Union's request and will co-sign it.** A joint statement from Management, the state, and the Family Holding to the workforce this week is a reasonable, low-cost step that materially reduces the risk of this process being read as bad faith from outside. I'll put my name to it on the condition the "no shadow closure preparation" commitment is explicit in the text, not implied.

Where I stand entering the audit period: I support the independent auditor structure without reservation and will use my board standing to enforce its neutrality. I have now stated my actual threshold — substantial majority closure of the gap keeps closures off the table in my calculus; a materially structural shortfall does not automatically guarantee my veto, and I won't pretend otherwise to keep everyone comfortable. I require the dividend magnitude, not just the framework, tested within the three weeks, and I will treat a trivial figure as a fairness failure with board consequences. And I will not accept a closure resolution used as a political instrument against my veto rather than a genuine response to the audited numbers — if that's what "bringing it forward regardless" turns out to mean, that changes how I regard Management's good faith for the remainder of this process, not just this vote.
