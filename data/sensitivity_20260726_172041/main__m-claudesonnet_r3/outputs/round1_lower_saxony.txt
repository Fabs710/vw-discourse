Thank you. Let me place the state's position clearly, because I think all three of you have been talking as though this is a two-sided negotiation between capital and labour, with me as a background veto to be counted at the end. That's not accurate, and it's not how I intend to operate here.

**On the arithmetic, since everyone is fond of it:** The Works Council is right that closures need two-thirds. I hold 20% under the special statute, with two board seats, alongside my own veto over major decisions. So even setting aside the Works Council's half of the board, this restructuring does not happen over my objection either. I say this not to escalate the standoff further — we have enough parties asserting blocking power — but so that management stops treating the state as a passive shareholder who will eventually fall in line once labour and management strike a deal. I have an independent basis for judging this proposal, and I will use it.

**Where I agree with Management:** A veto is not a plan, and I don't intend to hide behind one indefinitely. I am not interested in a permanent standoff that leaves the company bleeding competitiveness while everyone points fingers. That serves no one in this state — not the workforce, not the supply chain of small and mid-sized firms that depend on these plants, not the tax base. If the company's underlying cost position is genuinely uncompetitive, pretending otherwise is not a service to the workforce I'm meant to be protecting. I accept the premise that something has to give.

**Where I firmly side with the Works Council and the Union:** Closures and compulsory redundancies are not a starting point I will help management reach. Not because of sentiment, but because I judge — and I think the numbers management itself has produced elsewhere in this industry bear out — that closures destroy regional economic capacity in ways that are not reversible on any timescale that matters to this state. A plant, once closed, does not reopen when the market improves. A workforce, once dispersed, does not reassemble. I am risk-averse about exactly this kind of irreversible action, and I will not lend my votes to it unless I am shown, in detail, that every non-closure alternative has genuinely been exhausted — not asserted, shown.

**On management's request for a quantified counter-proposal:** That is a fair ask, and I'd add my own version of it. I want to see, side by side: the savings delivered by closures, and the savings deliverable by the capacity reductions, working-time measures, and attrition-based headcount reduction the Union and Works Council have outlined. If there is a genuine, material gap between the two — not a rounding difference, but not the gulf management is currently implying either — then that gap is exactly what this table needs to negotiate over, not skip past by declaring closures inevitable. I am not going to accept "it doesn't close the gap" as a bare assertion. Show me the modelling. I have board-level standing to demand that, and I will.

**On management and ownership sacrifice:** I support the Union's point here without qualification. I will not back — and I do not believe I could defend to the public in this state — a restructuring that asks the workforce to give up guaranteed income and job security while executive compensation and the controlling family's dividend expectations continue largely undisturbed. That is not just a fairness argument, though it is that. It is also a practical one: no deal survives contact with the workforce, and no deal survives the next election cycle, if it looks lopsided in that way. If management wants my votes and my political backing, visible, quantified shared sacrifice from executive pay and ownership is a condition, not a nicety.

**On site guarantees:** I understand management's resistance to a blanket six-year guarantee with no conditions attached. I am open to guarantees that are tied to competitiveness commitments — provided the investment and product commitments from management's side are equally binding, not vague promises that can be quietly deprioritized in two years. A guarantee that runs one direction only is not a guarantee, it's a hope.

So here is where I stand at the end of round one: I am willing to help broker a negotiated restructuring — genuinely willing, and I will use my board position to push management toward one rather than simply obstructing. But I will not supply votes for a plan built around closures, and I will not accept a package where labour bears the adjustment alone. Bring me the comparative numbers, bring visible ownership and management sacrifice, and bring a genuine multi-year framework — and you will find me a constructive partner, not an obstacle. Absent that, my veto stands, and I am prepared to use the longer political game to make sure it does.
