[Dispersed Capital-Market Shareholders (Institutional Investors) — Round 2]

Let me respond to the specific challenge put to us by the Union and echoed by the state government, because it deserves a precise answer rather than a repeated assertion.

**On "if it's cheap, do it on both fronts":** I want to push back on this directly, because I think it elides a real distinction rather than exposing hypocrisy. Executive compensation and dividend policy are not the same kind of variable, and treating them as interchangeable "shared sacrifice" line items misunderstands what each one actually does. Executive pay concessions are a legitimacy signal — material to workforce morale and public optics, immaterial to the company's capital structure or its ability to fund the transition. Dividend policy is different in kind: it is priced by every debt investor and equity holder in this stock right now, it affects the cost of capital for the very investment programme this restructuring is supposed to fund, and unlike an executive bonus, cutting it doesn't free up cash for anything — the cash was already available to the business either way. So when I said we'd accept reduced or reinvested distributions as a rational trade for durable margin recovery, that wasn't a concession extracted by table pressure — it was already our position in round one, unprompted. What I resisted, and still resist, is dividend policy being set as a *symbolic match* to the workforce's concessions rather than as a function of what the restructuring actually requires to be funded and credible. The family holding's answer this round — that ownership contribution will be sized against a credible, quantified package, and the framework presented before workforce ratification — is the right structure. I'll take that as confirmation, not as something still owed.

**On the three-week timeline and joint methodology:** I support this without reservation, and I'd add one thing from the capital-markets side: whatever comparison emerges needs to be built to a standard that survives scrutiny from rating agencies and equity analysts, not just this table. If the "durability" claim on either side — closure savings or the labour-contribution package — turns out to be front-loaded and thin in years three through five, the market will find that within a quarter of the numbers becoming public, and it will discount the stock accordingly regardless of how the negotiation concluded internally. I'd ask that the joint audit explicitly model a multi-year path, not a single headline saving figure, because that is the actual test we will apply once this is disclosed.

**On the reciprocal pause versus public suspension of closures:** Management's offer is the correct structure, and I'd encourage the Works Council and Union to take it. I understand the union's instinct that ambiguity preserves leverage, but a concrete, time-boxed, mutually conditioned pause is not ambiguity — it's a defined instrument with a deadline. From where we sit, an open-ended public renunciation of closures, before the alternative is proven out, would itself be a negative signal: it would tell the market that management surrendered its only structural lever before establishing that the substitute actually closes the gap. That is precisely the kind of move that would make us look harder at the credibility of whatever comes out of the three weeks, not less hard.

**On conditional guarantees:** I support the direction this has moved — jointly agreed metrics fixed at signature, a narrow and pre-agreed set of exceptional circumstances triggering renegotiation rather than unilateral override, joint monitoring including the state. That is financeable. A guarantee with a floating, unilaterally redefinable trigger is not something I could treat as durable, and I'd have flagged it as a real problem with the package regardless of who proposed it — the Works Council and Union were right to resist that version, and management's clarification addresses it adequately for our purposes.

**Where I want to be blunt, because this round has generated a lot of process agreement and I don't want that mistaken for the substantive question being settled:** everyone at this table — including us — has now converged on a good process: three weeks, joint methodology, joint audit, sequencing, conditional guarantees. None of that tells me yet whether the actual number closes the gap. Process discipline is necessary but it is not the thing we are being asked to underwrite. In three weeks I expect a modelled, multi-year, audited comparison. If the non-closure package closes the cost gap on a defensible timeline with genuine durability, we will support it over closures without hesitation — execution risk on closures is real and we'd rather avoid it. If it closes a fraction of the gap and this table nonetheless proceeds to ratify it as a "negotiated settlement," we will not treat board sign-off as a substitute for our own judgment. We will mark that outcome down as a deferral, not a fix, and reallocate capital accordingly. That is not a threat aimed at any party here — it is simply what happens next if the number doesn't add up, and I'd rather state it plainly now than have it read as bad faith in three weeks' time.

To close: three-week process — supported. Dividend framework presented before workforce ratification — supported, and I'll hold the family holding to it. Conditional, jointly monitored guarantees — supported. Executive pay and dividend treated as distinct instruments serving different functions, not interchangeable sacrifice tokens — that is our position, not a concession. And the only thing that ultimately moves us is the number in three weeks. Bring it.
