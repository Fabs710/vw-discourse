[Group & Brand Management — Round 1]

Let me respond to both of you directly, because I think it's important we don't spend the next several rounds talking past each other.

**On the board arithmetic:** I'm not disputing it. I know how the co-determination structure works — I don't need reminding that labour holds half the seats and that closures require two-thirds. But I want to be equally direct back: the fact that you can block a closure vote does not make the cost problem disappear. It means the consequence of blocking without offering an equivalent alternative falls on the company's competitiveness, and eventually on the jobs you're trying to protect. A veto is not a plan. If your position is "no closures, full stop, and we'll discuss the rest," you haven't given me a path to the 2030 competitiveness the owners, the capital markets, and frankly the workforce's own long-term job security depend on. So let's be honest about what the veto actually buys you: it buys time, not a solution.

**On the strike threat:** I take it seriously — I'm not naive about what indefinite, coordinated strikes would cost us, in output, in reputation, in customer confidence at a moment when we're already losing ground to competitors who don't carry our cost base. But I'd ask the union to take equally seriously what happens if this drags on for months with no structural outcome: we lose orders we don't get back, competitors who don't have this cost structure gain share we don't recover, and the eventual adjustment required is larger and more painful, not smaller. Escalation is a real risk for both sides. I'm prepared to absorb it if the alternative is a cosmetic deal — I want to be clear about that now, because I don't want anyone at this table thinking I will fold simply because a strike is costly. I will not sign a deal that leaves our cost base uncompetitive just to end a strike faster.

**Where I actually hear something I can work with:** Both of you have put a genuine labour-cost contribution on the table — bonus forgoing, a restructuring fund, working-time changes, attrition and early retirement instead of compulsory redundancy. That is the first constructive element I've heard today, and I want to engage with it seriously rather than dismiss it. If capacity and cost can come down by a comparable magnitude to what closures would deliver, through non-compulsory means, I am genuinely open to that path. I said at the outset I'm flexible on closures versus other structural savings — I meant that. What I am not flexible on is the scale. Half-measures that shave a few percentage points off the cost gap while leaving structural overcapacity in place do not solve the problem; they defer it, and I will not put my name to a deferral dressed up as a settlement.

So here is what I need from you to test whether your counter-proposal is real: quantify it. Tell me the euro figure, the capacity reduction in units and shifts, and the timeline. If it closes the gap to competitive cost levels by a date I can defend to the supervisory board and to investors, we have something to negotiate. If it closes a third of the gap, we don't.

**On shared sacrifice — management and ownership contribution:** I won't pretend this is irrelevant, and I won't stonewall it. Executive compensation and bonus structures can and should move, visibly, as part of any package — I'm prepared to put that on the table as a condition of a negotiated deal, not as a favour. What I will resist is treating this as a substitute for the scale of savings we actually need. The gap between our cost base and target is not closed by trimming management pay — it's a rounding error against the numbers involved. It matters for legitimacy and for what your members will accept, and I accept that. It does not substitute for structural change.

**On site guarantees to 2030:** I understand why you want that in writing before you move an inch. I'm not going to hand out a blanket, unconditional guarantee across every site for six years — that would simply recreate the rigidity that got us into this cost position in the first place. What I can discuss is guarantees tied to sites and product lines that are made competitive through this restructuring — investment commitments in exchange for confirmed cost and capacity outcomes at those sites. That's a negotiation, not a precondition either side gets for free.

So: bring me the numbers behind your counter-proposal. I will bring management and ownership contribution elements to the table in return. But I want to be unambiguous — if what comes back doesn't add up to a durable, structural fix, I will not sign it, and I will take this to the board and to the public as a failure of the workforce side to engage with the actual scale of the crisis, not a failure of management to negotiate in good faith.
