Let me deal with the number first, then the guarantee structure, because both moved today, and I want to be precise about what that movement is actually worth.

**On the 20% figure.** I credit management for finally naming it, and I credit the acknowledgment that withholding it for two rounds was an error rather than a strategy — that's a more honest posture than most of what preceded it. But now that it's on the table, I have to treat it the way I'd treat any number a management team brings to a board I sit on: it needs to be testable, not just stated. "In the order of 20 percent on unit production cost" and "several billion euros annually" are still ranges wide enough to drive very different negotiations through. Before I ask the union or works council to size anything against this figure, I want a breakdown: which plants, which cost categories beyond labour, what portion is genuinely structural versus what portion reflects underutilization that a demand recovery or product allocation decision could address without a single euro of labour-cost reduction. I am not disputing the figure in bad faith — I'm saying a 20% number without a plant-by-plant breakdown is exactly one more level of abstraction removed from something the works council's and union's own economists can actually model. I want that breakdown before round four closes, not as a delay tactic but because my board seat requires me to test management's numbers the same way I'd test anyone else's.

**On the "second lever" — procurement, platform consolidation, non-labour fixed cost.** I want to say clearly: this is the single most useful thing management has added today, and I want it pursued harder, not left as a passing mention. If part of this 20% gap can be closed through measures that touch neither jobs nor wages, that is exactly the kind of shared-sacrifice architecture I've been pushing for since round one — because it means the burden doesn't fall on labour by default just because labour was the first line item discussed. I want a specific ask back from management on this: what magnitude of the gap can plausibly come from procurement and platform consolidation, on what timeline, and what does management need from the board to unlock it. Don't let this remain a rhetorical olive branch to make the labour numbers look smaller by comparison — size it, the same way you were asked to size everything else.

**On the closure guarantee — this is where I need to be exact, because the difference between what management said and what I need is narrower than it looks, but it isn't zero.**

I'll acknowledge real movement: a binding, time-bound commitment through 2030 — no plant closures, no compulsory redundancies, enforceable the same way the labour contribution is enforceable, conditional on jointly-verified milestone delivery through a body with binding factual determination — is substantially more than "no current intention," and substantially more than where management started. I don't want to understate that.

But here is the question nobody has yet answered, and it's the one that decides whether I can actually put board weight behind this: **what happens if the joint body determines a milestone was missed?** Management, you've said the guarantee is "conditional only on milestones being met" — but conditional how? Does a missed milestone automatically reopen closures as a live option, effective immediately? Or does a missed milestone trigger a further joint process — renegotiation of the package, corrective measures, a genuine second attempt — before closures become a real possibility again? Those are two very different guarantees wearing the same words. The first is a deferred closure notice with a jointly-run stopwatch. The second is genuine shared risk. I will not accept the first, and I want that stated as plainly as I'm stating it: if "conditional on delivery" means "one missed interval and closures are back on the table without further process," then the works council and union are right that this is the same trap in new vocabulary, and I will not support it.

My concrete ask: build a graduated response into the milestone mechanism. If targets are missed, the joint body's first obligation is to determine why, and the parties' first obligation is to renegotiate the package — timeline, scale, additional non-labour measures — before closures re-enter consideration as an option. Closures should be the last resort in a documented, joint, good-faith process that has already failed to find an alternative — not the automatic default the moment one review interval comes up short. That is not me trying to make the guarantee unconditional through the back door. It's me insisting that "shared risk" actually describes a shared process when things go wrong, not just a shared process when things go right.

**On the family holding's dividend method.** Management set a clear, testable standard just now: executive compensation reduction, as a percentage of total compensation, no smaller than the percentage reduction in effective labour cost. That is exactly the kind of comparable-basis commitment I asked for in round two. Family Holding — I need the equivalent from you, on the same standard, now. Not the number — I've accepted your sequencing logic on that — but the method. Is the dividend adjustment measured as a percentage of the total dividend, and will that percentage be no smaller than the percentage figure applied to labour and management? If your answer is "we'll decide the method once we see the final figures," I have to say plainly: that is not consistency, it's the same deferral dressed differently, and the union and I will read it as such. Match management's standard, on the same terms, or explain specifically why the comparison doesn't hold for capital the way it holds for labour and executives — but "we'll size it later" without a stated method is no longer an adequate answer at this stage of the negotiation.

**On the joint body's authority — I support management's distinction, with one addition.** I think management's line — binding factual determination on "was the milestone delivered," board authority over "what follows for capital allocation and strategy" — is a fair and workable boundary, and I don't think the works council's or union's formulation actually conflicts with it once the graduated-response mechanism I described above is built in. The binding fact-finding protects against management marking its own homework. The graduated response before closures re-enter consideration protects against a technically "joint" process producing the same outcome as pure discretion, just slower. I think this closes the gap between the works council's arbitration instinct and management's board-authority concern, without creating the paralysis investors are worried about — a renegotiation window measured in a fixed number of weeks is not the multi-year drift investors are rightly wary of.

**Bottom line for round four:** give me the plant-level breakdown behind the 20% figure, size the non-labour lever concretely, commit to a graduated response — renegotiation before re-opened closures, not automatic reversion — and get the family holding to match management's calculation method on dividends. Do that, and I think we have the actual shape of a deal I can defend to my board and to the public that put me in this seat. Leave the missed-milestone consequence undefined, and I will treat the 2030 guarantee as provisional rhetoric, not the binding commitment it's being presented as — and I will not move my board weight on that basis.
