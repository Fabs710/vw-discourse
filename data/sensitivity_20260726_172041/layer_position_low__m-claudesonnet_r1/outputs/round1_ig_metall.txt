The Works Council has set out the co-determination arithmetic and I want to build on it, not repeat it — because the union brings something different to this table: the members outside the boardroom, and the tariff weapon that operates on a completely different clock than any supervisory board vote.

Let me start with where we align, because it's substantial. No plant closures. No compulsory redundancies. Protection of the collective wage framework. These are not negotiating tactics for us either — they are the floor. The works council can block closures structurally, through the two-thirds requirement. We can block them operationally, through the workforce. Warning strikes have already begun since the peace obligation lapsed, and I want management to understand precisely what that means: this is not a symbolic gesture, it is the first stage of an escalation ladder we are fully prepared to climb. If this proceeds toward a closure list dressed up as inevitability, you will see full-scale industrial action, coordinated across sites, and it will not be short.

Now, where I want to add precision to what's already been said. We have already put a concrete counter-proposal on the table — not vague willingness, an actual package: capacity reductions, an employee financial contribution, adjustments to labour costs. That offer stands. It is real. But I need to be explicit about what makes it real and what would make it evaporate.

It is conditional on shared sacrifice. That means: before I ask a single worker to accept a euro less in their pay packet or a single hour of reduced working time, I need to see management and ownership contribution on the table too — executive compensation, dividend policy, capital investment commitments from the family holding. If the answer to this crisis is that workers absorb 100% of the pain while returns to capital continue undisturbed, that is not a restructuring, that is a redistribution, and I will not bring my members into that arrangement quietly. Fairness is not decoration on this deal — it is the precondition for it existing at all.

Second, I want to be direct about something the works council gestured at but I'll state flatly: any proposal that treats co-determination and collective bargaining as a formality to be managed around, rather than a genuine design process, will fail. Not because we're being obstinate, but because a wage and workforce agreement imposed rather than negotiated has no durability — it collapses at the next flashpoint, and we'll be back here in eighteen months with less trust and more anger.

Third — and this is where I differ slightly in emphasis from the works council's framing — I am thinking not only about this year's crisis but about the durability of whatever we agree. A settlement that protects jobs on paper but leaves the company structurally uncompetitive two years from now protects nobody. So I am open, genuinely, to serious capacity reduction, to working-time reform, to a wage-side contribution with real magnitude — provided it comes inside a framework with site guarantees, job security, and proportional pain-sharing from management and owners. That is a fair deal. I will fight hard for it and I will fight hard against anything less.

So to management, when you present your numbers: don't bring me a closure list with a savings requirement attached and call it a negotiation. Bring me the alternative-savings framework the works council is asking for, bring me your own contribution alongside ours, and we can move quickly toward something durable. Refuse that, and you will find out exactly how much leverage this union still has — and it is considerably more than a spreadsheet accounts for.
