I'll respond to the direct challenges, because I was asked a specific question by the state government and the union, and dodging it now would be worse than answering it plainly — even though I'd normally let this play out in the boardroom rather than here.

**On the capital-side number.** I understand why the union and the state government want a figure next to my principle rather than a sentence about openness. Fair pressure. Here is what I can say honestly, and what I won't pretend to say.

Executive compensation: I support a reduction proportional to what the workforce is being asked to absorb, calculated on a comparable basis — total compensation, not just base salary, as management has already framed it — and I expect the supervisory board's remuneration committee to size that formally once the overall package is defined, not before. I am not going to invent a percentage in this forum ahead of that process, for the same reason the union declined to give a euro-precise figure before its bargaining commission finalizes one. What I will commit to now is the principle with teeth: the executive contribution will move in step with the labour contribution's actual magnitude, not settle at a token fraction of it. If the workforce package lands at a mid-single-digit cost reduction over several years, the executive contribution will be visibly proportionate to that, not a rounding error next to it.

Dividend policy: I will not suspend or cut the dividend as a substitute for structural savings — Investors are right about that, and so, in substance, is the union when it says symbolism doesn't fix a cost base. But I accept that dividend policy is a legitimate part of a comprehensive package's optics and its substance, and I'm prepared to have the holding's dividend expectations reviewed as part of the same board resolution that fixes everything else, tied to the same performance horizon. I will not pre-commit a number today divorced from the total package, because doing so before I know what the workforce and management sides actually deliver would be exactly the "symbolic gesture calibrated for optics" I said in round one I'd resist — just aimed at myself instead of at labour. That's not a stall; it's consistency.

**On the standing joint oversight body.** I want to engage with this directly, because I think the state government's proposal is more workable than external arbitration, and better than pure management discretion — but I have a real reservation, and I'll state it rather than let it surface later as an obstruction.

A joint body with works council, union, management, and state representation reviewing delivery at fixed intervals — fine in principle, and probably the right shape. What I cannot support is that body functioning as a parallel decision-making structure that sits above or outside the supervisory board's own authority. The supervisory board already contains labour representation at parity and the state's seats — that is where co-determination lives, structurally, under German law. If we build a second body with its own tie-breaking power, we risk creating exactly the kind of governance ambiguity that erodes accountability rather than strengthening it — nobody will be sure whether the oversight body or the board itself carries final responsibility when it matters. My preference: this joint body reports into and operates under the supervisory board, with its findings binding on how the board applies the review — not a freestanding authority competing with it. That's a governance point, not a trust point, and I'd ask the state government to consider it in that spirit rather than as resistance to accountability.

**On the fallback question the state government raised — whether closures remain a live parallel track.** I'll answer this honestly rather than sidestep it, because it's been asked twice now.

I am not instructing management to keep a closure list alive as leverage, and I don't believe that's what's happening. What I will not do — and here I part ways with the framing that closures are simply off the table as a matter of legal arithmetic — is declare, as the family holding, that closures are permanently and unconditionally removed from consideration regardless of what happens over the coming years. Not because I want them, and not because I doubt the board-vote math the works council and union have both correctly described. I accept that arithmetic; I am not disputing it. But my obligation is to the long-term value of this asset across market cycles, not just this negotiation, and locking the company into an irreversible structural position — whatever the outcome — removes flexibility that may matter five or ten years from now in circumstances none of us can currently predict. That is a stewardship point, not a threat, and I'd ask it not be read as one.

What I can say with confidence: if the package on the table — labour contribution, capacity measures, joint review, proportional capital contribution — genuinely closes the competitiveness gap on a credible timeline, I have no reason, none, to want closures, and I will support site guarantees built on that delivered reality. That has been my position since round one and it hasn't moved.

**Where I still need more before I'd back this at board level:** management's actual target number for the gap — the state government is right to press for that, and I'd like it too, because without it I cannot judge whether the union's package, even scaled up, gets there. I'd rather have that honest number now than discover in eighteen months that the package was sized to what was comfortable rather than what was required. That serves no one, including the workforce.
