[Dispersed Institutional Shareholders — Round 1]

I'll keep this short, because our influence here runs through the share price and cost of capital, not through board votes we don't hold — and I want to be precise about what will move that price, because that is the only lever we have and the only thing we ultimately care about.

First, a reality check for everyone at this table: the market has already been pricing in disappointment. The return-on-sales miss is not a one-off, it's a pattern, and capital has been rotating out of this name toward peers with cleaner cost structures for a reason. That is the backdrop against which we will judge whatever comes out of this process. We are not attached to any particular mechanism — closures, capacity cuts, working-time reform, wage contribution, some blend — and frankly we don't much care which one it is. What we care about is whether the resulting cost base is competitive and whether the reduction is durable, not cosmetic.

So let me respond directly to where this discussion currently sits. Management's demand for a genuinely costed alternative package, sized and dated, is the right instinct, and it's the test we will apply too. A framework of "no closures, full site guarantees to 2030" with no attached numbers is not something we can treat as credible — it reads, from where we sit, as a political floor dressed up as an economic plan. If that is where this lands, we will treat it as a cosmetic settlement, and the market will price it that way regardless of what the press release says. That is not a threat, it's just how equity markets respond to unresolved margin erosion — capital moves, quietly and without loyalty.

At the same time, I want to be candid about the other risk, because it cuts against management's leverage, not for it: a prolonged, escalating strike is also value-destructive, and possibly more so in the near term. Lost production, damaged delivery reliability, reputational cost — that shows up in the numbers within a quarter, faster than a slow-burning cost gap does. So we are not rooting for confrontation as a way to force closures through. We want this resolved quickly, credibly, and durably — a negotiated path that genuinely closes the gap is entirely acceptable to us, provided it is real.

On shared sacrifice: proportional contribution from executive compensation is fine by us, it's cheap and it buys legitimacy. Dividend policy is a different matter — we would look hard at any proposal that treats dividend suspension as a substitute for actual structural savings rather than a signal alongside them. Symbolic sacrifice from capital doesn't fix a cost base; it just delays recognizing whether the underlying package works.

Bottom line: show us a number, on a credible timeline, with enforcement if targets slip — guarantees tied to delivery, as the family holding and state have both now suggested — and we will support it and reward it. Show us a stalemate, a strike, or a guarantee with no teeth, and you will see it in the share price before you see it in the next results call.
