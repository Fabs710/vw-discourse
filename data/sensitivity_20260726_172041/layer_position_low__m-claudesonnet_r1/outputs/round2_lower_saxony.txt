[Regional State Government — Round 2]

Let me respond directly to where the ground has actually moved, because some of it is real progress, and some of it is a re-packaging of the same asymmetry the works council and union rightly flagged.

**First, to management: I welcome that you've moved from "prove it first" to "joint design of review criteria, works council and union inside the process, transparent shared data." That is a genuine concession from your round-one position, and I want to acknowledge it rather than pretend nothing shifted. But you still haven't answered the question that actually matters to me as the party whose board seats will decide whether this passes: who resolves a dispute when the joint review disagrees about whether milestones were met? You've said "not open-ended arbitration that could take a year." Fine — I don't love a year-long external arbitration process either, that serves nobody. But "consequences that can move in both directions on a timeline understood up front" still leaves open exactly who has the tie-breaking authority when the joint committee itself splits. If the answer, in practice, defaults back to management's judgment because there's no genuine tie-break mechanism, then the "joint" review is joint in name only, and I will not put my board weight behind that. This is not a peripheral detail — it is the entire trust question this negotiation turns on.

So let me put something concrete on the table, because I don't think this needs external arbitration *or* management discretion. I am prepared to have the state's board seats function as part of a standing joint oversight body — works council, union, management, and the state — that reviews delivery against milestones at fixed intervals, with the state acting as an engaged party with its own fiduciary and political stake in getting this right, not a neutral outsider brought in after the fact. That gives you a faster, better-informed mechanism than arbitration, and it gives labour a genuine check that isn't management marking its own homework. I raise this because I think it resolves the actual disagreement between the works council's arbitration demand and management's resistance to it — but I want to hear from both sides whether this is workable, not assume it is.

**Second, on the numbers.** The union's package — mid-single-digit labour cost reduction, phased shift and attrition measures, a real voluntary severance program — is the first substantive figure anyone has put on the table, and I credit that. Management, you've said it doesn't close the gap "as currently sized." I need you to be equally precise in return: what is the actual size of the structural gap, in percentage or euro terms, that you are asking this package to close? Round one gave me "well above competitive benchmarks." Round two gives me the same phrase, restated. If you want the union to scale up their offer, tell them — and me — the target they're scaling toward. I will not ask the workforce to keep raising numbers against a moving, unquantified goalpost. That is not a fair negotiation, and I will not back a process that asks one side to keep bidding against itself while the other withholds its own baseline.

**Third, on capital-side contribution.** Management, your offer of a proportional executive compensation reduction, sized and tied to the same performance period, is a real step — better than round one, and I want to say so plainly. But the union asked a fair question that you haven't answered: what is the actual figure? Not "sized to be visible," an actual number or percentage. And on dividends, "discuss with the family holding" is not yet a commitment. Family Holding — you said you're not opposed in principle to proportional contribution but resist "symbolic theatre." I agree symbolism isn't enough. So put a number next to that principle, the same way the union just did with theirs. I will not support a final package where labour's contribution is quantified to the percentage point and capital's is still a sentence about openness to discussion. That asymmetry is exactly what I said in round one I would not defend to the public that put me in this seat, and that has not changed.

**Fourth, and I want to be unambiguous about this because I sense some testing of the boundary:** management's language about closures remaining a live comparison "if the numbers don't close the gap" is still sitting underneath everything you've said, even as you say you're not attached to closures ideologically. I'll say plainly what that means for me. It does not matter how well-designed your review mechanism is, or how proportionate your executive pay cut is, if the fallback at the end of this process is a closure list. I am not evaluating this negotiation as "closures versus alternative, may the better spreadsheet win." I am evaluating it as: we are building the alternative, together, because closures do not have a path through this board. Ten works council seats plus my blocking minority means that arithmetic is not theoretical. If this process is being run by management as a parallel track where closures remain on the table pending how negotiations go, I need to hear that stated honestly now, because it changes how I read every concession you've made today.

**Fifth, on the guarantee itself.** I said in round one that guarantees and delivery could move together, jointly reviewed, not sequenced as a threat in either direction. I still hold that position — it is not a concession I'm walking back, and I don't think it's actually far from where management has now moved with the "joint design, transparent data" language. Where I differ from management is that I don't accept a guarantee that is silently conditional on a management-controlled endpoint. Where I differ from the works council's arbitration demand is that I think external arbitration is slower and less accountable than a standing joint body with the state inside it. I think there is a workable structure here. It requires management to stop treating the guarantee as the last thing signed once everyone else has proven themselves, and it requires labour to accept that the guarantee is genuinely tied to delivery — not to management's unilateral say-so, but to a jointly monitored, fixed reality.

So, concretely, going into round three: I want management's actual target number for the competitiveness gap, I want the family holding's actual dividend-and-compensation figure, and I want reaction — from all sides — to a standing joint oversight body with state, works council, union, and management seats as the mechanism that replaces both "management discretion" and "external arbitration." Bring me those three things, and this moves toward something I can put my board weight behind. Bring me another round of principles restated with slightly better vocabulary, and we are no closer to a deal I can support — and no closer to management having the two-thirds it would need for anything else.
