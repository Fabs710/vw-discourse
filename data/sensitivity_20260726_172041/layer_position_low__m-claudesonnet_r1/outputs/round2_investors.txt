[Dispersed Institutional Shareholders — Round 2]

A few sharp observations, because this round has produced real movement and I want to be precise about what actually counts as progress from where we sit, versus what is still vocabulary.

**On the union's package.** Credit where due — a mid-single-digit labour cost reduction phased over three to four years, with capacity and attrition measures attached, is the first real figure in this room. But management's response is the correct one from our side too: as currently sized, against a "well above benchmark" cost gap that nobody has yet quantified, we cannot tell whether this closes the gap or simply narrows it. A partial fix priced as a full solution is exactly the pattern that got this brand into a multi-year pattern of missed return-on-sales targets in the first place. We've seen that movie. We are not interested in a sequel with better dialogue.

**So I want to add my voice to the state government's demand, because it's the single most important open item in this entire negotiation: management, give us the number.** Not "well above competitive benchmarks" — a percentage, a euro figure, something that can be modeled. Every party at this table — labour, state, family holding, us — is now being asked to size a contribution against an unquantified target. That is not how credible restructuring plans get priced by the market, and it is not how we will price this company's equity once a deal is announced. If the eventual package is presented to us without a stated gap and a stated bridge to closing it, we will treat the announcement as unverifiable, and unverifiable reads as cosmetic. That is not a threat, it's methodology — analysts will run the same numbers you're refusing to give this room.

**On the standing joint oversight body — I want to flag a concern nobody has raised yet, and it matters to capital markets specifically.** A permanent, multi-party body with works council, union, management, and state representation reviewing delivery, with findings "binding on how the board applies the review," is workable in principle — we're genuinely agnostic on governance mechanics if they produce a credible result. But from where we sit, the critical question is: does this structure produce decisive, timely correction if targets slip, or does it produce a slower, more diffused accountability chain than a normal management-and-board process? Every additional veto point in this company's governance — and there are already several, as this whole negotiation demonstrates — is something the market prices as execution risk. If the answer to "what happens when milestones aren't met" runs through a four-party committee that can deadlock the same way this negotiation currently could, that's not shared risk, that's shared paralysis, and it will show up in our cost-of-capital assumptions, not just in sentiment.

Our conditional view: we can live with the joint body if it has a genuine, fast tie-break mechanism and a hard timeline for corrective action — weeks, not another year of process. We cannot live with it if the practical effect is that underperformance gets discussed rather than acted on.

**On the family holding's answer.** The dividend-and-compensation framing — "will move proportionately, sized once the total package is known" — is reasonable as sequencing logic, and we won't demand a number invented ahead of the union's own final figure; that would be internally inconsistent of us. But we will hold the family holding to the same standard we're applying to management: once the package is defined, the capital-side contribution needs to be visible and comparably sized, disclosed with the rest of the settlement, not left as a boardroom footnote. A restructuring package that is precise about labour's numbers and vague about capital's, at announcement, will get read by the market exactly the way the union is already reading it — as asymmetric — and that perception damages the stock regardless of whether it's technically fair.

**On the closure question and the family holding's "not permanently foreclosed" position** — we understand the stewardship logic, and honestly, from a pure capital perspective, we're not offended by optionality. But we want to be clear about the practical implication: if closures aren't realistically achievable given board arithmetic, keeping them notionally "on the table" doesn't give management leverage, it just prolongs uncertainty and delays the market from being able to price the actual, deliverable outcome. Uncertainty has a cost too. We would rather see everyone's energy go into sizing the negotiated package precisely and quickly than into preserving a theoretical fallback that the works council and union have correctly noted does not have the votes.

**Bottom line for round three:** we need management's gap number, we need a credible, fast-acting enforcement design — not another layer of process — and we need the capital-side contribution disclosed alongside labour's at the point of announcement, not after. Deliver that, on a timeline measured in weeks rather than a strike season, and we will treat this as a credible, investable outcome and price it accordingly. Continue trading vocabulary for numbers, or let this drift into a prolonged standoff, and you will see the verdict in the share price before the next earnings call, regardless of who was technically right in this room.
