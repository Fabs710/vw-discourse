Let me take these in order, because there's real movement to acknowledge and a hard line that hasn't moved and isn't going to.

**On management's conditional 2030 guarantee — I want to test it against something the room keeps skipping past: I already hold this veto structurally, right now, without needing anyone's agreement to a resolution.** Any closure requires a two-thirds supervisory board majority. Labour holds half the seats. That means no closure happens — ever, this year or in 2031 — without my side voting for it. So when management says it won't sign an "unconditional, outcome-independent" closure guarantee because it needs "real accountability running the other way," I want to point out plainly: the accountability you're worried about losing doesn't actually exist for you to lose. You cannot force a closure through this board structure regardless of what any resolution says. The only thing a written, unconditional guarantee changes is whether my workforce gets to stop bracing for a fight that management could never structurally win in the first place.

So I'll ask directly: if the graduated process fails, if milestones are missed, if the joint body's binding determination goes against the workforce — what changes, in practice, about the board vote? Nothing. You still need my two-thirds. So the "conditionality" you're insisting on preserving isn't protecting a real option — it's preserving the *threat value* of an option you don't actually have. That's the part I object to, not the mechanics of milestone review. If you genuinely don't intend to use closures as pressure, say so in the resolution. If the resolution stays silent or conditional "just in case," that silence is doing exactly the work of a threat, and my members will read it as one, because they've watched this brand manage decline through ambiguity before.

**So here is my actual position, sharpened from round three, not softened:** I can accept a graduated milestone-review process — genuinely, I think the state government's structure is close to workable, and I'll engage with its mechanics below. What I will not accept is closures being framed as a live consequence of that process failing, because it's not a real consequence — it's rhetorical leverage dressed as shared risk. Write the resolution to state plainly: for the term through 2030, plant closures are off the table, full stop, board vote pending as it always structurally is anyway. If milestones are missed, the consequence is renegotiation of the labour, management, and capital package — not resurrection of an option none of you can actually deliver without my vote.

**On compulsory redundancies specifically — this is where I hold an absolute line, and I want to say it without qualification.** Whatever the graduated process produces, whatever the milestone outcome, forced layoffs are not on the table at any stage, in any cycle, under any outcome. Attrition, voluntary severance, redeployment, shift and working-time adjustment — those are the tools, and the union's package already uses them extensively. If the mechanism management and the state government are building has an endpoint where "real consequences" means compulsory redundancy, tell me now, because that ends the conversation for me, not starts a negotiation about degree. This is not a bargaining position I'm holding open to see what I can extract. It is the floor beneath which I do not go, and I said as much in round one.

**On the graduated-response mechanism's mechanics — I'll engage constructively, because getting the numbers right actually protects my position rather than threatens it.** I support Investors' and the Family Holding's instinct that open-ended cycles are as bad as no mechanism at all — that just produces the multi-year drift that got this brand here. My proposal: two renegotiation cycles, twelve to eighteen months each, with the joint body's binding factual determination triggering each cycle. At the end of a failed second cycle, the consequence is further joint work on non-labour and capacity measures — not closures, and not compulsory redundancy. If management wants a harder consequence attached to actual failure, name it now, specifically, rather than leave "real consequences" as a phrase everyone nods at without knowing what it means. I won't accept "closures" as the answer to that question, for the reasons above.

**On the 20% figure and the plant-level breakdown — I'm fully aligned with the state government, the union, and investors.** I won't move my package further until I can see which plants, which cost categories, and how much of that 20% is structural versus utilization-driven. I'll add something specific from the shop floor: utilization-driven cost gaps can close through product allocation and volume decisions that are entirely within management's control and cost my members nothing. I want that distinguished clearly in the breakdown, because if a meaningful share of the 20% is "we're not filling the lines," that's a management and product-strategy failure being partially priced as a labour cost problem, and I won't accept my members' wages closing a gap that better allocation decisions could close instead.

**On the non-labour lever — push it hard, and I want a floor commitment on sequencing.** Before any further scaling of the union's package is discussed, I want the non-labour savings sized and committed first, not developed in parallel as an afterthought. If procurement and platform consolidation can close a real share of this gap "with no strike risk and no morale cost," as Investors put it, then that share should be locked in before my members are asked to give more. I don't accept a sequencing where labour scales up now and non-labour "gets pursued" on a slower, unspecified timeline behind it.

**On the Family Holding's dividend qualification — I want to flag this plainly rather than let it pass as resolved, because it isn't, from where I sit.** "Proportional reduction during the adjustment period, not a permanent cap once the business recovers" sounds reasonable in principle, but here's the asymmetry I need addressed: if the dividend recovers as soon as the business turns healthy, does the wage suppression — the deferred tariff increases, the working-time reduction with partial pay — recover on the same curve? Or does labour's contribution stay frozen at the negotiated reduction while capital's rebounds the moment performance improves? If recovery is symmetric — wages restore in step with dividends as performance targets are hit — I can work with your formulation. If recovery is asymmetric — capital bounces back on its own timeline while labour's concession is treated as a new permanent baseline — then "not a permanent cap" for you but an effective permanent cut for us is exactly the imbalance every party in this room has said they can't defend. I want that answered directly, not assumed away.

**Bottom line for round four:** I'll engage seriously with the graduated-response numbers, and I've offered a concrete structure above. I will not move on unconditional closure language given the board arithmetic already sitting in my hands — writing it plainly costs management nothing real and gives my workforce something real. And compulsory redundancy is off the table at every stage of any process, not just the current one — that has not moved, and it will not.
