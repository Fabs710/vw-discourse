Let me engage with the actual numbers, because that's the first time this discussion has moved from principle to substance, and I want to test them against the thing that actually matters: does this close the gap.

**On the union's package as stated: it is progress, but I have to be honest — as described, it does not yet close the competitiveness gap, and I won't pretend otherwise to keep the room comfortable.** A mid-single-digit percentage reduction in effective labour cost, phased over three to four years, combined with shift reductions and voluntary attrition — that is a real number, and I credit the union for putting a shape to it rather than leaving it abstract. But let's be precise about the scale of the problem: German plant costs here are running well above competitive benchmarks — not by a marginal percentage, by a structural gap that a mid-single-digit labour cost adjustment, phased over years, does not fully close, especially set against a market that continues shrinking over the same horizon. If I take this package to the supervisory board as the full alternative to capacity actions, and the brand is still missing return-on-sales in 2027 because the fix was sized to what was politically deliverable rather than what the gap actually required, that is not a win for anyone in this room — including the workforce whose jobs I'm told this protects.

So my ask is not rhetorical: I need this scaled up, or I need to see additional levers stacked alongside it — more aggressive capacity consolidation at specific underutilized lines, faster attrition timelines, a larger voluntary severance program with real uptake, not modest participation. Bring me a package where the combined effect — labour cost, capacity, working time — actually closes the gap on a credible timeline, and I will engage with it as a genuine alternative to closures. Bring me this package as currently sized, and I will tell the board honestly that it is a partial answer, not a structural solution, because that is what my fiduciary obligation requires me to say.

**On sequencing and the arbitration proposal — I want to separate two things the works council has bundled together, because one I can move on, one I cannot.**

I can move on this: I accept that "management unilaterally judges whether targets were met" is not a credible enforcement structure, and I understand why labour won't sign up to that after everything that's been said about broken pledges. A joint review mechanism, with works council and union inside the process, checkpoints at defined intervals rather than open-ended discretion — I can build that. That is a legitimate answer to the trust problem, and I'm not going to pretend it isn't.

What I cannot accept is binding, unconditional site guarantees to 2030 written into the board resolution *before* the delivery mechanism exists, with disputes resolved by external arbitration that can override the review findings. Here's why, plainly: if guarantees are locked in regardless of outcome and the only enforcement path runs through arbitration that treats "did the workforce deliver" as a contestable legal question rather than a jointly reviewed factual one, I have handed away the only mechanism that makes the guarantee credible to the investors and the family holding who have to fund this company's future. That's not me protecting a closure option I secretly want to keep — it's me protecting the thing that makes the whole deal financeable. A guarantee nobody enforces isn't stability, it's a delayed version of exactly the crisis we're in now, except with less capital left to fix it, which the state government itself flagged as the pattern it doesn't want to repeat.

So here's what I'll actually put forward: joint design of the review criteria, works council and union representation in evaluating delivery against agreed milestones, transparent shared data rather than management's private judgment — but with real consequences that can move in both directions if milestones aren't met, on a timeline understood by everyone up front, not litigated after the fact through arbitration that could take a year and leave the underlying problem frozen in place. That is shared risk. It is not risk transfer, and it is not a rubber stamp either.

**On the demand for management and capital-side numbers — fair challenge, and I'll answer it directly rather than retreat to principle.** I am prepared to bring to the board a proportional executive compensation reduction at brand and group management level, tied to the same performance period as the workforce contribution — not symbolic, sized to be visible and comparable as a share of total compensation, not just base salary. On dividend policy, I will discuss it with the family holding as part of the overall package, but I want to be honest with the union and investors both: I am not going to present dividend suspension as a substitute for structural savings, because Investors are right that it doesn't fix a cost base — it would just let all of us claim fairness while the actual problem persists. What I can commit to is that capital's contribution will be real and proportionate, discussed openly alongside labour's numbers, not held back until last.

**To the family holding and investors** — I said in round one I'd take a genuine alternative to the board if it closes the gap. I meant it. But "genuine" has to mean sized to the actual problem, not sized to what avoids conflict this quarter. I'd rather have this argument now, with everyone's numbers on the table honestly, than sign something in six months that unravels in 2027.

So: scale up the package, agree a joint-but-consequential review structure rather than open-ended arbitration, and put real capital-side numbers next to mine — and I will go to the board with an alternative I can defend, not a closure list. Short of that combination, I am not going to pretend the gap is closed when it isn't.
