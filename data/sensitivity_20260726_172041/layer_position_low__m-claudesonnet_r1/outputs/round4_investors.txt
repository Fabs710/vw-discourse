# Institutional Investors — Round 4

Let me respond to the substantive movement this round, then flag where the arithmetic still doesn't close and where a genuine risk to our position has emerged that nobody else in this room is incentivized to name.

**On the Family Holding's dividend answer — this is the correct answer, and I want to defend it against the pressure it's about to get.** The distinction you drew is structurally sound and I'll say plainly why: a dividend is a return on capital at risk across the entire ownership base, including capital that isn't party to this labour negotiation at all. Pegging dividend policy permanently to a wage-recovery curve doesn't just constrain the family holding — it constrains us, and every other holder of this equity, indefinitely, based on the outcome of one restructuring cycle rather than the company's actual capital position five or ten years out. If the company needs to retain earnings for the next technology transition in 2033, that decision should be made on the merits of 2033, not pre-committed today because of a formula built to resolve a 2025 dispute. Bounded to the agreement's term, milestone-linked, reverting to ordinary board judgment afterward — that is the right answer, and it is one we can underwrite. I want to be explicit that this is not us protecting dividends for their own sake; a healthier dividend from a genuinely fixed company is exactly the outcome we're all trying to reach, and permanently shackling capital allocation to a labour formula would work against the very recovery this whole process is meant to produce.

**On the works council's board-arithmetic argument — I think it's analytically correct and I want to say why it doesn't change our position.** You're right that closures can't pass this board without labour votes. That's a real structural fact, not rhetoric. But I'd push back gently on the conclusion some in this room seem to be drawing from it — that because the guarantee "costs management nothing," it's therefore a costless, purely symbolic concession to celebrate. From where we sit, it's the opposite: removing the ambiguity was necessary precisely because ambiguity was pricing risk into this stock regardless of whether the underlying option was real. Markets don't discount based on legal arithmetic buried in a board statute; they discount based on stated uncertainty. So this concession has genuine value to us even though the works council is right that it was never really available to management as leverage. Good — that's progress, and I don't need to relitigate the theory behind it.

**Where I have to be sharper than I've been in prior rounds: the product-allocation lever is the thing that actually worries me now, and not for the reason the state government raised.** The state government wants joint-body transparency on allocation decisions so that decline-by-neglect doesn't happen invisibly. I support that transparency requirement for the same reason but a different motive: if product and platform allocation becomes the mechanism by which underperforming sites are quietly starved, that is not a labour-relations problem for us — it's a capital-efficiency problem. A joint body with veto-adjacent influence over where investment gets allocated, however "advisory" it's framed, is exactly the kind of governance drag that makes capital allocation slower and less responsive to market signals, which is the opposite of what this restructuring is supposed to deliver. So: yes to transparency and reporting. No to any construction where the joint body's visibility becomes de facto co-management of product strategy. Management, I'd ask you to be precise in round five about where "joint visibility" ends and "board's ordinary capital-allocation authority" begins, because that boundary is currently doing a lot of work with very little definition, and it directly affects whether this company can actually execute the turnaround fast enough to matter to us.

**On the arithmetic — I want to hold the line I set last round, because nothing this round has closed the gap.** We now have: a mid-single-digit labour reduction that management itself says doesn't close the 20% gap; an indicative quarter-to-third non-labour lever, unsized and unconfirmed; and a plant-level breakdown still not delivered. Add those together, even generously, and there is a real possibility this still doesn't add up to the stated target on the stated timeline. I want to be direct about what that means for us: governance architecture — binding fact-finding, graduated cycles, symmetric recovery, transparency conditions — is all necessary, but none of it is sufficient if the underlying cost bridge doesn't close. We will read an elegant process wrapped around an unclosed gap as exactly what I called it last round: deferred value destruction with better vocabulary.

**On the redundancy floor and closure guarantee — no objection from us.** These were never our binding constraint; we've said from round one we're agnostic on mechanism. If achieving the target through attrition, redeployment, and working-time tools rather than compulsion is what labour and management agree delivers the number, that's fine by us — provided it delivers the number. Our concern is not how the savings are achieved; it's whether they're achieved, on time, at the scale required.

**Bottom line for round four, restated plainly:** the governance architecture is converging well — closure guarantee, redundancy floor, graduated mechanism, symmetric dividend recovery, product-allocation transparency. We can support all of it. But we are not going to treat this negotiation as resolved, and we will not price this as an investable restructuring, until three numbers land: the plant-level breakdown, the firm (not indicative) sizing of the non-labour lever, and confirmation that labour's package plus non-labour savings plus whatever else gets identified actually sums to something close to the stated gap on a credible timeline. Everything agreed so far is necessary scaffolding. It is not yet a deal we'd underwrite, because the arithmetic that determines whether margins actually return to competitive levels is still incomplete. Close that, and we support this fully. Leave it open past round five, and our patience — like our capital — moves elsewhere.
