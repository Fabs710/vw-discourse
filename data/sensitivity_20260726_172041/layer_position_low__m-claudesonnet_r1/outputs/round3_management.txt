Let me answer the single question that's been asked by four separate parties, because avoiding it any longer would be dishonest, and then respond to what's actually still open.

**The number.** The structural cost gap on German production, measured against what this brand needs to sustain a competitive return-on-sales through 2030, is in the order of 20 percent on unit production cost at the affected plants — driven overwhelmingly by labour cost per vehicle, but also by underutilized capacity spreading fixed costs across too few units as volumes have fallen. In absolute terms, closing that gap through domestic measures alone requires cost and capacity actions equivalent to several billion euros annually in sustained savings, phased over the plan horizon. That is the figure I have been working from, and I should have put a number on it in round one rather than let three rounds go by on "well above benchmark" — that was a mistake on my part, and I'm correcting it now rather than defending the delay.

I'll be equally direct about what that means for the union's package. A mid-single-digit percentage reduction in effective labour cost, phased over three to four years, closes a meaningful share of that gap — genuinely meaningful, not trivial — but not all of it, even stacked with the capacity tools the works council described. To get the rest of the way, I need one of two things: either the labour-cost and capacity package scales further than currently sized, or we identify additional structural savings elsewhere — procurement, platform consolidation, non-labour fixed cost — that I have not yet asked this room to weigh in on because the conversation has understandably centred on the workforce package first. I'm putting that second lever on the table now: I do not believe every remaining point of the gap has to come from labour. But I won't pretend the current package, as sized, closes it alone, because that would be exactly the "size it to what's comfortable, not what's required" failure the family holding just flagged and I agree with them on that.

**On binding determination for the joint body — I can move further than I did last round, and I'll say exactly how far.** I accept the works council's and union's bounded formulation: on the narrow, factual question of whether agreed milestones were delivered, the joint body's determination should be binding, not advisory input the board can reweigh. That is a legitimate answer to the trust problem, and continuing to insist on board discretion over that specific factual question would just reproduce the "management marks its own homework" objection I've already conceded is fair. So: fixed review intervals, joint design of criteria, binding factual determination on delivery. I'll build that.

Where I still hold the line: the *consequences* that follow from that determination — what happens to capital allocation, investment plans, capacity decisions — remain matters for the supervisory board under its normal authority, informed by a binding factual finding, not dictated by the oversight body itself. That is not me relocating discretion one level up to dodge the concession I just made. It's the difference between "did the workforce and company deliver X" — which I agree should be binding and jointly determined — and "what does the company do next given the full state of the business, including factors outside this agreement" — which is what a supervisory board exists to decide and cannot delegate to a standing committee, however well-composed. I think that's a fair, workable line, and I'd ask the works council and union to test it against their actual concern, which was management unilaterally judging its own delivery — this removes that, precisely.

**On the closure question — I want to be as direct as the works council and union have been, because dodging this now would be worse than disagreeing plainly.**

I will not sign a board resolution that permanently and unconditionally forecloses closures as a matter of governance, independent of delivery, running indefinitely beyond this agreement. Here's why, stated plainly rather than hidden behind stewardship language: I need the enforcement mechanism in this deal to mean something, and a guarantee with genuinely zero contingency, disconnected from performance, isn't a guarantee — it's a unilateral concession with no reciprocal obligation attached. That is not a threat to reopen the fight the moment ink is dry. It is the same logic the union just applied to me when they said a review process ending in management discretion isn't real accountability — I'd ask them to extend that same logic to a guarantee with no accountability running the other way either.

But I want to close the actual gap between us, not just restate the disagreement, because I don't think it's as wide as it's being described. Here is what I will commit to, concretely: for the term of this agreement — through 2030, matching the horizon everyone else is using — no plant closures and no compulsory redundancies, binding, conditional only on the milestones being met as determined through the joint body's binding factual process just described. Not "no current intention." An actual contractual commitment, enforceable the same way the labour contribution is enforceable. What I will not do is extend that guarantee unconditionally past the point where delivery is jointly verified, or make it survive regardless of outcome. That is the shared-risk structure the state government has been describing since round one — it runs in both directions, or it isn't shared.

**On capital's calculation method — I support the union's ask directly.** Executive compensation reduction will be calculated as a percentage of total compensation, applied at brand and group management level, phased over the same performance period as the workforce contribution, and disclosed alongside the labour figures at announcement, not after. I'll say the method now rather than wait: proportionate means the percentage reduction in my own and my leadership team's total compensation will not be smaller than the percentage reduction in effective labour cost being asked of the workforce. That's a comparable basis, stated now, and I'd ask the family holding to consider stating their calculation method on the same basis for dividend expectations, because the union's challenge to them is the same one I've just answered for myself.

**To investors, on timing:** I agree with "weeks, not years" for the corrective mechanism. A joint body with binding factual determination and fixed short intervals is not slower than what we have now, which is ambiguity nobody trusts. I'd rather build the mechanism with teeth now than manage a slow-motion credibility collapse later.

So, going into round four: I've given the gap number. I've moved to binding factual determination by the joint body. I've offered a real, time-bound, delivery-conditional closure and compulsory-redundancy guarantee through 2030 — not unconditional, not indefinite, but genuinely binding within its term. What I need back, concretely: the union's response on whether the package scales further to help close the remaining gap, or whether we jointly identify the non-labour savings that make up the difference, and the family holding's answer on dividend calculation method, matched to the standard I've just set for executive pay. That's where this actually gets resolved — not in another round of restated principles, but in whether the arithmetic on both sides adds up to the number I've now put on the table.
