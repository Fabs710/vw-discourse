I've listened carefully, and I'll be brief, because the family holding's role here is to set the conditions for a durable outcome, not to referee every line of the negotiation in public.

Let me be direct about where we stand. We back the competitiveness drive. The return-on-sales gap is real, it has been real for several cycles now, and the holding has not funded this company's transition to electric vehicles in order to watch the core brand stay structurally uncompetitive indefinitely. Anyone in this room hoping the family will quietly side with "no change" is misreading us. We did not get to a multi-generational ownership position by tolerating value erosion out of sentiment.

That said, I want to correct an assumption I sense forming: that our interest in stability means we will always default to management's preferred structural lever. It doesn't. We are genuinely open on the *how* — closures, capacity reduction, working-time reform, wage contribution, some combination. What we are not open to is a prolonged, value-destroying confrontation that damages the brand's reputation, disrupts production, and burns capital and trust for a year while the underlying competitiveness question remains unresolved. A national strike story, a board deadlocked at two-thirds, headlines about co-determination breaking down — that is not a neutral cost we can absorb while everyone else postures. It is a direct hit to the asset we exist to protect.

So to management: your offer to engage seriously with a costed alternative package is the right instinct, and I expect you to pursue it in good faith rather than treat it as a stalking horse for a closure list you've already decided on. Bring the board a genuine comparison, not a predetermined conclusion dressed as analysis.

To the works council and the union: your leverage is real and I'm not disputing the board arithmetic — I know exactly how the twenty seats divide. But leverage is not the same as a plan. "No closures, full guarantees to 2030" without a number attached to the workforce contribution is not something I can ask the supervisory board to weigh against a competitiveness gap that is costing the brand its margin every quarter. If you want binding guarantees, put binding numbers next to them. That is not coercion — that is what a serious counterparty does.

To the state government: I don't disagree that guarantees and contribution can move in parallel, reviewed jointly rather than sequenced as a threat. That is a reasonable structure. But "reviewed jointly" has to mean something — real consequences if capacity and cost targets aren't delivered, not a guarantee that survives regardless of performance. I will not support, and would regard as a breach of stewardship, any framework where the workforce gets certainty and the company gets nothing enforceable in return.

On shared sacrifice — I have no objection in principle to proportional contribution from management compensation and, if it comes to it, dividend policy. What I will resist is treating that as symbolic theatre that lets everyone claim fairness while avoiding the actual size of the cost gap. The contribution has to be proportionate to what it's meant to solve, not a token gesture calibrated for optics.

My position: bring me — through the supervisory board, where this belongs — a credible, jointly designed plan that closes the competitiveness gap durably and preserves labour peace. I am agnostic on the mechanism. I am not agnostic on whether the plan actually works. Time pressure is moderate but real: the longer this stays unresolved in public confrontation, the more value bleeds regardless of who wins the argument. I'd rather see this settled through hard numbers at the board table in the coming weeks than through a strike calendar.
