I'll speak plainly, because that's what this moment demands.

Let me be clear from the outset: plant closures are not on the table. Not one. Compulsory redundancies are not on the table. Not one. These are not opening positions I've picked to negotiate down from — they are red lines, and management knows exactly why. This company has stood on a historic job-security pledge for decades. Workers built their lives, their mortgages, their children's futures on that pledge. Tearing it up unilaterally, announcing closures as if it were a foregone conclusion, is not restructuring — it is a breach of trust with the entire workforce, and we will treat it as such.

Let's be equally clear about the arithmetic of power here. Any closure or relocation requires a two-thirds supervisory board majority. Labour holds ten of twenty seats. We do not need to persuade anyone that closures are wrong in order to stop them — we simply do not have to vote for them, and they do not happen. That is not a threat, it is the structure of German co-determination, and it exists precisely so that a crisis like this cannot be resolved by management fiat. I raise it not to posture but so everyone in this room negotiates in light of the actual constraints, rather than pretending the board vote is a formality.

That said — and this matters — I am not here to deny reality. I know the market is shrinking, I know Chinese competitors are undercutting on cost, I know the return-on-sales numbers are bad. My members are not blind to that. We are prepared to talk seriously about capacity reductions, working-time models, shift reductions, even a negotiated wage or working-time contribution from the workforce — but only within a framework that guarantees every German site stays open and no one is forced out of a job involuntarily. Contribution has to be earned by security, not extracted by threat.

What I will not accept is a fait accompli — a list of plants marked for closure presented to us as the "necessary" option, with co-determination reduced to rubber-stamping management's spreadsheet. If that is the approach, you will see what tens of thousands of workers on warning strike look like, and it will not stay contained to factory floors — it will be a national story about whether co-determination in this country means anything at all.

So my opening position is this: give me binding site and job guarantees to 2030, give the works council and unions a genuine seat in designing the alternative savings package — not consultation after the fact — and I will bring the workforce to the table on real, substantial contributions. Without those two things, there is no deal, and there is no board majority for closures either. That is not a threat. That is simply how this is structured, and I'd rather everyone plan around that reality now than discover it the hard way in three months.
