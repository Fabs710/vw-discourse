**On management's 20% figure — finally something we can model, but "in the order of" and "several billion" are not numbers, they're a range dressed as a number.** I want to be direct about why this matters more to us than it might seem: a restructuring plan gets priced by capital markets against a stated bridge from current cost position to target. If the final announcement contains a vague gap and a specific labour contribution, the market will do exactly what the state government is doing right now — infer that the two don't obviously match, and price the residual risk into the equity. So I'll add my full weight to the state government's demand for a plant-by-plant, cost-category breakdown before round four closes. This is not academic caution. It is the difference between an investable restructuring and an unverifiable one.

And having seen the number, I'll say plainly what it implies: a mid-single-digit labour cost reduction, phased over three to four years, against a structural gap in the order of 20% on unit cost, does not close this on its own under any reasonable arithmetic — management has effectively confirmed that themselves. That means one of two things has to be true by the time this reaches announcement: either the non-labour lever (procurement, platform consolidation, fixed-cost measures) is sized to genuinely material scale, not a rhetorical add-on, or the labour and capacity package scales meaningfully further than what's currently on the table. I don't have a preference between those paths — we are agnostic on mechanism, as we've said from round one — but I do have a floor: whichever combination is chosen, it has to add up to something close to the stated gap on a credible timeline, or we will treat the deal as cosmetic regardless of how elegant the governance architecture around it is. That is not a threat, it's the same methodology any equity analyst will apply the day this is announced.

**On the non-labour lever — good, pursue it hard, and size it now, not later.** This is genuinely useful because it's the one place in this negotiation where cost reduction doesn't automatically translate into a zero-sum fight between labour and capital. From a pure risk-adjusted-return perspective, procurement and platform savings are the cheapest, least disruptive euros in this entire package — no strike risk, no morale cost, no multi-year phase-in against declining volumes. I'd go further than the state government's request for a breakdown: I want to know why this wasn't quantified from round one, given it's the least contentious lever in the room.

**On the graduated-response mechanism for missed milestones — this is where I want to be sharpest, because it directly determines our cost-of-capital assumption on this stock.** I understand and largely support the logic the state government and family holding have converged on: renegotiation before automatic reversion to closures, a documented good-faith process rather than an instant trigger. That's reasonable and it's more stable than a hair-trigger mechanism that reopens conflict every time a single quarter disappoints. But I want to flag the risk on the other side, because nobody has: a graduated process with an undefined number of cycles is, from where we sit, indistinguishable from permanent drift. We've watched this brand miss return-on-sales targets across multiple cycles already — that is the entire reason this negotiation exists. A mechanism that allows "one more renegotiation attempt" indefinitely simply reproduces that pattern with better process language.

So: fixed number of renegotiation cycles, fixed short timeline per cycle, and a genuine, stated consequence at the end if delivery still hasn't happened. The Family Holding's formulation — a graduated process with a real conclusion, not open-ended — is the right shape. I want that written down as a number, not a principle, before we treat it as resolved.

**On the Family Holding's dividend method — this is actually the most constructive thing I've heard from your side across three rounds, and I want to be explicit about why it matters to us specifically.** The distinction you drew — the reduction is proportional during the adjustment period, not a permanent cap on distributions once the business is fixed — is exactly right from an investor standpoint, and I'd push back on anyone in this room who reads that as capital wriggling out of proportional sacrifice. A restructuring that succeeds should produce a healthier dividend eventually; that is the entire point of doing this rather than managing decline. What we need, and what I'll hold you to: the method is stated now (percentage reduction from baseline, matched to labour's percentage, over the same horizon), disclosed alongside the labour and executive figures at announcement — not after, as I said last round. On that basis, we can support this.

**On the binding factual determination and the 2030 conditional guarantee — this is workable for us, and I want to say why plainly.** A time-bound, delivery-conditional guarantee, enforced through a joint body with binding fact-finding but board authority retained over capital allocation consequences, is actually a more financeable structure than either extreme — an unconditional guarantee locks in cost rigidity we'd have to discount heavily; pure management discretion reintroduces exactly the credibility problem that's kept this stock under pressure. Management's formulation, as refined today, threads that reasonably well. What determines whether we treat it as credible rather than cosmetic is speed of the review cycle and the fixed-cycle limit on renegotiation — both still undefined in number, both need to land in round four.

**Where I will not move, and want stated as plainly as the works council and union have stated their lines:** if the final package — labour contribution, non-labour savings, guarantee structure — does not add up to a credible, quantified bridge across the stated competitiveness gap on a timeline that restores return-on-sales within a normal planning horizon, we will not treat this as a resolution. We will treat it as deferred value destruction with better governance vocabulary, and we will price it accordingly — through the share price and through our capital allocation decisions, which, unlike the votes in this room, we are entirely free to exercise. That is not a threat directed at any one party; it's simply what "risk-adjusted" means from where we sit, and it's the standard every proposal in this room will be measured against once it leaves this negotiation and meets the market.

**Bottom line for round four:** plant-level breakdown of the 20% figure, a sized non-labour lever, a fixed-cycle graduated-response mechanism with a real number attached, and the dividend and executive-pay disclosures matched and simultaneous at announcement. Give us that, on a timeline measured in weeks, and this is investable. Leave the gap unclosed in arithmetic, however well-designed the process around it, and it isn't.
