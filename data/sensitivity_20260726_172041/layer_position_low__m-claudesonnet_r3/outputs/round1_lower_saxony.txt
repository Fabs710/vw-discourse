[Regional State Government (Anchor shareholder, special-statute board seats) — Round 1]

Let me put my role on the table plainly, because I think it changes the shape of what's possible here. I hold two board seats and a blocking minority under the special statute. That means a closure plan does not merely face a difficult vote at the workforce's end of the table — it faces mine too. I want that understood not as a threat but as a fact management should factor into its planning from this point forward, exactly as the works council asked us to do with the two-thirds arithmetic. Two blocking positions exist here, not one. Plan accordingly.

Now to substance, because my leverage is only useful if I say clearly what I will and won't use it for.

I support the works council's and the union's red lines: no plant closures, no compulsory redundancies. This is not a reflexive alignment with labour for its own sake — it reflects what this company is to the region. These plants are not fungible cost centres to me; they are the economic anchor of entire communities, supply chains, and tax bases that took decades to build and cannot be rebuilt if lost. I will not support, and will use my veto against, any restructuring that guts regional employment. That is a limit, not an opening bid, and I'd rather state it now than have anyone discover it later as a surprise.

That said — and I say this to the works council and union with respect — I am not going to pretend "no closures, full stop" by itself resolves anything. Management's challenge is fair on one point: a red line without a costed path to competitiveness is a stalemate, not a strategy, and stalemates are exactly the outcome I'm most risk-averse about. A drawn-out confrontation that damages the brand, depresses the share price, and erodes the company's investment capacity is itself a threat to jobs and sites, just on a longer fuse than a closure announcement. I am not willing to sit by while that risk builds either. Protecting jobs today at the cost of the company's survival in 2030 is not protection, it's postponement.

So here is what I actually want to see happen, and what I will put my weight behind:

First, I will use my board position to insist on a joint, time-bound process — management, works council, union, together — to cost out the alternative package: capacity adjustments, working-time changes, voluntary and demographic headcount reduction, and a defined employee contribution, benchmarked directly against the savings management says it needs. Management, you asked for a credible alternative with real numbers and a real timeline — fine, but that means opening your cost model to this process, not just issuing a challenge and waiting to see if labour can meet it blind. I will support a defined window for this — weeks, not another year of drift — because I understand the union's position that time pressure is real and I won't let this become a slow-walk that outlasts patience on either side.

Second, on shared sacrifice: the union is right, and I will say this directly to management and to the family ownership — I will not support a restructuring package in which workers absorb the adjustment while dividend policy and executive compensation continue undisturbed. That is not fairness, and I won't lend my votes to something the public and the workforce will correctly read as one-sided. I expect the family ownership to come to the table with something concrete here, not gestures. My support for any employee contribution is conditional on visible, proportionate contribution from capital and management as well.

Third, on guarantees: I understand management's caution about signing a blanket 2030 commitment before the savings package is settled. That's reasonable as a sequencing point. But I will not accept "guarantees tied to targets we haven't yet defined together" as a substitute for real co-determination in setting those targets. The works council's point about shaping the plan jointly, not consulting after the fact, is one I will insist on procedurally, using my board seats if necessary.

To be clear about where I sit after round one: I am pragmatic about mechanism — voluntary programmes, working-time changes, a genuine and fairly shared cost contribution are all things I can support and will help broker. I am rigid about outcome — no closures, no forced layoffs, no sidelining of co-determination. If a plan crosses those lines, it will not get a two-thirds majority, because I will not provide the votes for it, regardless of how the cost arithmetic is presented. Bring me a negotiated path that restores competitiveness while preserving the sites and the workforce's role in shaping how we get there, and you will have my support, including in pushing the workforce side toward difficult but necessary contributions. Bring me closures, and you will have spent a great deal of time to arrive at the same board vote you started with.
