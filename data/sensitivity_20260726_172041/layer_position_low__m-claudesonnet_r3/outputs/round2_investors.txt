[Dispersed Institutional Investors (Capital Markets) — Round 2]

We'll keep this tight and focused on what's actually changed since round one, because several things have, and we want to be precise about which of them matter to us and which are noise.

**On the sequencing resolution.** Process-first, simultaneous signature at the end — we support this, and we'd note it's the only version of "shared sacrifice" sequencing that doesn't create a governance problem for us as shareholders. A board-enforceable guarantee signed against an unvalidated outline of levers is a contingent liability with no offsetting evidence it's affordable. We were prepared to say so plainly if the works council's original "guarantee first" sequencing had held, so we'll say the opposite plainly now that it hasn't: this is the correct outcome, not a concession to be relitigated. Good.

**On the family holding's dividend and compensation proposal.** We want to be precise about what was actually offered, because "freeze on dividend growth" and "dividend cut" are not the same instrument, and we'd ask the family holding to be clear about which one the board will actually vote on. A freeze on growth, with the family forgoing its own proportional increase ahead of other shareholders, is a real and non-trivial signal — it costs the controlling shareholder more than it costs us proportionally, and we note that. As dispersed holders, our own near-term income is affected by this too; we're not indifferent to it in isolation. But we'll say what we said in round one and mean it more sharply now that a figure is on the table: this is a legitimacy instrument, not a structural one, and we will judge its size against one test only — does it look proportionate enough to get labour's signature without becoming the story that substitutes for the cost-per-vehicle work. If it's sized to do that job credibly, we accept it, including the income effect on us. If it's sized as a token that management or the family expect to be treated as equivalent to the labour contribution in scale, we won't accept that framing, because it isn't true and the market will see through it immediately.

**On management keeping closures live.** We understand the logic — and frankly, from a pure incentive-design standpoint, it's correct. Remove the credible alternative and every party in this room negotiates less seriously. We're not going to object to management holding this position through the costing process. What we will flag, directly to the state government: your statement that the veto is exercised "without hesitation" if a validated alternative emerges and management pursues closure anyway — we read that as useful information, not as escalation. It removes ambiguity about what happens at the end of the process, and reduced ambiguity is something we price favorably. What we would price unfavorably is if that veto gets exercised in a way that leaves the underlying cost gap only partially closed — a validated alternative that closes, say, sixty percent of the structural gap is not the same as one that closes it, and "the board vetoed closure" doesn't answer the question of whether margins actually recover. We need that distinction to stay visible through the process, not collapse into "labour proposed something, board blocked closure, deal done."

**On the works council's four levers — specifically the skepticism management raised.** We share it, and we'll sharpen it. Working-time reduction with partial pay compensation is the lever most likely to look like a cost saving on a slide and turn out cost-neutral or worse once you net the compensation offset against reduced output. We want this tested with the same rigor as any closure scenario — net saving after compensation, not gross headline reduction. Same discipline on voluntary separation timelines: "sized against demographic attrition" sounds prudent but if the realistic attrition curve stretches savings past three or four years, that is not a credible timeline against a return-on-sales target that's already being missed today. We'd ask the works council directly: when you say these levers close a comparable gap, on what net basis, and by which specific year does the run-rate saving land? That's the number we need to see, not the category list.

**On the two-to-three-year timeline management floated.** We'll accept that as the outer boundary of what we'd call credible, and we want to be explicit about that now so it isn't relitigated later as if it were generous. Two to three years to the bulk of the savings is close to our limit for tolerating continued margin drag while this gets implemented. If the joint process concludes with a mechanism whose savings don't materially show up in reported numbers until year four or five, we will treat that as failing our test regardless of how elegant the governance around it was.

**On the timeline for the process itself.** Everyone keeps saying "weeks, not months." We'd ask the state government, since you've volunteered to chair the oversight, to put an actual date on that in the next round — a specific week by which the joint number is published — because "weeks" without an anchor date is exactly the kind of soft commitment that turns into the drift everyone claims to fear. We will be watching whether a firm date gets named or whether it stays comfortably vague.

**Where we stand after round two.** Genuine progress: sequencing resolved sensibly, a capital contribution now has a shape (even if not yet a final figure), transparency is agreed across the board. Not yet resolved, and this is what we'll be judging in round three: whether the alternative package's savings are net, quantified, and land within a timeframe that actually protects margins rather than merely protecting the workforce from disruption while the P&L keeps bleeding. We remain unmoved on the one point that matters most to us: a deal that trades site and job security for a cost base that's still uncompetitive in three years is not a deal we'll support, no matter how well-governed the process that produced it. Bring us the net numbers and a dated deadline, and we'll keep engaging in good faith. Without them, "process" is just a word.
