I need to speak plainly this round, because two things have converged that I cannot let pass without comment: the works council and union have now stated, explicitly, that their veto and their rights are not conditioned on the certified outcome at all, and management has responded with the investment-reallocation warning. Those two statements together are the actual crux of this negotiation, more than the audit design or the calendar, and I have avoided saying directly where I stand on that crux. I won't avoid it any longer.

**On the unconditional veto.** Works council, union — I heard you both precisely, and I want to be equally precise back, because this is close to where I part company with the framing you've offered. I accept, and always have accepted, that the co-determination structure gives you a veto that exists independent of anything I think about it. That is the law and the governance structure this company operates under, and I am not disputing your right to exercise it as the structure allows. What I will say plainly, for the first time without hedging: a settlement in which a certified, independently validated structural gap is acknowledged as real, and the response from this table is that nothing changes regardless — no further capacity action, no further concession, just "see you in three years" — is not a stable outcome for this company, and I will not treat it as one. I said in round three that I will not support a settlement that requires pretending a certified gap isn't real. I want to extend that now: I also will not treat "the gap is real but we've decided in advance nothing follows from it" as materially different from pretending it isn't real. It amounts to the same thing from where I sit as the shareholder carrying the long-term exposure. The company either closes the gap through some combination of levers, or its competitiveness keeps eroding — the arithmetic doesn't care whose veto is exercised over it.

**On the state government's answer — conditional on process, unconditional on outcome.** This is a more honest and more workable formulation than the works council's flat version, and I want to say so, because it leaves room for what I actually need: a genuine, exhausted search through every non-closure lever, footprint measures, capacity actions, product-mix shifts, before anyone declares the process finished. If that search is real and rigorous and still leaves a residual gap, I don't expect the veto to vanish. I expect the state government, as it has now said, to bring real pressure to bear on finding the rest — investment reallocation, regional support, whatever it takes — rather than treating the veto's survival as the end of the conversation. That is a materially different posture than the works council's, and I want to note the difference on record rather than let it be flattened into "labour says no, full stop."

**On management's investment-reallocation warning.** I understand the fiduciary logic, and I won't pretend I don't share some of the underlying concern — capital allocation has to follow competitiveness, that is not negotiable for me either. But I agree with the state government here: if that lever is used as a deniable substitute for a closure vote you'd lose, rather than a genuine capital-allocation judgment made on its own merits, that is not a path I will support quietly through the board. It converts an open, governed decision into something opaque, and opacity is exactly the kind of reputational and governance risk that damages this holding's long-term position, not just labour's. I will support the disclosure commitment the state government asked for — investment reallocation decisions affecting German sites go to the joint steering group and to board oversight before finalization. Management, I expect you to give that commitment without qualification, and I will not support quietly waved-through allocation shifts as a backdoor around the board vote.

**On the auditor's mandate.** I side with the state government's formulation, not the works council's narrower one and not management's broader one taken to its logical end. Certify the gap size and whether a specific package closes it, in euros — that is fact, not politics. What the board does with that fact remains a board vote. But I want to say to the works council and union directly: don't confuse "the board decides the response" with "the response can be decided in advance regardless of the facts." Those are different things, and the second one is what worries me in what you've said this round.

**On the family holding's figure.** No change — week three stands, tested against the proportionality metric as specified. I have nothing further to add on timing; I've already committed to that discipline and I won't relitigate it under pressure to produce a number today.

I'll close with the thing I actually came into this round to say: I am not moved by the argument that a durable settlement can be built on a permanent, outcome-independent veto sitting opposite a certified competitiveness gap. That is not stability to me. It is deferred instability with better process design around it. Bring me a genuinely exhausted search for alternatives, a proportionate capital contribution, and a real answer to what happens if a residual gap remains — and I will support it fully through the board. Ask me to accept that nothing follows from a certified shortfall, no matter how real, and you are asking me to accept a structural decline dressed up as labour peace. That I will not do.
