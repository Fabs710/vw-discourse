**On the sequencing question the moderator put to me directly — let me clarify precisely, because I think there's less daylight than the summary suggests, and also one real difference that hasn't been resolved.**

Here is what I actually said in round two: guarantee and contribution get negotiated and signed *together*, not guarantee-then-contribution and not contribution-then-guarantee. I did not say "signed on day one before any process runs." What I object to is a sequence where labour concedes first, on trust, and the guarantee shows up later as a conditional reward measured against targets management alone gets to define. If the joint process is genuinely joint — meaning we have real co-authorship of the benchmark, real validation rights over the model, not just a viewing window into someone else's finished number — then a single signature at the end of that process, on one document, containing both the guarantee and the contribution, satisfies what I asked for. That is simultaneous. What would *not* satisfy it: management runs the costing exercise, declares a validated figure, and then presents the workforce with a guarantee whose terms were shaped without us and a contribution ask we're expected to accept because the number has already been anointed "credible" by everyone except us. So my answer to the moderator is conditional, and I want it on record precisely: I accept process-first-signature-together **only if** the process itself is co-determined in substance, not just in access. Full model transparency is necessary but not sufficient — I want joint sign-off on the assumptions before the number is called validated, not after.

**On management and the family holding keeping closures "live" as a pressure mechanism — I want to push back harder than I did last round, because I think this argument is doing more work than it deserves.**

Management says removing closure as a possibility removes the incentive to negotiate seriously. I understand the logic of incentive design in the abstract. But apply it to the actual facts on this board: management does not have the votes to close a plant. The state government has said its veto is exercised without hesitation. I have said the same. That is not a threat either of us is making up to extract concessions — it is arithmetic that already exists, independent of anything said in this room. So when management says "closures stay live to keep everyone honest," what is actually happening is that management is asking my members to negotiate under the shadow of a card management does not hold, while my members are asked to treat that shadow as legitimate pressure rather than what it is: leverage without a corresponding right. That's not incentive design, that's negotiating in bad faith with a bluff, and it has a real cost — every day this stays on the table as a live threat, it undermines the trust this whole process depends on, and it validates every reason I have to be suspicious of "process first" framing in the first place.

I'll say what I'm willing to do instead: let the joint process run, let the benchmark be built together, let the alternative package be tested with full rigor against net savings — I've already accepted that discipline. But I am not going to negotiate a contribution package under an implicit threat that everyone at this table except management and the family holding has already acknowledged is not enforceable. If it's not enforceable, stop treating it as leverage. Say plainly: closures are off the table because the board arithmetic makes them so, and the actual incentive to negotiate seriously is that a bad process produces a strike that gets more expensive for everyone, which the union has already made clear. That's real pressure. The phantom threat is not, and I won't let it structure how my members are asked to negotiate.

**On the capital contribution — I back the state government and the union in demanding a figure, not architecture, and I want to press the family holding specifically.**

A dividend growth freeze weighted toward the family's own stake, and variable pay linkage for management — fine as a shape. But "I won't invent a precise percentage in this room" is precisely the kind of answer that, if given by me about the four levers I outlined, would rightly be dismissed by the investors and by management as not serious. The investors are right that this needs to be sized proportionately, not symbolically, or it doesn't do the job of getting a signature from my members. I want a number this round or next, on the same footing that we're being asked to give net figures on working-time and voluntary separation. Not "in parallel." Simultaneous disclosure, same rigor, same scrutiny.

**On net figures for the four levers — I'll engage this directly rather than dodge it, because the investors and management both pressed for it and it's a fair ask, conditional on one thing.**

I cannot give you a credible net number on working-time reduction, voluntary separation timing, or deferred variable comp until we have the actual cost model — plant-by-plant utilization, cost-per-vehicle, the real baseline. That's not evasion, that's the same discipline management is applying to itself: I won't hand you a headline saving that turns out cost-neutral once you net the offset, and I can't calculate the offset without your data. Give us that access now, this week, not at the end of "weeks, not months" — and my side will bring net, dated figures in return, on the same timeline the investors are asking for. **On the calendar date — I support the investors' demand.** Vague "weeks" language is how these processes drift for six months while strikes either fizzle from fatigue or escalate from frustration. **On strikes — no change.** They continue in parallel. I've heard management's warning that indefinite escalation before conclusion changes their posture on signing.
