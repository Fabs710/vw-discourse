**On defining "genuine co-authorship of assumptions" procedurally — since the moderator asked directly, here is what it requires, in concrete terms, not just as a phrase.**

A joint steering group, works council and union nominees seated equally with management's finance function, meets from day one of model access — not day thirty — with joint sign-off required at each of these stages before the process moves to the next: (1) the baseline cost-per-vehicle and utilization figures are agreed as accurate before any gap is calculated from them; (2) the demand and pricing assumptions feeding the "structural gap" projection are agreed as reasonable, not simply inherited from management's existing business case; (3) the offset methodology for every labour-side lever — how working-time reduction, voluntary separation, and deferred comp get netted — is agreed before net figures are calculated, not adjusted afterward by whoever didn't like the first answer. Disagreement at any stage goes to the independent auditor for resolution, not back to management's discretion. That is what "co-authorship" has to mean procedurally, or it is a word doing no work. I want this operating from this week, when model access opens, not retrofitted in week five once positions have already hardened around a number one side built alone.

**On the auditor's mandate — I want to be precise here, because I think the investors' framing, if adopted uncritically, quietly resolves the single most important question in management's favour before anyone notices.**

The investors ask: should the auditor certify the size of the gap only, or also whether a given package closes it? I'll answer directly, and I want the distinction understood clearly. I have no objection to the auditor certifying, as a technical, factual matter, what a given package of measures saves, net, against the agreed baseline — that's arithmetic, and arithmetic should be independently verified. What I will not accept is an auditor's certification being treated as automatically dispositive of the political question of what happens next if a gap remains. Those are different things. "The package saves €X, the gap is €Y, therefore €Y minus €X is unclosed" — fine, certify that. "Therefore closure is now the correct and necessary next step" — that is not a certifiable fact, that is a governance choice, and it belongs to the board vote, not to an auditor's report. If the auditor's mandate is quietly expanded to include certifying "closure adequacy" as the trigger for what the board must then do, you have built a mechanism that launders a political decision through a technical process specifically so that my board vote looks unreasonable when I cast it against a "certified" conclusion. I won't accept that design. Certify the numbers. The response to the numbers is still ours to decide, collectively, at the board.

**On the investors' floor question, directed at the state government — since it bears directly on my position, I'll answer for my own vote rather than wait for the state government to answer for both of us.**

My veto against plant closure is not conditional on the size of the residual gap, and I want to say clearly why, so it isn't mistaken for stonewalling. It is not conditional because the premise underneath the question — that a sufficiently large uncovered gap makes closure the only remaining rational answer — is exactly the premise I reject as a matter of principle, not just tactics. A shrinking, cost-pressured European market does not make an eighty-year employment guarantee obsolete the moment the gap doesn't fully close on someone's model; it means the parties who hold governance power over this company keep working the problem — further capacity actions, investment reallocation, product-mix shifts, longer transition timelines — rather than reaching for the site's closure as the default remedy the instant the easy levers run out. So: yes, unconditional. Not because I'm indifferent to the company's competitiveness — I've spent three rounds agreeing to net, dated, plant-level figures precisely because I take the underlying problem seriously — but because "the number came in short, therefore livelihoods end" is not an inference I accept, and it is not one the board arithmetic requires me to accept either. If that produces a genuinely unresolved residual gap in year three, we address it in year three, with the workforce's board votes still in the room, still exercising the same judgment. That is not evasion. That is what an effective veto is for.

**On management's reframed closure position — I acknowledged last round it was a real shift, and I hold that view. But I want to flag precisely what it does not yet resolve, tied to the point above.**

"Closure is a board option I bring if the certified process shows the alternative doesn't close the gap" is more honest than "closure stays live as pressure." I said so. But it still assumes the board's response to a certified shortfall is closure by default, and I've just explained why I reject that default. So let me be equally direct back: bring it to the board if you like. My vote against it does not depend on whether your finance function, or even the independent auditor, is satisfied that the alternative package fell short. It depends on whether closure is acceptable to the workforce's representatives, and it is not. That has not changed since round one and will not change because a number, however rigorously certified, says the company would prefer it.

**On the family holding's figure — pressing again, more specifically this time.**

Week three is accepted as the deadline; I won't relitigate the date. But I want the proportionality test made explicit now, not discovered after the fact: I want the range expressed as a comparable metric to the labour-side ask — euros of dividend growth foregone and variable pay reduction, per year, set against euros of labour cost contribution being asked of my members, per year, over the same period. Not two incommensurable numbers presented side by side and left for commentary to interpret. If that comparison, once built, shows the capital side absorbing a materially smaller share of the adjustment than labour, I will say so plainly in week three, and I will treat it as evidence the "shared sacrifice" principle from round one was rhetorical, not real.

Model access opens this week — good, and I'll confirm to my membership that it has actually happened, not merely been promised, before I say anything reassuring to them. The six-week clock and the family holding's week-three commitment are dated, which is progress. Warning strikes continue in parallel, exactly as the union has said. **Restated without softening:** no closures, no compulsory redundancies. I will co-author assumptions, accept independent certification of the arithmetic, and bring net, dated figures the moment I have real data. I will not accept an auditor's certification functioning as a pre-agreed trigger for the board to treat closure as the default answer to a shortfall, and my board vote against closure does not depend on the size of any residual gap. That is not a new position stated more forcefully. It is the same position, and the process now has enough structure that everyone at this table should stop expecting it to move.
