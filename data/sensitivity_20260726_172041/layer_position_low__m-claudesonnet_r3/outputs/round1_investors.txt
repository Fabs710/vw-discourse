[Dispersed Institutional Investors (Capital Markets) — Round 1]

We don't sit on this board and we won't pretend our leverage is equal to those who do. But since every party in this room has now spoken about "shared sacrifice" and "credible paths," it's worth being explicit about what the capital side is actually watching, because it is not the same thing as political comfort or industrial peace for its own sake. It is margins, and it is durability.

Let me react to what's been said so far.

**On the two red lines.** We take no ideological position on closures versus alternatives. Our position is only this: whatever mechanism is chosen has to close the structural cost gap on a timeline that is visible in the numbers within a small number of reporting periods, not a decade. Management is correct that "no closures, no compulsory redundancies" without a matching costed alternative is a boundary with no content. We would add: it's also, from our seat, indistinguishable from every other restructuring cycle this company has run in the past fifteen years — voluntary programmes and attrition that softened headlines without moving return-on-sales. If the works council and union want the market to treat "no closures" as credible rather than as a repeat of history, the alternative package needs to show, in euros, that it closes the same gap on a comparable schedule. We are not persuaded by intent. We are persuaded by arithmetic that survives an analyst's model.

**On the joint costing process.** We support this, conditionally. A time-bound, transparent process that actually opens the cost model and produces a number the market can evaluate is exactly the kind of thing that could restore some confidence in this management team's credibility — which, frankly, has taken a hit every quarter this brand has missed its ROS target. What we will not tolerate well is this process becoming another year of "drift," as the state government put it. Weeks, as proposed, is the right order of magnitude. If this stretches into an open-ended negotiation with strikes continuing indefinitely in parallel, that is value destruction in real time, and we will read it as a governance failure regardless of who is technically at fault.

**On shared sacrifice — dividend policy and executive compensation.** We understand why the union and the state government are pushing this, and we won't pretend it's illegitimate. Visible restraint from management and ownership may well be the price of getting a workforce to accept a genuine contribution, and if that's what it takes to get a durable deal rather than another stalemate, that's a reasonable trade from a purely financial standpoint too — a strike-damaged quarter costs us more than a symbolic dividend adjustment. But we want this named for what it is: a legitimacy and negotiating instrument, not a substitute for the structural cost work. If "capital contribution" becomes the headline and the actual cost base is left materially unaddressed, we will treat that as a cosmetic settlement — and we will not support it. Do not mistake our tolerance for a symbolic gesture as tolerance for using that gesture to avoid the harder decisions.

**On site guarantees through 2030, unconditioned.** We agree with management and the family holding here without reservation. A blanket, board-enforceable guarantee across every plant, fixed before the savings package is even sized, is precisely the kind of commitment that locks in cost structure regardless of performance — that is the opposite of what we need. If guarantees are to exist, they belong at the plant level, tied to specific productivity and cost targets, reviewed against results. Anything broader reads to us as the company negotiating away its own flexibility before it has even measured the size of the problem it's solving.

**On the strikes.** We are not panicked by warning strikes as a signal in isolation — some disruption is a normal, even expected, cost of a serious renegotiation, and we don't reward capitulation to avoid short-term noise. But we are watching duration and trajectory closely. Prolonged, escalating industrial action that drags into next quarter without a visible path to resolution is the scenario that will show up in the share price and in our reallocation decisions, independent of who "wins" the standoff. We have other places to put capital. This company does not have unlimited time to demonstrate it can execute.

So, to state our position as plainly as everyone else has stated theirs: we will support whatever combination of instruments — closures, voluntary programmes, working-time changes, capacity cuts, a fairly sized capital contribution — actually delivers a credible, quantified, time-bound return to competitive margins. We have no attachment to any specific mechanism and no loyalty to any party in this room. What we will not support, and what would push us toward exit rather than patience, is a settlement that preserves employment and site guarantees at the cost of leaving the cost base structurally uncompetitive — dressed up as "peace" and revisited in another crisis two years from now. That outcome is the one genuinely unacceptable result from where we sit. Bring us the numbers from this joint process, on the timeline proposed, and we will judge the deal on its merits, not on which side made the most concessions along the way.
