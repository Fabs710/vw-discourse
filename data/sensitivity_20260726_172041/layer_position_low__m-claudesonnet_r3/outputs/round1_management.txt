I hear the arithmetic lesson on two-thirds majorities. I don't dispute the board math. What I dispute is the idea that "off the table, full stop" is a serious opening position rather than a pre-emptive veto dressed up as negotiation. Let me respond to both of you directly.

**On closures.** I did not put closures on the table because I enjoy confrontation. I put them there because every alternative measure this workforce has offered so far — voluntary programmes, attrition, working-time reduction — has been tried before, in previous cycles, and it did not close the structural cost gap. It bought time, not competitiveness. If you want closures genuinely off the table, you need to show me a package that closes the same gap without them. That's not a rhetorical challenge — it's the actual test. Saying "no closures" without a costed alternative that matches the savings is not a counter-proposal, it's a boundary with no content behind it yet.

**On compulsory redundancies.** I'm open to voluntary and demographic mechanisms as the primary route — I've said from the start I'm agnostic on *how* we get durable savings, not *whether*. But "voluntary only" has to actually deliver the headcount reduction on a credible timeline, not become a slow-walk that leaves cost structure untouched for three years while the market keeps shrinking under us. Show me the numbers add up on a timeline that matters, and I'll engage seriously on mechanism.

**On the union's "shared sacrifice" point** — this is the one argument here that actually lands, and I'll say so plainly. If we're asking for a genuine labour-cost contribution, management and ownership can't sit untouched. I'm prepared to discuss executive compensation restraint and to raise dividend policy with the family ownership and the state's board representatives — that's a fair precondition and I won't pretend otherwise. But be precise about scale: management compensation is a rounding error against the structural gap we're discussing. It matters for legitimacy and for getting your members to sign, not for closing the cost gap itself. Don't conflate the two.

**On site and investment guarantees.** In principle, yes — durable savings should buy durable product commitments, that's the whole logic of what I'm trying to do here: trade short-term pain for 2030 competitiveness, not just extract cost and leave you exposed again. But a *board-enforceable* guarantee through 2030 covering every plant, agreed before we've even settled the size of the savings package, is not something I can commit to blind. Guarantees get tied to specific plants that hit specific cost and productivity targets — not a blanket promise regardless of performance.

So here is where I actually stand after round one: the strikes don't change my read of the underlying numbers, and I won't pretend I'm alarmed by warning strikes at this stage — the cost gap doesn't shrink because output is disrupted for a few weeks. What would change my position is a credible, costed alternative package — real euros, real timeline, matching the structural savings I need — plus a genuine, verifiable labour and management contribution together. Bring me that, and closures become genuinely negotiable, including off the table entirely for plants that hit the target. Bring me "no closures, no layoffs, some pay restraint we'll define later," and we're not negotiating, we're posturing, and I'd rather you know that now than after another month of strikes.
