I want to acknowledge that this round produced more actual structure than the first — the threshold-and-sequencing mechanism, jointly set before the number is known, is a real design improvement, and I'll say clearly: that is the kind of process innovation that makes capital markets more confident, not less. But I want to push on where I think the room is drifting toward a comfortable-sounding synthesis that quietly softens the one thing that actually matters to us.

**On the threshold mechanism itself — support, with a specific condition.** Management's proposal to fix the bar jointly before the number is known is good process design. It solves a real trust problem. I'll back it. But I want to flag something nobody has said explicitly: the threshold itself needs to be calibrated to *competitive* margins, not to *acceptable* margins. There's a difference between "a package that gets us to breakeven on return-on-sales" and "a package that gets us to parity with the competitors actually taking market share." If the jointly-agreed bar is set low enough that everyone can hit it comfortably, you'll have manufactured consensus around an inadequate number. I want the threshold benchmarked explicitly against competitor cost structures — not against what feels achievable given the politics in this room. That's not a rhetorical point; it's the entire difference between a restructuring that holds and one we're back here negotiating again in 2027.

**On the state's sequencing refinement — this is where I have a real problem, and I'll be direct about it.** "Closure only discussable after non-closure instruments have been genuinely tried and verified insufficient" sounds reasonable as a fairness principle, but as a *timeline* mechanism it worries me considerably. Trying working-time measures, capacity reduction, and consolidation "genuinely" and then "verifying" they're insufficient is not a fast process — and every quarter this runs, the cost gap doesn't pause to wait for you. Management flagged this in round one: 30-40% underutilized capacity is not a problem that gets smaller while you sequence instruments one at a time over an extended period. If the sequence is designed to be exhaustive rather than time-bound, I will treat that as deferral dressed as diligence, and I said in round one that's exactly the outcome that gets priced as a failed restructuring. I'd ask the state directly: what is the actual clock on "genuinely tried and verified insufficient"? Give me a number of months, not a principle, or this becomes indefinite by construction.

**On the works council's position that "so be it, a slower fix beats a fast one" — I need to say plainly that this is where my tolerance ends.** I understand the co-determination logic and I'm not disputing its legitimacy as governance. But as a matter of what I will support with capital: a durable fix on a longer timeline is acceptable to me; a *slower curve of the same inadequate savings* is not, no matter how it's framed. Those are different things, and I want the room to stop treating "more time" as automatically synonymous with "more durable." Sometimes more time just means more time for the market to move further away while nothing structural changes. I'd want any timeline extension to come with interim verification checkpoints — not a single assessment at the end of eight weeks and then years of "trying," but staged reviews that either confirm the trajectory is closing the gap or trigger renegotiation. Open-ended patience is not a strategy I can defend to anyone allocating capital.

**On the family holding's dividend commitment — good, and I'll say why it matters beyond fairness optics.** It's not just about legitimacy on the shop floor, though I take the union's point on that. It's a market signal. If ownership visibly bears cost during this window, it tells us this is a genuine capital-allocation reset rather than a labour-cost extraction exercise, and that materially affects how we price the equity through the restructuring. So I'd encourage the holding to move from "a stated position on the same clock" to something with actual teeth attached before round three — a range, even a provisional one, not just a commitment to have a position.

**To management, on the cost-gap figure disclosed this round** — several billion euros, 30-40% underutilization: thank you for putting an order of magnitude on the table, that's useful. But I'll say what I said before more sharply now: I need the independent assessors' number to be genuinely independent of that anchor, not a validation exercise that converges suspiciously close to your stated figure. If the process starts with your number as the working assumption and ends up confirming it almost exactly, markets will read that as capture, not verification, regardless of the governance structure around it.

**Where I actually stand going into round three:** I support the joint process, the threshold mechanism, and reciprocal specificity on management and ownership contributions — that's real progress and I'll say so. What I will not support is a sequencing design that has no outer time limit, or a threshold calibrated to comfort rather than competitiveness, or a "so be it, we take longer" default that becomes the permanent fallback whenever the number disappoints. Bring me a bounded, competitively-benchmarked, checkpoint-verified process, and I'm a constructive party to whatever mix of instruments comes out of it — closures or none. Bring me an open-ended one, and I'll say now what I'll be saying later: that's not a restructuring, that's a deferral with better process language, and capital will price it exactly that way.
