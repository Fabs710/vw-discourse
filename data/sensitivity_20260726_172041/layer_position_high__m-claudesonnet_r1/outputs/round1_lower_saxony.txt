Let me set out where the state stands after hearing all three of you, because I think there's a real path here, but also real limits that I won't pretend away for the sake of sounding constructive.

To management: I take seriously that you didn't relitigate the past and that you're willing to put executive pay and dividend policy on the table. Good — that needed to be said plainly, and I'll hold you to it, not as a symbolic gesture but as a genuine line item. But I want to be equally plain back: "guarantees first is not a structure I can defend" is a position I understand from a management logic, but it is not one I will simply accept either. You are asking the workforce to open its books, quantify its contribution, and trust that closures become "genuinely negotiable" if the number is close enough — on your assessment of close enough. That is a lot of trust to extend to a management team that, as you yourself conceded, misjudged EV timing and cost position. I am not going to sit on this board and wave through a plant closure on the argument that an alternative package almost but didn't quite close a gap you define unilaterally. If closures are on the table, the methodology behind that cost gap — the numbers, the assumptions, the benchmarking against competitors — needs to be transparent to the works council, the union, and to us as a shareholder, not asserted from your side and taken on faith.

To the works council and the union: your red line on closures and compulsory redundancies is one I have real sympathy for, and historically I've stood with exactly that position. But I need to be honest with you too — I am not going to commit myself, right now, in round one, to an absolute veto exercised regardless of what the numbers eventually show. My statutory role is not simply to be labour's guarantor on the board; it's to protect this company's long-term viability as the anchor of this region's economy, because a company that isn't competitive in five years protects nobody's jobs. If your quantified contribution genuinely closes the gap, I will use every ounce of my blocking-minority weight to stop closures and enforce guarantees. If it's a fraction of the gap dressed up as a solution — management's phrase, but I'd use it too — then I have to weigh a hard truth: signing off on an unfunded peace deal now that unravels in eighteen months and forces a worse round of cuts later serves no one, least of all the workers I'm trying to protect.

So here's my actual position, and it's a mediating one, not a fence-sitting one: I will not authorize a two-thirds board resolution for closures without site and job guarantees on the table as part of the same package — sequenced together, negotiated in parallel, not "substance now, guarantees maybe later" as management wants, and not "guarantees now, unconditional" as the works council wants. I want to see the real cost-gap numbers, I want the union's quantified contribution scored against them by an independent process the workforce trusts, and I want executive and ownership contributions specified in the same document, not gestured at. If that process produces a credible package that saves sites through capacity and working-time measures plus a genuine shared contribution, I will back it fully and use my seats to make it stick against any side that later reneges. If management brings me closures without having seriously tested the alternative on fair terms, I will block it — that's not a threat, that's what the statute is for. And if the union escalates strikes before that process has even run, I'd say that also damages the region I'm accountable to, and I'll say so publicly.

That's where I stand: open to a negotiated restructuring that protects jobs and restores competitiveness, closed to being asked to ratify either an extraction dressed as necessity or a guarantee that isn't grounded in real numbers.
