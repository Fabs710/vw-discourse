We've listened to four rounds of positioning, and I want to bring this back to what actually matters for the people who supply this company's capital at market prices, without a board seat and without a veto — which is: does this restructuring produce a credible, durable return to competitive margins, or doesn't it?

So let me be blunt about where dispersed capital-market holders sit on each of the positions staked out so far.

To the works council: I understand the logic of the veto, and I'm not going to pretend co-determination doesn't exist. But "no closures, no compulsory redundancies, full stop, as a precondition for even discussing numbers" is not a position that reassures capital — it's the position that makes us start asking whether this company can restructure at all under its own governance, or whether every cost problem simply gets deferred until it's a crisis again. If that becomes the pattern, capital doesn't stay and argue about it. It reprices the equity and moves to competitors who can act. That's not a threat, it's just what dispersed holders do — we have no loyalty to defend, only returns to protect.

To the union: your offer of a quantified contribution is the most useful thing said so far, and I'd encourage you to put a real number on the table quickly, because that number is what determines whether this becomes a credible package or a slow-motion value destruction. But I'll push back on the framing that management and family ownership contributing "something" is a precondition equal in weight to whether the cost gap actually closes. Fairness matters to your members; it does not, on its own, restore margins. A package can be perfectly fair in its distribution of pain and still leave the cost base uncompetitive. That outcome is unacceptable to us regardless of how equitably the burden was shared getting there.

To management: I welcome that you won't accept a symbolic alternative package, and I welcome the openness to executive pay restraint — though let's be honest, that number is rounding error against a structural gap in the hundreds of millions, and I don't want it dressed up as more than it is. What I need from you is the methodology point the state raised, taken seriously: an independently credible cost-gap number, not a management-asserted one, because right now capital markets have no way to judge whether your figure is disciplined analysis or a negotiating anchor. If that number is opaque, we can't distinguish a real restructuring from a bargaining performance, and we'll price the uncertainty accordingly — which means a worse cost of capital for exactly the investment this company needs.

To the state and the family holding: the independent scoring process is the right instinct, and it's the only thing said today that could actually produce a number all sides trust. I'd support that mechanism strongly. But I'd flag a risk in the state's position specifically — "protecting the region's viability" and "protecting competitive margins" are not always the same test, and if push comes to shove between a site guarantee and a genuinely closed cost gap, dispersed capital cares about the second, not the first. We're not indifferent to social cost, but it isn't our mandate to weigh it.

Where we actually stand: we don't have a fixed view on closures versus alternatives — we're indifferent to the instrument. What we're not indifferent to is the outcome. If the independent process produces a package — whatever combination of capacity cuts, working-time changes, labour contribution, and, yes, site guarantees — that credibly and durably closes the return-on-sales gap on a timeline that doesn't just push the reckoning to 2027, we will back it, and the share price will reward it. If it produces a settlement that buys peace at the cost of leaving the German cost base structurally uncompetitive — however fairly distributed, however politically stable — we will treat that as a failed restructuring, and capital will act accordingly: lower multiple, higher cost of equity, pressure for further intervention down the line.

One more thing, directed at everyone: prolonged strikes are not a neutral backdrop for us. Every week of disruption erodes the exact margin base this whole negotiation is meant to protect. So while I won't tell the union to stand down — that's not my call to make — I'll say plainly that escalation without a credible process attached to it damages the very outcome all sides claim to want, including the workforce's own job security. Get the independent number, get it fast, and negotiate against it. That's the only path that keeps us at the table rather than at the sell button.
