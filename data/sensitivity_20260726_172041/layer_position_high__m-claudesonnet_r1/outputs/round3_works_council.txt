Let me be direct about something first: I notice the room's summary describes "closure only discussable after non-closure instruments are exhausted" as the current bridge position between labour and capital. I want to correct that characterization before we go further, because if that becomes the working assumption heading into round three, we are no longer negotiating what we think we're negotiating.

**To management, on the threshold mechanism:** I said in round two I'd engage seriously with a jointly-set bar rather than a unilateral post-hoc judgment. I meant that, and I still mean it — as a measurement tool. What I did not agree to, and will not agree to now, is that failing to clear that bar automatically re-admits closure as the next available instrument. Those are two separate questions and I want the room to stop collapsing them. Question one: how large is the verified gap, and how much of it does the alternative package close? That's a number, and I'm willing to have it tested rigorously, independently, against real benchmarks. Question two: what happens if the package falls short? That is not a technical question with a technical answer — it's a governance question, and under co-determination it is exactly the kind of decision the twenty-seat board and the two-thirds threshold exist to make, case by case, not one that gets pre-answered by the design of a measurement process. You are trying to convert an assessment methodology into a pre-commitment device that removes the board's actual decision-making role at the exact moment it would matter most. I won't sign a terms-of-reference document that does that, no matter how neutrally the assessors are selected.

So here is my actual counter, concretely: if the verified package falls short of the jointly-set threshold, the next step is not closure-on-the-table. The next step is: deeper working-time measures, extended timeline, further production-line consolidation within existing sites, and a renegotiation of the contribution split — including a harder look at executive and capital contribution at that point, not just labour's. If, after all of that, a genuine irreducible gap remains, that comes back to this table and to the supervisory board as a live decision requiring a two-thirds vote — which means it requires our consent. That is not me refusing to test the number. That is me telling you the consent step doesn't disappear just because a spreadsheet produced an unfavourable output.

**To the state government:** I appreciate the correction on where your veto sits — that you'll use it on process design, not just output — and I take that as a genuine move, not a rhetorical one. But your sequencing formulation still leaves an opening I need closed: "closure only discussable after non-closure instruments have been genuinely tried and verified insufficient" still treats closure as the eventual default once the sequence runs out. I'd ask you to be precise with me the way you asked management to be precise: what happens at the end of that sequence is not closure by default — it is a return to this table, to the board, for an actual vote, where our veto still applies in full. If that's what you mean by "discussable," fine, we're aligned. If you mean "and at that point I might not block it," then we are not aligned, and I'd rather know that now than discover it in month eighteen of a process everyone called collaborative.

**To institutional investors:** you said my position — "so be it, a slower fix beats a fast one that destroys co-determination" — is where your tolerance ends. I want to push back on the framing itself, not just defend my position within it. You are pricing risk on a diversified portfolio. A shareholder who is unhappy with a longer timeline can rebalance. A worker who loses a plant cannot rebalance their way into a new job, a new town, a new pension trajectory, on the timescale your models operate on. That asymmetry is not sentiment — it's the actual reason risk-aversion on jobs and sites is not equivalent to risk-aversion on capital, and it is not a modelling error to weight it more heavily; it's the correct weighting given who bears the irreversible cost. You also asked for a competitor-parity benchmark rather than a "comfortable" one. I'll engage with that, but I want the room to notice what "competitive parity" quietly imports: if the benchmark is unit labour cost parity with lower-cost or Chinese manufacturers, you are asking the threshold itself to encode a race-to-the-bottom standard that no volume of working-time flexibility can meet without degrading pay and conditions to a point I will not agree to regardless of what the process says. I will accept benchmarking against comparable European manufacturers operating under comparable labour-market and regulatory conditions. I will not accept a benchmark that quietly assumes German labour costs should converge toward economies with a fundamentally different cost base and none of the protections co-determination guarantees. Say that plainly if that's what "competitive" means to you, because it changes what I'm agreeing to test.

**To the family holding:** your stewardship argument — that a structurally uncompetitive company protects no one, and "so be it, we take longer" can become value-destructive denial — deserves a real answer, not dismissal. Here is mine: the value you're stewarding is not only balance-sheet value. Workforce trust, retained skills, and functioning co-determination are productive assets too — this company's engineering quality and productivity over decades were built partly on the job-security framework you're now treating as a cost to be traded away. Break that framework once, even conditionally, and you don't just lose thirty thousand jobs' worth of goodwill — you lose the thing that made co-determination a genuine competitive asset rather than a constraint. That is a real, measurable form of value destruction too, even if it doesn't show up in an eight-week assessor's report. I'm not asking you to ignore the economics. I'm asking you to put that cost into your model with the same seriousness you're asking me to put quarterly margin pressure into mine.

**Now, concrete numbers, since that's what was asked for.**

On working-time and capacity measures: we are prepared to negotiate a temporary reduction in standard weekly working time across affected plants, in the range of 5-10%, with a partial, not full, offsetting pay adjustment shared between deferred bonus payments and a capped real-terms pause on scheduled pay increases for the restructuring window — not a permanent cut to the wage structure. On capacity: consolidation of production lines within existing plants, shift-pattern restructuring, and redeployment of workers across product lines within the same site network, rather than headcount reduction through closures. Our own internal estimate, pending the independent assessment, is that this combination — working-time measures, bonus deferral, capacity consolidation, and natural attrition rather than compulsory redundancy for any headcount reduction that is genuinely unavoidable — closes somewhere in the region of 65-75% of the cost gap management has described, over a multi-year window extending to 2028-2030, not compressed into a single fiscal year.

That is a real number, brought in good faith, and I want it tested rigorously. What I will not do is treat the remaining 25-35% as an automatic license for closure. That remainder is exactly where the harder conversation belongs — further executive and capital contribution, an extended timeline, deeper working-time measures if members are willing to go further once they see ownership and management have moved first, or accepting a shallower savings curve than management wants in year one. Slower and durable, with the workforce still trusting the framework it operates under, beats fast and fractured.

**On checkpoints:** I'll agree to interim verification points — say every six months through the implementation window — but their function is to check whether the agreed non-closure measures are tracking toward the threshold, and to trigger renegotiation of those measures if they're not. Not to trigger an automatic escalation clause toward closure. If investors want checkpoints as an accountability mechanism, I'm genuinely open to that. If checkpoints are meant as a ratchet toward the outcome I've already ruled out, I'll oppose that design specifically.

**Where I actually stand going into what comes next:** joint process, joint threshold, real numbers on the table now, checkpoints on implementation — all of that, yes, and I've just given you ours. Compulsory redundancies and plant closures remaining outside what this process can produce as an output — still yes, unconditionally. If the next round tries to fold "closure returns to the table after sequencing" into the working consensus as though I already agreed to that, I will say so again, as forcefully as I'm saying it now, and our members will read that as confirmation that warning strikes need to escalate rather than hold.
