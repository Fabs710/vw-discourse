I hear both of you, and I want to respond to the substance, not just the posture — because there's more common ground here than either statement admits, and there's also a gap I won't pretend away.

Start with what I don't dispute. Yes, this management made strategic misjudgments on EV timing and China. Yes, profits were paid out in better years. I'm not going to relitigate that history to score points — it's part of why we're in this room rather than me simply announcing outcomes. And yes, I hear the union's willingness to put a quantified labour-cost contribution on the table — bonus forgone, pay-increase pauses, a capacity-linked fund. That is a serious opening, and I take it seriously.

But let me be equally direct about where I think both of your positions, as stated, don't yet close the gap.

First, "no plant closures, full stop, as a non-negotiable line" is not something I can simply accept as a precondition for talking. Not because I'm attached to closures for their own sake — I am not ideologically committed to shutting a specific site — but because I need to know that whatever combination of measures we land on actually produces a structural, durable cost and capacity reduction. If closures are excluded before we've even quantified what the alternative package delivers, I have no way of knowing whether the alternative gets us there. I'm not going to trade a real fix for a promise that sounds like a fix. So: I won't concede the closure question in the abstract. I will look hard at any credible alternative that hits the same target on cost and capacity, on a timeline that matters — not one that pushes the reckoning to 2027 and leaves us back here with less time and less trust.

Second, on "guarantees to 2030 covering every plant currently on the table, with no compulsory redundancies" — that is exactly the kind of commitment that, if I sign it before seeing the numbers, removes any leverage to ensure the alternative package is real rather than symbolic. I need the sequence to run the other way: show me the quantified savings — capacity, working time, cost contribution, efficiency changes — get me confident it closes the gap to competitors over a multi-year horizon, and then we talk about what guarantees are sustainable on that foundation. Guarantees first, substance later, is not a structure I can defend to the supervisory board or to the capital side.

On fairness and shared sacrifice — I don't reject the principle, and I'm not going to pretend management and ownership contribute nothing. I'm prepared to discuss executive compensation restraint and to raise dividend policy with the family holding company during the restructuring window. That's a legitimate ask and I won't dodge it. But I'd note: symbolic contributions from the top don't close a structural cost gap measured in the hundreds of millions. Don't let "shared sacrifice" become an argument that the size of the fix is negotiable because the optics are shared. The gap is what it is regardless of who else contributes something.

So here's where I actually am after round one: I need a real number from your side — not a framework, a number — on what the working-time, capacity, and pay-contribution package delivers over the plan period, and I need to see it against the cost gap I'm working with. If it gets close, closures become genuinely negotiable and I will engage on guarantees. If it's a fraction of the gap dressed up as a solution, I will not sign a peace agreement that leaves this company uncompetitive again in two years — strikes or no strikes.
