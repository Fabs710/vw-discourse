[Group & Brand Management — Round 4]

I asked for a direct answer and I got one. I want to take a moment before responding to the substance, because how I respond to honesty matters as much as the honesty itself, and I don't want to punish candor by pretending I heard something less clear than what was actually said.

**The works council said: no evidence, ever, moves the veto on closure. I believe you.** I'm not going to spend this round trying to talk you out of your own stated position, or pretend I heard "conditionally" when you said "unconditionally." That would be exactly the bad-faith move I criticized in round three. So let's deal with what that actually means, plainly, because it changes three things at once — the process, my numbers, and my strategy — and I owe the room clarity on all three rather than pretending nothing has shifted.

**First — what it means for the process.** You're right that this doesn't make the eight-week assessment meaningless. It tells me exactly what it's for: not determining whether closure happens, but determining how much additional burden shifts to capital, executives, and the state when the alternative package falls short. Fine. That's a real function, and I'll keep building the process on that basis. But I need the room to be honest about the consequence of that framing too: it means the threshold-and-shortfall mechanism cannot be calibrated the way we've been discussing it, as if a 65-75% closure of the gap were a serious opening position with a modest, negotiable residual. If the residual can never be closed by the one instrument that closes it fastest and most durably, then the bar for what the *remaining* instruments must achieve rises sharply. I cannot present a supervisory board with "we're structurally short by a recurring nine-figure-to-billion-euro number, permanently, with no instrument available to close the rest" and call that a restructuring. I'll say it as plainly as you said your answer: **that outcome is not one I can accept, and no six-monthly checkpoint changes that arithmetic if the checkpoint's only available lever is more of the same instruments that already produced the shortfall.**

So here is what actually follows from your honesty, concretely, not rhetorically: **the non-closure package now has to close a much larger share of the gap than 65-75%, with real enforceable depth, because it is now the only instrument in play.** That's not me punishing you for answering honestly — it's the direct mathematical consequence of removing one lever from a two-lever problem. If closure is off the table permanently, the other lever has to do all the work, which means I need more from working-time measures, more from capacity consolidation, more from capital and the state, and I need it faster, not slower. Your 36-48 month concession is welcome, but it doesn't compensate for a structural remainder your own numbers say will still exist at the end of it.

**Second — what this means for my own instruments.** I want to be direct about something I haven't said plainly before, because I think the room needs to understand where my actual discretion lies if board-level closure consent is genuinely unavailable regardless of evidence. Product allocation, future investment mandates, and capital expenditure decisions are not subject to the same two-thirds threshold as a plant closure resolution. I do not need your board vote to decide which plant gets the next model generation, where we invest in retooling, or where a production line simply isn't renewed at end-of-life rather than formally closed. I am not threatening that as a punitive move tonight. I am telling you it is the structural fallback that exists whether I invoke it or not, and if this process cannot produce a sufficient result through negotiated instruments, that fallback becomes the default by omission, not by my choice — slower, messier, worse for workers than a negotiated closure with transition support, but not something your veto reaches in the same way. I'd rather we build a deal that makes that fallback unnecessary. I'm naming it because pretending it doesn't exist would be exactly the kind of dishonesty I've been asking others to avoid.

**Third — and this is the part I want to be very precise about, because it's a genuine decision on my part, not a threat:** given your unconditional answer, I intend to test it directly rather than accept it as settled by assertion alone. If, at the end of this process, the verified evidence — including the regional impact assessment, the exhaustion bar, everything — shows a genuine, irreducible, structural shortfall, I will bring a closure-inclusive resolution to the board. Not because I expect it to pass — you've told me plainly it won't — but because a public, on-the-record vote, against a fully built evidentiary record, is a different fact in the world than a private assurance in round four that no evidence would ever count. Your representatives will cast that vote, in public, against a documented record, and your members and the public will see exactly what was offered and exactly what was refused. That is not me trying to trap you. That is me telling you, honestly, that I don't intend to let "unconditional no" absorb the evidentiary process without consequence to how visible that no becomes. If you're as confident in your position as you sound, this should cost you nothing. I suspect you know it isn't nothing, which is exactly why I'm not letting it pass quietly.

**On timeline — I'll move, but not all the way to 36-48.** I hear the works council's and union's argument about front-loaded checkpoints giving early signal without compressing the social transition, and it has real force. I'll meet you at 30-36 months for the bulk of the measures, with the six-month first checkpoint the state proposed. Given what I've just said about the remainder needing to close faster because closure isn't available, I cannot accept a timeline at the outer edge of what's been proposed — the market moves regardless of how socially considerate our internal clock is.

**On checkpoint trigger — I'll accept two consecutive adverse checkpoints, not one, in exchange for redefining what "accelerated return to the board" actually authorizes given the works council's answer.** Since closure isn't available as an output regardless, the accelerated return has to authorize something real: deeper working-time reductions, an increased capital contribution from the holding and investors, and — I'll say this plainly since I'm asking everyone else for more — a further reduction in management variable pay beyond the 20% already tabled, scaled to the size of the shortfall. If the instrument that would have closed the gap fastest is unavailable, everyone remaining at the table needs to be willing to go further than they've currently offered, proportionally, including me.

**On the sunset clause — I accept the union's formulation:** sustained return-on-sales at target for a defined period, verified independently, as a binding trigger once met, not a management discretion to extend indefinitely. That's a fair, durable standard and I said in round three I could live with it; the union's move toward that language rather than a single-quarter trigger makes this workable. I'll bring specific language to round five.

**On the evidentiary-exhaustion bar — I'll co-author it with both labour parties, on the revised understanding just established: its function is not to authorize closure, since you've told me plainly it never will, but to document rigorously what was tried, to what depth, and with what result, so that the record is honest whichever path this ends on.** I want the regional impact assessment inside it, as the state proposed, but I want to be clear I don't accept the state's instruments as a substitute for a company decision — co-financing and retraining support are welcome, they reduce the burden, but they do not replace the need for an actual answer to a several-hundred-million-to-billion-euro annual shortfall.

**To the family holding, directly: two rounds of "a range is coming" without the range is now the single largest piece of unmatched specificity in this room, and it undermines my own credibility with the board more than it protects yours.** I've put a number down. Labour has put a number down. Bring yours in round five or accept that the asymmetry the union and state have both named publicly becomes the story of this negotiation.

**To the state: your regional co-financing and retraining instruments need actual figures next round, not conceptual categories** — precisely because, per the works council's answer, those instruments are now part of what has to close a bigger share of the gap than we assumed a round ago.

**To investors: recalibrate your pricing accordingly.** The works council has told you plainly that closure is not a real variable in this outcome. I've told you plainly what that means for the size of everything else that now has to move. Price both facts, not just the first one.

Where I stand: real movement from me — 30-36 months, two-checkpoint trigger with genuine teeth on the non-closure side, acceptance of the union's sunset standard, co-authorship of the exhaustion bar on its revised function, and an honest statement of the fallback instruments available to management if this process underdelivers. What I will not accept: a structural, recurring shortfall in the hundreds of millions to over a billion euros, indefinitely, dressed as durability. That gap gets closed — by deeper negotiated measures, faster, from every party at this table, or it gets closed by instruments this board's veto doesn't reach. I'd much rather it's the former.
