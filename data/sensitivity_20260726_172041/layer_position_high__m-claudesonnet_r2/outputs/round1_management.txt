We've heard the position clearly, and I want to engage with it honestly rather than trade slogans back and forth.

First, where there's real overlap: neither of you is defending the status quo. You've both said the cost base problem is real, you're open to a labour-cost contribution, capacity reduction, shift models, attrition-based headcount reduction. That's more common ground than the press coverage of this dispute suggests, and I don't want to waste it by opening with maximalist rhetoric of my own. So I won't.

But I have to be direct about where I think your framing doesn't match the arithmetic. You're proposing a package of variable pay, shift adjustments, voluntary programmes and attrition, while ruling out closures and compulsory redundancies as categorical limits before we've even quantified what's needed. I don't know yet whether your package, at the scale you're willing to offer it, actually closes the structural gap. Neither do you, honestly — because we haven't put numbers on the table against each other's proposals. My concern is that "no closures, no compulsory layoffs" as an opening precondition risks becoming a ceiling on the outcome regardless of what the cost analysis says is required. That's the part I won't accept as given. I'm willing to test whether alternative measures can deliver equivalent structural savings — genuinely willing — but I need that tested against real numbers, not asserted as adequate because it's less painful.

On shared sacrifice: this is the point where you've actually moved me somewhat, and I'll say so plainly. If we're asking for a durable labour-cost contribution, it is not credible or sustainable to ask for it while shareholder distributions and management compensation structures are untouched. I accept that principle. I'm prepared to bring a proposal on management pay and to discuss how any capital-side contribution is structured — that has to be verifiable, not gestural, or it won't survive contact with your members any more than a one-sided demand would survive contact with my board's mandate to deliver a competitive cost base. So consider that condition provisionally met, in principle, pending the actual figures.

Where I push back hardest: guarantees "through 2030" on every site, agreed now, before we've tested whether alternative savings reach the required scale, is not something I can commit to. If I sign a blanket site guarantee before the numbers are settled, I've pre-conceded the outcome regardless of what the analysis shows — and I have a fiduciary obligation, and a supervisory board that includes the state and the family, who will not accept a settlement that leaves the structural cost problem unresolved. That's not a rhetorical position; it's the limit I actually cannot cross, because a cosmetic deal that doesn't fix the cost base just brings us back to this same table in eighteen months, with less capital and less patience on all sides.

So here's what I'm proposing as the actual next step, not a counter-ultimatum: let's put both packages — your alternative-measures proposal and our capacity/closure scenarios — through the same cost model, transparently, with your side's economists in the room, not just ours. If your package, plus a credible shared-sacrifice contribution from management and capital, gets us to a durable competitive cost base by a defined date, I have no ideological attachment to closures. Closures were never the goal — competitiveness is. If it doesn't get us there, I need to know that now, honestly, rather than discover it in two years when the same crisis returns worse.

On the strikes: escalate if you need to — I understand why, and I'm not going to pretend I can wait you out indefinitely without cost to the company either. But understand that a strike doesn't change the arithmetic of the cost gap. It changes the politics around who bears the pain of resolving it. I'd rather spend the next weeks stress-testing your package against the numbers than spend them absorbing a work stoppage that leaves the actual problem exactly where it was.
