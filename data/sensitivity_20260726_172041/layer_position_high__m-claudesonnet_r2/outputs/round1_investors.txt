I'll speak plainly, because that's what this room needs from the capital side rather than another restatement of good intentions.

We don't have votes on this board. We can't block closures, and we can't force them either. What we do is price the outcome — continuously, in real time, through the share price and through whether capital keeps flowing to this company or moves to competitors who've already solved this problem. So let me tell you how we're actually reading this discussion so far, because it differs from the framing some of you are using.

We have no ideological view on closures versus alternative measures. None. That distinction matters enormously to the works council and the union, and it matters politically to the state, but from where we sit it is a second-order question. The first-order question is: does whatever emerges from this process durably restore the return-on-sales target and competitive unit costs, on a credible timeline, with real enforcement mechanisms — or does it not. Everything else is implementation detail.

On that basis, here's where I actually agree and disagree with what's been said.

I agree with management and the family holding that a joint, transparent cost model is the right next step, and I'd add: we want to see it too, or at least its outputs and assumptions, because that's exactly the kind of disclosure that determines whether we treat the eventual settlement as credible or cosmetic. If this process produces a deal that's presented as resolved but the underlying model shows the gap isn't actually closed, we will not wait around to find out the hard way. We'll mark it down immediately, and reallocate. That's not a threat, it's just how capital behaves when it doesn't trust a number.

I agree with the union and the state that shared sacrifice on the capital side is not unreasonable — dividend adjustment during a defined restructuring window, tied to a credible turnaround, is something we can absorb without changing our fundamental read of the company. We are long-only holders in many cases, we understand cyclicality, we understand that a competent multi-year fix is worth more to us than a payout preserved at the cost of the underlying business degrading further. So don't mistake us for a constituency that will punish any dividend flexibility — we'll punish an unresolved cost base far more severely than we'll punish a temporary payout cut with a clear rationale.

Where I part from the works council and the union: "no closures, no compulsory layoffs" as a fixed precondition, stated before the numbers are run, is exactly the kind of thing that makes us nervous, regardless of how it's justified socially. Not because we're attached to closures — we're not — but because pre-committing to a ceiling on the toolset before you know whether the remaining tools reach the required savings is how you end up with a settlement that buys peace and not competitiveness. If the joint cost model comes back and shows that shift changes, attrition, capacity reallocation and a labour contribution genuinely close the gap, fine — that's a fine outcome, and honestly a better one from a disruption-avoidance standpoint. But if it comes back short, and the response is political and social pressure to sign anyway, that is the scenario in which we start moving capital elsewhere, and that's not a bluff, it's just what a dispersed shareholder base does when it loses conviction in the story.

And to the regional state government directly: I understand your mandate and I'm not disputing your right to use your veto. But I want to be honest about how markets will read a veto that blocks closures "full stop" regardless of what a rigorously modelled analysis shows is required. That reads as a structural constraint on the company's ability to fix itself, not as a negotiating position — and it will be priced as one. It doesn't have to play out that way. If the alternative package is genuinely sufficient, your position and ours converge completely. I'm only flagging that if it isn't sufficient, and your veto holds anyway, that's the point where we conclude the German operating model can't self-correct, and that has consequences for how this stock and this credit are rated for years, not just this cycle.

So, concretely, what would move us, and what wouldn't:

We'd support — and would be vocal in supporting, through the channels available to us — a settlement built on alternative measures, attrition, capacity and shift adjustments, and a labour contribution, provided the joint cost model shows it closes the gap to a competitive cost base on a defined timeline, and provided management and capital contributions are real and verifiable, not gestures. That is a genuinely acceptable outcome to us. We are not demanding closures.

We would not support, and would actively penalize through capital reallocation, a settlement where the numbers don't add up but get signed anyway under political or industrial pressure, dressed up with site guarantees that pre-empt the analysis. That is the cosmetic-deal scenario everyone here claims to want to avoid — management said it, the family said it, the state said it in different words. I'm simply telling you what happens to the share price and the cost of capital if that's what this actually produces despite the rhetoric.

Run the model. Show the work. Make the contributions real on all sides, including ours. We'll follow the evidence, not the theatre.
