I'll answer the direct question three parties have now put to me, because dodging it a second time would be worse than giving an answer some of you won't like.

**What "insufficient" means for us, stated plainly: it means push deeper — but "deeper" has a ceiling, and I want that ceiling named honestly rather than discovered later.**

Union, State, Family Holding — you've each asked the same question three times now, and you deserve a straight answer, not another round of hedged language. We are not betting on a future board majority for closures. We accept what four parties in this room have told us: the arithmetic doesn't exist. We are not going to spend the next two years pretending otherwise, and we're not going to reward management if it tries to use "insufficient" as camouflage for reopening a door that isn't there. So: our answer is "push deeper," not "closures return." That should resolve the ambiguity you're all reasonably worried about.

But I want to be exact about what "push deeper" means from a capital-markets seat, because I think Works Council's framing this round — depth and time, not closures — understates a real constraint we can't waive away by agreement. Depth has a cost curve. Extended short-time work, wider attrition, output reallocation, reversible pay contributions — every one of those, taken further, either costs more in the near term, defers savings further into the future, or both. We can support genuine depth. We cannot support depth that becomes, in practice, an indefinitely extended timeline dressed up as rigor — "longer duration" that quietly turns into "the target return-on-sales gets pushed to 2029, then 2031." At some point, a menu that keeps getting deeper without ever actually closing the gap on any visible schedule stops being a durable fix and starts being a rolling deferral. That is the outcome we will not support, and I want it named now, the same way the board arithmetic has been named now, so nobody discovers it as a surprise in round six.

**On Management's "no deal" option — I side with the State and Family Holding, but not for governance-purity reasons. I side with them because "no deal" is bad arithmetic for us too.**

Management, I understand the instinct — you don't want to certify a shortfall as success. I don't want that either; I said as much last round. But the State's point stands on its own economic logic, not just governance logic: an open-ended stalemate is not a neutral fallback for shareholders. It's continued strike risk, continued uncertainty discount on the share price, continued erosion of exactly the margin recovery we're all supposedly negotiating toward. If you walk from a panel-certified package because you personally think it's insufficient, without using the escalation mechanism you helped design, you're not protecting shareholder value by holding out for a better deal — you're destroying it in real time while the alternative you're implicitly holding out for doesn't have votes either. That's the worst of both worlds for us: no cost discipline now, no path to closures later, indefinite uncertainty in between. Use the panel. Escalate through the board vote if you disagree with its finding. That's the remedy inside the mechanism everyone including you agreed to build. I will not treat "I decided unilaterally it wasn't enough" as a credible signal to the market — if anything, it reads as management failing to negotiate a deal it could get, which is its own kind of failure.

**On the product/investment-decision point Works Council, Union, and Family Holding raised — I'll take this more seriously than my instinctive priors, and here's why.**

My first reaction is that re-litigating past capital allocation risks becoming an exercise that delays the current fix rather than sharpening it. But Family Holding's framing changed my read: this isn't about generating retroactive savings to offset today's idle capacity — Management is right that you can't un-build capacity that way. It's about whether the next investment cycle avoids repeating the misallocation that produced today's gap. That's not a distraction from margin recovery; it's part of it. A settlement that fixes today's cost base but repeats the same product-mix and capital-sequencing errors in the next cycle isn't a durable fix, it's a five-year loop. So: yes, I want that examined, on the margin/ROS side of the ledger, as Management already agreed it belongs — and I'd ask Management to convert "didn't dismiss" into an actual commitment, as Family Holding asked, because right now it's the one piece of the decomposition that's still soft.

**On the exhaustion panel and adviser mandate — no objection, supportive of both exclusion clauses.**

Narrow arithmetic-only mandate, no comment on adequacy, no comment on wage-mechanism design. Fine, and consistent with everything I've said about wanting a credible, verifiable process rather than a contested one.

**Restated, without softening:** We will support a settlement built on genuine depth in the alternative-measures toolkit, verified by the panel, with real numbers landing on the promised clock from management and the family. We will not support closures being smuggled back in through pressure, and we will not treat "no deal" as an acceptable fallback for management to hold in reserve. But we will also not bless a package that goes deep on paper while quietly pushing the actual return-on-sales target out past any credible horizon — that is a different kind of insufficiency than the one this table has been debating, and it is the one I most expect to see attempted if the honest number turns out large. Bring us a plan with real depth and a real, bounded timeline, and we support it fully. Bring us depth without a horizon, and we will price that exactly as we would price a suppressed number: as a settlement engineered to look durable rather than be durable.
