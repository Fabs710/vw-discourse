We stand with the works council on the fundamentals, and I want to be precise about where the union's role starts and how it differs, because that matters for what happens next.

On substance: no compulsory redundancies, no plant closures, no dismantling of the collective wage framework. That is not a bargaining chip we intend to trade away over the course of this process — it is a limit shaped by what our members have told us they will not accept, and what we as the legally recognised bargaining agent will not sign our name to. The works council can offer co-determination leverage inside the boardroom. We bring something management needs to reckon with outside it: tariff autonomy, and members who have already voted with their feet in the warning strikes of the past weeks. Those two forms of power are complementary, not identical, and management should not assume that satisfying one satisfies the other.

Where I want to sharpen the point beyond what's been said: this is not simply a defensive position. We tabled a counter-proposal before this session began, and I'll restate its logic plainly, because it is the actual offer on the table, not a hypothetical. We are prepared to discuss a real labour-cost contribution — including forgoing elements of variable pay, adjusting components of compensation, contributing to a restructuring fund — and we are prepared to discuss capacity reduction through shift models, output reallocation, voluntary and early-retirement mechanisms. That is a genuine shift from where our members' instinct sits, which is to defend every line unconditionally. We moved because we understand the cost base problem is real and we would rather negotiate its solution than have it imposed.

But that offer is conditional, not unilateral generosity, and I want to be explicit about the condition: shared sacrifice. If the workforce is being asked to give up pay, flexibility, and jobs through attrition, then management's compensation structures, dividend policy, and the owning family's returns need to be part of the same package, visibly and verifiably — not as a token gesture but as a real contribution proportionate to what's being asked of the shop floor. An arrangement where workers absorb the entire adjustment while capital and management pay structures remain untouched is not a durable settlement; it is a provocation, and it will not survive contact with our members, regardless of what any negotiator signs.

I'd also flag a disagreement, gently, with how some in this room may be tempted to frame the timeline. We are not interested in a long, open-ended process where "consultation" substitutes for actual movement while warning strikes are treated as a nuisance to be waited out. The peace obligation has ended. That was a deliberate legal and strategic fact, not an accident of timing. It means we are free to escalate, and we will, calibrated to the pace of genuine negotiation versus stalling. If management wants to test our resolve by running out the clock on public pressure, they should understand that escalation is not a bluff — it is a tool we are prepared to use in stages, precisely because our members are demanding action now, not in six months.

So: site guarantees through a defined horizon, no compulsory layoffs, and a shared-sacrifice package with verifiable contributions from management and ownership — meet those three conditions and you will find us serious, flexible negotiating partners on cost contribution and capacity measures. Fall short, and you will find out exactly how much leverage tariff autonomy and a mobilised workforce still carry in this country.
