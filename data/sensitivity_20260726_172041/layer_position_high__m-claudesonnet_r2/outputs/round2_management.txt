Let me take these one at a time, because both of you have sharpened the disagreement rather than papering over it, and I'd rather meet that directly than pretend we're closer than we are.

**On the model's design and whether closures are a live variable or a "dead letter":**

I hear the logic — the board arithmetic is what it is, so why spend analytical effort modelling something that can't pass a vote. But I have to reject that framing, and I want to be precise about why, because it's not stubbornness for its own sake.

If we build the model with closures excluded as a boundary condition rather than as a tested variable, we lose the one thing the model is actually for: a benchmark. Without a closure scenario run through the same cost assumptions, nobody in this room — including me — has a rigorous answer to the question "how much did the alternative-measures package actually cost us in savings, compared to the harder option, and was it worth the trade?" That comparison isn't an idle intellectual exercise. It's what I take to my supervisory board to argue for the alternative package with credibility, instead of asking them to trust that it's "enough" on faith. It's also what the institutional investors need to see to judge whether this is a real fix or a negotiated peace. If I walk into that board meeting with a model that was designed from the outset to never quantify the option my board fiduciaries will ask about, I've undermined my own case for the very outcome you want me to accept.

So: the model tests both. That doesn't mean I'm holding closures hostage over the negotiation — both the state and the family have told me plainly where the votes sit, and I'm not naive about that. It means I want the comparison on paper, honestly, so that when I recommend the alternative package, I'm doing it because the numbers support it, not because I ran out of options to model.

**On the Union's distinction between capacity/utilisation gap and the return-on-sales target — this is the sharpest point raised today, and I'll engage with it directly rather than wave it off.**

You're right that these are analytically different problems, and I'll agree now: the model must decompose the gap explicitly — how much is excess capacity relative to shrunk demand, versus how much is a margin target that reflects what this brand needs to earn to fund its own product and technology investment through 2030. I'm not going to pretend those are the same number, and I won't let the model blur them to make the ask on labour look bigger than it is.

But I won't accept the implication that follows — that because part of the gap is "a profitability aspiration," labour cost isn't a legitimate lever for it. Here's why: unit labour cost at excess capacity is precisely the mechanism connecting the two problems. Idle capacity spreads fixed labour cost over fewer units, which depresses margin on every car we build. Closing the capacity gap through shift models and attrition *is* margin restoration, in large part — they're not two separate asks stacked on top of each other, they're substantially the same fix viewed from two angles. Where I'll agree with you is that if, after the capacity fix, there's a residual margin gap that's really about pricing power, product mix, or capital allocation rather than headcount, that residual shouldn't be closed by squeezing labour further — that's a fair boundary, and I'll hold to it. But don't ask me to accept that the ROS target is illegitimate as a frame. It's the target that funds the electrification and product investment this whole workforce needs the company to survive making, ten years from now.

**On management's compensation contribution — you're both right that "pending figures" isn't good enough, and I'll fix that now.**

I'll commit to this: a concrete proposal on management compensation structure — variable pay reduction, bonus deferral tied to the same restructuring metrics, duration matched to the restructuring window — on the table within two weeks, in parallel with the joint model's design phase, not after its results land. I'm not going to let my side's contribution lag behind the granular scrutiny yours is under. That asymmetry would be fair criticism if I let it stand, so I won't.

**On jointly agreed model assumptions before running it:**

Agreed, with one condition of my own: joint design means joint design, not one side vetoing scenarios the other wants tested. If your economists sit down with mine and we build shared assumptions on demand forecasts, cost baselines, and savings mechanics together, that's real co-determination in the process, and I want that too — it protects me from being told after the fact that my numbers were rigged. But joint design cannot mean the closure scenario gets vetoed from the model before it's built. Test everything on agreed assumptions; argue about the conclusions after.

**On the escalation warnings — from both of you, and echoed by the state's political exposure:**

I'm not dismissing the cost of a prolonged confrontation — I said as much last round, and the family holding and investors have both flagged the same risk from their side. But I'd ask you to hear this plainly: escalation pressure changes the politics of who absorbs the pain, it doesn't change the arithmetic of the cost base. If the model comes back showing the alternative package falls meaningfully short, no amount of strike action makes the shortfall disappear — it just determines who's exhausted first when we're still sitting at this table looking at the same gap. I'd rather spend the next month building a model neither side can credibly dismiss than spend it finding out where everyone's breaking point is through attrition of trust and output.

So, concretely: joint assumptions, decomposed gap, closures modelled as comparison not foreclosed, my compensation proposal in two weeks. That's where I am. I still cannot sign a blanket guarantee, or agree to exclude an option from analysis, before that work is done — not because I'm holding out for closures, but because pre-deciding the answer is exactly the failure mode all of us claim to want to avoid.
