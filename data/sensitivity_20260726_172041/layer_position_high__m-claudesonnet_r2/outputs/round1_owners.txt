I've listened to all three positions, and I'll be brief, because the holding operates through governance, not through the press or public argument. But this discussion deserves a clear signal of where the family stands.

First: we support management's push for a durable competitiveness fix. That is not a preference among many options — it is the precondition for everything else. The holding's wealth is concentrated in this company. A settlement that preserves labour peace but leaves the structural cost gap open is not stability, it is deferred value destruction, and we have seen that film before in this industry. So when the union says "shared sacrifice or no deal," and management says "test the numbers before conceding outcomes," those two things are not in tension from our seat. We want the numbers tested honestly, and we accept that if a credible cost model shows capital and management need to contribute verifiably, they should.

Second, on shared sacrifice specifically: I will not pretend this doesn't touch us directly. If dividend policy needs to adjust to fund this transition credibly, the family holding is prepared to accept that, provided it is proportionate, temporary in scale to the restructuring window, and tied to a plan that actually restores margins — not an open-ended transfer with no defined endpoint. We are long-horizon owners. We can absorb a lower payout for several years if it buys a genuinely competitive company at the end of it. We cannot accept a permanent structural drag dressed up as solidarity.

Third, on closures and site guarantees: I will not pre-commit the family's board votes to blanket guarantees through 2030 before the cost model is run — that would be exactly the kind of unconditional concession management rightly warns against, and it would bind us regardless of what the analysis shows. But I also won't pretend the arithmetic on this board is anything other than what the works council and the state have both stated plainly: closures require two-thirds, and two of the blocking parties have just told you they won't provide it. Management should treat that as a fact to plan around, not a negotiating position to erode. Pursuing a closure-centric path that cannot pass this board is not strategy, it is a way to burn a year and both sides' trust for nothing.

So our actual position: run the joint cost model, as management and the state both proposed. If alternative measures — capacity reduction, shift changes, attrition, a real labour-cost contribution, matched by a real capital and management contribution — close the structural gap credibly and durably, we will use our position on the supervisory board to back that outcome and to press management to commit to it properly, including reasonable site commitments once the numbers justify them. If they don't close the gap, we will not indefinitely shield an unresolved cost problem out of sentiment, and we will say so then — but that is a conversation for after the analysis, not before it.

What we will not accept, from any side: a prolonged public confrontation that damages the brand and the balance sheet while everyone waits for the other to blink. That path erodes exactly what we are here to protect — the long-term value of this asset — regardless of who "wins" the standoff. Get to the table, run the numbers honestly, and bring us a plan with real, verifiable contributions from all sides. That is what will move us. Ultimatums in either direction will not.
