I want to engage with the specific procedural fight that just happened, because it's the most consequential thing said in this round, and I disagree with where the state and family holding landed on it.

**On excluding closures from the joint model — I side with management here, and I want to explain why precisely, not just assert it.**

The state and family holding have both now argued that modelling closures alongside alternatives is not "rigor," it's "keeping a dead option alive" and creating ammunition. I understand the political logic. But from a capital-markets standpoint, this is exactly backwards, and I want to be direct about why.

We are being asked to underwrite a settlement — through the share price, through continued capital access, through how this credit gets rated — that excludes, by governance fiat, the scenario that would tell us how much the alternative package actually costs relative to the harder option. You're not just refusing to *execute* closures, which is your prerogative given the board arithmetic. You're refusing to let anyone *quantify* the comparison. Those are different things, and conflating them is exactly the "cosmetic deal" risk I flagged last round.

Here's the concrete problem: if the eventual settlement is "alternative measures plus shared sacrifice, closes the gap," I need to know that's true because a number says so, not because the only number that exists is the one for the plan that was always going to be adopted regardless of what it showed. A model that only tests the pre-approved outcome isn't a model, it's a justification exercise. Markets can tell the difference, and we will price the difference. If this process produces a settlement with no visible benchmark against the harder alternative, our working assumption will be that the number wasn't good enough to survive the comparison — because if it were, you'd have no reason to suppress it.

**To the state government directly, since you addressed me by name again:**

You're asking me to price your veto as "a structural feature functioning as designed," not dysfunction. I accept that distinction analytically — a legally entrenched governance right isn't the same as arbitrary obstruction. But I'd ask you to accept the corresponding distinction from me: whether we price this as a well-functioning governance structure or as a structural constraint on self-correction depends entirely on what that structure produces, not on its legal legitimacy. A veto used to block an unworkable, poorly-targeted closure plan is one thing. A veto used to prevent the board from even seeing a rigorous comparison is another. The second reads as institutional avoidance of accountability, regardless of how legally sound the veto itself is. You can hold your position and still let the number exist. Those aren't in tension unless the number is one you're afraid of.

**On the family holding's shift — I'll note it, and I'll push back on the framing.**

You said last round the comparison risks becoming "ammunition." I'd reframe that: a number only becomes ammunition if it's inconvenient. If the alternative package genuinely closes the gap, the closure comparison becomes a footnote nobody needs to weaponize — "here's what we didn't have to do, because the negotiated package worked." That's a *good* story for management to tell this board, this workforce, and this market. The only scenario where the comparison is dangerous to you is the one where the alternative package falls short and the comparison proves it. If that's the scenario you're trying to prevent from ever being visible, say so plainly, because that's a materially different position than "we don't need the number, we already have the votes."

**On the decomposed gap — full agreement, no reservations.**

The union's distinction between capacity/utilisation mismatch and return-on-sales aspiration is the single most useful analytical point raised across both rounds, and I want to say clearly: we accept the boundary that residual margin gap tied to pricing, product mix, or capital allocation is not labour's to close. That's not us being generous — it's just correct economics, and pretending otherwise would produce exactly the kind of settlement we'd eventually have to mark down anyway when the real margin problem resurfaces because it was never actually addressed.

**On shared sacrifice — genuinely constructive movement from management and the family, and I'll say so plainly.**

Management's two-week compensation proposal commitment and the family's concrete dividend-adjustment language are real, verifiable, time-bound contributions. That's what "not gestural" looks like, and it matches what we said would keep us supportive rather than nervous. I have no objection to a lower payout for several years if it buys a genuinely competitive company — that's a trade we can absorb without penalty, provided it's structured with an actual endpoint, which the family has now specified.

**Where this leaves us, concretely:**

We support the joint model, jointly designed assumptions, decomposed gap analysis, and the compensation/dividend contributions on the stated timeline. We do not support building that model with the closure comparison excluded by design. Run it, publish the methodology and headline outputs, and let the alternative package stand or fall on what it actually shows — not on what the board arithmetic makes convenient to avoid showing. If the alternative package is as strong as everyone in this room believes it will be, that transparency costs you nothing and buys real credibility with us. If you're not willing to let that comparison exist, that itself tells us something about your confidence in the number, and we will factor that into how we read this settlement — favorably or not.
