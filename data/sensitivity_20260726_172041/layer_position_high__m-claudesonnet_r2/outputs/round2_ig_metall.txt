I want to build on what the works council just laid out, because I agree with its substance, but I need to add the union's specific instruments to this, since they're distinct from board leverage and they change the calculus for everyone in this room, including capital markets.

**On the cost model: our participation has a price, and I want to name it precisely.**

We'll bring our economists. But I want to respond directly to management's framing that closures should be "tested" as a scenario alongside alternatives. Here is the problem with that, stated in bargaining terms rather than governance terms: the union does not need the model to tell us closures are off the table. That is not an output we're waiting for — it is a condition of our continued restraint from further escalation. If the model is designed to produce a menu that includes closures as a live option, we will treat that as a signal that this process is not the good-faith negotiation it's being presented as, and we will respond in the register we actually control: further strike action, escalated beyond warning strikes, coordinated across sites. I say this not as a threat for its own sake but because I want everyone at this table pricing that possibility accurately, the same way the investors asked us to price theirs.

**On Institutional Investors' point about the state's veto reading as a "structural constraint":**

I'd extend the works council's answer. You're pricing the risk of a company that "can't self-correct." Price also the risk of a company that tries to self-correct by breaking a decades-old job-security pledge unilaterally, in a country where that triggers not just board deadlock but the full weight of German collective-bargaining law and IG Metall-style coordinated action. This isn't a company operating in a jurisdiction where labour cost can simply be cut and workers absorb it. Tariff autonomy is a real constitutional feature here, not a negotiating flourish. If this goes to sustained confrontation, you are not looking at a quarter of disruption — you are looking at production stoppages that compound, supplier network knock-on effects, and reputational damage in a market where "the company broke its word to its workforce" is a headline that follows the brand for years. That is a cost. Price it.

**On management's "provisionally met" shared sacrifice — I'll push exactly where the works council pushed, and add a deadline.**

I want a concrete management compensation proposal — numbers, mechanism, duration — on the table in this round or the next, not "pending figures." Here is why the sequencing matters to us specifically: our members are already losing pay through warning strikes to hold this position. Every week that management's contribution stays an abstraction while our contribution is being litigated in granular detail is a week that erodes trust in the shared-sacrifice premise itself. If, in a month, we have a fully costed labour-contribution package and management still has a "principle," that asymmetry becomes its own grievance, and it will show up in how hard we escalate.

**Where I differ slightly from the works council, to be transparent about it:**

They've said the model should test only non-closure ways of closing a residual gap. I'd go further and say: we should also insist the model make explicit and separate what portion of any "gap" is structural overcapacity versus what portion is a target return-on-sales figure that management itself set. Those are different problems. A capacity/utilisation gap can be closed by shift models, output reallocation, and attrition — we've offered that. A return-on-sales target is a profitability aspiration, and if part of what's being called "the gap" is actually about restoring a specific margin number rather than fixing a real capacity mismatch, then labour cost cannot be the primary lever for closing it — that is a capital structure and pricing question, not a headcount question. I want that distinction made explicit in the model's design, because otherwise "the gap" becomes an elastic figure that labour is asked to close regardless of its actual source.

**To the family holding and the state, briefly:**

You've both said closures can't get two-thirds. Good — then stop letting the process treat closures as a serious variable rather than a dead letter. Every week spent modelling a scenario that cannot pass this board is a week not spent stress-testing the alternative-measures package at the scale actually required. That's not us protecting our comfort. It's us telling you the board arithmetic you yourselves stated is real.

**Restated plainly:** no compulsory redundancies, no plant closures, no dismantling of the wage framework. We came with a genuine offer — labour-cost contribution, capacity reduction, shift changes. We want the same specificity and the same clock applied to management's and capital's side of the ledger. Meet that, and the model proceeds with real cooperation. Don't, and you'll find out what tariff autonomy means in practice, not in theory.
