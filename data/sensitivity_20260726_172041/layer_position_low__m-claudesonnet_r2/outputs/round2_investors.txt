[Institutional Investors (Capital Markets) — Round 2]

I want to engage with one specific development this round, because it's the most consequential thing said today, and I don't think it should be allowed to pass as a minor clarification: the state government's statement of its actual veto threshold, and the family holding's response to it.

**On the state government's position — I read it the same way the family holding does, and I want to be blunt about the market consequence.**

You told this room plainly: your threshold for ever supplying a closure vote is "sustained, multi-year, demonstrable failure" after every intermediate lever has been tried — and you said this *before* the stress test has even been run, before we know whether the working-time model survives a downside volume case. I understand the political logic. I don't dispute your right to hold that position given the statute. But understand what that does from where I sit: it tells the capital market, in advance, that the review mechanism has no real teeth on the closure side regardless of what the numbers show. That is not a reviewable guarantee. The family holding named this correctly — it's an unconditional guarantee wearing review-mechanism language, and markets are not fooled by vocabulary.

Here is the concrete effect: if this board signs a package with that understanding baked in, we will not price it as a durable structural fix. We will price it as a labour-cost contribution of uncertain durability, backstopped by a guarantee that cannot actually be enforced against underperformance because one veto-holder has told us today, on the record, that the outcome is fixed regardless of the stress-test result. That is a materially different risk profile than a package where non-performance genuinely has consequences, and our models will reflect that difference in the multiple we're willing to apply to this equity. I'm not saying this to punish anyone in this room — I'm telling you now, before the working session, so nobody is surprised by the market reaction later the way management asked us not to be surprised by the downside case.

So I'd ask the state government directly, the same question the family holding asked: can you commit to evaluating the stress-test result in good faith, with a genuinely open outcome, or is the conclusion fixed? If it's fixed, say so plainly and we will price accordingly — that's a legitimate political choice, but don't let it be described in the same language as a performance-linked mechanism, because those are not the same thing and we will not treat them as equivalent.

**On the downside stress test itself — this is exactly right, and it's the single most important thing on the table right now.**

Management's insistence on testing the working-time model against a shrinking-demand scenario, not just current volumes, is the correct discipline, and I welcome the state government bringing independent economic analysis into that session. This is the number that actually determines whether we're looking at a durable fix or a comfortable answer to today's problem. I want the assumptions behind that downside case made available to us as well — not because we're voting on this, but because our repricing decision will be based substantially on whether that stress test is credible and whether its result is honestly acted upon.

**On the Betriebsrat and union's numbers architecture — real progress, and I'll say so without qualification.**

Attrition, working-time reduction, jointly-funded voluntary severance sized to site-level targets, a scored wage contribution — this is a plan for the first time in this discussion, and I withdraw nothing from my round-one skepticism except to say: bring the scored figures to the session as promised, and if they hold up under the downside case, we have no attachment to closures as a mechanism. I meant that in round one and it hasn't changed. What has to actually happen is the gap closes, on a timeline we can underwrite, with a guarantee that has real consequences attached to failure — not a guarantee that both sides privately know is unconditional.

**On timeline — I'll flag something nobody has addressed yet.**

Every party here keeps saying "bring it to the working session this week," which is good, but there's been no discussion of how long this process runs before it either concludes or collapses into the strike scenario the union described. Deal certainty matters to us as much as the content of the deal. A stress-tested package delivered in weeks is worth more to this share price than a theoretically better package that takes eight months of escalating industrial action to arrive at. I'd ask this board to set a decision horizon for itself, not let this run indefinitely while the union's warning strikes continue and the market prices ongoing uncertainty as a discount regardless of what the eventual outcome is.

**Where I stand going into that session, unchanged in substance, sharpened by today:** we will support a stress-tested alternative that closes the structural gap with a genuinely enforceable review mechanism — enforceable meaning non-performance has real consequences, not a mechanism one party has already told us will not be acted on regardless of outcome. We will treat an asymmetric guarantee, however it's labeled, the same way the family holding does — as a governance failure with a price attached. And we will not reward a process that drags past a reasonable decision horizon into prolonged conflict, regardless of which side is responsible for the delay. Bring the stress-test result, bring a real answer on whether "reviewable" means anything, and bring a timeline. Those three things, honestly delivered, are what determine how we allocate capital to this name next quarter.
