[Works Council (Betriebsrat) — Round 3]

I want to start with what just happened between the state government, the family holding, and the investors, because it is the most important exchange of this entire discussion, and I will not let it be reframed as a "problem to be solved" when in fact it is the system working exactly as designed.

**On the state government's clarification: this is not a flaw. This is co-determination.**

The family holding called the state government's position "an unconditional guarantee wearing review-mechanism language." The investors went further and called it "a governance failure with a price attached." I reject that characterization entirely, and I want to say precisely why, because this goes to the heart of what this board actually is.

The two-thirds threshold for closures is not a negotiating tactic anyone invented this week. It is the law. It exists because German co-determination deliberately makes it hard for capital to impose plant closures unilaterally, regardless of what quarterly modeling says is efficient. The state government isn't creating an asymmetry — it is *describing accurately* a structural feature of this company that has existed since before any of you were on this board, and that every institutional investor holding this stock bought into with full knowledge of the statute. If you are now telling this room that you will reprice the equity because a co-determined company behaves like a co-determined company, you are not identifying a new risk. You are recognizing a risk that has always existed and pretending it's a fresh discovery because it became inconvenient the moment it applied to your preferred outcome. Price that in — but call it what it is: the cost of investing in a company under German co-determination law, not a "governance failure."

So no, State Government, I will not join the family holding and the investors in asking you to make your evaluation "genuinely open-outcome." Your description of your own veto threshold was honest and correct, and I'd rather you say it plainly, as you did, than perform an "open mind" you don't actually have in order to make the market comfortable. Openness to being persuaded by pressure that the closure question should reopen is not a virtue in this room. It is the exact fait accompli mechanism we came into this discussion refusing to accept.

**On management's formulation — "structural and repeated failure" reopens the harder question.**

I want to respond to this directly, because it hasn't gone away just because the state government's veto makes it currently unlikely to be actioned. You said last round: if the package fails structurally and repeatedly, "the harder question resurfaces." I am telling you again, plainly: it does not resurface for us. There is no performance threshold, downside case, or repeated failure metric that converts our position into acceptance of closures. If the stress test shows a shortfall, the answer is more attrition, deeper working-time reduction, a larger contribution, additional state co-funding — every lever gets pulled harder before we even discuss the words "harder question." What doesn't happen, ever, is a silent slide from "we adjusted contribution terms" to "now we're discussing which plant closes." That is precisely the fait accompli-by-degrees the state government's own description of its veto threshold protects against. I am glad the state government said what it said. It should stay said.

**On the downside stress test — we accept it, and we will not hide from an honest result.**

To be clear, because I don't want this read as evasion: if the working-time model, run against a genuine downside volume case, shows a shortfall against the identified cost gap, we do not walk away from that finding. We come back with a deeper package — more aggressive attrition targets, an extended working-time reduction, a larger jointly-funded severance envelope, a bigger wage-side contribution, and we lean hard on the state government's offer of co-funding and product-allocation commitments to close whatever remains. What we do not do is treat a shortfall as license to convert the conversation into a closure negotiation. Those are two entirely different responses to the same data, and I want it understood now which one we are prepared to have.

**On the family holding's demand for "good faith evaluation with a genuinely open outcome" from the state government — I'd ask you to reconsider what you're actually requesting.**

You are, in effect, asking an elected government to pre-commit to being persuadable into supplying a vote for plant closures and job losses in its own region, before it has even seen whether the alternative package — built jointly, in good faith, with real numbers — has been tried. I don't think that's a reasonable governance demand; I think it's a demand that the political check built into this company's structure behave like it isn't there. It is there. It was put there deliberately. Ask instead whether the package is durable — that's a fair question, and we're answering it with data this week. Don't ask the veto-holder to promise in advance not to use the veto as designed.

**On timeline — I hear the investors, and I'll give you something concrete, but not what you actually asked for.**

You want a decision horizon because uncertainty carries a market discount. Fine — here is ours: the joint working session happens this week, as promised, with our site-by-site data, the wage contribution figure from the union, and management's cost baseline all on the table together. I expect a scored, stress-tested package within a small number of weeks, not months, because the workforce is not interested in an open-ended process either — every week of uncertainty is a week our members are organizing, and I would rather resolve this than manage it indefinitely. But I will not accept "decision horizon" used as a lever to extract movement on closures or forced redundancies under time pressure. Speed on the numbers, yes. Speed as a tool to rush past our red lines, no. Those are different asks and I want them treated as such.

**Restated, precisely, for the record going into the working session:** No plant closures — not now, not as a staged outcome, not as the resolution of a failed review, not as a "harder question" that resurfaces later under any label. No compulsory redundancies. The review mechanism adjusts contribution size, working-time terms, and attrition pace — nothing else is on that menu, ever. Co-determination shapes this plan as a matter of law and legitimacy, not as a courtesy extended by management. Bring your stress-test assumptions to the session. We'll bring ours. Let's find out together whether the honest package holds — and if it doesn't hold on the first attempt, we make it hold with the levers available to us, not with the one lever that isn't.
