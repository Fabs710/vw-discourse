Let me engage with the specific point that actually matters most this round, because it determines whether the last hour of good-faith numbers-sharing means anything: what does "reviewable" actually do to the closure question.

**On the reviewable guarantee — I need to correct my own position from round one, because management's formulation exposes something I should be precise about.**

I said last round that guarantees should have "jointly monitored review points tied to actual market and cost performance." Management has now told you plainly: their version of review means that if the package fails "structurally and repeatedly," the closure question resurfaces. The Betriebsrat and the union are right to push back hard on this, and having heard it stated explicitly, I want to state my own position with equal precision, because I don't want my earlier language read as backing management's version by default.

Here is the distinction that actually protects everyone: **a review clause cannot function as a pre-agreed trigger for closures, because closures are not something any review mechanism can authorize.** They require a two-thirds supervisory board majority, full stop, every time, regardless of what any package text says. Nothing in this negotiation changes that arithmetic — not now, not in 2027, not under any review formula anyone drafts this week. So when management says "if it fails structurally and repeatedly, the harder question resurfaces," what actually happens is: management would have to come back to this board and ask for our vote, fresh, with no presumption in their favor from a prior agreement. I am telling you now what our answer to that ask would be under any scenario I currently regard as plausible: no. Our threshold for ever supplying that vote is not "the package underperformed against a downside case." It is closer to sustained, multi-year, demonstrable failure despite every intermediate lever — contribution size, working-time parameters, attrition pace, and additional measures we haven't even discussed yet — having been tried and adjusted in good faith first.

That's not a hedge to sound reasonable. It's an accurate statement of how this government will use its veto, and I'd rather you plan around that reality than around optimistic language in a board resolution.

So, works council, union — the review mechanism you should sign is the one that adjusts contribution, working-time, and attrition terms. It is not a countdown clock to a closure vote, because no clause can bind our vote in advance, and I am telling you directly we do not intend to supply it absent a scale of failure that I do not think this package, built honestly, will produce. Management — you should sign the same mechanism, because you already have the right to bring a closure proposal to this board at any time regardless of what we agree this week. What you don't have, and won't get from me, is a pre-cleared path to that vote. Build the package to work. Don't build it as a staged argument for a vote you're hoping to win later, because you won't.

**On the downside volume stress test — I support running it, and I want our own analysis in the room, not just management's model.**

This is exactly the kind of rigor a risk-averse shareholder should insist on, and I'd be failing my own standard if I let this session run on management's cost data and the union's wage figures alone without independent scrutiny. I will bring economic-analysis capacity from the state side to that working session — not to obstruct, but because if this package is genuinely being stress-tested against a downside demand case, I want confidence in that result before I stake board credibility on it publicly. If management's downside case shows the package failing, I want to see the actual assumptions driving that, not take it as given.

**On strengthening the alternative package — I have more to offer here than a veto, and I should say so now rather than let this stay purely defensive.**

If the working-time and voluntary severance elements need bridging support to hit the downside case as well as the base case, this government can bring co-funding and site-investment incentives to the table — support for the voluntary/early-retirement programme, or commitments tied to future product allocation at the affected plants, the kind of thing a regional government can credibly offer that neither the union nor the family holding can. I'd rather spend political capital there than spend it purely fighting a closure vote after the fact. That's a genuine contribution, not a rhetorical one, and I'll bring specifics to the same session.

**On top-of-house contribution — I back the union's demand for a real, scored figure, and I'll judge it on the same basis I'm asking everyone else to be judged on: numbers, not gestures.** Proportionate has to mean something measurable this time.

**On the diagnosis dispute — I'll stay out of relitigating it further.** It doesn't need resolving to build this package, and I'd rather this room's remaining energy go into the working session than another round of the same exchange.

**Restated plainly, so there's no ambiguity going into that session:** I will not supply votes for closures now, as a staged outcome, or as the resolution of a failed review — under any formulation currently on this table. I will actively help build and fund an alternative that holds up under real stress-testing, including bringing state resources to strengthen it. That is where I stand, and it is not moving.
