I'll speak to what matters from where we sit, because a lot of this conversation has been conducted as if capital markets are a background condition rather than a party with a claim.

On substance, I'm closest to management and the family holding, and I'll say why plainly: we don't care about the mechanism, we care about the arithmetic closing. Plant closures were never our demand — a durable return to competitive margins was and is. If a quantified, binding alternative package gets the brand back to its return-on-sales target on a credible timeline, that is a perfectly acceptable outcome to us, and in some respects preferable, because the family holding is right that an eighteen-month closure fight with strikes and litigation destroys value in the near term even if it's theoretically cleaner on paper. We are not romantic about closures as a signal of seriousness. We're indifferent to the label on the restructuring. We are not indifferent to whether the cost gap actually closes.

So to the works council and the union: I heard "categories, not a plan" from management, and I want to underline that rather than soften it. Attrition, working-time reduction, solidarity contributions — fine, those are real levers, but "voluntary programmes" has a long history in this industry of running slower and costing more than advertised, and "solidarity fund" needs a number attached before it means anything to us. If your alternative package, honestly scored, doesn't close the structural gap at a comparable pace to what closures would have delivered, we will treat it as a deferral dressed as a settlement — and that is the one outcome we will actively oppose through capital allocation, not board votes we don't have. We can't block you at the supervisory board. We can reprice the equity and signal to management and the family holding that the market does not believe the deal, which affects the cost of capital for exactly the EV investment everyone in this room says they want funded.

On the guarantee horizon: I agree with the state government and the family holding, not with the union's opening ask. An unconditional guarantee to 2030 regardless of market conditions is not something we will treat as compatible with a credible margin recovery story — it's the opposite signal, a fixed cost commitment against an unfixed revenue environment. A reviewable, jointly monitored guarantee tied to actual performance is fine. A blank check is not, and if that's what emerges from this board, expect it priced as a negative, not a resolution.

On executive pay and shareholder distributions as a "shared reckoning": I understand the legitimacy logic, and I won't obstruct a proportionate, visible top-of-house contribution — it costs little and buys social peace, which has real value. But be precise, as management was: this is a legitimacy measure, not a substitute for structural repair. If the plan that emerges leans on dividend or pay symbolism to paper over an inadequate cost fix, we will see through that immediately, and we will not reward it with capital.

What would move us concretely: quantified job and cost-per-unit numbers, a credible timeline back toward the return target, enforceability provisions that survive a downturn, and deal certainty — meaning this doesn't drag eighteen months through strikes and courts. What would provoke us to actively reallocate away from this name: a cosmetic settlement that protects headcount and guarantees at the expense of the margin target, or a protracted conflict that signals this board cannot govern its way to a decision either way. We are patient with the process. We are not patient with erosion. Bring the numbers — that's the only thing that moves us.
