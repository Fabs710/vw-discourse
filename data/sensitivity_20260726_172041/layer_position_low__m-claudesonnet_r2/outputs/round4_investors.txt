**On the state government's "silence on closures" proposal — I'll support it, and I want to be precise about why, because it's not a neutral drafting compromise from where I sit, it's a genuine improvement in how this package will be priced.**

A document littered with adversarial clauses — one side inserting "closures remain available," the other inserting "closures are permanently excluded" — is itself a negative signal. It tells the market that the parties spent their negotiating capital on symbolism instead of substance, and that the underlying package might not be strong enough to stand on its own without a flag planted on either side of it. Silence on this point, with the substance — attrition curve, self-scaling contribution, top-of-house figure, state co-funding — fully specified and load-bearing, is the version of this agreement I can actually value. I'd score a document built the state government's way more highly than either extreme, not because I care about the legal niceties, but because it signals the parties are done fighting over language and have moved to fighting over whether the numbers are real. That's the fight I want this room having.

**On management retaining its statutory right regardless of what's written — I still back this, but I want to correct something in how the Works Council and Union are now describing our position.**

You've characterized our support for management's retained option as us "wanting it both ways" — calling the self-scaling contribution the most investable idea in the room while also wanting the closure fallback preserved. I don't think that's a contradiction, and the state government's own framing this round actually resolves it cleanly: the retained option isn't a term anyone is negotiating into existence. It exists in law regardless of what any of us prefer. The real question was never "should management have this option" — it always has it. The real question was whether the document should pretend otherwise. Now that the state government has proposed silence, that question dissolves. Nobody has to trade confidence in the mechanism for confidence in the fallback, because the document isn't asking anyone to choose. I'd ask the Works Council and Union to take the state government's point seriously: what you're actually protected by is the political reality on record in this room, not a clause. That protection is real. I'd rather underwrite a company where that's true than one where a symbolic clause papers over the same reality.

**On the attrition numbers — genuinely useful, but I want to push harder on timing than even management did.**

A third to two-fifths of headcount reduction through attrition to 2030 is a real figure, but management is right that the curve matters more than the total, and I want to be blunter about why: my mandate is weighted toward near- and medium-term margins, not a 2030 endpoint. If the bulk of that attrition sits in years four and five, this package does very little for the return-on-sales target that's actually driving the current share price discount. I want the year-by-year curve at the working session, scored against the near-term cost gap specifically, and I want the state government's front-loaded bridge funding sized against that same near-term number, not the aggregate. If the near-term relief is thin, I will say so plainly, regardless of how strong the 2030 figure looks — a package that fixes the problem in year five while margins erode for four more years first is not the durable fix I need, and I will price accordingly.

**On the self-scaling contribution's ceiling — I want to align with the Family Holding's flag and sharpen it.**

The mechanism is the strongest instrument on the table and I said so last round without hedging. But a cap sized against a moderate downside, not a genuinely severe one, is a mechanism that looks robust on paper and fails exactly when it matters. I want the ceiling stress-tested against management's actual worst-case scenario — not the base case, not a mild downside — before I treat this as the thing that makes the closure question moot in practice, as several parties have suggested it does. If the ceiling is undersized, the entire architecture the state government is proposing — substance carrying all the weight, silence on closures — doesn't hold, and the practical effect is that we're back to relying on a fallback that everyone agrees can't functionally be used. That's not an acceptable place to land. Size the ceiling honestly.

**On top-of-house — I'll add pressure alongside the Union and Works Council, for my own reasons.**

I want the figure at the next session too, not because of proportionality-as-fairness in itself, but because a top-of-house number that looks token relative to the workforce contribution is a market signal of its own — it tells analysts the board isn't serious about shared sacrifice, which affects how credible the whole package reads externally. Bring a real number, scored against the standard already agreed.

**Restated plainly:** the state government's silence proposal is the right frame, and I support it. Management's retained option costs nothing under that frame and I don't want it re-litigated as a symbolic fight. What determines my position next quarter is unchanged from round one: the attrition curve by year, the contribution mechanism's ceiling stress-tested against a genuine worst case, and a credible top-of-house figure. If those land solid, this is a package I'd back publicly. If the near-term relief is thin or the ceiling is soft, no amount of process goodwill changes my assessment — I will reprice on the substance, not the tone of the room.
