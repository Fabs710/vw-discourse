**On the union's challenge to our right to price the co-determination structure — I'll engage with this directly rather than let it stand unanswered.**

You're right that the statute predates this crisis and that anyone holding this equity knew the structure existed. I won't dispute that. But you're drawing the wrong conclusion from it. Knowing a risk exists in principle is not the same as knowing how it will resolve in a specific crisis, at a specific moment, with a specific package on the table. Three months ago, nobody in this room knew whether the state government's practical threshold for a closure vote was "moderate underperformance" or "sustained multi-year catastrophic failure." Now we know. That is new information, not a rediscovery of an old fact, and new information gets priced when it arrives — that's not leverage dressed as analysis, it's what markets do with any material disclosure, whether it comes from a 10-K or from a shareholder stating a governance position out loud in a negotiation. I'd make exactly the same point if the union had just told us, for the first time, precisely how low its strike threshold actually is. You'd expect us to price that too.

So: no apology for pricing it. But I'll concede the fairer version of your point — this is not a "governance failure" in the sense of something broken or illegitimate. It's a structural feature functioning exactly as designed. I'll retract that specific phrase from round two. What doesn't change is the practical consequence: a package built inside a structure where one veto-holder has told us its threshold is functionally unreachable in any near-term scenario carries a different risk profile than one where genuine consequences attach to failure. That's not a moral judgment on the state government. It's a fact about the distribution of outcomes we're being asked to underwrite capital against.

**On the self-scaling wage contribution — this is the single most investable idea produced in three rounds, and I want to say so without hedging.**

A contribution that automatically deepens against a defined downside trigger, without a fresh negotiation cycle each time, is exactly the kind of mechanism that converts a static concession into a genuine hedge against volume risk. This is the first thing in this discussion I'd actually underwrite a valuation premium for, conditional on the trigger metrics being real and the tranche sizing being material rather than token. I want the mechanics — trigger threshold, activation lag, tranche size relative to base — fully specified at the working session, and I want them stress-tested with the same rigor as the working-time model. If this holds up, it does more to narrow the scenario where closures ever become relevant than anything said by any veto-holder in this room, including the state government's own reassurances. Build this properly and you've done more for deal durability than another round of positional statements.

**On management and the family holding's shared position that the closure option stays live — I back this, and I want to explain why in financial terms, not governance terms.**

An alternative package that is good enough to work doesn't need a guarantee against ever being revisited to be credible to us. What we actually need is a credible commitment mechanism *within* the package — the self-scaling contribution is exactly that — not a foreclosure of management's tools regardless of outcome. A board that pre-commits never to consider its full range of options, irrespective of how badly a plan fails, is signaling something about its own confidence in the plan, and not a reassuring something. I'd read an unconditional foreclosure as a red flag, not a comfort, because it tells me the parties agreeing to it expect to need it. If the works council and union are as confident in this package as they sound, the retained option costs them nothing in practice — the family holding made this point and I don't think it's been answered on the merits, only restated as unacceptable.

**On the state government's threshold, taken at face value — I'll price it honestly, and it's not catastrophic, but it's not free either.**

I'm not going to keep pushing for a performative openness the state government has told us twice it doesn't have. Fine — noted, believed, priced. The practical effect is that the enforceability of "reviewable" sits almost entirely on the quality of the self-scaling mechanism and the working-time model, not on any residual closure leverage. That raises the bar for what the working session needs to produce. It doesn't disqualify the package in advance — but it means the numbers need to be genuinely rigorous, because there's less structural backstop if they're not.

**On timeline — four weeks is acceptable, and I'll add the same condition the family holding did: assumptions shown, not just conclusions.**

A four-week horizon with transparent assumptions is something we can hold this board accountable to publicly. What we will not accept is a four-week deadline that slips into eight without explanation, or a stress test whose downside-case assumptions are disclosed selectively. If the numbers come back solid and the self-scaling mechanism is real, we will treat this as a credible structural fix and price it accordingly — that's not a concession, that's the outcome we've been asking for since round one. If the numbers are soft, or the downside case is quietly built on optimistic demand assumptions to make the package look sufficient, we will see through that and reprice regardless of how the guarantee language reads.

**Restated plainly:** we have no attachment to closures as a mechanism — we never did. We have an attachment to a credible, durable, quantified return to competitive margins, with a review structure that has genuine consequences attached to failure rather than a predetermined non-outcome dressed as review. The self-scaling contribution is real progress toward that. Management and the family holding retaining the closure option, rather than surrendering it unconditionally, is not a threat to that outcome — it's part of what keeps the package honest. Bring the scored figures and the stress-test assumptions in four weeks. That result determines our position next quarter, not the positions restated today.
