Let me address what actually moved this round, because two things happened that bear directly on how I'll advise the supervisory board, and one thing didn't move that needed to.

**On the state government's restatement — I take you at your word, and I want to be precise about what that means for me, not perform disappointment I don't feel.**

You've now said twice, in the same terms, what your threshold is. I believe you. I'm not going to keep asking you to describe an openness you've told us you don't have — the union is right that asking a statutory veto-holder to pre-commit to persuadability is a strange thing to demand, and I withdraw the framing of it as a request you need to satisfy. What I will not withdraw is the underlying point: a mechanism where one veto-holder's threshold is functionally unreachable by any near-term outcome is a structural fact this board has to build around, not a temporary negotiating posture. That's not a criticism of you. It's a description of the governance reality I have to price into my own judgment about whether the package we build is genuinely durable or is durable only because the alternative was never really available. Those are different kinds of "durable," and I want this board to know which one it's getting.

Practically, this changes what I need from the working session. If the political check means the harder question effectively doesn't reopen absent catastrophe, then the entire weight of making this work sits on the quality of the package itself — the attrition numbers, the working-time model, and especially the union's self-scaling contribution. There is no fallback. I want that understood plainly by everyone building it this week: this has to actually work, because the mechanism everyone keeps calling a "review" will not function as a corrective if it doesn't.

**On management retaining the right to bring a closure proposal regardless — I back this, and I want to say why directly, since it's the one place I'll take a clear side today.**

Not because I'm attached to closures. I've said twice now I have no preference for them over a package that works. I back it because a board that has voluntarily surrendered its own ability to consider its full range of options, in advance, regardless of performance, is a board that has given away something the family holding is not willing to give away on the ownership side either. Management is not asking anyone to agree closures will happen. Management is asking not to be asked to promise they never will, no matter what. That is a reasonable thing for an executive team to hold onto, and I'd want the same reserved for the shareholder side if the position were mirrored. Works Council, Union — I understand why this reads to you as a suspended sentence. I'd ask you to consider that from where I sit, an unconditional foreclosure looks like exactly the same thing pointed the other direction: a commitment that removes accountability from one side while the company and its shareholders carry the downside if it turns out to be wrong. I am not going to ask this board to sign that, whichever direction it points.

**On the union's self-scaling contribution — this is the most useful thing produced in three rounds, and I want to say so plainly rather than let it pass as one line among many.**

A contribution that automatically deepens against a defined downside trigger, without requiring the whole agreement to be reopened each time, is precisely the kind of mechanism that could make the state government's high threshold genuinely safe rather than merely convenient. If this is built with real trigger metrics and sized honestly, it does more to protect this company from a future crisis than anything else on the table, including closures — because it responds automatically to conditions rather than requiring another eighteen months of confrontation to activate. I want this built out in full at the working session, and I want management to score it rigorously against the downside case, as promised. If it holds, my own answer to "should the harder question resurface" gets considerably easier, in the direction the works council and union want — not because anyone conceded a principle, but because the mechanism made the question moot in practice.

**On timeline — four weeks is acceptable to me as well, with one condition attached.**

I want the stress-test assumptions shared transparently with all parties in this room before the result is presented, not just the conclusion after the fact. A number without visible assumptions is not something I can evaluate, and I suspect the union and works council would say the same about management's cost baseline. Show the work, all sides, before the verdict — that's not distrust, it's the only way this board can actually judge whether the package is real.

**Where I stand, restated with more edge than last round:** I will support a stress-tested, self-correcting package that closes the structural gap on a durable basis, including one where the practical likelihood of closures becomes remote because the mechanism works. I will not support, and will say so to the full board myself, any framework — from any side of this table — that locks in a fixed outcome irrespective of performance, whether that's an unconditional guarantee against closures or a guarantee that closures are inevitable. Bring me the stress test, with assumptions shown. That result, not the positions restated today, is what I'll act on.
