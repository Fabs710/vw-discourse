We stand with the Betriebsrat on the essentials, and I want to be precise about where our mandate sits, because the union's role here is distinct from the works council's — we bring the strike weapon and tariff autonomy, and we intend to use both if this goes the way management's briefings to the financial press suggest it will.

No compulsory redundancies. No plant closures. These are not opening bids we expect to trade away in month four of talks — they are the line. I say that having already led warning strikes at multiple sites since the peace obligation lapsed. Members did not walk out for a "constructive dialogue." They walked out because they've watched cost pressure get manufactured into a crisis narrative before, and they know exactly how this script usually ends: workforce concessions banked early, guarantees walked back later, executives citing "unforeseen market conditions" for the next round.

So let me address management's framing directly, because it will come. "The German cost base is uncompetitive" is doing a lot of work in that sentence, and it conveniently omits who set that cost base, who approved the capital allocation into an EV strategy that hasn't delivered, and who is still collecting dividends and board compensation while asking the shop floor to absorb the correction. If this is genuinely a shared crisis, I expect a shared reckoning — management pay, shareholder distributions, and capital expenditure discipline should be on the table alongside labour cost, not as a rhetorical afterthought but as line items with numbers attached. Fairness isn't a slogan for us; it's the precondition for anything we sign.

Here is what we already put on the table, and what we're prepared to defend seriously: a labour-cost contribution — yes, that can mean forgoing bonuses, it can mean a solidarity fund, it can mean working-time adjustments that reduce capacity without severing anyone's employment. We are not pretending the market hasn't shrunk. We are not pretending zero adjustment is realistic. What we are saying is that adjustment through attrition, voluntary programmes, working-time reduction, and a negotiated financial contribution is available to management right now, today, if they want it — in exchange for binding site and job guarantees running to 2030, not a memo of understanding management's lawyers can reinterpret at the next downturn.

What moves us: a package where the pain is genuinely distributed — management and owners contributing visibly, not just workers — and where the security we get in return is durable and enforceable, not aspirational. That is a real offer, and it's on the table now.

What does not move us, under any framing, dressed in any language about competitiveness or transformation: closures, forced layoffs, or an open-ended "we'll revisit guarantees later" clause. If management's position tomorrow is "closures are non-negotiable, discuss the rest," there is nothing to discuss — we escalate, and we escalate in coordination with the works council's board vote, because that combination is precisely why several parties in this room hold blocking positions rather than one. Use that as you will. We intend to.
