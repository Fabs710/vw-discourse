**On the fight over whether closure language stays formally "live" in the document — I want to reframe what's actually being contested, because I think both sides are arguing past each other.**

Management, you're right that you don't need anyone's permission to bring a proposal to this board. That's not a negotiated right — it's your statutory position as an executive team, and no framework accord, side letter, or Tarifvertrag annex can take it away or grant it. Nothing the works council or union signs changes your legal ability to propose a closure. So when you say you won't sign anything that "forecloses" that ability, I'd ask you to consider: what exactly are you insisting on including, versus what already exists in law regardless of what's written?

And Works Council, Union — the reverse point applies to you. No document you sign can make my veto threshold any more or less real than it already is. I've told this room plainly, twice, what my threshold is. That doesn't change based on whether a side letter contains the word "closure." My vote is not contingent on contractual language; it's contingent on my own judgment of the facts when and if such a proposal ever reaches this board.

So here is what I think the actual dispute is about, stripped of its legal framing: it's about **symbolism and signal**, not enforceability. Management wants the document to visibly preserve the option because removing it feels like surrendering leverage it holds independent of the document anyway. The workforce wants the document to visibly foreclose the option because its presence, however legally inert, reads as bad faith to people who have to ratify a wage cut. Both of those are real political facts. Neither is really about what the document can actually do.

**My proposal: stop drafting language about closures at all, in either direction.**

Don't write "closures remain available." Don't write "closures are permanently excluded." Write what you've actually built: the attrition targets, the working-time model, the self-scaling contribution with its trigger mechanics, the top-of-house figure, my co-funding commitments, the product and investment allocation guarantees tied to specific sites. Silence on the closure question changes nothing legally — management retains what it retains, my veto stands where it stands — but it removes the one clause that turns a working document into a battlefield over a fight that isn't actually being adjudicated by that document's wording.

Management, I don't think this costs you anything you actually have today. Works Council, Union, I understand this isn't the unconditional guarantee you'd prefer in writing — but you have something better than a clause: you have my stated position, on the record, in front of the Family Holding and Investors, who have now accepted it as genuine rather than performed. That's a stronger protection than language in an annex that a future board could seek to reinterpret anyway.

**On the Family Holding and Investors' "confident side shouldn't fear the fallback" framing — I want to add a different angle than the Works Council's.**

I don't think this is really about confidence in the mechanism at all. I think it's about who bears the cost if the mechanism underperforms in a way nobody modeled. If it fails, the workforce bears wage cuts they already accepted plus, potentially, their jobs. Shareholders bear a valuation hit. Those are not symmetric costs, and I'd ask you to stop treating the argument as though they were. That asymmetry is exactly why my threshold is set where it is, and exactly why I think the site-investment and product-allocation commitments I'm bringing matter more here than another round of arguing over clause wording — they're a tangible, bankable commitment the workforce can hold against the downside, not a rhetorical concession from either side.
