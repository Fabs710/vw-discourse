1. Context: rescuing a collapsing carmaker

This section gives the background needed to understand the decision. It describes how such a decision works; individuals appear only at the level of stakeholder roles. This is presented as a live decision whose outcome is open.

1.1 The company. At the centre of this decision is one of the world's largest carmakers, an industrial icon of its home country with a vast network of plants, dealers, suppliers, and hundreds of thousands of current and retired workers. A deep recession and a credit freeze have collapsed vehicle sales; the company is burning cash and, without external support, is weeks from failure. Its collapse would cascade through a shared supplier base and could take much of the domestic industry down with it.

1.2 The situation. The national legislature declined to authorise a dedicated rescue. The executive branch stepped in with emergency bridge loans and required the company to produce a plan demonstrating long-term viability. The company's plan - cuts to brands, plants, and costs, but no fundamental balance-sheet restructuring - has just been rejected by the government's task force as not aggressive enough. The task force has forced the resignation of the chief executive, installed new leadership, and granted only sixty days of working capital for the company and its stakeholders to agree a far deeper restructuring. Two constraints frame everything: the company owes a very large funding obligation to a retiree health trust negotiated with the industrial union, and it carries an even larger amount of unsecured bond debt held by dispersed investors, including many ordinary savers. The task force insists that any rescue requires sacrifice from every stakeholder - management, labour, and creditors alike - and has made clear that if consensual terms cannot be reached in time, it is prepared to fund the company through an expedited court-supervised process that can impose terms.

1.3 The decision. Within the sixty-day window, the parties must settle the shape of the restructuring: how much of the labour cost structure and the retiree health obligation is renegotiated, and in what form the trust's claim is honoured; what recovery the unsecured bondholders receive for their claims, and whether they accept it voluntarily; how deep the cuts to plants, brands and the dealer network go; whether a neighbouring government co-funds the rescue and on what conditions; and whether the restructuring is completed consensually out of court or through the court-supervised route. Ownership of the restructured company - who ends up holding its equity and in what proportions - is itself part of the bargain.

2. The parties

The following stakeholders have a claim on the decision: the government task force controlling the only available financing; the company's management under newly installed leadership; the industrial union together with the retiree health trust; the committee of unsecured bondholders; and the neighbouring governments weighing co-funding to protect their domestic plants. Their interests are only partially aligned, and the task force holds decisive financial leverage while labour and creditors each hold consent that matters.

3. The question

The stakeholders must deliberate the terms of the restructuring within the sixty-day deadline: the distribution of sacrifice among labour, creditors and the public purse; the ownership of the restructured company; and the path - consensual or court-supervised - by which it is done. The outcome is open and is the subject of this discussion.
